# Unit 3: Sedatives, muscle relaxants, psychiatric drugs, anticonvulsants and other CNS drugs

This is the second half of the large INC Unit III (drugs acting on the nervous system). Unit 2 covered pain, anaesthesia and gases; this unit covers drugs that calm or steady the brain and the muscles: hypnotics and sedatives, skeletal muscle relaxants, antipsychotics and mood stabilisers, antidepressants, antianxiety drugs, anticonvulsants, drugs for brain-wasting (neurodegenerative) disorders, stimulants, and the handling of alcohol and methyl-alcohol poisoning. For each group learn the action, uses, main adverse effects and the nurse's role. No doses are given; always take dose, strength and route from the written order.

## 3.1 Hypnotics and sedatives

- A **sedative** calms and reduces anxiety and activity.
- A **hypnotic** produces drowsiness and sleep.
The same drug is often a sedative in a small amount and a hypnotic in a larger one, so the two are studied together.

### Benzodiazepines (the main group)

**Action.** They attach to a site on the **GABA-A receptor** and make the brain's main calming transmitter, **GABA**, work better (more chloride enters the neuron, so it is harder to fire). They do not open the channel by themselves, which is why they are much safer in overdose than the older barbiturates.

| Short / intermediate acting | Long acting |
| --- | --- |
| Alprazolam, lorazepam, oxazepam, temazepam, midazolam | Diazepam, chlordiazepoxide, clonazepam, nitrazepam |

**Five actions ("the five A's")** — Anxiolytic (anti-anxiety), Anticonvulsant, Amnesic (loss of memory for events during the drug), muscle relaxant, and sedative-hypnotic.

**Uses.** Anxiety, short-term insomnia, status epilepticus and fits (lorazepam, diazepam, midazolam), alcohol withdrawal (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam), premedication and sedation for procedures (midazolam), muscle spasm.

**Adverse effects.** Drowsiness, poor coordination and memory, falls in the elderly, slurred speech; **tolerance and dependence** with long use; rebound anxiety and insomnia on stopping; breathing can be depressed when combined with opioids or alcohol.

**Antidote.** **Flumazenil** reverses benzodiazepines (used with care — it can cause fits in a dependent patient).

### Other hypnotics and sedatives

| Group | Examples | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Z-drugs" (non-benzodiazepine) | Zolpidem, zopiclone, eszopiclone | Act at the same receptor; for short-term insomnia; sleep-walking and next-day drowsiness possible |
| Barbiturates (old) | Phenobarbitone, thiopentone | **Open** the chloride channel directly → dangerous in overdose (coma, stopped breathing), strong enzyme inducers, high dependence; now mostly only phenobarbitone (epilepsy) and thiopentone (anaesthesia) |
| Melatonin receptor type | Melatonin, ramelteon | Help the sleep–wake rhythm; mild |
| Sedating antihistamine | Diphenhydramine, promethazine | Occasional use for sleep (Unit 1) |

**Nursing responsibilities (sedatives and hypnotics)**
- Assess sleep pattern and the reason for sleeplessness; try non-drug measures first (quiet, dark, no caffeine late, a fixed routine).
- Give a hypnotic at bedtime; raise the cot sides and keep the call bell within reach; help the patient to the toilet to prevent falls.
- Warn against alcohol and against driving or working with machines.
- Teach that these drugs are for short-term use and must not be stopped suddenly after long use (withdrawal fits, severe anxiety).
- Count and store controlled sedatives as the law and hospital policy require.

## 3.2 Skeletal muscle relaxants

These relax voluntary (skeletal) muscle. They are of two broad kinds.

```text
SKELETAL MUSCLE RELAXANTS
├─ Centrally acting (reduce spasm from the brain/cord)
│    Baclofen, tizanidine, diazepam, chlorzoxazone, methocarbamol
│    Use: muscle spasm, spasticity in cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, stroke, back pain
│
└─ Peripherally acting = NEUROMUSCULAR BLOCKERS (act at the end-plate)
     ├─ Non-depolarising (compete with acetylcholine): vecuronium, rocuronium,
     │    atracurium, pancuronium → reversed by neostigmine (+ atropine/glycopyrrolate)
     └─ Depolarising (over-stimulate then block): succinylcholine (suxamethonium)
          → short action; NOT reversed by neostigmine
```

**Neuromuscular blockers** are used in the operation theatre and ICU to relax muscle for surgery, intubation and ventilation. They **do not relieve pain and do not cause sleep** — the patient must also receive an anaesthetic and an analgesic, or they will be awake and paralysed. Breathing stops, so ventilation is essential.

- **Succinylcholine:** very fast and short; used for rapid intubation. Can cause muscle pains, a rise in potassium (dangerous in burns and crush injury), and is a trigger of **malignant hyperthermia** (treated with dantrolene).
- **Dantrolene** acts inside the muscle cell (blocks calcium release) and is used for malignant hyperthermia and severe spasticity.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Centrally acting relaxants cause drowsiness and low BP — the same fall and driving precautions as for sedatives; do not stop baclofen suddenly (rebound spasticity, fever).
- For neuromuscular blockers: these are theatre/ICU drugs; ensure airway, oxygen, ventilation and monitoring are ready; keep the reversal drug and resuscitation equipment at hand; **a paralysed patient may still feel and hear** — give sedation and explain, and protect the eyes and pressure areas.

## 3.3 Antipsychotics (neuroleptics) and mood stabilisers

Used for schizophrenia, mania, severe agitation and, in small amounts, for severe nausea and hiccups.

**Action.** They block **dopamine (D2) receptors** in the brain. Newer ones also block serotonin receptors.

| Type | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Typical (first generation) | Chlorpromazine, haloperidol, fluphenazine, trifluoperazine | Strong on positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions); more movement side effects |
| Atypical (second generation) | Olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, clozapine, aripiprazole | Fewer movement effects; help negative symptoms; more weight gain, raised sugar and lipids |

**Adverse effects**
- **Extrapyramidal side effects (EPS):** drug-induced parkinsonism (tremor, stiffness), acute dystonia (muscle spasm of the neck/eyes), akathisia (restlessness), and **tardive dyskinesia** (late, often lasting involuntary movements of the mouth and tongue). More common with typical drugs.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS):** rare, dangerous — high fever, severe muscle rigidity, confusion, unstable BP and pulse. **Stop the drug, cool the patient, report at once.**
- Sedation, low BP on standing, dry mouth and constipation (anticholinergic), weight gain, raised blood sugar and lipids, raised prolactin (breast changes, menstrual upset), prolonged QT on the ECG.
- **Clozapine** can cause a dangerous fall in white cells (**agranulocytosis**) — regular blood counts are required.

**Mood stabilisers**
- **Lithium** for bipolar disorder (mania and prevention). It has a **narrow safety margin**, so blood levels are checked. Early toxicity: tremor, nausea, diarrhoea, drowsiness; severe toxicity: coarse tremor, confusion, slurred speech, fits. Dehydration, low salt and diuretics and NSAIDs raise the level. Long use affects the thyroid and kidney. Teach steady salt and water intake and to report vomiting, diarrhoea or fever.
- **Valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine** (also antiepileptics, see 3.6) are used as mood stabilisers.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Watch for and report movement disorders, fever with rigidity (NMS), and in clozapine patients a sore throat or fever (infection).
- Check BP (especially on standing), weight, blood sugar and, where ordered, the ECG and drug levels.
- Rise slowly, sip water for dry mouth, use sunscreen (photosensitivity with chlorpromazine).
- Depot (long-acting injection) forms help patients who forget tablets; give deep IM and rotate sites.
- Support the patient and family, encourage adherence, and watch mood and suicide risk.

## 3.4 Antidepressants

**Action.** Most increase the brain levels of **serotonin and/or noradrenaline**. The mood lifts slowly over **2–4 weeks**, although side effects start at once — an important teaching point.

| Group | Examples | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **SSRIs** (first choice) | Fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine | Block serotonin reuptake; nausea, headache, sexual problems, early restlessness; safer in overdose |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine, duloxetine | Serotonin + noradrenaline; also used for nerve pain |
| **Tricyclics (TCAs)** | Amitriptyline, imipramine, nortriptyline | Effective but many anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, retention, constipation), low BP, **heart rhythm danger in overdose** |
| **MAO inhibitors** | Phenelzine, tranylcypromine; moclobemide (reversible) | Rarely used; a serious **tyramine ("cheese") reaction** — a dangerous rise in BP with aged cheese, red wine, pickled and fermented foods |
| Atypical / others | Mirtazapine, bupropion, trazodone | Mirtazapine aids sleep and appetite; bupropion also helps smoking cessation |

**Serotonin syndrome** — too much serotonin from combining serotonergic drugs: agitation, fever, sweating, tremor, fast pulse, rigidity. Stop the drugs and report.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Teach that the mood effect takes a few weeks; do not stop early; do not stop suddenly (discontinuation symptoms).
- **Watch suicide risk closely in the first weeks**, especially in young people — energy may return before the mood lifts.
- Do not combine serotonergic drugs without advice; on an MAOI, teach the food and drug restrictions.
- For TCAs, watch for constipation, retention and postural fall; keep only small supplies with at-risk patients.

## 3.5 Antianxiety drugs

- **Benzodiazepines** (see 3.1) — fast relief, but for short-term use because of dependence.
- **SSRIs/SNRIs** — the mainstay for long-term anxiety disorders.
- **Buspirone** — a non-sedating anti-anxiety drug; works slowly, no dependence.
- **Beta-blockers** (propranolol) — reduce the physical signs of anxiety (racing heart, tremor), useful for performance anxiety.
- **Hydroxyzine** — a sedating antihistamine used for anxiety.

## 3.6 Anticonvulsants (antiepileptic drugs)

Epilepsy is repeated fits from sudden excessive electrical discharge in the brain. These drugs **reduce that excess firing** (block sodium or calcium channels, or boost GABA).

| Drug | Useful in | Points for the nurse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phenytoin** | Generalised tonic–clonic and focal seizures, status epilepticus | Gum overgrowth, hair growth, acne, unsteadiness; many interactions (enzyme inducer); give IV slowly with saline (not glucose) and watch the ECG |
| **Carbamazepine** | Focal seizures, trigeminal neuralgia, bipolar | Dizziness, low sodium, blood and skin reactions; serious rash risk |
| **Sodium valproate** | Most seizure types, absence, mania | Weight gain, hair loss, tremor, liver and pancreas injury; **avoid in pregnancy — causes birth defects** |
| **Phenobarbitone** | Fits, neonatal seizures | Sedation, dependence |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Status epilepticus (lorazepam/diazepam), absence (clonazepam) | First-line to stop a fit |
| **Ethosuximide** | Absence (petit mal) only | Nausea |
| Newer drugs | Levetiracetam, lamotrigine, gabapentin, topiramate, oxcarbazepine | Fewer interactions; lamotrigine — serious rash if increased too fast; levetiracetam — mood changes |

**Status epilepticus** is a fit lasting a long time or repeated fits without recovery in between — an emergency. Protect the airway, give oxygen, turn to the side, do not force anything into the mouth, and give the ordered IV benzodiazepine, then a longer-acting drug such as phenytoin.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- **Never stop an antiepileptic suddenly** — this can trigger status epilepticus; teach steady, regular dosing.
- Safety during a fit: protect the head, loosen tight clothes, turn to the side when able, time the fit, do not restrain or put anything in the mouth; stay and record what happened.
- Teach: carry an identity card, take drugs at the same time daily, avoid triggers (missed sleep, alcohol, flashing lights), and report any rash at once.
- **Pregnancy:** women of childbearing age need advice before conceiving; folic acid is given and valproate avoided; report pregnancy early.
- Phenytoin: teach good gum care and dental visits.

## 3.7 Drugs for neurodegenerative and miscellaneous disorders

**Parkinson's disease** — loss of dopamine neurons causes tremor, rigidity and slow movement. Drugs restore dopamine or reduce the opposing acetylcholine.

| Group | Examples | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dopamine precursor | **Levodopa + carbidopa** | Most effective; carbidopa stops breakdown outside the brain so less nausea; "wearing-off" and involuntary movements after years; low BP; dark sweat/urine |
| Dopamine agonists | Bromocriptine, ropinirole, pramipexole | Nausea, sleepiness, impulse-control problems |
| MAO-B / COMT inhibitors | Selegiline, entacapone | Prolong dopamine action |
| Anticholinergics | Trihexyphenidyl, benztropine | Help tremor; dry mouth, retention |
| Others | Amantadine | Mild benefit |

**Alzheimer's disease / dementia** — **cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) raise acetylcholine; **memantine** (an NMDA blocker) for moderate–severe disease. They slow symptoms but do not cure. Side effects: nausea, slow pulse, diarrhoea.

**Nursing:** give levodopa on time (fixed schedule matters for mobility); it can be reduced by protein-rich meals and vitamin B6; watch for falls, low BP and confusion; support the carer; keep a safe, calm environment for people with dementia.

## 3.8 Central stimulants, ethyl alcohol and methyl-alcohol poisoning

**Central stimulants** speed up brain activity.

| Drug / class | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Caffeine | Mild stimulant; apnoea of prematurity |
| Methylphenidate, atomoxetine | Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) |
| Modafinil | Excessive daytime sleepiness/narcolepsy |
| Doxapram | Respiratory stimulant (rarely) |
| Amphetamines | Mostly drugs of abuse |

**Ethyl alcohol (ethanol)** is a CNS **depressant**, not a stimulant (the early "lively" feeling comes from loss of self-control). It is used medically as an antiseptic skin preparation, and as the **antidote for methanol and ethylene-glycol poisoning**.

**Methyl alcohol (methanol) poisoning** — methanol itself is not very harmful, but the body changes it into **formic acid**, which causes a dangerous acid build-up (metabolic acidosis), **blindness** (damage to the optic nerve) and death. It occurs with illicit/spurious liquor.
- Treatment: **fomepizole** or **ethanol** (both block the enzyme that makes formic acid, so methanol can be removed unchanged), **sodium bicarbonate** for the acidosis, **folinic acid**, and **haemodialysis** to remove methanol and formate.
- Nursing: support breathing and circulation, protect against aspiration, monitor vision and acid–base status, give the ordered antidote and prepare for dialysis, and teach the community about the danger of illicit liquor.

## Remember

- Benzodiazepines enhance GABA (safer); barbiturates open the chloride channel (dangerous overdose). Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines.
- Hypnotics at bedtime; fall precautions; short-term use; do not stop suddenly after long use.
- Neuromuscular blockers paralyse but give no sleep or pain relief — always need ventilation, sedation and analgesia; succinylcholine → high K+, malignant hyperthermia (dantrolene).
- Antipsychotics block dopamine → EPS, tardive dyskinesia, NMS (fever + rigidity, emergency); clozapine → agranulocytosis (blood counts).
- Lithium: narrow margin, check levels, steady salt and water; diuretics/NSAIDs/dehydration raise it.
- Antidepressants act over 2–4 weeks; watch suicide risk early; SSRIs first-line; MAOIs → tyramine reaction; serotonin syndrome if combined.
- Antiepileptics: never stop suddenly; valproate avoided in pregnancy; protect the patient during a fit, never restrain or put things in the mouth; status epilepticus — IV benzodiazepine first.
- Levodopa + carbidopa for Parkinson's — give on time; cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil) for Alzheimer's.
- Ethanol is a depressant and the antidote for methanol poisoning; methanol → formic acid → acidosis and blindness.
