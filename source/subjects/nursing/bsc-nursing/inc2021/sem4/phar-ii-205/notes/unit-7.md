# Unit 7: Fundamental principles of prescribing

This final unit of Pharmacology II shifts from individual drug groups to the system-level skills a nurse needs: how prescriptions are written and checked, how errors are prevented, how adverse reactions are reported, and how drugs are safely dispensed and administered. These skills are examined in the professional practice paper as well.

---

## 7.1 The WHO model of rational prescribing

The World Health Organisation's guide to good prescribing defines **six steps** that every prescriber should follow. Understanding these steps helps nurses verify prescriptions and educate patients.

| Step | What to do | Nurse's link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Define the patient's problem | Accurate diagnosis before prescribing | Ensure the diagnosis is confirmed before giving medicines |
| 2. Specify the therapeutic objective | What should the drug achieve? (e.g. lower BP to <130/80) | Know the clinical target so you can monitor it |
| 3. Choose the drug | Is the drug effective, safe, suitable for this patient, and affordable? Use the "P-drug" concept — a personal drug the prescriber knows well | Query an unusual choice; check interactions and allergies |
| 4. Start treatment: write the prescription | Legible, complete, unambiguous prescription | Check all elements are present |
| 5. Give information, instructions and warnings | Explain to the patient how to take the medicine, what to expect, what to watch for | Patient education; reinforce at discharge |
| 6. Monitor treatment | Is the drug working? Are there adverse effects? | Monitor therapeutic and toxic endpoints; report to prescriber |

**P-drug concept:** A "personal drug" is a drug a prescriber (or institution) has selected as their first choice for a condition, based on evidence of efficacy, safety, suitability (for typical patients) and cost. National essential medicines lists and standard treatment guidelines embody this concept at a system level.

## 7.2 Legal aspects of prescribing in India

### Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940 and Rules 1945

India's principal legislation governing manufacture, sale and distribution of drugs. Classified drugs by Schedule to restrict availability:

| Schedule | What it covers | Prescribing requirement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Schedule H** | Drugs that must be sold only on a prescription from a registered medical practitioner | Prescription required; kept in prescription register |
| **Schedule H1** | Reserve antibiotics and certain high-risk drugs (e.g. some fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, carbapenems) | Prescription with separate sales register entry; quantity limits; aim to restrict inappropriate use and slow resistance |
| **Schedule G** | Drugs that should only be used under medical supervision (mostly hormones and prescription-only items in the original act) | Caution label required |
| **Schedule X** | Psychotropic substances and opioids (e.g. morphine, diazepam, tramadol) | Triplicate prescription (doctor/chemist/government copy); DEA-like controls; stricter quantity and validity limits |
| **OTC (Over the Counter)** | Not in a schedule requiring prescription | Available without prescription (paracetamol, antacids, ORS) |

---

### A complete prescription

A valid prescription must contain:

```
1. Date of prescription
2. Patient's full name, age, sex, address
3. Diagnosis (optional but good practice)
4. Drug name (generic preferred), strength (dose), dosage form (tablet/injection)
5. Dose and frequency (e.g. 500 mg twice daily)
6. Route of administration
7. Duration of treatment
8. Quantity to dispense
9. Prescriber's name, qualification, registration number and signature
10. For Schedule X: in triplicate with controlled drug details
```

**Generic vs brand names:** INC and national policy recommend generic names (non-proprietary names) on prescriptions to reduce cost and improve understanding.

---

### Verbal orders and transcription

Verbal orders should be recorded immediately in writing (read back to confirm), signed by the prescriber at the earliest opportunity. Use SBAR for hand-off communication. Abbreviation errors cause medication mistakes — avoid dangerous abbreviations (e.g. write "units" not "U"; "micrograms" not "mcg" or "µg" in handwriting; "every 24 hours" not "OD" if ambiguous in context).

## 7.3 Medication errors

**Definition:** Any preventable event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm.

### Types of medication errors

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Prescribing errors** | Wrong drug, wrong dose, wrong patient, drug interaction not checked, allergy ignored |
| **Transcription errors** | Misreading a handwritten order; wrong decimal point; abbreviation misread |
| **Dispensing errors** | Wrong drug dispensed, wrong strength, wrong label |
| **Administration errors** | Wrong patient, wrong time (>1 hour from scheduled time for some drugs), wrong route, wrong technique |
| **Monitoring errors** | Failing to check renal function before a renally-cleared drug; not checking INR before warfarin dosing |

### Causes

- Illegible handwriting, verbal orders misheard
- Fatigue, distraction, high workload
- Poor labelling, look-alike/sound-alike drugs (LASA)
- Lack of knowledge, inadequate training
- Poor handover communication

### Reporting and root-cause analysis

- National pharmacovigilance programme includes medication error reporting
- Hospitals maintain an incident-reporting system (IRS) — focus on system failure, not individual blame
- **Root-cause analysis (RCA):** identifies underlying system failures rather than blaming individuals; asks "why" multiple times (5-whys technique)

## 7.4 The five rights of medication administration (and the five more)

### Original five rights

| Right | What to check |
| --- | --- |
| Right Patient | Two identifiers: name and date of birth or ID number; never rely on bed number alone |
| Right Drug | Match the drug name exactly on the prescription and the container label; look-alike, sound-alike (LASA) alert |
| Right Dose | Calculate independently; double-check calculations for high-alert drugs |
| Right Route | Oral, IM, IV, SC, topical — route affects dose; some drugs are fatal by the wrong route (e.g. vincristine must NEVER be given intrathecally) |
| Right Time | Timing matters for insulin (before meals), antibiotics (even levels), anticoagulants (consistency) |

### Five additional rights

| Right | Importance |
| --- | --- |
| Right Reason | Is this drug indicated for this patient's diagnosis? |
| Right Response | Monitor for the expected therapeutic effect and for adverse effects |
| Right Documentation | Record the dose given (time, route, batch number for high-risk), and any refusal or error |
| Right Education | The patient understands the drug's purpose, how to take it and what to watch for |
| Right to Refuse | A competent patient has the right to refuse medication; document, inform prescriber, never force |

## 7.5 High-alert medications

Drugs that bear a heightened risk of causing significant harm when used in error. The Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) list includes:

- **Insulin** — always double-check with a second nurse; verify blood glucose; use an insulin syringe (not a standard syringe); never abbreviate "Units" as "U"
- **Anticoagulants** (heparin, warfarin, enoxaparin) — weight-based dosing; check INR or anti-Xa before dose; have reversal agents available
- **Concentrated electrolytes** (potassium chloride > 20 mEq — must be diluted; never give IV bolus; magnesium sulfate; hypertonic saline)
- **Chemotherapy drugs** — two-nurse sign-off; PPE; specialist pharmacy preparation
- **Opioids** — controlled drug procedures; monitor respiratory rate and sedation score; have naloxone available
- **Neuromuscular blocking agents** (vecuronium, succinylcholine) — must be labelled "PARALYTIC AGENT"; only on ventilated or anaesthetised patients; never stored on general wards without restriction

## 7.6 Pharmacovigilance and ADR reporting

**Pharmacovigilance:** The science of detecting, assessing, understanding and preventing adverse effects of medicines and other medicine-related problems.

**Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR):** An unintended, harmful response to a drug at a dose normally used for prevention, diagnosis or treatment.

**PvPI — Pharmacovigilance Programme of India:**
- Coordinating centre: **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC)**, Ghaziabad, under CDSCO (Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation)
- Peripheral centres in medical colleges and hospitals
- Reports collected in **VigiBase** (WHO global database managed by Uppsala Monitoring Centre)
- Nurses are expected to report ADRs — their proximity to the patient means they often observe ADRs first

**What to report:** Any suspected ADR (not just severe or unexpected ones); suspected counterfeit or substandard drug; medication error resulting in harm; adverse reactions to vaccines (AEFI).

**Serious ADRs:** Death, life-threatening, hospitalisation (or prolonged), disability, congenital anomaly.

**Nurse's duty to report:** Complete the ADR reporting form (available from PvPI); include patient details (anonymised), drug details, nature of reaction, outcome; report within 7 days for non-serious, 15 days for serious reactions.

**Causality assessment:** WHO causality assessment — Certain, Probable, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional, Unassessable.

## 7.7 Polypharmacy and medication reconciliation

**Polypharmacy:** Use of five or more regular medicines simultaneously (often more). Common in elderly patients with multiple chronic diseases.

**Risks of polypharmacy:**
- Drug–drug interactions
- Increased adverse effects
- Poor adherence (complexity)
- Falls (from sedatives, antihypertensives, diuretics)
- Hospitalisation

**Beers Criteria:** Evidence-based list of potentially inappropriate medications in older adults (>65 years) — includes sedating antihistamines, long-acting benzodiazepines, some antihypertensives, NSAIDs.

**Medication reconciliation:** Creating the most accurate possible list of all medicines a patient is taking and comparing it against prescriptions at admission, transfer and discharge to identify and resolve discrepancies.

| Resource | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| WHO Essential Medicines List (EML) | Evidence-based list of medicines considered essential for a basic health system; updated every 2 years |

| National Treatment Guidelines (NTG) India | Disease-specific standard treatment guidelines from the Ministry of Health |
| BNF (British National Formulary) / MIMS India | Clinical reference for drug doses, interactions, monitoring |

## 7.9 Nurse's role in safe medication management

1. **Prescription receipt:** Check completeness, legibility, correct patient details, prescriber registration number
2. **Five rights (+ five) on every administration**
3. **High-alert medications:** Double-check with a second nurse; use a CPOE (computerised prescriber order entry) alert if available
4. **Patient education:** Name of drug, what it is for, how to take it, what side effects to watch for, importance of adherence, storage
5. **Controlled drugs:** Count, double-lock, sign the controlled drug register with a witness; never leave unattended
6. **Medication administration record (MAR):** Document every dose immediately after giving; never pre-sign; record dose omissions and reasons
7. **Allergy check:** Verify allergy status on admission and before every new drug; document allergy clearly (including nature of reaction)
8. **Discharge medication counselling:** Written and verbal instructions; ensure patient can open all containers; teach self-injection if applicable
9. **ADR reporting:** Recognise and report adverse reactions through PvPI

## Remember

- WHO six steps: define problem → specify objective → choose drug → write prescription → educate patient → monitor.
- Schedule H: prescription only; Schedule H1: reserve antibiotics (separate register); Schedule X: triplicates (opioids, psychotropics).
- Five rights: Right Patient, Drug, Dose, Route, Time — plus five more: Reason, Response, Documentation, Education, Refusal.
- Medication error types: prescribing, transcription, dispensing, administration, monitoring.
- High-alert medicines: insulin (never abbreviate "U"), concentrated KCl (never IV bolus), chemotherapy, anticoagulants, opioids, neuromuscular blockers.
- PvPI: pharmacovigilance reporting to IPC/CDSCO; nurses report ADRs; serious ADR within 15 days.
- Polypharmacy risks in elderly: falls, drug interactions, poor adherence; Beers Criteria guides inappropriate prescribing review.
- Medication reconciliation: accurate medication list at every transition of care.
- Verbally confirm and read back verbal orders; write up immediately; prescriber signs as soon as possible.
