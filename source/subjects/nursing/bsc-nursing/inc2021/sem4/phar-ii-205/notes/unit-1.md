# Unit 1: Drugs for ear, nose, throat and eye disorders, and drugs acting on the urinary system

INC Units I and II (4 + 4 hours): antihistamines and topical drugs for the eye, ear, nose and mouth; the renin–angiotensin system, diuretics and antidiuretics, drugs that harm the kidney, urinary antiseptics, and acidifiers and alkalinisers used in urinary tract infection (UTI). For each drug group learn the action, uses, contraindications, interactions, side effects and the nurse's role.

## 1.1 How to write a drug study

The INC syllabus asks the same points for every group. Use this order in every long answer and in your clinical drug study book.

```text
DRUG STUDY FRAME
1 Group / class and examples
2 Composition or preparation (eye drop, ointment, mouthwash, tablet)
3 Mechanism of action
4 Indications (uses)
5 Contraindications and cautions
6 Drug interactions
7 Side effects and adverse effects
8 Nurse's responsibilities: before, during and after giving the drug
   (assess → give safely → monitor → teach → record)
```

Doses are not given in these notes. In the ward, always take the dose, strength and route from the written order and the hospital formulary, and check the "rights" of drug administration (right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, and the patient's right to information and to refuse).

## 1.2 Antihistamines (H1 receptor blockers)

**Histamine** is released from mast cells in allergy. Through **H1 receptors** it causes itching, sneezing, runny nose, dilatation and leakiness of small vessels (wheal and flare), and bronchial constriction. Through **H2 receptors** it increases gastric acid (H2 blockers belong to the GI drugs of Pharmacology I).

**Mechanism.** H1 antihistamines occupy the H1 receptor and stop histamine from acting (more exactly, they act as inverse agonists). They prevent the allergic response better than they reverse one that has already developed.

| Generation | Examples | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First (older, sedating) | Chlorpheniramine (CPM), promethazine, diphenhydramine, pheniramine, hydroxyzine, cyproheptadine, dimenhydrinate | Cross into the brain → drowsiness; anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention); some also stop motion sickness and vomiting |
| Second (newer, less sedating) | Cetirizine, levocetirizine, loratadine, desloratadine, fexofenadine | Little entry into the brain, mostly once-daily, few anticholinergic effects |

**Uses**
- Allergic rhinitis (hay fever), allergic conjunctivitis, urticaria, itching, insect bites.
- Add-on in anaphylaxis (after adrenaline, never instead of it — see Unit 5).
- Motion sickness and vertigo (promethazine, dimenhydrinate, cinnarizine).
- Sedation and pre-anaesthetic use (promethazine), cough and cold mixtures.
- Cyproheptadine has been used as an appetite stimulant; this use needs medical advice.

**Side effects.** Sedation and poor attention (older drugs), dry mouth, constipation, blurred vision, urinary retention, thickened secretions; excitement in young children; headache. Promethazine injection irritates tissues.

**Cautions.** Glaucoma (angle-closure), enlarged prostate, the elderly (confusion, falls), young children, people who drive or work with machines, pregnancy and breast-feeding (check with the prescriber).

**Interactions.** Alcohol, sleeping pills, opioids and other CNS depressants add to the drowsiness; other anticholinergic drugs add to dryness and retention.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Ask about allergy history, glaucoma, prostate problems and driving or machine work.
- Give sedating antihistamines at bedtime where possible.
- Raise the cot sides / keep the call bell near for elderly patients; help with walking.
- Teach: avoid alcohol and driving after older antihistamines; sip water or suck sugar-free sweets for dry mouth.
- Observe for urinary retention in older men.

### Other drugs for the nose

| Group | Examples | Points for the nurse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nasal decongestants (alpha agonists) | Oxymetazoline, xylometazoline, phenylephrine; oral pseudoephedrine | Shrink swollen nasal mucosa by vasoconstriction. Use for a few days only: longer use causes **rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa)**. Oral forms can raise BP — caution in hypertension and heart disease. |
| Intranasal corticosteroids | Fluticasone, budesonide, mometasone, beclomethasone | Best long-term control of allergic rhinitis; take daily; effect builds over days; occasional nose bleed and dryness. |
| Mast cell stabiliser | Sodium cromoglycate | Prevents release of histamine; used before exposure. |
| Saline drops or spray | Normal saline | Safe for infants; loosens crusts and mucus. |

**Instilling nose drops:** clear the nose by gentle blowing; tilt the head back (or lie with the head over the edge of the bed); insert the dropper just inside the nostril without touching it; stay in position for a few minutes so the drug spreads; clean the dropper. Rationale: avoiding contact keeps the dropper clean; the position lets the drop reach the mucosa rather than run down the throat.

## 1.3 Topical drugs for the eye

The INC syllabus names **chloramphenicol and gentamicin eye drops**. Learn these two well and know the other eye drug groups for MCQs.

### Chloramphenicol eye drops and ointment

- **Class:** broad-spectrum antibiotic, mainly bacteriostatic.
- **Action:** binds the 50S ribosomal subunit of bacteria and blocks protein synthesis.
- **Uses:** bacterial conjunctivitis, superficial eye infections, prevention of infection after minor eye injury or foreign body removal.
- **Side effects:** brief stinging, blurred vision after ointment. Very rarely, blood disorders have been linked even to eye use, so prolonged use is avoided and it is used with care in people with a past blood disorder.
- **Nursing:** check the expiry (the opened bottle is usually discarded after the period printed on it, often about four weeks); do not share bottles; teach hand-washing.

### Gentamicin eye drops

- **Class:** aminoglycoside antibiotic, bactericidal.
- **Action:** binds the 30S ribosomal subunit → misreading of the genetic code → faulty proteins → bacterial death.
- **Uses:** bacterial conjunctivitis, keratitis and blepharitis caused by Gram-negative organisms such as Pseudomonas.
- **Side effects:** burning, irritation, local allergy. Systemic effects (kidney and ear damage) do not occur with normal eye use but are important when gentamicin is injected.

### Other eye drugs

| Group | Examples | Use | Nursing point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Other antibacterials | Ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin, tobramycin | Conjunctivitis, corneal ulcer | Complete the prescribed course |
| Antivirals | Acyclovir eye ointment | Herpes simplex keratitis | Steroid drops must not be used in herpes keratitis |
| Antifungals | Natamycin | Fungal keratitis | Common after injury with plant matter |
| Mydriatics / cycloplegics | Atropine, homatropine, tropicamide, cyclopentolate; phenylephrine (mydriatic only) | Eye examination, refraction, iritis | Blurred vision and glare — patient must not drive; atropine effect lasts many days; caution in angle-closure glaucoma |
| Miotics | Pilocarpine | Glaucoma (angle-closure), reverse dilatation | Dim vision in poor light, brow ache |
| Anti-glaucoma drugs | Timolol (beta blocker), latanoprost (prostaglandin), dorzolamide (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor), brimonidine (alpha agonist); acetazolamide tablets | Lower intra-ocular pressure | Timolol can be absorbed and slow the heart or worsen asthma; latanoprost can darken the iris |
| Local anaesthetic drops | Proparacaine, tetracaine | Tonometry, removing foreign body | Protect the eye after use: blink reflex is lost; never give to the patient to take home |
| Corticosteroid drops | Prednisolone, dexamethasone | Allergic and post-operative inflammation | Only under an eye doctor: can raise eye pressure, cause cataract and worsen infection |
| Lubricants | Carboxymethylcellulose, hypromellose | Dry eye | Safe for frequent use |

### Instilling eye drops (steps with rationale)

1. Check the order, the eye (right, left or both) and the label. **Rationale:** wrong-eye errors are common.
2. Wash hands; clean the eyelids from the inner to the outer corner with a separate swab for each eye. **Rationale:** avoids carrying discharge into the tear duct or the other eye.
3. Ask the patient to look up; pull the lower lid down to form a pocket.
4. Hold the dropper about 1–2 cm above the eye and drop into the **lower conjunctival sac**, not onto the cornea. **Rationale:** the cornea is sensitive and the drop would make the patient blink it out.
5. Ask the patient to close the eye gently (not squeeze) and press the **inner corner (nasolacrimal duct)** for about a minute. **Rationale:** keeps the drug in the eye and reduces absorption into the body (important with timolol and atropine).
6. With two different drops, wait about 5 minutes between them; drops before ointment.
7. Do not let the tip touch the eye; record the drug, eye and time.

## 1.4 Topical drugs for the ear

### Soda glycerin ear drops

- **Composition:** sodium bicarbonate (soda) in glycerin and water.
- **Action:** the alkali softens and loosens hard wax; glycerin lubricates. It is a **ceruminolytic (wax softener)**.
- **Use:** impacted wax before syringing or suction, or to let wax come out by itself.
- **Side effects:** temporary fullness, mild irritation, sometimes giddiness if the drop is cold.
- **Contraindication:** perforated ear drum, ear discharge.

### Boric spirit ear drops

- **Composition:** boric acid dissolved in rectified spirit (alcohol).
- **Action:** mild antiseptic and antifungal (boric acid) plus a drying effect (spirit), which removes the moist conditions germs like.
- **Uses:** otitis externa, fungal infection of the ear canal (otomycosis), after swimming.
- **Side effects:** burning or stinging, especially on raw skin.
- **Contraindication:** perforated ear drum (it burns the middle ear), and not for infants or long-term use.

### Other ear preparations
- Antibiotic and antibiotic–steroid drops: ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin (ofloxacin is chosen when the drum may be perforated, because some aminoglycoside drops can harm the inner ear through a perforation).
- Antifungal: clotrimazole drops for otomycosis.
- Hydrogen peroxide and olive oil drops also soften wax.

### Instilling ear drops (steps with rationale)

1. Warm the bottle in the hand to body temperature. **Rationale:** cold drops stimulate the inner ear and cause giddiness and vomiting.
2. Patient lies on the side, affected ear up.
3. Straighten the ear canal: **adult — pull the pinna up and back; child under 3 years — pull it down and back.** **Rationale:** the canal of a young child is directed upward.
4. Instil along the wall of the canal, not directly on the drum.
5. Press the tragus gently; keep the position for a few minutes; a loose cotton plug may be placed if ordered.
6. Teach the patient not to put sticks, pins or oil into the ear without advice.

## 1.5 Drugs for the mouth and throat: chlorhexidine mouthwash

- **Class:** biguanide antiseptic.
- **Action:** the positively charged molecule binds the negatively charged bacterial cell wall, damages the membrane and causes leakage of cell contents. It also sticks to teeth and gums and keeps acting for hours (**substantivity**).
- **Uses:** gingivitis and plaque control, oral care of ill, unconscious and ventilated patients (part of bundles to reduce ventilator-associated pneumonia), mouth ulcers, after oral surgery, oral care in patients receiving cancer chemotherapy.
- **Side effects:** **brown staining of teeth and tongue**, altered taste, mild burning, rarely allergy.
- **Interaction:** ordinary toothpaste (anionic detergents) reduces its effect — leave a gap of about 30 minutes, or rinse well between them.
- **Nursing:** teach to rinse and spit, **not swallow**; avoid food and drink for a while after use so the drug stays on the teeth; in unconscious patients use a swab or toothbrush with suction, with the head turned to the side to prevent aspiration.

Other mouth and throat products: povidone-iodine gargle (sore throat; avoid in iodine allergy and thyroid disease), benzydamine (pain-relieving mouthwash for mucositis), clotrimazole troches and nystatin suspension (oral thrush), glycerin-based paints, warm saline gargles.

## 1.6 The renin–angiotensin system and drugs that act on it

```text
Fall in BP / fall in renal blood flow / low sodium
            ↓
Kidney (juxtaglomerular cells) releases RENIN
Angiotensinogen (liver) ──renin──► Angiotensin I
Angiotensin I ──ACE (lungs)──► ANGIOTENSIN II
            ↓
• Strong vasoconstriction → BP ↑
• Aldosterone release → Na+ and water kept, K+ lost
• Thirst and ADH release
• Long term: growth of heart and vessel walls (remodelling)

ACE also breaks down BRADYKININ (a vasodilator that causes cough).
```

| Group | Examples | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ACE inhibitors | Captopril, enalapril, lisinopril, ramipril | Block conversion of angiotensin I to II; bradykinin also builds up |
| Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) | Losartan, telmisartan, olmesartan, valsartan | Block the AT1 receptor; no bradykinin build-up, so no cough |
| Direct renin inhibitor | Aliskiren | Blocks renin itself |
| Aldosterone antagonists | Spironolactone, eplerenone | Block the end of the chain (see diuretics) |

**Uses.** Hypertension, heart failure, after myocardial infarction, **diabetic kidney disease** (they reduce protein loss in urine and slow kidney damage).

**Adverse effects (remember "CAPTOPRIL")** — Cough (dry, persistent), Angioedema (swelling of lips, tongue, throat — stop the drug, emergency), Proteinuria (rare), Taste change, Orthostatic and first-dose hypotension, Pregnancy harm (contraindicated), Renal function worsens in bilateral renal artery stenosis, Increased potassium (hyperkalaemia), Leucopenia (rare, captopril). ARBs share most of these except the cough.

**Interactions.** Potassium supplements, salt substitutes and potassium-sparing diuretics → high potassium; NSAIDs reduce the BP-lowering effect and with diuretics can harm the kidney; lithium levels rise.

**Nursing responsibilities**
- Check BP lying and standing; give the first dose at bedtime or with the patient lying down; watch for dizziness.
- Check serum potassium and creatinine before and during treatment.
- Ask about pregnancy or plans for pregnancy; teach women to report pregnancy at once.
- Teach the patient to report dry cough, and to seek help at once for swelling of the face, lips or tongue.
- Avoid potassium-rich salt substitutes unless advised.

## 1.7 Diuretics

A diuretic increases the output of urine by increasing the excretion of sodium (natriuresis) and water. Where a diuretic acts in the nephron decides how strong it is.

```text
NEPHRON AND SITE OF ACTION
Glomerulus
   ↓
PCT  ← carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide); osmotic diuretics (mannitol) act here and in the loop
   ↓
Loop of Henle, thick ascending limb ← LOOP diuretics (furosemide) block Na+/K+/2Cl– carrier  — strongest
   ↓
DCT  ← THIAZIDES (hydrochlorothiazide) block Na+/Cl– carrier — moderate
   ↓
Collecting duct ← POTASSIUM-SPARING: spironolactone (blocks aldosterone), amiloride/triamterene (block Na+ channel) — weak
```

| Class | Examples | Main uses | Main adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Loop (high-ceiling) | Furosemide, torsemide, bumetanide | Acute pulmonary oedema, oedema of heart, liver and kidney failure, hypercalcaemia | Low K+, Na+, Mg2+; dehydration and low BP; **ototoxicity** (ringing, deafness, especially with fast IV injection or with aminoglycosides); raised uric acid and sugar |
| Thiazide and thiazide-like | Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, indapamide, metolazone | Hypertension (first-line), mild oedema, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, calcium kidney stones | Low K+ and Na+, raised uric acid (gout), raised sugar and lipids; **calcium is kept** (high calcium) |
| Potassium-sparing — aldosterone antagonists | Spironolactone, eplerenone | Heart failure, liver cirrhosis with ascites, primary hyperaldosteronism; given with loop/thiazide to save K+ | **Hyperkalaemia**; spironolactone causes gynaecomastia and menstrual changes |
| Potassium-sparing — Na+ channel blockers | Amiloride, triamterene | With thiazides to prevent K+ loss | Hyperkalaemia |
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | Acetazolamide | Glaucoma, altitude sickness, some epilepsy | Metabolic acidosis, tingling, kidney stones; weak diuretic |
| Osmotic | Mannitol (IV) | Raised intracranial pressure, raised eye pressure, to keep urine flowing | Expands blood volume first — can cause pulmonary oedema in heart failure; crystals form in cold, so warm and use an in-line filter |

**Important interactions.** Low potassium from loop or thiazide diuretics makes **digoxin toxicity** more likely; diuretics raise **lithium** levels; aminoglycosides plus loop diuretics → more ear and kidney damage; NSAIDs reduce the diuretic effect; potassium-sparing diuretics plus ACE inhibitors or K+ supplements → dangerous hyperkalaemia.

**Nursing responsibilities with diuretics**
1. **Before:** record weight, BP (lying and standing), pulse, intake–output, serum electrolytes, creatinine, blood sugar; note hearing.
2. **Timing:** give in the **morning** (or the second dose by early afternoon) so the patient is not up at night to pass urine — less sleep loss and fewer night-time falls.
3. **IV furosemide:** inject slowly as ordered; fast injection increases the risk of hearing damage.
4. **Monitor:** daily weight at the same time with the same clothes and scale (the best guide to fluid loss), intake–output chart, oedema, lung sounds, signs of low potassium (muscle weakness, cramps, irregular pulse) or high potassium with K-sparing drugs.
5. **Safety:** postural hypotension — rise slowly; keep a urinal or bedpan within reach.
6. **Teach:** potassium-rich foods (banana, orange, coconut water, tomato) with loop and thiazide diuretics unless on a K-sparing drug; report weakness, giddiness, ringing in the ears; diabetic patients to check sugar.

## 1.8 Antidiuretics

**Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin)** from the posterior pituitary acts on **V2 receptors** in the collecting duct, inserting water channels (aquaporins) so water is reabsorbed and urine becomes concentrated. Through **V1 receptors** it constricts blood vessels.

| Drug | Action | Uses | Adverse effects and nursing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vasopressin | V1 + V2 | Bleeding oesophageal varices (vasoconstriction), vasodilatory shock, diabetes insipidus | Chest pain from coronary constriction, abdominal cramps, pallor; monitor ECG and BP |
| Desmopressin (DDAVP) | Mainly V2, long acting; given by nose, mouth or injection | **Central (cranial) diabetes insipidus**, bed-wetting (nocturnal enuresis) in children, haemophilia A and von Willebrand disease (raises factor VIII) | **Water retention and low sodium** (headache, confusion, fits). Restrict fluids in the evening for enuresis; daily weight and serum sodium |
| Thiazides | Paradoxically reduce urine volume | **Nephrogenic** diabetes insipidus | As above |

## 1.9 Drugs toxic to the kidney (nephrotoxic drugs)

The kidney receives a large share of the cardiac output and concentrates drugs in its tubules, so it is easily injured.

| Drug or group | How it damages |
| --- | --- |
| Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin) | Damage to tubule cells (acute tubular necrosis) |
| Amphotericin B | Tubular damage and narrowing of renal vessels |
| NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac) | Block prostaglandins that keep renal vessels open; interstitial nephritis |
| Vancomycin, polymyxins | Tubular damage, worse with aminoglycosides |
| Cisplatin, methotrexate (high dose) | Tubular damage; crystals in tubules |
| Cyclosporine, tacrolimus | Constriction of renal vessels |
| Iodinated contrast media | Contrast-associated kidney injury |
| ACE inhibitors / ARBs | Fall in filtration in bilateral renal artery stenosis or dehydration |
| Acyclovir (IV), sulphonamides | Crystals in tubules |
| Lithium | Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, chronic damage |

**Patients at higher risk:** the elderly, dehydrated patients, diabetes, existing kidney disease, heart failure, and those taking two or more of these drugs together (the "triple whammy" of diuretic + ACE inhibitor/ARB + NSAID is a well-known cause of kidney injury).

**Nursing:** keep the patient well hydrated unless fluid is restricted; record intake and output; check serum creatinine and urea; report urine output falling below the expected level; give nephrotoxic drugs exactly at the ordered times; help with drug level monitoring (blood samples at the stated time before or after a dose); point out to the prescriber if two kidney-toxic drugs are ordered together.

## 1.10 Urinary antiseptics and drugs for UTI

**Urinary antiseptics** reach useful levels only in urine, not in blood, so they treat or prevent lower UTI (cystitis) but not kidney infection or blood infection.

| Drug | Action and uses | Adverse effects | Nursing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nitrofurantoin** | Damages bacterial DNA and proteins; uncomplicated cystitis and long-term prevention | Nausea (take with food), **brown discoloration of urine (harmless)**, peripheral neuropathy, lung reactions with long use, haemolysis in G6PD deficiency | Avoid in poor kidney function (does not reach urine) and near term in pregnancy; tell the patient about urine colour |
| **Methenamine (hexamine)** | In **acid urine** breaks down to formaldehyde, which kills bacteria; long-term prevention | Gastric upset, bladder irritation | Needs acid urine — given with an acidifier; no use in an indwelling catheter (urine does not stay long enough) |
| **Nalidixic acid** | Older quinolone | GI upset, photosensitivity | Largely replaced by newer drugs |
| **Fosfomycin** | Blocks cell-wall synthesis; single-course treatment of cystitis | Diarrhoea | Dissolve the sachet in water |
| Systemic antibiotics used for UTI | Cotrimoxazole, fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin), cephalosporins (cefixime, ceftriaxone), amoxicillin–clavulanate, aminoglycosides | See Pharmacology I | Choose by culture report |
| Urinary analgesic | Phenazopyridine | Turns urine orange-red; relieves burning only, does not cure infection | Short use only |

### Acidifiers and alkalinisers of urine

| Group | Examples | Why used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Urinary acidifiers** | Ammonium chloride, ascorbic acid (vitamin C), methionine | Keep the urine acid so methenamine works; acid urine also slows growth of some germs and dissolves phosphate stones |
| **Urinary alkalinisers** | **Potassium citrate, sodium citrate**, sodium bicarbonate | Relieve the burning of cystitis; prevent uric acid and cystine stones; keep sulphonamides dissolved so they do not crystallise; aminoglycosides and some other antibiotics work better in alkaline urine |

Cautions: alkalinisers containing potassium are avoided in kidney failure and with K-sparing diuretics (high potassium); sodium salts add to the salt load in heart failure and hypertension. An acidifier and an alkaliniser must never be given together.

**Nursing role in UTI**
1. Collect a **clean-catch midstream urine sample** (or from the catheter sampling port, never the bag) **before** the first antibiotic dose — the culture guides the choice of drug.
2. Encourage fluids (about 2–3 litres a day unless restricted) to flush the bladder.
3. Teach the patient to finish the full course even after symptoms settle; stopping early encourages resistance and relapse.
4. Teach prevention: perineal care front to back, passing urine after intercourse, not holding urine for long, catheter care.
5. Watch for fever, loin pain or rigors (spread to the kidney) and report.

## Remember

- Drug study order: class → composition → action → uses → contraindications → interactions → side effects → nurse's role.
- First-generation antihistamines sedate and dry; second-generation (cetirizine, fexofenadine) hardly sedate. In anaphylaxis, adrenaline first.
- Nasal decongestant sprays: a few days only — rebound congestion.
- Chloramphenicol: 50S, bacteriostatic. Gentamicin: 30S, bactericidal, Gram-negative.
- Eye drops into the lower conjunctival sac; press the inner canthus; 5 minutes between drops; steroid drops never in herpes keratitis.
- Ear drops warm; pull pinna up and back in adults, down and back in children under 3.
- Soda glycerin = wax softener; boric spirit = antiseptic and drying; both not in perforated drum.
- Chlorhexidine: stains teeth, do not swallow, keep away from toothpaste for ~30 minutes.
- ACE inhibitors: dry cough (bradykinin), angioedema, high K+, avoid in pregnancy.
- Loop diuretics strongest; ototoxic; low K+ raises digoxin toxicity. Thiazides keep calcium, raise uric acid. Spironolactone: high K+, gynaecomastia.
- Give diuretics in the morning; daily weight is the best guide.
- Desmopressin: V2, diabetes insipidus and bed-wetting; watch for low sodium.
- Nephrotoxic: aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, NSAIDs, cisplatin, contrast, cyclosporine.
- Nitrofurantoin turns urine brown; methenamine needs acid urine; citrates alkalinise.
- Urine culture before the first antibiotic dose.
