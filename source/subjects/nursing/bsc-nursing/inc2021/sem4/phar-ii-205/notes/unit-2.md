# Unit 2: Analgesics, antipyretics, anaesthetics and medical gases

INC Unit III, first part (the unit has 10 hours in all): NSAIDs, antipyretics, opioids and other central analgesics; general anaesthesia (techniques and pre-anaesthetic medication); local anaesthetics; and medical gases — oxygen, nitrous oxide, carbon dioxide and others. The rest of Unit III (sedatives, psychiatric drugs, anticonvulsants and others) is in Unit 3 of these notes.

## 2.1 Pain and the groups of analgesics

**Pain** is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked to actual or possible tissue damage. Injured tissue releases **prostaglandins, bradykinin and histamine**; prostaglandins make nerve endings more sensitive (hyperalgesia). Signals travel to the spinal cord and brain, where opioid receptors can block them.

| Group | Where it acts | Examples | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Non-opioid analgesics (NSAIDs, paracetamol) | Mainly at the site of injury by stopping prostaglandin formation | Aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac, paracetamol | Mild to moderate pain, inflammation, fever |
| Opioid analgesics | Brain and spinal cord (opioid receptors) | Morphine, fentanyl, tramadol | Moderate to severe pain |
| Adjuvant analgesics | Other mechanisms | Amitriptyline, gabapentin, pregabalin, carbamazepine, steroids | Nerve (neuropathic) pain, bone pain, swelling |

```text
WHO PAIN LADDER (for cancer and chronic pain)
Step 3  Severe pain    → strong opioid (morphine, fentanyl) ± non-opioid ± adjuvant
Step 2  Moderate pain  → weak opioid (tramadol, codeine) ± non-opioid ± adjuvant
Step 1  Mild pain      → non-opioid (paracetamol, NSAID) ± adjuvant
Principles: by mouth, by the clock, by the ladder, for the individual, with attention to detail
```

Nursing pain assessment comes first: site, character, severity on a 0–10 numerical scale or faces scale (children), what makes it better or worse, effect on sleep and movement. Reassess after every analgesic (about 30 minutes after IV, about an hour after oral).

## 2.2 Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)

**Mechanism.** NSAIDs block the **cyclo-oxygenase (COX)** enzyme that makes prostaglandins and thromboxane from arachidonic acid.

```text
Cell membrane phospholipids
        ↓ phospholipase A2   (blocked by corticosteroids)
Arachidonic acid
        ↓ COX-1 and COX-2     (blocked by NSAIDs)
Prostaglandins + thromboxane A2 + prostacyclin
COX-1 ("housekeeping"): protects stomach lining, keeps kidney blood flow, platelet aggregation
COX-2 (induced in inflammation): pain, fever, swelling
```

**Four actions:** analgesic, antipyretic, anti-inflammatory, and antiplatelet (mainly aspirin).

**Classification**

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Non-selective COX inhibitors | Aspirin (irreversible), ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, indomethacin, piroxicam, ketorolac, mefenamic acid |
| Preferential COX-2 inhibitors | Nimesulide, meloxicam, aceclofenac |
| Selective COX-2 inhibitors (coxibs) | Celecoxib, etoricoxib |
| Analgesic–antipyretic with poor anti-inflammatory action | Paracetamol (acetaminophen), metamizole |

**Uses.** Headache, toothache, dysmenorrhoea (mefenamic acid), musculoskeletal pain and sprains, arthritis (rheumatoid and osteoarthritis), gout, post-operative pain (ketorolac injection), fever, renal colic (diclofenac), and closure of patent ductus arteriosus in preterm babies (indomethacin, ibuprofen) under specialist care.

**Adverse effects**
- **Gastric irritation, peptic ulcer and GI bleeding** (loss of protective prostaglandins) — the most important.
- **Kidney**: salt and water retention, raised BP, kidney injury in dehydrated or elderly patients.
- **Bleeding tendency** (less platelet thromboxane).
- **Asthma** worsened in aspirin-sensitive people.
- **Heart**: selective COX-2 inhibitors and high-dose NSAIDs increase the risk of heart attack and stroke.
- **Pregnancy**: avoided in the third trimester — can close the baby's ductus arteriosus early and delay labour.
- Allergy, rashes; nimesulide carries a liver risk and is not used in children.

### Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid)
- Acetylates COX **irreversibly**; in platelets (which cannot make new enzyme) the effect lasts for their life span of about 7–10 days.
- Uses: low-dose aspirin as an **antiplatelet** to prevent heart attack and stroke; pain, fever and inflammation at higher doses (now less used).
- **Reye's syndrome:** aspirin given to children and teenagers with a viral illness (influenza, chickenpox) can cause serious liver and brain damage. **Aspirin is not given to children for fever.**
- Stop before surgery as advised by the surgeon (bleeding).
- Interactions: warfarin and other anticoagulants (bleeding), methotrexate (toxicity rises), other NSAIDs (stomach damage).

### Nursing responsibilities with NSAIDs
1. Ask about peptic ulcer, bleeding disorders, asthma, kidney disease, heart disease, pregnancy, and current anticoagulants.
2. Give **after food or with milk**; a proton pump inhibitor is often co-prescribed for long courses.
3. Watch for black stools, blood-stained vomit, epigastric pain, reduced urine, oedema and rising BP.
4. Do not combine two NSAIDs; check that "cold and pain" medicines the patient buys do not contain another NSAID.
5. **Dengue fever:** NSAIDs and aspirin are avoided because of bleeding risk; paracetamol is used for fever.

## 2.3 Antipyretics and paracetamol

**Fever** occurs when pyrogens (from infection or tissue damage) cause prostaglandin E2 to raise the "set point" of the heat-regulating centre in the hypothalamus. Antipyretics lower the set point back to normal; they do not lower a normal temperature.

### Paracetamol (acetaminophen)
- **Action:** weak COX inhibitor, acting mainly in the brain; little effect in inflamed tissue, so it is a good analgesic and antipyretic but **not anti-inflammatory**.
- **Advantages:** no stomach irritation, no effect on platelets, safe in asthma; preferred antipyretic in children, pregnancy and dengue.
- **Uses:** fever, headache, mild to moderate pain; part of multimodal post-operative analgesia (oral or IV).
- **Adverse effects:** rare at the right dose; rash. Taken above the safe daily limit, or in people with liver disease, heavy alcohol use or malnutrition, it can cause serious **liver damage**. N-acetylcysteine is its specific antidote (Unit 5).
- **Nursing:** count all paracetamol from every source (many cold remedies contain it); follow the minimum gap between doses on the order; record temperature before and about an hour after; combine with cooling measures.

**Nursing care in fever:** check temperature, pulse and respiration regularly; light clothing and a ventilated room; **tepid sponging** in high fever (not cold or ice water, which causes shivering and raises heat production); plenty of oral fluids; watch children for febrile fits; find and treat the cause.

## 2.4 Opioids and other central analgesics

**Opioid receptors:** mu (μ), kappa (κ), delta (δ). Most strong opioid effects — analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, constipation, dependence — are through **mu receptors**.

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Natural (opium alkaloids) | Morphine, codeine |
| Semi-synthetic and synthetic agonists | Pethidine (meperidine), fentanyl, methadone, tramadol, tapentadol |
| Partial agonist | Buprenorphine |
| Mixed agonist–antagonists | Pentazocine, butorphanol, nalbuphine |
| Pure antagonists | **Naloxone** (reverses opioid effects; short-acting, by injection), **naltrexone** (oral; used in deaddiction, Unit 5) |

### Morphine — the prototype

| System | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| CNS | Strong analgesia, sedation, mood elevation (euphoria), suppression of cough centre, **respiratory depression** (main danger), stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone → nausea and vomiting, **pin-point pupils (miosis)** |
| GI tract | Reduced movement → **constipation** (no tolerance develops to this); spasm of the sphincter of Oddi → biliary pain |
| Urinary | Urinary retention |
| CVS | Vasodilatation, postural hypotension; reduces preload — helps acute pulmonary oedema |
| Skin | Histamine release → itching, flushing; can trigger bronchospasm |
| Other | Raised intracranial pressure (from CO2 retention) |

**Uses:** severe acute pain (fractures, burns, myocardial infarction, post-operative), cancer pain, acute left ventricular failure with pulmonary oedema, pre-anaesthetic medication; codeine and dextromethorphan for dry cough; loperamide (opioid that stays in the gut) for diarrhoea.

**Contraindications and cautions:** head injury (masks pupils and consciousness, raises ICP), respiratory disease and acute asthma, liver failure, hypothyroidism, the elderly and infants (more sensitive), labour close to delivery (can depress the newborn's breathing), biliary colic.

**Tolerance and dependence:** with repeated use larger amounts are needed for the same effect, and stopping suddenly causes withdrawal (yawning, watering eyes, runny nose, gooseflesh, cramps, diarrhoea, restlessness). In cancer pain these are not a reason to withhold good pain relief; doses are reduced gradually rather than stopped suddenly. Opioids are **narcotic drugs under the NDPS Act, 1985**, so hospitals keep them in a double-locked cupboard with a register.

**Interactions:** alcohol, benzodiazepines, antihistamines and other sedatives add to respiratory depression; MAO inhibitors with pethidine can cause a dangerous reaction; tramadol with SSRIs can cause serotonin syndrome.

**Other opioids — key points**
- **Pethidine:** shorter acting; anticholinergic-like effects; its breakdown product can cause fits with repeated doses; not preferred for long use.
- **Fentanyl:** very potent and short-acting; used in anaesthesia, as skin patches in chronic cancer pain (heat over the patch increases absorption), and by infusion in ICU.
- **Tramadol:** weak mu agonist that also blocks reuptake of serotonin and noradrenaline; less respiratory depression; can cause fits and nausea.
- **Buprenorphine:** partial agonist with a ceiling effect; used for pain (patches) and in opioid substitution therapy.
- **Pentazocine:** can cause hallucinations and raise BP; can bring on withdrawal in people dependent on morphine.

### Nursing responsibilities with opioids
1. **Assess before giving:** pain score, **respiratory rate**, sedation level, BP and pulse, allergies, last dose time. If the respiratory rate is low or the patient is very drowsy, **withhold and inform the doctor** according to the ward protocol.
2. Check the order, then **count and record in the narcotic register with a second nurse as witness**; sign for every ampoule, including wasted portions.
3. After giving: monitor respiration and sedation regularly (especially after the first dose and in the first hours after IV use); keep **naloxone and an Ambu bag** at hand.
4. Prevent constipation from the start: fluids, fibre, a laxative as ordered.
5. Watch urine output and bladder distension; nausea (antiemetic as ordered).
6. Safety: raise side rails, help with walking (postural hypotension, drowsiness).
7. Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA): teach that only the patient presses the button, not relatives.
8. Teach patients going home: no alcohol or sleeping pills alongside, no driving while drowsy, keep the medicine locked away from children, return unused stock to the pharmacy.

### Adjuvant analgesics
Amitriptyline (tricyclic antidepressant), gabapentin and pregabalin (diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia), **carbamazepine (drug of choice in trigeminal neuralgia)**, corticosteroids (raised ICP, nerve compression), bisphosphonates (bone pain), and drugs for migraine (triptans for attacks; propranolol, flunarizine for prevention).

## 2.5 General anaesthesia

**General anaesthesia (GA)** is a reversible state of loss of consciousness with **analgesia, amnesia, muscle relaxation and loss of reflexes**, produced by drugs so that surgery can be done without pain or awareness.

### Stages of anaesthesia (Guedel's stages, seen best with ether)

| Stage | Name | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Stage of analgesia | From start of induction to loss of consciousness; pain is reduced, patient still conscious |
| II | Stage of delirium (excitement) | Unconscious but restless, irregular breathing, vomiting possible — modern IV induction passes through it quickly |
| III | Stage of surgical anaesthesia | Regular breathing, muscle relaxation, eye movements cease; divided into four planes; surgery done in plane 2–3 |
| IV | Medullary paralysis | Failure of breathing and circulation — must never be reached |

### Techniques of GA
- **Induction:** usually by an IV agent (rapid and pleasant), or by inhaled gas in children who fear needles.
- **Maintenance:** inhaled agent or continuous IV infusion (total intravenous anaesthesia, TIVA).
- **Airway:** face mask, laryngeal mask airway, or endotracheal intubation (needs a muscle relaxant).
- **Balanced anaesthesia:** a combination of drugs, each doing one job — an IV inducer, an opioid for analgesia, a muscle relaxant, an inhaled agent for maintenance — so that each drug is used in a smaller amount with fewer side effects.
- **Recovery:** stopping the agents, reversal of muscle relaxant (neostigmine with glycopyrrolate, or sugammadex), extubation, and care in the post-anaesthesia care unit.

### IV anaesthetics

| Drug | Features | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thiopentone sodium | Ultra-short barbiturate; unconsciousness within one arm-brain circulation time; wakes quickly by **redistribution** from brain to muscle and fat | Extravasation or arterial injection is very irritant; can cause laryngospasm |
| Propofol | Rapid smooth induction and clear-headed recovery; also antiemetic | Pain on injection, fall in BP; preferred for day-care surgery; milky emulsion — strict asepsis |
| Ketamine | **Dissociative anaesthesia** — patient appears awake but feels no pain; good analgesia; raises BP and heart rate; relaxes bronchi | Emergence reactions (vivid dreams, hallucinations) — recover in a quiet room; useful in shock, asthma, burns dressings and children |
| Etomidate | Keeps heart and BP stable | Suppresses adrenal steroid production |
| Midazolam | Benzodiazepine; sedation and amnesia | Used for sedation in procedures and as premedication |

### Inhalational anaesthetics

| Agent | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Nitrous oxide | Weak anaesthetic, good analgesic; used as a carrier gas (see 2.7) |
| Sevoflurane | Pleasant smell, fast smooth induction — choice for inhaled induction in children |
| Isoflurane | Common maintenance agent; pungent |
| Desflurane | Very rapid recovery; irritant to airways |
| Halothane | Older; can sensitise the heart to adrenaline (arrhythmias) and rarely causes hepatitis |

**Malignant hyperthermia:** a rare inherited reaction to volatile anaesthetics and succinylcholine — rapid rise in temperature, muscle rigidity, high CO2. Treated with **dantrolene**, stopping the trigger, and cooling.

### Pre-anaesthetic medication

**Aims:** relieve anxiety, provide amnesia and analgesia, reduce secretions and vagal reflexes, prevent nausea and vomiting, reduce the risk of acid aspiration, and make induction smooth.

| Aim | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Anxiety relief, sedation, amnesia | Benzodiazepines — midazolam, diazepam, lorazepam |
| Analgesia | Opioids — fentanyl, morphine |
| Reduce secretions; prevent bradycardia from vagal reflexes | Anticholinergics — **glycopyrrolate**, atropine |
| Prevent nausea and vomiting | Ondansetron, metoclopramide, dexamethasone |
| Reduce gastric acid and volume (prevent aspiration pneumonitis — Mendelson's syndrome) | H2 blockers (famotidine), proton pump inhibitors (pantoprazole), metoclopramide, oral sodium citrate before caesarean section |

**Nurse's role before anaesthesia:** confirm written informed consent; nil by mouth as ordered (usually solids for several hours and clear fluids for a shorter time — follow the hospital policy); remove dentures, jewellery, nail polish and make-up; ask the patient to pass urine; record vital signs, weight and allergies; give premedication exactly at the ordered time; after a sedative premedication, **the patient stays in bed with side rails up** and does not sign any further documents.

**Nurse's role after GA:** maintain the airway (lateral position until reflexes return), monitor oxygen saturation, pulse, BP and level of consciousness; watch for nausea, shivering, pain and bleeding; do not give oral fluids until swallowing and cough reflexes are back.

## 2.6 Local anaesthetics

**Definition:** drugs that, applied locally to nerve tissue, reversibly block nerve conduction, causing loss of sensation in a limited area without loss of consciousness.

**Mechanism:** they enter the nerve fibre and **block voltage-gated sodium channels** from inside, so an action potential cannot be generated. Small, thin fibres are blocked first: **pain → temperature → touch → pressure → motor**; recovery is in the reverse order.

| Type | Examples | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Esters** | Procaine, chloroprocaine, tetracaine, benzocaine | Broken down by plasma cholinesterase; short-acting; more allergy |
| **Amides** | **Lignocaine (lidocaine)**, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, prilocaine | Broken down in the liver; allergy rare. Trick: amides have an extra "i" before "-caine" |

- **Lignocaine:** quick and medium-acting; surface, infiltration, nerve blocks; also an antiarrhythmic (IV).
- **Bupivacaine:** long-acting; spinal and epidural anaesthesia; more harmful to the heart if it reaches the blood.
- **Ropivacaine:** long-acting, less cardiotoxic, less motor block — good for labour epidurals.
- **EMLA cream** (lignocaine + prilocaine): numbs skin before cannulation in children (apply under a dressing for the time stated).

**Adrenaline with local anaesthetic:** constricts vessels so the drug stays longer at the site, lasts longer, absorbs less into blood and causes less bleeding. **Never in areas supplied by end arteries** — fingers, toes, penis, ear lobe, tip of nose — because gangrene can result. Avoid in hypertension and heart disease.

**Techniques:** surface (topical) anaesthesia, infiltration, field block, nerve block, **spinal (subarachnoid) anaesthesia**, **epidural anaesthesia** (lumbar for labour and surgery; caudal in children), and IV regional anaesthesia (Bier's block).

**Adverse effects** when too much reaches the blood: numbness of tongue and lips, metallic taste, ringing in the ears, dizziness, muscle twitching, convulsions; fall in BP, slow heart rate, arrhythmias. Allergy (mainly esters).

**Spinal anaesthesia complications and nursing:** fall in BP (sympathetic block — IV fluids and vasopressor as ordered; monitor BP closely); slow heart rate; nausea; **post-dural puncture headache** (worse on sitting up — rest, fluids, analgesics, report); urinary retention; high block causing breathing difficulty.

**Nursing responsibilities with local anaesthetics**
- Ask about allergy to "caine" drugs and earlier reactions.
- Aspiration before injection is done by the operator to avoid injecting into a vessel; keep resuscitation drugs and equipment ready.
- Protect the numb part from injury (burns from hot bottles, pressure); after throat spray, **nothing by mouth until the gag reflex returns** (aspiration risk).
- Record when sensation and movement return.

## 2.7 Medical gases

### Oxygen
**Uses:** hypoxaemia from any cause — pneumonia, COPD exacerbation, heart failure, shock, cardiac arrest, carbon monoxide poisoning, anaesthesia and recovery, newborn resuscitation (as guided by saturation).

| Device | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Nasal cannula | Low flow; comfortable, patient can eat and talk |
| Simple face mask | Moderate concentration |
| Venturi mask | Delivers a fixed, known concentration — preferred in COPD |
| Non-rebreathing mask with reservoir bag | High concentration in emergencies |
| High-flow nasal oxygen, CPAP, ventilators | ICU use |

**Hazards of oxygen therapy**
- **Fire:** oxygen supports burning — no smoking, no naked flames, no oil or grease on fittings, no woollen or synthetic blankets that make sparks; display "No smoking" signs.
- **CO2 narcosis in chronic COPD:** some patients depend on low oxygen to drive breathing; uncontrolled high oxygen can make breathing slow and CO2 rise. Give controlled oxygen to the **target saturation set by the doctor** (commonly lower in COPD than in other patients).
- **Oxygen toxicity of the lungs** with high concentrations for long periods; **absorption collapse** of alveoli.
- **Retinopathy of prematurity** in preterm babies — strict saturation targets in NICU.
- **Drying of mucosa** — humidify at higher flows; oral and nasal care.

### Nitrous oxide (N2O, "laughing gas")
- Sweet-smelling, non-irritant; **good analgesic, weak anaesthetic**; quick onset and offset.
- Uses: carrier gas with other anaesthetics; **Entonox (50% N2O + 50% O2)** for pain relief in labour, dressings and ambulances.
- **Diffusion hypoxia:** at the end of anaesthesia N2O floods out into the alveoli and dilutes oxygen — give 100% oxygen for a few minutes.
- Enters closed air spaces and expands them — avoid in pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, middle-ear surgery and after some eye surgeries using gas bubbles.
- Long or repeated exposure interferes with vitamin B12 → bone marrow and nerve damage.

### Carbon dioxide (CO2)
- Stimulates breathing (through chemoreceptors) and dilates cerebral vessels.
- Uses: **insufflation of the abdomen in laparoscopy**; cryotherapy (as solid "dry ice" or liquid CO2) for warts and cervical lesions; mixed with oxygen in some respiratory and anaesthesia uses.
- After laparoscopy, CO2 under the diaphragm causes **shoulder-tip pain**.
- Excess CO2 (hypercapnia) causes headache, flushing, confusion and acidosis.

### Other medical gases
- **Medical air:** for ventilators and to drive nebulisers.
- **Helium–oxygen (heliox):** low density, eases breathing past a narrowed airway.
- **Nitric oxide (inhaled):** relaxes pulmonary vessels in newborns with persistent pulmonary hypertension.
- **Ethylene oxide:** a gas used for sterilising heat-sensitive equipment, not for patients.

### Cylinder safety (nurse's responsibility)
- Read the **label** on the cylinder; colour codes (oxygen commonly black body with white shoulder, nitrous oxide blue, carbon dioxide grey) help, but the label is what you rely on.
- Store upright, chained or in a trolley, in a cool, ventilated place away from heat and flames.
- Open the valve slowly; check the pressure gauge and the flow meter; never use oil or grease; replace a near-empty cylinder in time.
- Use pin-index connections so that the wrong gas cannot be fitted.

## Remember

- NSAIDs block COX → less prostaglandin → analgesic, antipyretic, anti-inflammatory (± antiplatelet).
- Main NSAID dangers: stomach ulcer and bleeding, kidney injury, asthma, third-trimester pregnancy. Give after food.
- Aspirin: irreversible COX block, antiplatelet for the platelet's life; Reye's syndrome — not for children with viral fever.
- Paracetamol: no stomach or platelet effect; liver damage when the safe limit is exceeded; antidote N-acetylcysteine. Use in dengue fever.
- Morphine: mu agonist; respiratory depression, miosis, constipation, retention, itching, vomiting. Check respiratory rate first; naloxone reverses.
- Opioids are controlled under the NDPS Act: locked cupboard, register, witness.
- WHO ladder: by mouth, by the clock, by the ladder.
- GA = unconsciousness + analgesia + amnesia + relaxation; balanced anaesthesia uses several drugs.
- Thiopentone wakes by redistribution; propofol for day surgery; ketamine dissociative with emergence reactions; sevoflurane for children; dantrolene for malignant hyperthermia.
- Premedication: midazolam, fentanyl, glycopyrrolate, ondansetron, H2 blocker/PPI.
- Local anaesthetics block Na+ channels; pain fibres first; no adrenaline in fingers, toes, penis, ear, nose.
- Spinal anaesthesia: watch BP, headache, retention.
- Oxygen: fire hazard; controlled oxygen in COPD; ROP in preterms; humidify.
- N2O: analgesic; Entonox for labour; diffusion hypoxia — give 100% O2 at the end.
- CO2: laparoscopy insufflation (shoulder-tip pain), cryotherapy.
