# Unit 4: Sex hormones, contraception, uterine drugs and drugs used in pregnancy and labour

This unit covers drugs that act on the reproductive system. Hormones, contraceptives and uterine drugs all appear in examinations. Learn each drug group as class → mechanism → uses → adverse effects → nurse's responsibilities.

---

## 4.1 Sex hormones

### Oestrogens

Oestrogens are the primary female sex hormones. The natural hormone is **oestradiol**; the synthetic form used in medicines is **ethinylestradiol**.

**Mechanism:** Bind to oestrogen receptors in the uterus, breast, bone, liver and brain, stimulating female secondary sexual characteristics and regulating the menstrual cycle.

**Uses:**
- Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in menopause — relieve hot flushes, night sweats, vaginal dryness
- Contraception (combined with progestogen)
- Prevention of post-menopausal osteoporosis
- Primary ovarian failure, delayed puberty
- Palliative treatment of some hormone-sensitive cancers (prostate cancer)

**Adverse effects:** Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism, risk of breast and endometrial cancer (endometrial cancer reduced if combined with progestogen), nausea, fluid retention, hypertension, headache, reduced libido, breakthrough bleeding.

**Contraindications:** Personal or family history of DVT or thromboembolism, oestrogen-sensitive cancers, undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, active liver disease, pregnancy.

**Nurse's responsibilities:** Explain the risk–benefit discussion, encourage regular breast self-examination and mammography, teach warning signs of DVT (calf pain, swelling), advise that smoking greatly increases DVT risk, remind patients not to stop suddenly.

---

### Progestogens

Progestogens are synthetic progesterone-like hormones. Examples: **progesterone** (natural), **medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA)**, **norethisterone**, **levonorgestrel**, **desogestrel**.

**Mechanism:** Bind to progesterone receptors, suppress LH surge, thicken cervical mucus, thin the endometrium, maintain early pregnancy.

**Uses:**
- Contraception (alone or combined with oestrogen)
- HRT (added to oestrogen to protect the endometrium)
- Endometriosis (suppress cyclical endometrial bleeding)
- Dysfunctional uterine bleeding, premenstrual syndrome
- Secondary amenorrhoea
- Threatened and recurrent miscarriage (progesterone support)

**Adverse effects:** Mood changes (depression, irritability), weight gain, acne, reduced libido, irregular bleeding, headache. Depo-MPA may cause prolonged loss of periods and delay return of fertility.

**Nurse's responsibilities:** Warn about mood changes, advise on expected bleeding patterns, explain that fertility returns slowly after injectable progestogens.

---

### Androgens

**Testosterone** and its analogues are male sex hormones also used medically. **Danazol** is a modified androgen (attenuated androgen/antigonadotrophin).

| Drug | Uses | Adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testosterone (oral, injection, patch, gel) | Male hypogonadism, delayed puberty, hormone-sensitive cancers | Acne, fluid retention, polycythaemia, aggression, liver toxicity (oral), testicular atrophy, azoospermia |
| Danazol | Endometriosis, fibrocystic breast disease, hereditary angio-oedema | Androgenic effects — acne, weight gain, hirsutism, voice deepening; hepatotoxicity; teratogenic (use reliable contraception) |

## 4.2 Contraception

### Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP)

Contains an oestrogen (ethinylestradiol) plus a progestogen (e.g. levonorgestrel, norethisterone, gestodene).

**Mechanism:**
1. Inhibit the LH and FSH surge → no ovulation
2. Thicken cervical mucus → sperm cannot penetrate
3. Thin endometrium → implantation unfavourable

**Efficacy:** >99% if taken correctly.

**Adverse effects:** DVT and pulmonary embolism, hypertension, nausea, breast tenderness, headache, mood changes, reduced libido, breakthrough bleeding. Rare: stroke and myocardial infarction (especially in smokers).

**Contraindications:** Age >35 and smoker, hypertension, migraine with aura, personal history of DVT/PE, oestrogen-sensitive cancers, active liver disease, breastfeeding in the first 6 weeks.

**Missed pill advice:** One missed pill — take as soon as remembered; if >12 hours since last pill, take the missed pill and use barrier contraception for the next 7 days.

**Drug interactions:** Enzyme-inducers (rifampicin, some anticonvulsants) reduce efficacy → use a higher-dose pill or alternative method.

---

### Progestogen-only pill (POP / minipill)

Examples: norethisterone, levonorgestrel, desogestrel.

**Mechanism:** Mainly thickens cervical mucus; some suppress ovulation (desogestrel more reliably).

**Advantages over COCP:** Can be used when oestrogen is contraindicated — smokers, hypertension, migraine, breastfeeding, age >35.

**Missed pill:** Traditional POP — take within 3 hours of normal time; desogestrel POP — 12-hour window.

---

### Emergency contraception

| Method | Timing | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg oral | Within 72 hours; more effective the sooner taken | Delays or inhibits ovulation; not an abortifacient |
| Ulipristal acetate 30 mg oral (ellaOne) | Within 120 hours (5 days) | Blocks progesterone receptor, delays ovulation |
| Copper intra-uterine device (IUD) | Within 120 hours (5 days) | Toxic to sperm and egg; also prevents implantation; most effective method |

Nurse's role: provide non-judgmental advice, explain that it does not protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), offer regular contraception counselling.

---

### Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC)

| Method | Duration | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Injectable DMPA (Depo-Provera) | 150 mg IM every 3 months | Highly effective; amenorrhoea common; delay in return of fertility (up to 12 months) |
| Subdermal implant (etonogestrel — Nexplanon) | 3 years | Inserted under skin of upper arm; progestogen-only; immediate efficacy; reversible |
| Copper IUD | 5–10 years | Non-hormonal; can also be used as emergency contraception; dysmenorrhoea and heavier periods |
| Hormonal IUS (levonorgestrel — Mirena) | 5 years | Lightens or stops periods; also treats menorrhagia and endometriosis |
| Male condom | Per use | Protects against STIs; no prescription needed |

---

### Permanent methods

Bilateral tubal ligation (women) and vasectomy (men) — surgical, considered irreversible; must be fully informed and consent freely.

## 4.3 Drugs for endometriosis and uterine fibroids

**Danazol:** Suppresses FSH and LH → low oestrogen → atrophy of endometriotic deposits. Adverse effects: androgenic (see above), not used in pregnancy.

**GnRH analogues (leuprolide, goserelin, nafarelin):** Continuous use downregulates GnRH receptors → markedly reduced FSH and LH → "medical menopause." Used for endometriosis and to shrink uterine fibroids before surgery. Adverse effects: menopausal symptoms (hot flushes, osteoporosis with long-term use); add-back oestrogen reduces bone loss. Given as monthly or three-monthly depot injection, or as nasal spray.

**Ulipristal acetate (Esmya):** Selective progesterone receptor modulator; shrinks fibroids; used short-term pre-surgery.

**Tranexamic acid:** Antifibrinolytic; reduces menorrhagia by preventing clot breakdown; also used in fibroid-associated heavy periods. Dose 500–1000 mg three times daily only on heavy days.

## 4.4 Uterotonic drugs (drugs that contract the uterus)

### Oxytocin

Synthetic version of the pituitary hormone. Stimulates uterine contractions and milk let-down.

**Uses:**
- Induction of labour (IV infusion, titrated carefully)
- Augmentation of slow labour
- Prevention and treatment of postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) — 10 IU IM (or IV slowly) immediately after delivery of the baby or placenta

**Adverse effects of IV infusion:** Uterine hyperstimulation → foetal distress; hypotension; water intoxication (antidiuretic effect at high doses — restrict fluid intake); nausea; rapid IV bolus can cause cardiovascular collapse.

**Nurse's responsibilities:** Use an infusion pump; titrate to contractions (3 in 10 minutes, lasting 60 seconds); continuously monitor uterine contractions and foetal heart rate; stop infusion if hyperstimulation occurs; keep IV line in situ for drug administration if emergency arises.

---

### Misoprostol

Synthetic **prostaglandin E1 (PGE1)** analogue. WHO essential medicine for PPH and obstetric use, particularly in settings where oxytocin and refrigeration may not be available.

**Uses:**
- Prevention and treatment of PPH: 600 mcg oral or sublingual (often used in community or home births)
- Cervical ripening before induction of labour
- Medical management of incomplete miscarriage and medical abortion (combined with mifepristone)

**Adverse effects:** Fever, shivering (common, especially sublingual route — reassure and monitor temperature), diarrhoea, uterine hyperstimulation.

**Nurse's responsibilities:** Explain likely shivering and fever; monitor temperature and contractions; available without refrigeration — important for community setting.

---

### Ergometrine (ergot alkaloid)

Causes sustained tonic uterine contraction (unlike oxytocin which causes rhythmic contractions).

**Uses:** Prevention and treatment of PPH — given after delivery of the placenta; combined with oxytocin as **Syntometrine** (oxytocin 5 IU + ergometrine 0.5 mg IM).

**Adverse effects:** Hypertension, nausea, vomiting, vasospasm. Peripheral vasoconstriction may cause Raynaud's phenomenon.

**Contraindications:** Pre-eclampsia, hypertension, cardiovascular disease.

**Nurse's responsibilities:** Check BP before giving; do not use in hypertensive patients; have anti-emetic ready.

---

### Dinoprostone (prostaglandin E2)

Vaginal gel or pessary for cervical ripening before induction of labour. Can cause hyperstimulation; continuous CTG monitoring after administration.

| Drug | Route | Main use | Key caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oxytocin | IV infusion / IM | Induction, augmentation, PPH | Hyperstimulation; water intoxication |
| Misoprostol | Oral / sublingual / vaginal | PPH, cervical ripening, abortion | Shivering; no refrigeration needed |
| Ergometrine | IM / IV | PPH (after placenta delivered) | CI in hypertension |
| Dinoprostone | Vaginal gel | Cervical ripening | Hyperstimulation |
| Syntometrine | IM | PPH prevention at delivery | CI in hypertension |

## 4.5 Drugs used in pregnancy

### Tocolytics (drugs that delay premature labour)

Goal: delay birth by 24–48 hours to allow betamethasone to mature foetal lungs or transfer to a unit with neonatal intensive care.

| Drug | Class | Mechanism | Monitoring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nifedipine | Calcium channel blocker | Relaxes uterine smooth muscle | Blood pressure, headache |
| Atosiban | Oxytocin antagonist | Blocks oxytocin receptor | IV infusion protocol |
| Terbutaline | Beta-2 agonist | Relaxes smooth muscle | Tachycardia, hypokalaemia, hyperglycaemia |

Nurse: continuous maternal and foetal monitoring; bedside rest; emotional support.

---

### Magnesium sulfate (MgSO4)

**Uses:**
- Prevention and treatment of eclamptic fits in pre-eclampsia/eclampsia
- Neuroprotection of the preterm foetus (reduces cerebral palsy risk when given before 32 weeks)

**Dosing (Pritchard/Zuspan regimen — commonly taught):** Loading dose 4 g IV over 15–20 minutes, then maintenance 1–2 g/h IV infusion.

**Signs of magnesium toxicity — monitor HOURLY:**
- Loss of patellar (knee-jerk) reflex → early sign
- Respiratory rate < 12 breaths/minute → dangerous
- Urine output < 25 mL/h (magnesium excreted by kidneys)
- Serum magnesium > 7.5 mEq/L → cardiac arrest risk

**Antidote:** Calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% solution IV slowly — have it drawn up at the bedside.

**Nurse's key role:** Hourly reflex, respiratory rate and urine output checks; know the antidote location; never leave the patient alone during loading dose.

---

### Betamethasone (antenatal corticosteroids)

**Use:** Foetal lung maturity in threatened preterm labour before 34–35 weeks. Two doses 24 hours apart. Reduces risk of respiratory distress syndrome, necrotising enterocolitis and intraventricular haemorrhage in the preterm neonate. 
Monitor maternal blood sugar (steroids raise glucose, important in gestational diabetes).

---

### Anti-emetics in pregnancy

| Drug | Safety | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) | First choice | Often combined with doxylamine; safest |
| Metoclopramide | Generally safe | Short-term use; extrapyramidal effects in young women |
| Ondansetron | Used in severe cases | Concerns in first trimester (small cardiac defect risk); benefit-risk discussion needed |
| Promethazine | Used | Sedating |

---

### Iron and folic acid supplementation

- **Folic acid 400 mcg/day** before conception and in first 12 weeks — reduces neural tube defects. 5 mg/day if high risk (previous NTD, antiepileptics, diabetes, obesity).
- **Iron**: routine supplementation in India (National Iron Plus Initiative) — ferrous sulfate or fumarate; take on empty stomach with vitamin C for best absorption.

---

### Drugs to avoid in pregnancy (teratogens — selected)

| Drug | Risk |
| --- | --- |
| ACE inhibitors (e.g. enalapril) | Renal tubular dysgenesis, neonatal renal failure (second/third trimester) |
| Warfarin | Warfarin embryopathy (first trimester), foetal haemorrhage; use heparin instead |
| Sodium valproate | Neural tube defects, foetal valproate syndrome (cognitive impairment) |
| Retinoids (isotretinoin) | Severe craniofacial, cardiac and CNS malformations — teratogen of highest concern |
| NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen) | Premature closure of ductus arteriosus if used in third trimester; oligohydramnios |
| Aminoglycosides (e.g. gentamicin) | Foetal ototoxicity (hearing loss) |
| Thalidomide | Phocomelia (historical example; still used in leprosy/myeloma with strict REMS) |

**Nurse's role:** Ask about all medicines (including OTC and herbal) at every antenatal contact; never stop essential medication without medical advice; report any concerns to prescriber.

---

## Remember

- OCP mechanism: inhibit LH/FSH → no ovulation; thicken cervical mucus; thin endometrium.
- OCP absolute contraindications: smoking over 35, migraine with aura, personal history of DVT, oestrogen-dependent cancer, active liver disease.
- Missed OCP rule: >12 hours late — take missed pill + barrier for 7 days.
- Emergency contraception: levonorgestrel within 72 h; ulipristal within 120 h; copper IUD within 120 h (most effective).
- PPH drugs in order: oxytocin (first line, 10 IU IM) → misoprostol (community setting) → ergometrine (CI in hypertension) → tranexamic acid.
- MgSO4 toxicity signs: lost knee-jerk reflex first; then RR < 12; antidote = calcium gluconate at the bedside.
- Betamethasone: given before 34–35 weeks to mature foetal lungs; two doses 24 h apart.
- Teratogens: valproate (neural tube), warfarin (embryopathy), retinoids (severe malformations), ACE inhibitors (renal), NSAIDs in third trimester (ductus arteriosus).
