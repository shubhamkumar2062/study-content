# Unit 5: Nursing management of neurological disorders

This unit covers INC Unit V: anatomy and physiology of the nervous system, neurological assessment, and nursing management of common neurological disorders. Neurological emergencies (stroke, status epilepticus) require rapid recognition and action — learn the nursing priorities for each.

---

## 5.1 Review of anatomy and physiology

The nervous system is divided into:
- **Central nervous system (CNS)**: brain and spinal cord.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS)**: 12 pairs of cranial nerves, 31 pairs of spinal nerves, and the autonomic nervous system (sympathetic and parasympathetic).

The brain is protected by the skull, three meninges (dura, arachnoid, pia), and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). The blood-brain barrier restricts entry of many substances into brain tissue.

```text
   BRAIN
   ├── Cerebrum — voluntary movement, sensation, speech, cognition, personality
   ├── Cerebellum — coordination, balance, fine movement
   ├── Brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla) — heart rate, breathing, consciousness, cranial nerves
   └── Limbic system — emotion, memory, smell

   SPINAL CORD — relay for sensory (ascending) and motor (descending) signals
```

Neurones transmit electrical impulses; synaptic transmission uses neurotransmitters (acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA, glutamate). Neurones cannot regenerate significantly; glial cells (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia) support them.

## 5.2 Neurological assessment

A systematic neurological assessment should be done on every patient with known or suspected neurological illness, and as a baseline for all critically ill patients.

### Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

| Component | Response | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Eye opening (E)** | Spontaneous | 4 |
| | To voice | 3 |
| | To pain | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| **Verbal (V)** | Oriented | 5 |
| | Confused | 4 |
| | Inappropriate words | 3 |
| | Incomprehensible sounds | 2 |
| | None | 1 |
| **Motor (M)** | Obeys commands | 6 |
| | Localises pain | 5 |
| | Withdraws from pain | 4 |
| | Abnormal flexion (decorticate) | 3 |
| | Abnormal extension (decerebrate) | 2 |
| | None | 1 |

**Maximum GCS = 15 (fully conscious). GCS ≤ 8 = severe neurological impairment — intubation and ventilation must be considered.**

Document as E_V_M_ (e.g. E3V4M5) and total score. Record separately, not just the total, so trends are visible.

### Pupils

Normal: 2–5 mm, round, equal, briskly reactive to light. Abnormal:
- Unilateral dilated, fixed pupil: uncal herniation (raised ICP compressing CN III on that side) — neurosurgical emergency.
- Bilateral pin-point pupils: opioid overdose, pontine lesion.
- Bilateral dilated, fixed: brain death, severe hypoxia.

### Signs of raised intracranial pressure (ICP)

- **Cushing's triad** (late, ominous sign): hypertension (rising systolic), bradycardia, irregular breathing.
- Early signs: headache (worse in the morning or on coughing), nausea, vomiting (often sudden without nausea), altered consciousness, unilateral pupil change.

### Additional assessment

- **Motor**: limb strength (grade 0–5), tone, coordination.
- **Sensory**: light touch, pain/temperature, proprioception.
- **Cranial nerves**: 12 pairs (I–XII); test as clinically indicated (II — visual acuity/fields; III/IV/VI — eye movements; V — facial sensation; VII — facial movement; IX/X — gag reflex; XII — tongue deviation).
- **Vital signs**: blood pressure, pulse, temperature, respiratory pattern.
- **Orientation**: to person, place and time (mini mental state).

## 5.3 Headache

### Tension headache

Bilateral, non-pulsating, pressing/tightening quality; mild to moderate intensity; not aggravated by activity; not accompanied by vomiting. Most common type. Management: paracetamol, NSAIDs, rest; identify and reduce triggers (stress, poor posture, dehydration).

### Migraine

Episodic, often unilateral, pulsating, moderate to severe; nausea/vomiting; worsened by activity; photophobia and phonophobia. Aura (visual zigzag, blind spot, tingling) precedes headache in classic migraine.

**Management**:
- Acute: triptans (sumatriptan) — specific, most effective; NSAIDs; anti-emetics (metoclopramide, domperidone); rest in a dark quiet room; ice pack.
- Preventive (for ≥4 attacks/month): beta-blockers (propranolol), amitriptyline, topiramate, sodium valproate, CGRP antagonists.

**Nursing**: teach trigger identification and diary; avoid overuse of analgesics (medication overuse headache); take triptan early in the attack.

## 5.4 Epilepsy

**Epilepsy** is a chronic disorder characterised by recurrent unprovoked seizures due to abnormal excessive neuronal discharge.

### Classification

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Focal (partial) onset aware | Remains conscious; motor, sensory, autonomic or psychic symptoms |
| Focal (partial) onset impaired awareness | Altered consciousness; automatisms (lip-smacking, hand-wringing) |
| Focal to bilateral tonic–clonic | Begins focal, then generalises |
| Generalised tonic–clonic (GTC) | Sudden loss of consciousness, tonic rigidity then clonic jerking; tongue biting; incontinence; post-ictal confusion |
| Absence | Brief (< 30 s) staring, unresponsiveness; no post-ictal phase; childhood onset |
| Myoclonic | Brief, sudden muscle jerks; often in the morning |
| Atonic | Sudden loss of muscle tone → fall ("drop attack") |

### Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs)

| Drug | Main use | Key adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phenytoin | GTC, focal | Gum hypertrophy, ataxia, nystagmus, skin rash, folic acid deficiency; zero-order kinetics — small dose increase → large level rise |
| Carbamazepine | Focal, GTC | Rash (rarely Stevens-Johnson syndrome), diplopia, hyponatraemia, teratogenic |
| Sodium valproate | All types | Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity; highly teratogenic (neural tube defects) — do not use in women of childbearing potential without specialist review |
| Lamotrigine | All types | Rash (slow titration reduces risk), hepatotoxicity rare |
| Levetiracetam | Broad spectrum | Irritability, mood changes — often well tolerated |
| Ethosuximide | Absence only | Nausea, hiccup |

### Nursing during a seizure

1. **Do not restrain** — guide gently to the floor if the patient is standing.
2. **Protect the head** — place something soft under it; remove hard objects from the area.
3. **Time the seizure** (note start and end time).
4. **Turn to recovery position** (lateral) after the tonic-clonic phase to maintain airway and prevent aspiration.
5. **Do not put anything in the mouth** (old practice of inserting a spoon is harmful — it causes dental/oral injury and does not prevent tongue biting).
6. **Stay with the patient** throughout.
7. Post-ictally: reassure (confusion is normal), check for injury, allow rest.
8. **Call for help immediately** if the seizure lasts > 5 minutes or if there is no return to baseline.

### Status epilepticus

**Definition**: a seizure lasting > 5 minutes, or two seizures without return to baseline between them. A medical emergency.

**Management**:
```text
   0–5 min: Secure airway, 100% O₂, IV access, glucose check, safety
   5–10 min: IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg (or IV diazepam 0.2 mg/kg) — FIRST LINE
   10–20 min: Repeat benzodiazepine if still seizing
   20–40 min: IV phenytoin (or fosphenytoin, levetiracetam, or valproate) — SECOND LINE
   >40 min: Anaesthesia (thiopentone, propofol, midazolam infusion) + intubation — refractory status epilepticus
```

**Safety advice for epileptic patients**: do not drive until seizure-free for the prescribed period (6–12 months in India); avoid unsupervised swimming, heights, or unprotected fires; take medications regularly; identify and avoid triggers (sleep deprivation, alcohol, flashing lights for photosensitive).

## 5.5 Stroke (cerebrovascular accident)

**Stroke** is sudden-onset focal neurological deficit caused by either ischaemia or haemorrhage. Time is brain — every minute of delay means more neurons die.

| Feature | Ischaemic stroke (80%) | Haemorrhagic stroke (20%) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Thrombosis or embolism of cerebral artery | Rupture of blood vessel — intracerebral (hypertension) or subarachnoid (aneurysm) |
| CT appearance | Normal in first few hours; later hypodense (pale) area | Hyperdense (bright white) blood immediately |
| Level of consciousness | Variable | Often sudden severe headache, rapid LOC |
| Blood pressure | Variable | Usually very high |
| Treatment | Thrombolysis (alteplase) within 4.5 hours if no haemorrhage; aspirin + statin; mechanical thrombectomy | Lower BP gradually, surgical if large haematoma, treat aneurysm (coiling/clipping) |
| Anticoagulation | For atrial fibrillation (cardioembolic) | Contraindicated |

**Signs**: FAST — **F**acial droop (smile test), **A**rm weakness (hold both arms out), **S**peech difficulty (slurred/confused), **T**ime to call emergency services.

**Investigation**: CT head immediately (before thrombolysis — to exclude haemorrhage), then MRI; ECG (atrial fibrillation), echocardiogram, carotid Doppler.

**Thrombolysis with alteplase**: within 4.5 hours of symptom onset; contraindicated if haemorrhage on CT, BP > 185/110, recent surgery, coagulopathy.

**Nursing care**:
- Airway management: nurse with head elevated 30° unless hypotension; watch for dysphagia (nil by mouth until a swallowing assessment — NIHSS dysphagia screen); aspirate secretions if needed.
- Monitor neuro (GCS, pupils, limb power) hourly for first 24 hours.
- Blood pressure: in ischaemic stroke do NOT lower acutely unless extremely high (permissive hypertension for the first 24–72 hours; allow BP up to 220/120 if no thrombolysis).
- Prevent secondary complications: pressure sores (turn 2-hourly), DVT (compression stockings, anticoagulation when appropriate), constipation, urinary retention (bladder scan, catheterise if needed), chest infection (swallowing assessment, oral care), shoulder subluxation (position and support affected arm), falls.
- Rehabilitation: start physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy early; set realistic goals with the patient and family.

## 5.6 Meningitis

**Meningitis** is inflammation of the meninges. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency.

**Features**: sudden onset high fever, severe headache, neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity), photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, vomiting; Kernig's sign (inability to extend knee with hip flexed), Brudzinski's sign (hips flex when neck is flexed). **Non-blanching petechial/purpuric rash** = meningococcal septicaemia (most urgent).

**Investigations**: CT head first if any signs of raised ICP or focal deficit (to exclude herniation before LP); **lumbar puncture** (LP) — CSF analysis.

| CSF feature | Normal | Bacterial | Viral | TB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Clear | Turbid/purulent | Clear | Clear/slightly turbid |
| WBC | < 5 lymphocytes | Thousands (PMNs) | Hundreds (lymphocytes) | Hundreds (lymphocytes) |
| Protein | 0.2–0.4 g/L | High (> 1 g/L) | Slightly raised | Raised |
| Glucose | > 60% of blood | Very low (< 40%) | Normal | Low |

**Management**:
- Bacterial: IV antibiotics without delay (do not wait for LP results if LP is not immediately possible) — ceftriaxone 2 g IV 12-hourly; dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg 6-hourly for 4 days (reduces inflammation and risk of deafness).
- Viral (usually enteroviral): supportive care (analgesia, fluids); herpes simplex encephalitis must be treated with IV aciclovir.
- TB meningitis: standard anti-TB drugs (RRHZE) + dexamethasone.

**Nursing care**:
- Droplet precautions and gown/gloves until bacterial meningitis is excluded or pathogen identified (meningococcal — droplet precautions for 24 hours after antibiotic start).
- **Dark, quiet room** — patient is photophobic and phonophobic; minimise stimulation.
- Analgesia (paracetamol, NSAIDs) for severe headache.
- Monitor temperature, blood pressure, GCS, pupils and fluid balance closely.
- IV fluids (maintain euvolaemia — avoid fluid overload which worsens cerebral oedema, avoid dehydration which causes hypotension).
- Seizure precautions (padded cot sides, airway adjuncts at bedside).
- Notify public health if meningococcal — contacts need prophylaxis (ciprofloxacin or rifampicin).

## 5.7 Parkinson's disease

**Parkinson's disease (PD)** is a progressive neurodegenerative disease of dopaminergic neurones in the substantia nigra, leading to dopamine deficiency. Lewy bodies (alpha-synuclein aggregates) are the pathological marker.

**Features (cardinal motor signs)**:
- **T**remor — resting "pill-rolling" tremor, reduced during movement.
- **R**igidity — lead-pipe or cogwheel (ratchety) resistance to passive movement.
- **A**kinesia/bradykinesia — slowness and poverty of movement; mask face, reduced arm swing, micrographia (small writing).
- **P**ostural instability (late) — festinant gait (shuffling, forward-leaning), falls.

Non-motor features: depression, dementia (later), constipation, autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension), sleep disturbance, reduced sense of smell.

**Drug treatment**:

| Drug | Mechanism | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Levodopa + carbidopa (co-careldopa, Sinemet) | Levodopa converted to dopamine in CNS; carbidopa prevents peripheral conversion | Most effective; on-off fluctuations and dyskinesias with long use; give with food to reduce nausea |
| Dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) | Directly stimulate dopamine receptors | Useful early or adjunct; hallucinations, impulse-control disorders |
| MAO-B inhibitors (selegiline, rasagiline) | Slow dopamine breakdown | Neuroprotective effect debated; mild effect |
| Amantadine | Multiple mechanisms | Reduces dyskinesias; livedo reticularis |
| Anticholinergics (benzhexol) | Reduce acetylcholine (imbalance with dopamine) | Tremor; avoid in elderly (confusion, urinary retention, constipation) |

**Nursing care**:
- Medication timing is critical: late doses → severe "off" periods with inability to move; never stop abruptly.
- **Safety**: fall prevention (non-slip footwear, grab rails, raised toilet seat, bed rails), monitor for orthostatic hypotension (rise slowly), remove trip hazards.
- **Communication**: speech may be quiet and monotonous; face the patient, speak clearly, allow time; refer to speech and language therapy for dysarthria.
- **Swallowing**: semi-solid food if dysphagia; small frequent meals; upright position; thickened fluids if needed; PEG if aspiration is severe.
- **Activity**: physiotherapy for gait training, balance, range of motion; occupational therapy for ADL aids.
- **Psychological support**: depression is very common; screen regularly; antidepressants (SSRIs preferred).

## 5.8 Spinal cord injury (SCI)

**Spinal cord injury** may be complete (no motor or sensory function below the injury level) or incomplete (some function preserved).

**Level of injury determines function lost**:
- Cervical (C3–C5): diaphragm paralysis → ventilator dependent.
- C5–C6: some arm movement; hands/legs paralysed (tetraplegia/quadriplegia).
- Thoracic: paraplegia; bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction.
- Lumbar/sacral: lower limb weakness; bladder/bowel dysfunction.

**Neurogenic (spinal) shock**: in the first days after SCI — flaccid paralysis, loss of reflexes, bradycardia, hypotension, loss of sweating below injury; lasts days to weeks, then spasticity develops.

**Autonomic dysreflexia** (a medical emergency in injuries at T6 and above): sudden, massive sympathetic response triggered by a stimulus below the injury level (full bladder or bowel, skin pressure, tight clothing).
- Features: severe hypertension (can cause stroke), pounding headache, bradycardia, profuse sweating above injury level, flushing above, pallor below.
- Management: sit the patient upright (lowers BP); find and remove the trigger (check catheter for kinks/blockage, check for constipation/impaction, loosen tight clothing, check for pressure sores); if BP remains high, give nifedipine sublingual or GTN spray; call physician urgently.

**Nursing priorities in SCI**:
- **Airway**: monitor respiratory function in high SCI; have suction and resuscitation ready.
- **Bladder**: intermittent catheterisation programme (ICP) preferred over indwelling catheter to reduce infection; bladder training.
- **Bowel**: bowel management programme (diet, fluids, stimulant suppositories, digital stimulation).
- **Skin**: pressure area care every 2 hours; use pressure-relieving mattresses; teach patient self-pressure relief (wheelchair lifts every 30 min).
- **DVT**: anticoagulation and compression stockings.
- **Spasticity**: physiotherapy, baclofen, stretching exercises.
- **Rehabilitation**: multidisciplinary — physio, OT, speech, psychology, social work; aim for maximum independence.

## 5.9 Care of the unconscious patient

The unconscious patient cannot protect themselves — nursing care prevents every secondary complication.

| Need | Nursing action |
| --- | --- |
| Airway | Recovery position if no injury; oropharyngeal/nasopharyngeal airway; suction; intubation if GCS ≤ 8 |
| Breathing | Assess RR, SpO₂; supplemental O₂; monitor for apnoea |
| Circulation | IV access, fluids, monitor BP and HR |
| Eyes | Check pupils hourly; protect eyes (taping or eye pads if no blink) to prevent corneal abrasion |
| Mouth | Oral care every 2–4 hours (soft sponge, clean and moist); prevent parotitis |
| Skin | Pressure relief every 2 hours; pressure-relieving mattress; keep skin dry and clean |
| Bladder | In-dwelling or intermittent catheter; strict fluid balance |
| Bowel | Aperients; check for constipation; regular bowel chart |
| Nutrition | NG tube; commence tube feeding within 24 hours; check residuals; keep head elevated |
| Temperature | Manage fever (paracetamol, tepid sponging); hypothermia if indicated (post-cardiac arrest) |
| Position | Head neutral, 30° elevation (unless hypotension); avoid neck flexion or compression |
| Limbs | Passive ROM exercises twice daily; foot drop prevention (footboard or splints) |
| Communication | Explain all procedures to the patient (hearing may be intact even when unconscious) |
| Family | Regular information updates; allow visits; involve in simple care tasks |
