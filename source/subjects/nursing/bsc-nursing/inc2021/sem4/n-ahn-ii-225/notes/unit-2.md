# Unit 2 — Nursing management of disorders of the eye

This unit covers INC Unit II: common eye disorders, their causes, features, tests and management, and the nurse's role in eye donation, banking and transplantation. Learn each disorder as cause → change → signs → tests → treatment → nursing care and health teaching.

## 2.1 Review of anatomy and physiology

The eyeball has three coats. The outer coat is the tough white **sclera** at the back and the clear **cornea** in front (the cornea does most of the bending of light). The middle vascular coat is the **uvea** — the **iris** (coloured ring with the pupil in its centre), the **ciliary body** (makes aqueous fluid and changes the shape of the lens for focusing) and the **choroid**. The inner coat is the **retina**, which turns light into nerve signals carried by the optic nerve.

The clear **lens** sits behind the iris and fine-focuses light onto the retina. In front of the lens, the chamber is filled with watery **aqueous humour**, which is made and drained continuously; behind the lens the large cavity is filled with jelly-like **vitreous humour**. Tears from the lacrimal gland keep the front of the eye moist and drain through the puncta into the nose. Normal eye pressure (intraocular pressure) depends on a balance between aqueous made and aqueous drained.

```text
   EYE (side view, simplified)
   cornea -> aqueous -> iris/pupil -> lens -> vitreous -> retina -> optic nerve
   sclera forms the tough outer wall; choroid supplies blood; conjunctiva covers the front
```

## 2.2 History, physical and diagnostic assessment

Ask about blurred or reduced vision, pain, redness, watering, discharge, double vision, floaters, flashes of light, loss of part of the field of view, difficulty seeing at night, and any injury or chemical splash. Note diabetes and high blood pressure, which damage the retina.

Tests include **visual acuity** with a Snellen chart (distance vision) and near-vision charts; **refraction** to measure the spectacle number; **tonometry** to measure eye pressure; **ophthalmoscopy/fundoscopy** to view the retina and optic disc; **slit-lamp examination** of the front of the eye; perimetry for the field of vision; and colour-vision tests.

## 2.3 Refractive errors

The eye does not focus light exactly on the retina.
- **Myopia (short sight):** distant objects blur; the image falls in front of the retina; corrected with a concave (minus) lens.
- **Hypermetropia (long sight):** near objects blur; the image falls behind the retina; corrected with a convex (plus) lens.
- **Astigmatism:** uneven curve of the cornea blurs vision at all distances; corrected with a cylindrical lens.
- **Presbyopia:** age-related stiffening of the lens so near focus is lost, usually after the forties; corrected with reading glasses.
Refractive errors are also managed with contact lenses or corneal (laser) surgery.

## 2.4 Disorders of the eyelids and conjunctiva

- **Stye (hordeolum):** painful infected swelling at a lash root; warm compresses help it to point and drain.
- **Chalazion:** a painless firm lump from a blocked oil gland; may need a small incision.
- **Deformities:** *entropion* (lid turns in, lashes rub the eye) and *ectropion* (lid turns out, eye waters and dries); corrected by surgery.
- **Conjunctivitis:** inflammation of the conjunctiva — red eye, watering, discharge, gritty feeling. Infective (bacterial or viral) types are very catching, so teach hand washing, a separate towel and not sharing eye make-up; allergic conjunctivitis causes itching. Treatment is with the right medicated drops and strict hygiene.

## 2.5 Disorders of the cornea, lens and pressure

**Keratitis** is inflammation or infection of the cornea causing pain, redness, watering and blurred vision; it is serious because scarring reduces sight. Contact-lens misuse is a common cause, so teach correct lens care.

**Cataract** is clouding of the normally clear lens, usually with age but also from diabetes, injury, long steroid use and from birth. Vision becomes gradually blurred, dim and glary, with faded colours. It is treated by surgery — the cloudy lens is removed and an artificial **intraocular lens** is inserted (commonly by phaco-emulsification). After surgery teach the patient not to rub or press the eye, to avoid bending or lifting heavy weights and straining, to wear the protective shield especially at night, to use the prescribed drops correctly, and to report sudden pain, redness or loss of vision.

**Glaucoma** is a group of diseases in which raised eye pressure (or poor blood flow) damages the optic nerve and slowly destroys the field of vision, starting at the sides. *Open-angle glaucoma* is slow and silent — often no symptoms until sight is already lost, so screening matters. *Acute angle-closure glaucoma* is a sudden emergency with severe eye pain, headache, blurred vision with coloured haloes around lights, a red eye and vomiting. Management lowers the pressure with drops and medicines, laser or surgery (trabeculectomy). Lost vision cannot be regained, so **early detection and lifelong treatment** are the key message.

## 2.6 Retina, blindness and other emergencies

**Retinal detachment** is separation of the retina from its blood supply; the patient reports sudden floaters, flashes of light and a curtain or shadow across the vision. It is an emergency needing urgent surgery (laser, buckling or vitrectomy); rest the patient with the head positioned as ordered and avoid sudden movement.

**Blindness** may be avoidable (cataract, refractive error, glaucoma, infection, vitamin A deficiency, diabetic retinopathy) or unavoidable. Prevention through the national eye-care programme, cataract surgery, vitamin A for children, control of diabetes and blood pressure, and eye-injury safety is central. For a person with low vision or blindness the nurse arranges orientation, mobility aids, large print or Braille, a guide, and keeps the surroundings safe and consistent.

**Chemical eye injury** is a first-aid emergency: flush the eye at once with plenty of clean water or saline for a long time before anything else — *delay increases damage.*

## 2.7 Eye donation, banking and transplantation

**Corneal transplantation (keratoplasty)** replaces a scarred or diseased cornea with a healthy donor cornea, restoring sight. The cornea comes from an **eye bank**, which collects, tests and stores donated eyes.

- **Eye donation** is the gift of eyes after death. Any person can pledge; the family's consent is needed at the time. The eyes are removed within a few hours of death by trained staff, so the family should keep the body cool, close the eyes, raise the head slightly and contact the eye bank quickly.
- **Who can donate:** most people regardless of age or spectacle use. The cornea is not used if the donor had certain infections (such as HIV, hepatitis, rabies) or some other conditions; the eye bank screens for these.
- **Nurse's role:** motivate and give correct information to remove fear and myths, counsel grieving families sensitively, help with consent and paperwork, provide the simple after-death care above, and support eye-donation awareness drives.

## 2.8 Nursing procedures (with rationale)

**Instilling eye drops/ointment.**
1. Check the order, the eye (right/left) and the expiry; wash hands.
2. Position the patient with the head tilted slightly back.
3. Ask the patient to look up; gently pull down the lower lid to make a small pocket.
4. Drop the medicine into the pocket, not onto the cornea — *the cornea is sensitive and the drop may be blinked out.*
5. Ask the patient to close the eye gently and press on the inner corner for a minute — *this stops the drug draining into the nose and being absorbed.*
6. Wait a few minutes between two different drops; ointment is applied last.

**Eye irrigation.** Explain the procedure, position the head turned toward the affected side with a receiver below the ear, and let fluid run from the inner to the outer corner so it does not wash across the healthy eye.

**Applying an eye bandage/pad.** Close the lid first, place a sterile pad, and tape it firmly but comfortably; teach the patient not to peep under the pad.

## Remember

- Cornea and lens focus light on the retina; aqueous humour keeps up the normal eye pressure.
- Myopia → concave lens; hypermetropia → convex lens; astigmatism → cylindrical lens; presbyopia → reading glasses.
- Cataract = cloudy lens, treated by removal plus an intraocular lens; after surgery no rubbing, bending or straining, use the shield.
- Glaucoma damages the optic nerve and the side field; open-angle is silent, angle-closure is a painful emergency; lost sight cannot return, so screen and treat early.
- Retinal detachment = floaters, flashes and a curtain over vision — urgent surgery.
- Chemical eye burn: flush immediately with lots of water before anything else.
- Eye drops go into the lower-lid pocket; press the inner corner afterwards to limit systemic absorption.
- Eye donation is after death, within a few hours; the family keeps the eyes closed, head raised, body cool, and calls the eye bank; the nurse counsels and removes myths.
