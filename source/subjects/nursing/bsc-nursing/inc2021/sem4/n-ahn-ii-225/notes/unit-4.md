# Unit 4: Burns, reconstructive and cosmetic surgery

This unit covers INC Unit IV: classification, assessment and management of burns, principles of reconstructive surgery, and an overview of cosmetic procedures. Burns nursing is a high-acuity specialty; mastering fluid resuscitation, wound care and infection prevention saves lives.

---

## 4.1 Classification of burns

Burns are injuries to the skin and underlying tissues caused by heat, chemicals, electricity, radiation or friction.

### By depth

| Degree | Former name | Depth | Appearance | Sensation | Healing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Superficial (1st degree) | First degree | Epidermis only | Red, dry, no blisters | Painful | 3–5 days, no scar |
| Superficial partial-thickness | Second degree | Epidermis + superficial dermis | Red, wet, blisters | Very painful | 7–14 days, minimal scar |
| Deep partial-thickness | Deep second degree | Epidermis + deep dermis | White/mottled, dry, blisters may be absent | Decreased (dermis damaged) | 14–35 days, scar likely; may need grafting |
| Full-thickness (3rd degree) | Third degree | Full skin thickness | White, brown or black, leathery, dry | Painless (nerve endings destroyed) | Cannot self-heal; requires grafting |
| Fourth degree | — | Skin + underlying fat, muscle, bone | Charred, black | Painless | Requires debridement ± amputation |

### By cause

- **Thermal**: flame, hot liquids (scalds), steam, hot solids (contact burns).
- **Chemical**: acids, alkalis, phosphorus. Alkali burns are deeper (liquefactive necrosis) and more serious than acid burns.
- **Electrical**: entry and exit wounds; internal damage along current path (muscle necrosis, arrhythmia, renal failure from myoglobinuria). Current follows nerves and blood vessels, so visible skin injury underestimates the total.
- **Radiation**: sunburn (ultraviolet), X-ray or nuclear exposure.

## 4.2 Assessment of burn size

### Rule of Nines (adults)

```text
           Head and neck      9%
   Right arm      9%   Left arm      9%
   Anterior trunk  18%
   Posterior trunk 18%
   Right leg      18%   Left leg      18%
   Perineum        1%
                        TOTAL = 100%
```

### Lund and Browder chart

More accurate than the rule of nines; adjusts for age because children have proportionally larger heads and smaller legs. Used for children and large burns.

### Palmar surface method

The patient's palm (including fingers) ≈ 1% TBSA; useful for scattered burns.

**Note**: Superficial (first-degree) burns such as ordinary sunburn are **not included** in the TBSA calculation used for fluid resuscitation.

### Inhalation injury — recognition is critical

Suspect inhalation injury when:
- Burn occurred in an enclosed space.
- Singed nasal hairs or eyebrows.
- Soot in nostrils, mouth, or sputum.
- Hoarse voice, stridor (upper airway oedema — can close within hours).
- Burns to face, neck, or oral cavity.

**Action**: 100% oxygen via non-rebreather mask at once; early intubation before the airway swells shut; call anaesthetics/ICU urgently.

## 4.3 Burn shock and fluid resuscitation

### Pathophysiology of burn shock

Large burns cause massive release of inflammatory mediators (histamine, prostaglandins, cytokines) → vasodilation and dramatically increased capillary permeability. Plasma leaks from capillaries into the interstitium ("third-spacing"), causing:
- Hypovolaemia and reduced cardiac output (shock).
- Local oedema at the wound and distant oedema.
- Haemoconcentration (rising haematocrit as plasma is lost).

```text
   Burn injury
      ↓
   Inflammatory mediators released
      ↓
   Capillary leak (massive fluid shift to interstitium)
      ↓
   Hypovolaemia + oedema
      ↓
   Decreased organ perfusion (kidneys, gut, heart)
      ↓
   Multi-organ failure if not corrected
```

This capillary leak is greatest in the first 24 hours and largely stops by 48 hours.

### Parkland formula for fluid resuscitation

**4 mL × body weight (kg) × % TBSA burned = total fluid in first 24 hours**

Use Hartmann's solution (Ringer's lactate) in the first 24 hours.

- Give **half** in the **first 8 hours** from the **time of injury** (not from arrival).
- Give the remaining **half** in the **next 16 hours**.

**Example**: 70 kg adult with 30% TBSA burns.
4 × 70 × 30 = 8,400 mL total.
Give 4,200 mL in 8 hours, then 4,200 mL in 16 hours.

**Target urine output**: 0.5 mL/kg/h in adults (30–50 mL/h), 1 mL/kg/h in children — this is the best guide to adequate resuscitation. Adjust fluid rate according to urine output.

After 24 hours, colloids (albumin, FFP) may be added, and oral intake starts as tolerated.

## 4.4 Burn wound care

### First aid

- **Stop the burning**: remove from source, remove clothing (not stuck clothing), remove jewellery and watches before oedema makes this impossible.
- **Cool the burn**: cool running water (15–20°C) for 20 minutes — started within 3 hours of burn; do not use ice (causes frostbite and worsens injury).
- **Cover**: with a clean (ideally sterile) low-adherence dressing or cling film; do not burst blisters.

### Wound management

**Wound debridement**: removal of dead tissue (eschar), either:
- **Conservative**: daily cleansing and dressings; eschar separates gradually.
- **Surgical**: tangential excision under general anaesthesia — faster, reduces infection risk.

**Dressings**:
- Silver sulfadiazine (Flamazine) 1% cream — broad antibacterial cover; apply daily; can cause leucopenia with extended use.
- Silver-containing dressings (Mepitel Ag, Aquacel Ag) — fewer dressing changes (every 2–7 days), reduce pain.
- Biological dressings (allograft from cadaver, xenograft from pig, amniotic membrane) — temporary coverage until autograft is available.
- Synthetic substitutes (Biobrane, Integra) — for large burns without enough donor skin.

**Skin grafting**:
- **Split-thickness skin graft (STSG)**: the most common; epidermis + part of dermis harvested from a donor site (thigh, back, buttock) using a dermatome; can be meshed to cover a larger area; donor site heals in 10–14 days with epithelial cells from hair follicles.
- **Full-thickness skin graft (FTSG)**: epidermis + full dermis; better cosmetic outcome; donor site must be sutured closed; used for small areas (face, hands).

**Donor site care**: the donor site (where skin was taken) is a painful partial-thickness wound; cover with a simple dressing (paraffin gauze or Mepitel); keep clean and dry; heals in 10–14 days.

## 4.5 Infection prevention

Burns destroy the skin barrier — infection (wound sepsis, pneumonia, septicaemia) is the leading cause of death in burn patients after the first 48 hours.

- **Wound colonisation is inevitable; invasive infection is what must be prevented.**
- Wound cultures twice weekly to track colonising organisms.
- Topical antimicrobials (silver sulfadiazine, mafenide acetate) reduce bacterial load.
- Systemic antibiotics only for clinical infection (fever + wound changes + positive blood/wound cultures) — not prophylactically.
- Strict infection control: handwashing, gloves, aprons; isolated burn unit if available; limit visitors.
- Early wound closure (grafting) is the most effective way to prevent infection.
- Monitor: daily wound inspection for colour change (greenish = *Pseudomonas*, black = *Aspergillus*), odour, cellulitis in surrounding skin, temperature trend.

## 4.6 Nutritional support

Burns dramatically increase metabolic rate (by 1.5–2.5× basal metabolic rate in major burns). Insufficient nutrition → impaired healing, immunosuppression, muscle wasting.

- Nasogastric or nasojejunal tube feeding should start early (within 6 hours of injury) if oral intake is not adequate.
- High-calorie, high-protein diet: aim for 30–35 kcal/kg/day and 1.5–2 g protein/kg/day for major burns.
- Glutamine supplementation reduces infections and hospital stay.
- Monitor weight, prealbumin/albumin, wound healing — these reflect nutritional adequacy.

## 4.7 Pain management

Burns are extremely painful, especially dressing changes.

- Background pain: regular oral/IV analgesia — paracetamol, NSAIDs, oral morphine.
- Procedural (dressing change) pain: IV morphine or ketamine (small dose IV/IM) 20–30 minutes before the procedure; Entonox (nitrous oxide) inhalation during the procedure.
- Psychological support: explain each procedure; allow some patient control over pace of dressing change; music, distraction.
- Itching (pruritus) of healing wounds: antihistamines (chlorpheniramine, cetirizine), moisturisers, cool compresses.

## 4.8 Respiratory care

- Major facial/inhalation burns: endotracheal tube or tracheostomy (early, before oedema closes airway).
- Humidified oxygen to prevent drying of airways.
- Suction every 1–2 hours for secretions (sooty, thick initially).
- Circumferential chest burns → escharotomy (surgical release of eschar) if breathing is restricted.
- Nebulised N-acetylcysteine, heparin and salbutamol to manage chemical airway injury.

## 4.9 Contracture prevention and rehabilitation

Healed burn wounds and grafted skin contract as they mature, forming tight bands (**contractures**) that restrict movement, especially across joints.

- **Positioning**: anti-contracture positions from day one (e.g. neck extended, shoulders abducted, hands in position of safe immobilisation — wrist in slight extension, MCPs flexed, IPs extended, thumb abducted).
- **Splinting**: custom thermoplastic splints made by physiotherapists and occupational therapists; worn at night and when not exercising.
- **Physiotherapy**: range-of-motion exercises twice daily; encourage early ambulation.
- **Pressure garments** (Jobst/Tubigrip): worn 23 hours/day for 12–24 months over healed areas — reduce scar thickness and prevent contracture; must be fitted correctly and replaced when stretched.
- **Surgical release**: Z-plasty, skin grafting, or flap reconstruction for established contractures.

## 4.10 Psychological rehabilitation

Burns cause significant psychological trauma: disfigurement, long hospitalisation, repeated painful procedures, fear, grief, and body image change.

- Encourage the patient to express feelings about their appearance.
- Involve family and significant others from the start.
- Refer to psychologist or psychiatrist early (anxiety, depression, PTSD are common).
- Connect with peer support (burn survivors).
- Realistic counselling about scar progression (scars continue to mature for 12–24 months; laser and reconstructive options are available later).
- Occupational rehabilitation: plan return to work/school; provide vocational guidance if occupation needs to change.

## 4.11 Reconstructive surgery

**Reconstructive surgery** restores form and function after trauma, cancer, burns, or congenital defects. The goal is functional recovery first, then cosmesis.

| Technique | Indication | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary wound closure | Small clean wounds | Suturing edges directly |
| Skin graft (STSG/FTSG) | Burns, ulcers, skin loss | As described above |
| Local flap (Z-plasty, rotation, transposition) | Releasing contracture, closing near the wound | Surrounding tissue rearranged |
| Regional flap | Cover exposed structures | Pedicled flap from nearby area (e.g. gastrocnemius for tibia) |
| Free flap (microvascular) | Major reconstruction | Tissue with its own blood vessels transplanted, anastomosed under microscope |
| Tissue expansion | Scalp, breast, face | Silicone balloon inserted under skin, inflated over weeks; stretched skin used for closure |

**Timing**: acute burns need early excision and grafting; reconstructive procedures for established scars/contractures are done after the wound is stable and mature (6–12 months from injury).

## 4.12 Cosmetic (aesthetic) surgery

**Cosmetic surgery** aims to improve appearance. The nurse's role includes pre-operative counselling, ensuring informed consent, and post-operative care.

**Common procedures and nursing care**:

| Procedure | Purpose | Key nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liposuction | Remove localised fat deposits | Compression garment 6 weeks; monitor fluid shifts, haematoma, contour irregularity |
| Rhinoplasty (nose job) | Reshape the nose | Ice packs, head elevation (30°), nasal splint, avoid blowing nose, monitor for bleeding |
| Rhytidectomy (face lift) | Reduce facial wrinkles | Drain management, pressure dressings, avoid sun |
| Blepharoplasty | Remove excess eyelid skin | Eye care, cold compresses, follow up for vision changes |
| Breast augmentation | Increase breast size with implants | Drain care, support bra, wound monitoring, teach self-examination |
| Breast reduction | Reduce heavy breasts | Drain care, wound healing, bra support |
| Abdominoplasty (tummy tuck) | Remove excess abdominal skin | Compression garment, DVT prophylaxis, wound care |

**Pre-operative counselling points**: realistic expectations; photographs for comparison; discuss scars (all surgery scars); smoking cessation (impairs healing); stop NSAIDs/aspirin 2 weeks pre-op; psychological readiness; consent covers risks (haematoma, infection, asymmetry, scarring, anaesthesia risks).
