# Unit 3: Kidney, urinary and male reproductive disorders

This unit covers INC Unit III: anatomy and physiology of the urinary system, assessment, common disorders of the kidney and urinary tract, and selected male reproductive disorders. Learn each condition as cause → pathophysiology → features → investigations → management → nursing care.

---

## 3.1 Review of anatomy and physiology

The two kidneys lie retroperitoneally on the posterior abdominal wall. Each kidney contains about one million **nephrons**, the functional units. Each nephron consists of a glomerulus (a capillary tuft enclosed in Bowman's capsule) and a tubule (proximal convoluted, loop of Henle, distal convoluted, collecting duct).

**Functions of the kidney**

| Function | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Filtration | 180 L of filtrate/day; GFR normally 90–120 mL/min |
| Reabsorption | Most water, glucose, Na+, Cl−, HCO3−, amino acids |
| Secretion | H+, K+, drugs, creatinine (tubular secretion) |
| Excretion | Urea, creatinine, uric acid, drugs, toxins |
| Hormone production | Erythropoietin (stimulates RBC production), renin (blood pressure), active vitamin D (calcium) |
| Acid–base balance | Excretes H+ and retains HCO3− |

Urine passes from the collecting duct → renal pelvis → ureter → urinary bladder → urethra. The bladder stores urine; the internal urethral sphincter is involuntary (smooth muscle) and the external sphincter is voluntary (striated muscle). Micturition (voiding) requires a coordinated reflex: bladder stretch → detrusor contraction → sphincter relaxation.

```text
   Cortex (glomeruli, proximal and distal tubules)
      ↓
   Medulla (loops of Henle, collecting ducts)
      ↓
   Renal pelvis → Ureter → Bladder → Urethra
```

## 3.2 Nursing assessment of the urinary system

**History**: frequency, urgency, dysuria, nocturia, haematuria, loin pain, incontinence, hesitancy, poor stream, dribbling, reduced urine output, swelling, foamy urine, past UTIs, kidney stones, diabetes, hypertension, drug history (nephrotoxic drugs — aminoglycosides, NSAIDs, contrast agents).

**Physical examination**: blood pressure (hypertension is common with renal disease), weight and oedema (pitting around ankles and sacrum), flank/costovertebral angle tenderness (pyelonephritis, calculi), palpate for kidney enlargement (polycystic), bladder distension (retention), external genitalia.

**Urine examination**

| Test | Normal/Abnormal |
| --- | --- |
| Colour | Pale yellow; red (blood), dark amber (concentrated/bilirubin), cloudy (pus/phosphates) |
| Specific gravity | 1.001–1.030; fixed 1.010 in CKD |
| pH | 4.5–8.0; alkaline in infection/vegetarian diet |
| Protein | Negative; ++ to ++++ in nephrotic syndrome, GN, infection |
| Glucose | Negative; positive in diabetes or Fanconi syndrome |
| Ketones | Negative; positive in DKA, starvation |
| Nitrites/leucocytes | Positive = UTI (dipstick screening) |
| RBCs | 0–2/HPF; haematuria in infection, calculi, tumour, GN |
| Casts | Hyaline casts normal; RBC casts = glomerulonephritis; WBC casts = pyelonephritis |

**Blood tests**: urea (normally 7–20 mg/dL), serum creatinine (normally 0.6–1.2 mg/dL), eGFR (estimated GFR — calculated from creatinine, age, sex, race), electrolytes (Na+, K+, Cl−, HCO3−), albumin (low in nephrotic), full blood count (anaemia in CKD), uric acid.

**Imaging**: ultrasound (first-line, size/obstruction/stones), plain X-ray KUB (radiopaque calculi), intravenous urography (IVU/IVP), CT urogram (calculi/tumour), cystoscopy (direct bladder/urethra view), renal biopsy (definitive for GN).

## 3.3 Urinary tract infections (UTI)

**Urinary tract infections** are caused by ascending bacteria, most commonly *Escherichia coli* (80%), also *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*. They are more common in women (short urethra, proximity to anus) and in patients with catheters.

### Lower UTI: cystitis

- Features: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, cloudy or malodorous urine; no fever unless upper tract is involved.
- Investigations: urine dipstick, urine culture and sensitivity (MSU/mid-stream urine).
- Treatment: oral antibiotics — nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim, or cotrimoxazole for 3–7 days; increase fluid intake.
- Nursing: encourage fluid intake (2–3 L/day), complete the antibiotic course, perineal hygiene (wipe front to back), void after intercourse, avoid prolonged catheterisation.

### Upper UTI: pyelonephritis

- Features: high fever with rigors, loin pain and tenderness, nausea, vomiting, plus lower urinary symptoms.
- May be associated with urinary obstruction (calculus, BPH) or vesicoureteric reflux.
- Treatment: IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone, gentamicin) if systemically unwell, then oral; treat the underlying obstruction if present.
- Nursing: temperature monitoring, adequate hydration (IV then oral), analgesia, antipyretics, blood and urine cultures before antibiotics.

### Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI)

CAUTI is the most common hospital-acquired infection. Prevention is paramount:
- Insert catheters only when essential (surgical, retention, accurate output monitoring in critically ill).
- Use aseptic technique during insertion.
- Maintain a closed drainage system; keep the bag below the level of the bladder.
- Secure the catheter to the thigh to prevent movement.
- Perform catheter care with soap and water twice daily.
- Review the need daily and remove as soon as possible.
- Do not routinely change catheters or take cultures without clinical indication.

## 3.4 Urinary incontinence

**Urinary incontinence** (UI) is involuntary loss of urine. It is common in older adults and women but should not be accepted as normal.

| Type | Mechanism | Features | Management |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stress | Weak pelvic floor/sphincter | Leaks with cough, sneeze, exercise | Pelvic floor (Kegel) exercises, bladder training, surgery (TVT) |
| Urge | Detrusor overactivity (OAB) | Sudden urge, cannot reach toilet in time | Bladder training, antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, tolterodine) |
| Overflow | Incomplete emptying | Dribbling, distended bladder | Treat obstruction, catheterisation, alpha-blockers (BPH) |
| Functional | Cognitive/physical disability | Cannot reach toilet | Toileting schedule, assistive devices, environmental changes |
| Mixed | Combination of stress + urge | Mixed symptoms | Combined management |

**Nursing management of incontinence**
- Bladder diary (frequency, volume, leaks, triggers) for 3 days.
- Bladder training: gradually increase intervals between voiding.
- Prompted/scheduled voiding for cognitively impaired.
- Perineal skin care: keep dry, barrier cream to prevent dermatitis/pressure sores.
- Pelvic floor exercises: contract, hold 5–10 seconds, release, 10–15 repetitions 3×/day.
- Avoid bladder irritants: caffeine, fizzy drinks, excessive fluid at night.
- Pads/incontinence products as a last resort (not a first choice).

## 3.5 Urinary retention

**Urinary retention** is inability to void despite a full bladder. Acute retention is painful and requires urgent catheterisation. Chronic retention is painless overflow.

**Causes**: BPH (most common in older men), urethral stricture, drugs (anticholinergics, tricyclic antidepressants, opioids), spinal cord injury, post-operative (spinal anaesthesia, perineal surgery), prostate cancer.

**Management**:
- Urethral catheterisation (usually 14–16 Fr Foley catheter) under aseptic technique; drain bladder slowly in acute retention to avoid haematuria from decompression.
- Suprapubic catheter if urethral route is not possible.
- Treat the underlying cause (alpha-blockers for BPH — tamsulosin; surgery for obstruction).
- Monitor urine output after catheter removal (trial without catheter, TWOC).

## 3.6 Renal calculi (kidney stones)

**Renal calculi** are stones in the urinary tract formed from crystallised minerals.

| Type | Composition | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcium oxalate | Most common (~75%) | Radiopaque on X-ray, hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria |
| Uric acid | ~10% | Radiolucent, seen in gout, dehydration |
| Struvite (triple phosphate) | ~15% | Staghorn calculi, associated with urease-producing bacteria (*Proteus*) |
| Cystine | Rare | Hereditary cystinuria |

**Features**: sudden-onset, severe, colicky loin-to-groin pain (ureteric colic), nausea, vomiting, haematuria (frank or microscopic), restlessness; the patient cannot find a comfortable position.

**Investigations**: urine dipstick (blood), ultrasound (obstruction/hydronephrosis), CT urogram (most sensitive — detects all types), stone analysis after passage.

**Management**:
- Analgesia: diclofenac (NSAID) or IV pethidine/morphine; parenteral route until pain controlled.
- IV or oral hydration (2–3 L/day) to flush stone.
- Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) aid stone expulsion.
- Stones <5 mm usually pass spontaneously; >10 mm often need intervention.
- Intervention: extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) for large stones.

**Nursing care**:
- Adequate analgesia and anti-emetics; reassure patient.
- Strain all urine (use a filter or gauge) to catch passed stones for analysis.
- Encourage fluids unless contraindicated.
- Dietary advice: reduce oxalate-rich foods (spinach, tea, nuts) for calcium oxalate stones; reduce red meat and purine-rich foods for uric acid stones; reduce dietary salt and animal protein.
- Teach warning signs of infection (fever, rigors) → immediate medical attention.

## 3.7 Glomerulonephritis and nephritic syndrome

**Glomerulonephritis (GN)** is inflammation of the glomeruli. **Nephritic syndrome** is the clinical presentation of an acute aggressive GN: haematuria, oliguria, hypertension, mild proteinuria, oedema.

**Post-streptococcal GN (PSGN)** is the classic example — occurs 1–4 weeks after throat or skin infection with group A beta-haemolytic *Streptococcus*. It is more common in children.

| Feature | Nephritic syndrome | Nephrotic syndrome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main problem | Inflammation of glomerulus | Increased permeability to protein |
| Proteinuria | Mild (< 3.5 g/day) | Massive (> 3.5 g/day) |
| Haematuria | Prominent, RBC casts | Absent or mild |
| Oedema | Mild periorbital | Severe (generalised anasarca) |
| Hypertension | Present | Variable |
| Plasma albumin | Near normal | Very low |
| Main cause | PSGN, IgA nephropathy, lupus | Minimal change disease (children), membranous nephropathy (adults), diabetes, amyloid |

**Management of PSGN**:
- Rest; fluid restriction matching output + insensible losses.
- Sodium restriction (low-salt diet) for hypertension and oedema.
- Antihypertensives (nifedipine, hydralazine acutely; ACE inhibitors long-term).
- Penicillin to eradicate streptococcal infection.
- Monitor blood pressure, urine output, weight, electrolytes.
- Most patients recover fully; dialysis if severe uraemia or oliguria persists.

## 3.8 Nephrotic syndrome

**Nephrotic syndrome** = proteinuria > 3.5 g/day + hypoalbuminaemia + oedema + hyperlipidaemia + lipiduria.

- In children, **minimal change disease** is the commonest cause; responds well to corticosteroids.
- In adults, **membranous nephropathy**, **focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)**, diabetic nephropathy, amyloidosis.

**Management**:
- Corticosteroids (prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day for minimal change disease).
- Diuretics for oedema (furosemide, spironolactone) — cautiously to avoid volume depletion.
- ACE inhibitors reduce proteinuria.
- Statins for hyperlipidaemia.
- Low-sodium diet; protein intake should be adequate (not excessively restricted).
- Prophylactic anticoagulation considered (risk of deep vein thrombosis and renal vein thrombosis).

**Nursing care**: daily weight, fluid balance, blood pressure 4-hourly, urine protein (dipstick daily), skin care for severe oedema (position changes, avoid pressure), infection prevention (immunocompromised on steroids — isolation, oral care, hand hygiene), steroid education (take with food, do not stop suddenly, weight gain, mood changes).

## 3.9 Acute kidney injury (AKI)

**AKI** (formerly acute renal failure) is a sudden decrease in kidney function over hours to days, causing accumulation of nitrogenous waste and fluid/electrolyte disturbances.

| Classification | Cause | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prerenal | Reduced renal perfusion | Hypovolaemia (haemorrhage, burns, diarrhoea), heart failure, septic shock, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors |
| Intrarenal (Intrinsic) | Direct kidney damage | Acute tubular necrosis (ATN — ischaemia, nephrotoxins), GN, vasculitis |
| Postrenal | Obstruction to outflow | Calculi, BPH, tumour, bilateral ureteric obstruction |

**Features**: oliguria or anuria (< 400 mL/day or < 0.5 mL/kg/h), rising creatinine and urea, hyperkalaemia (most dangerous — arrhythmias), metabolic acidosis, fluid overload (oedema, hypertension), uraemic symptoms (nausea, confusion, pericarditis in severe).

**Management**:
- Treat the cause: fluids for prerenal; relieve obstruction for postrenal.
- Fluid challenge (500 mL 0.9% NaCl over 30–60 min) if hypovolaemic.
- Hyperkalaemia: ECG monitoring; IV calcium gluconate (cardioprotective), insulin-dextrose (shifts K+ intracellularly), salbutamol nebuliser, consider dialysis if >6.5 mEq/L or ECG changes.
- Restrict potassium and phosphate in diet.
- Avoid nephrotoxic drugs (aminoglycosides, NSAIDs, contrast agents) or dose-adjust for GFR.
- Renal replacement therapy (haemodialysis, haemofiltration) indications: refractory hyperkalaemia, volume overload, severe acidosis, uraemic symptoms.

**Nursing care**:
- Strict hourly urine output (catheterise if not voiding).
- Daily weight; fluid balance chart.
- Restrict fluid intake to previous hour's output + 500 mL/day (insensible) if oliguric.
- Cardiac monitoring for hyperkalaemia.
- Avoid nephrotoxic drugs, contrast, and NSAIDs.
- Nutrition: low potassium and phosphate, adequate calories (glucose and fat), limit protein in severe uraemia.

## 3.10 Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

**CKD** is a progressive, irreversible loss of kidney function over > 3 months.

| Stage | GFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ≥ 90 | Normal or increased GFR, kidney damage markers present |
| 2 | 60–89 | Mildly decreased |
| 3a / 3b | 45–59 / 30–44 | Moderately decreased |
| 4 | 15–29 | Severely decreased |
| 5 | < 15 | Kidney failure (uraemia, need replacement therapy) |

**Common causes**: diabetes mellitus (commonest worldwide — diabetic nephropathy), hypertension, chronic glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, obstructive uropathy.

**Complications**: hypertension, anaemia (low erythropoietin — treat with erythropoietin-stimulating agents + iron), renal osteodystrophy (low active vitamin D → secondary hyperparathyroidism → bone disease), fluid/electrolyte disturbances (hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, hyponatraemia), cardiovascular disease (leading cause of death in CKD), uraemic symptoms (nausea, pruritus, fatigue, restless legs, peripheral neuropathy, pericarditis).

**Renal replacement therapy (RRT)**:

| Type | Principle | Access | Nursing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Haemodialysis (HD) | Blood passes through extracorporeal dialyser; toxins and fluid removed by diffusion/ultrafiltration | Arteriovenous fistula (preferred), graft, or tunnelled line | Feel the fistula thrill and brachial pulse; never BP, blood draw or IV in the fistula arm; cannulate the fistula with aseptic technique |
| Peritoneal dialysis (PD) | Dialysate infused into peritoneal cavity; peritoneum acts as membrane | Tenckhoff catheter (permanent) | Sterile technique for bag exchanges; watch for peritonitis (cloudy effluent, abdominal pain, fever) → send effluent for C&S |
| Kidney transplant | Best long-term option | Donor kidney placed in iliac fossa | Immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, mycophenolate, prednisolone) — lifelong; watch for rejection, infection (immunocompromised) |

**Renal diet in CKD**: restrict potassium (avoid bananas, oranges, tomatoes, potatoes, nuts), phosphate (reduce dairy, meat, colas), sodium (no added salt, avoid processed foods), fluid (500 mL + previous day's urine output), and ensure adequate calorie intake to prevent malnutrition.

**Nursing care in CKD/HD**: monitor interdialytic weight gain (< 2 kg between sessions), blood pressure, fistula care (no constriction — no BP, no blood draw, no IV drip in that arm), dialysis access care, skin care for pruritus (moisturisers, antihistamines), encourage adherence to medications and diet, address psychological impact of chronic illness.

## 3.11 Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)

**BPH** is a non-cancerous enlargement of the prostate gland common in men over 50. The enlarged prostate compresses the urethra, causing lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS).

**Features**: urinary frequency, nocturia, urgency, hesitancy (waiting for the stream to start), weak/interrupted stream, feeling of incomplete emptying, post-void dribbling, eventually urinary retention or overflow incontinence.

**Investigations**: digital rectal examination (smooth, rubbery, enlarged prostate), urine flow rate and residual volume ultrasound, PSA (to exclude cancer — usually < 10 ng/mL in BPH, often > 10 in cancer), cystoscopy.

**Management**:
- Watchful waiting for mild symptoms.
- Medical: alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin) relax smooth muscle quickly; 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride) shrink the prostate over months — often used together.
- Surgical: TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate) — most common; laser prostatectomy.

**Nursing care post-TURP**:
- Three-way catheter with continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) using 0.9% NaCl to clear haematuria.
- Monitor output of irrigation fluid — input must equal or exceed output (stop if output less than input, may indicate clot obstruction → irrigate manually as ordered).
- Colour of urine: initially red → pink → clear (usually 24–48 h).
- Pain management; encourage leg exercises (DVT risk).
- Remove catheter when urine is clear (usually day 2–3); monitor first void after removal.
- Teach: some bleeding and clots may pass for 2–3 weeks post discharge; avoid constipation, heavy lifting and sexual intercourse for 4–6 weeks.
- Complication: retrograde ejaculation (semen goes into bladder not externally) — reassure, it is not dangerous but affects fertility.

## 3.12 Prostate cancer

**Prostate cancer** is the most common cancer in elderly men. Most are slow-growing adenocarcinomas arising in the peripheral zone. It tends to metastasise to bone (osteoblastic metastases).

**Features**: often asymptomatic early; LUTS, haematuria, bone pain (back, pelvis, hips), weight loss in advanced disease.

**Investigations**: PSA (elevated — usually > 10 ng/mL, may be higher in cancer but also raised in BPH and prostatitis; trending matters more than a single value), digital rectal examination (hard, irregular, asymmetric prostate), transrectal ultrasound-guided biopsy (definitive), bone scan (for metastases), MRI prostate (staging).

**Management**:
- Active surveillance: for low-risk, well-differentiated disease; PSA and biopsy repeated.
- Radical prostatectomy (open or robotic-assisted laparoscopic).
- Radiotherapy (external beam, or brachytherapy — seeds implanted).
- Hormone therapy (androgen deprivation) — LHRH agonists (goserelin, leuprolide) or orchidectomy — for metastatic disease.
- Chemotherapy (docetaxel) for castration-resistant prostate cancer.

**Nursing care**: pre- and post-operative care for prostatectomy (including catheter care and pelvic floor exercises for continence recovery), manage complications of hormone therapy (hot flushes, osteoporosis, fatigue, mood changes), bone pain management, psychological support, PSA follow-up education.
