# Unit 1: Special pathology — kidneys and urinary tract

This unit covers INC Unit I of Pathology II: diseases of the kidneys and urinary tract. Learn each disease as cause → mechanism → morphological changes → clinical and laboratory features → nursing relevance.

---

## 1.1 Review of anatomy and physiology

Each kidney contains about one million **nephrons** — the filtering units. Each nephron has a glomerulus (a tuft of capillaries enclosed in Bowman's capsule) where filtration happens, then a tubule that modifies the filtrate (reabsorption and secretion) to produce urine.

```text
Kidney structure (simplified)
Renal artery → afferent arteriole → glomerulus (filtration)
→ Bowman's capsule → proximal tubule → Loop of Henle
→ distal tubule → collecting duct → renal pelvis → ureter → bladder
```

| Normal kidney function | Reference value |
| --- | --- |
| Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) | ~125 mL/min (60–120 mL/min is accepted) |
| Serum creatinine | 0.6–1.2 mg/dL (men), 0.5–1.0 mg/dL (women) |
| Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) | 7–20 mg/dL |
| Urine output | ~1–2 L/day (minimum 400 mL/day) |

The urinary tract below the kidneys: two **ureters** carry urine to the **urinary bladder**, which stores urine until voiding through the **urethra**.

## 1.2 Glomerular diseases

### Acute glomerulonephritis (post-streptococcal)

- **Cause:** Group A beta-haemolytic Streptococcus infection (throat or skin) 1–3 weeks before. Immune complexes (antigen-antibody) deposit in the glomerular basement membrane and activate complement.
- **Mechanism:** Immune-complex deposition → complement activation → neutrophil and monocyte infiltration → hypercellularity of glomeruli → reduced filtration area.
- **Morphology:** Diffuse hypercellularity of glomeruli on biopsy; granular ("lumpy-bumpy") deposits of IgG and C3 on immunofluorescence.
- **Features (nephritic syndrome):**
  - Haematuria — dark, smoky, tea-coloured or frank red urine (blood in urine)
  - Proteinuria — mild (not massive)
  - Oedema — periorbital (puffy face, especially in the morning) and ankle oedema
  - Oliguria — reduced urine output
  - Hypertension — from salt and water retention
  - Raised anti-streptolysin O (ASO) titre — confirms recent streptococcal infection
- **Outcome:** Most children recover fully; some adults develop chronic GN.
- **Nursing:** Bed rest in acute phase; strict input-output chart; daily weight to track fluid; low-salt diet; antihypertensives as ordered; antibiotics to eradicate residual streptococcal infection; urine dipstick to track haematuria.

### Nephrotic syndrome

Defined as: massive proteinuria (>3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia and lipiduria. The common causes are:

| Cause | Age group | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimal change disease | Children (most common in children) | Podocyte foot process effacement only on electron microscopy; excellent steroid response |
| Membranous nephropathy | Adults | Subepithelial deposits; idiopathic or secondary to diabetes, SLE, hepatitis B, drugs |
| Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis | Any age | Secondary to hypertension, obesity, HIV; poor prognosis |
| Diabetic nephropathy | Adults | Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules on biopsy; most common cause of end-stage renal disease |

- **Mechanism:** Loss of glomerular basement membrane negative charge or structural damage → massive leakage of albumin → hypoalbuminaemia → low oncotic pressure → water and salt leak into tissues (oedema).
- **Features:**
  - Oedema — generalised (face, legs, abdomen), pitting, worse in the morning
  - Frothy urine (proteinuria)
  - Ascites and pleural effusion in severe cases
  - Hyperlipidaemia — the liver makes more lipoprotein to compensate for lost albumin
  - Lipiduria — fat globules and oval fat bodies in urine
  - Increased risk of infection (immunoglobulins lost in urine) and thrombosis (clotting factors lost)

**Comparison: nephritic vs nephrotic syndrome**

| Feature | Nephritic syndrome | Nephrotic syndrome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proteinuria | Mild | Massive (>3.5 g/day) |
| Haematuria | Prominent | Absent or mild |
| Oedema | Moderate | Severe, generalised |
| Hypertension | Yes | May be present |
| Serum albumin | Near normal | Markedly low |
| Serum lipids | Normal | High (hyperlipidaemia) |
| Typical cause | Post-streptococcal GN, IgA nephropathy | Minimal change, membranous GN, diabetic nephropathy |

- **Nursing:** Keep accurate fluid and weight records; skin care for pitting oedema (elevate legs, prevent pressure sores); low-sodium diet; treat infection early; explain steroid therapy (do not stop suddenly, monitor blood sugar and BP).

### IgA nephropathy (Berger's disease)

- Most common glomerulonephritis worldwide.
- IgA deposits in the mesangium.
- Presents with **recurrent episodes of frank haematuria** after upper respiratory tract infections or exercise.
- Typically benign in children; some adults progress slowly to renal failure.

## 1.3 Tubular diseases

### Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)

- Most common cause of **acute kidney injury** (AKI).
- Two types:
  - **Ischaemic ATN:** from prolonged hypotension (shock, major surgery, haemorrhage); inner medulla (thick ascending limb and straight segment of proximal tubule) is most vulnerable.
  - **Toxic ATN:** from nephrotoxins — aminoglycoside antibiotics (gentamicin), contrast dye, myoglobin (from rhabdomyolysis), haemoglobin (from haemolysis), heavy metals, organic solvents.
- **Morphology:** Tubular epithelial cell necrosis with flattening and loss of brush border; tubular casts in distal tubules.
- **Features:** Sudden rise in creatinine and BUN; oliguria or anuria; hyperkalaemia; metabolic acidosis; volume overload.
- **Recovery:** Most cases are reversible with time; during the recovery phase urine output increases dramatically (polyuria phase) — watch for hypokalaemia.
- **Nursing:** Strict fluid balance; daily weight; nephrotoxic drugs avoided; dietary protein restriction; dialysis if severe.

### Fanconi syndrome

- Generalised proximal tubule dysfunction → inability to reabsorb glucose, amino acids, phosphate, bicarbonate, uric acid.
- Causes: Wilson's disease, cystinosis, multiple myeloma, heavy metal poisoning, drugs (tenofovir, valproate).
- Features: glycosuria with normal blood sugar, aminoaciduria, phosphaturia, rickets/osteomalacia.

## 1.4 Renal vascular disease

- **Hypertensive nephrosclerosis:** Long-standing hypertension → thickening of afferent arteriole walls (hyaline arteriolosclerosis) → reduction of glomerular blood supply → glomerulosclerosis and tubular atrophy. Kidneys become small and granular ("granular contracted kidney").
- **Renal artery stenosis:** Usually from atherosclerosis or fibromuscular dysplasia → reduced renal perfusion → renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation → secondary hypertension (renovascular hypertension). Treat with angioplasty or surgery.

---

## 1.5 Interstitial nephritis

- Inflammation of the renal interstitium (not primarily glomeruli or tubules).
- **Acute interstitial nephritis:** Most often a drug hypersensitivity reaction (NSAIDs, penicillin, sulphonamides, diuretics) — fever, skin rash, eosinophilia and rising creatinine within weeks of starting the drug. Withdraw the offending drug; steroids hasten recovery.
- **Chronic interstitial nephritis:** From analgesic nephropathy (long-term NSAID use), reflux nephropathy (vesico-ureteric reflux with recurrent pyelonephritis), lead or cadmium poisoning, or myeloma. Leads slowly to chronic kidney disease.

## 1.6 Renal failure

### Acute kidney injury (AKI)

- Rapid rise in blood creatinine and urea over hours to days.
- Causes classified as: pre-renal (hypovolaemia, heart failure — most common, 60%), intrinsic renal (ATN, GN, interstitial nephritis), post-renal (obstruction — stones, BPH, tumour).
- Features: oliguria/anuria, rising creatinine and urea, hyperkalaemia (risk of cardiac arrest), metabolic acidosis, oedema, confusion.
- **Reversible** if the cause is treated quickly.

### Chronic kidney disease (CKD)

- Gradual, irreversible loss of nephrons over months to years.
- Causes: diabetic nephropathy (most common in India), hypertensive nephrosclerosis, chronic GN, reflux nephropathy.
- Staged GFR 1–5; stage 5 (GFR <15) = end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis or transplant.
- Features: anaemia (↓ erythropoietin), hypertension, bone disease (↓ vitamin D), uraemia (nausea, vomiting, pruritus, encephalopathy), pericarditis, peripheral neuropathy.

**Comparison: AKI vs CKD**

| Feature | AKI | CKD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Days to weeks | Months to years |
| Kidney size | Normal or enlarged | Reduced, shrunken |
| Anaemia | Mild or absent | Severe (normochromic normocytic) |
| Bone disease | Absent | Present (renal osteodystrophy) |
| Reversibility | Often reversible | Irreversible |
| Urinalysis | Granular casts (ATN), RBC casts (GN) | Waxy, broad casts |

**Nursing care in renal failure:**
- Accurate input-output record; daily weight.
- Restrict fluid (input = output + insensible losses).
- Low-potassium and low-phosphate diet; moderate protein restriction.
- Monitor ECG for hyperkalaemia (peaked T waves, wide QRS).
- Prepare patient for dialysis (haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis) as ordered.
- Fistula care for haemodialysis patients (no BP cuff or venepuncture on the fistula arm).
- Psychosocial support; education about diet, fluid restriction, and medications.

## 1.7 Renal tumours

### Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma)

- Most common **abdominal tumour in children** (peak age 3–4 years).
- Arises from embryonal renal tissue; associated with WT1 gene mutation.
- **Features:** Smooth, large abdominal mass (often the parent notices it while bathing the child), haematuria, hypertension, fever.
- **Morphology:** Triphasic tumour — blastema, stromal, and epithelial elements.
- **Treatment:** Surgery (nephrectomy) + chemotherapy ± radiotherapy; prognosis is good.
- **Nursing:** Do not palpate the abdomen repeatedly (can rupture the capsule and seed tumour cells); prepare child and family for surgery and chemotherapy; handle the child gently; support the family emotionally.

### Renal cell carcinoma (RCC)

- Most common renal malignancy in adults; peak age 50–70 years.
- Arises from renal tubular epithelium; clear cell type is most common.
- **Risk factors:** Smoking (doubles risk), obesity, hypertension, von Hippel-Lindau syndrome.
- **Classic triad:** Haematuria + flank pain + flank mass (less than one-third have all three today; often found incidentally on ultrasound).
- **Paraneoplastic syndromes:** Polycythaemia (erythropoietin production), hypercalcaemia (PTHrP), Cushing's syndrome, hypertension.
- **Metastases:** Lung (cannon-ball deposits), bone, liver, brain — through blood.
- **Treatment:** Partial or radical nephrectomy; targeted therapy (sunitinib) for advanced disease; RCC is resistant to chemotherapy.
- **Nursing:** Prepare for nephrectomy; post-operative wound and drain care; monitor for bleeding; educate on smoking cessation.

### Urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma of the bladder

- Most common malignancy of the urinary tract.
- **Risk factors:** Cigarette smoking (most important), occupational exposure to aniline dyes (rubber, textile, leather industries), cyclophosphamide, schistosomiasis (squamous cell type in Africa).
- **Features:** Painless haematuria (most common presenting symptom), frequency and dysuria, urinary obstruction.
- **Diagnosis:** Cystoscopy with biopsy (gold standard); urine cytology.
- **Treatment:** Transurethral resection (TURBT) for superficial; radical cystectomy + chemotherapy for muscle-invasive; intravesical BCG for high-grade superficial.
- **Nursing:** Educate on haematuria — any painless haematuria must be investigated; catheter care post-cystoscopy; post-cystectomy stoma care (urostomy or ileal conduit); smoking cessation counselling.

## Remember

- Nephritic syndrome: haematuria + mild proteinuria + hypertension + oliguria; post-streptococcal GN is the classic cause; check ASO titre.
- Nephrotic syndrome: massive proteinuria → hypoalbuminaemia → generalised oedema + hyperlipidaemia; minimal change disease is the most common cause in children.
- ATN is the most common cause of AKI; ischaemic (from shock) or toxic (aminoglycosides, contrast); usually reversible.
- CKD = small kidneys, severe anaemia, bone disease, uraemia — not reversible; stage by GFR.
- Wilms tumour: children, large abdominal mass — do not palpate aggressively; treat with surgery + chemotherapy.
- RCC: adults, haematuria + flank pain + mass; spreads via blood; smoking is the main risk; treatment is surgery.
- Bladder TCC: painless haematuria; smoking and dye industry exposure are key risks; diagnosed by cystoscopy.
