# Unit 6: Genetics — maternal, prenatal and genetic influences on defects and diseases

This unit extends inheritance into more complex topics: multifactorial disease, teratogens, genomic imprinting, repeat expansions, cancer genetics, and epigenetics — all with significant clinical and nursing relevance.

---

## 6.1 Multifactorial (polygenic) inheritance

Most common diseases are **not** caused by a single gene defect. Instead they result from the combined effect of many genes (polygenic) interacting with environmental triggers.

### Key concepts

- **Heritability:** The proportion of disease risk attributable to genetic factors (not 100% genetic, not 100% environmental).
- **Threshold model:** Disease appears when genetic susceptibility + environmental triggers exceed a threshold.
- **Recurrence risk:** Unlike Mendelian disease, recurrence risk in multifactorial disease is not a fixed fraction. It is typically the **square root of the population prevalence** (empirical risk from family studies) and rises if: more family members are affected, the index patient is severely affected, the index patient is from the less commonly affected sex.

| Condition | Approximate heritability | Environmental triggers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cleft lip and palate | ~75% | Folate deficiency, anti-epileptics, smoking |
| Neural tube defects (spina bifida, anencephaly) | 60–70% | Folate deficiency; prevent with 400 µg/day periconceptional folic acid |
| Congenital heart defects (CHD) | Variable | Rubella, diabetes in pregnancy, drugs, alcohol |
| Type 2 diabetes | ~40–80% | Obesity, sedentary lifestyle, high-calorie diet |
| Hypertension | ~50% | Sodium intake, obesity, stress |
| Schizophrenia | ~80% | Perinatal brain injury, cannabis use |
| Hirschsprung disease | Variable | More common in boys; RET gene variants |

**Nursing relevance:** Counsel mothers about folic acid supplementation before and during the first trimester; explain that risk is increased for siblings but not Mendelian; support families without overstating unavoidability.

## 6.2 Teratogenesis

A **teratogen** is any agent that causes a structural or functional abnormality in a developing embryo or foetus.

### Critical periods

- **Weeks 2–8 (organogenesis):** Period of greatest susceptibility; major structural malformations arise from teratogen exposure in this window.
- **Week 1:** "All-or-nothing" period (before implantation) — teratogen either kills the embryo or has no effect.
- **After week 8 (foetal period):** Structural organs formed; teratogen exposure now mainly affects growth, CNS maturation, and functional development.

### Key teratogens

| Teratogen | Defect caused |
| --- | --- |
| Thalidomide (sedative, used 1950s–60s) | Limb defects (phocomelia — seal limbs), ear defects, cardiac defects |
| Rubella virus (first trimester) | Cataracts, sensorineural deafness, patent ductus arteriosus (congenital rubella syndrome) |
| Alcohol (ethanol) | Foetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD): short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, growth restriction, intellectual disability — the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability worldwide |
| Valproic acid (sodium valproate) | Neural tube defects (↑ risk ~20×), cardiac defects, facial abnormalities; supplement with high-dose folic acid if unavoidable |
| Isotretinoin (vitamin A derivative for acne) | CNS defects, ear/face defects, cardiac defects; strict contraception required |
| Warfarin | Warfarin embryopathy (nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses in first trimester); CNS haemorrhage (second/third trimester); switch to heparin in pregnancy |
| ACE inhibitors | Foetal renal damage, oligohydramnios, neonatal hypotension |
| Tetracyclines | Discolouration and hypoplasia of deciduous teeth; bone growth inhibition |
| Cytotoxic drugs | Foetal death, malformations |
| Ionising radiation (high dose) | Microcephaly, intellectual disability, leukaemia |

## 6.3 Maternal conditions and foetal effects

| Maternal condition | Foetal/neonatal effect |
| --- | --- |
| Diabetes mellitus (poorly controlled) | Macrosomia, congenital heart defects, neural tube defects, neonatal hypoglycaemia, respiratory distress syndrome (premature surfactant delay) |
| Hypothyroidism (untreated) | Cretinism (hypothyroid newborn — intellectual disability, short stature, coarse features) if iodine deficiency; treat mother throughout pregnancy |
| Hypertension (severe) | Intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), placental abruption |
| SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus) | Neonatal lupus, congenital heart block (from anti-Ro/La antibodies) |

### TORCH infections

TORCH is a convenient acronym for perinatal infections that can cross the placenta or infect during delivery and cause congenital disease.

| Infection | Key features in the neonate |
| --- | --- |
| **T** — Toxoplasma gondii | Chorioretinitis, intracranial calcifications (periventricular), hydrocephalus; acquired by eating undercooked meat or handling cat litter |
| **O** — Others (syphilis, hepatitis B, varicella, parvovirus B19) | Congenital syphilis: snuffles, saddle-nose, Hutchinson's teeth, periostitis; Hepatitis B: chronic infection → cirrhosis → HCC; parvovirus B19: hydrops foetalis |
| **R** — Rubella | Cataracts, sensorineural deafness, patent ductus arteriosus; prevented by MMR vaccination |
| **C** — Cytomegalovirus (CMV) | Most common intrauterine infection; periventricular calcifications, hearing loss, hepatosplenomegaly, petechiae — most asymptomatic at birth |
| **H** — Herpes simplex virus (HSV) | Neonatal herpes from maternal genital HSV-2 during delivery; vesicles, encephalitis, disseminated disease; treat with IV aciclovir; caesarean section if active lesions at term |

**Nursing:** Educate pregnant women about TORCH prevention — cook meat thoroughly, avoid cat litter, complete MMR vaccination before pregnancy, use condoms for HSV-2 prevention, universal hepatitis B vaccination.

## 6.4 Genomic imprinting

**Imprinting** = epigenetic silencing of one parental allele so that only the maternal or only the paternal copy is expressed. The imprinting depends on which parent the allele came from, not on its DNA sequence. Loss of the normally expressed copy → disease.

| Region | Paternally expressed (maternal imprint) | Maternally expressed (paternal imprint) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chromosome 15q11-q13 | SNRPN and other genes | UBE3A |

| Syndrome | Mechanism | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prader-Willi syndrome | Loss of paternal expression (paternal deletion 15q11, or maternal uniparental disomy — both chromosome 15s from mother) | Hypotonia in infancy, hyperphagia and obesity in childhood, small hands and feet, intellectual disability, hypogonadism |
| Angelman syndrome | Loss of maternal expression (maternal deletion 15q11, or paternal uniparental disomy) | Happy demeanour ("happy puppet"), seizures, absent speech, ataxia, intellectual disability |

**Mnemonic:** Prader-Willi = **Paternal** deletion (or Paternal contribution missing); Angelman = **Absent** maternal contribution.

## 6.5 Trinucleotide repeat expansions

Some genes contain unstable regions of repeated short sequences (triplets). When these expand beyond a threshold, they cause disease. The number of repeats can increase with each generation, causing the disease to appear earlier and more severely — this is called **anticipation**.

| Disease | Repeat | Location | Features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Huntington's disease | CAG (>36 = disease; 36–39 = reduced penetrance; >40 = full penetrance) | HTT exon 1 | Autosomal dominant; involuntary movements (chorea), dementia, psychiatric changes; onset typically 40s–50s; anticipation with paternal transmission |
| Fragile X syndrome | CGG (>200 = full mutation) | FMR1 gene, Xq27 | Most common inherited cause of intellectual disability; large ears, macroorchidism, autism features; X-linked dominant with anticipation |
| Myotonic dystrophy type 1 | CTG expansion | DMPK gene, 19q | Autosomal dominant; myotonia (difficulty relaxing hand), distal muscle weakness, cataracts, cardiomyopathy; anticipation with maternal transmission |
| Friedreich ataxia | GAA expansion | FXN gene | Autosomal recessive; progressive ataxia, cardiomyopathy, diabetes; no anticipation |

## 6.6 Mitochondrial disorders

- Caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial proteins.
- mtDNA inheritance is maternal; heteroplasmy → variable expression.
- Organs most affected: those with highest energy demand — brain, muscles, heart, retina.

| Syndrome | Mnemonic / features |
| --- | --- |
| MELAS | Mitochondrial Encephalomyopathy, Lactic Acidosis, Stroke-like episodes; onset in childhood/young adulthood; raised lactate |
| MERRF | Myoclonic Epilepsy with Ragged Red Fibres (Ragged Red Fibres on muscle biopsy = hallmark); myoclonus, epilepsy, ataxia |
| Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) | Acute or subacute painless bilateral optic nerve degeneration → blindness; young men predominantly (incomplete penetrance) |

## 6.7 Cancer genetics — the molecular basis of malignancy

Cancer arises from an accumulation of somatic mutations in genes controlling cell growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. Two main classes:

### Tumour suppressor genes (TSGs)

- Normally **inhibit** cell growth; both alleles must be lost for cancer to develop.
- **Knudson's two-hit hypothesis:** In inherited cancer syndromes, one allele is mutant in every cell (germline mutation); the second allele is somatically mutated in the tumour cell → cancer develops earlier with less random chance.

| Gene | Syndrome / cancer |
| --- | --- |
| TP53 | Most commonly mutated gene in human cancer; Li-Fraumeni syndrome (germline TP53) |
| BRCA1 / BRCA2 | Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome; also prostate and pancreatic cancer risk |
| APC | Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) → colorectal cancer; loss of APC → Wnt pathway activation |
| RB1 | Retinoblastoma (prototype of two-hit model); osteosarcoma |
| VHL | Von Hippel-Lindau syndrome → renal cell carcinoma, haemangioblastoma |
| MLH1, MSH2 | Lynch syndrome (hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer, HNPCC) → colorectal and endometrial cancer via mismatch repair deficiency |

### Oncogenes

- Normally encode proteins promoting cell growth (proto-oncogenes).
- Gain-of-function mutations → constitutive activation → uncontrolled growth.
- One mutant allele is enough (dominant).

| Oncogene | Cancer |
| --- | --- |
| RAS (KRAS, NRAS, HRAS) | Pancreatic (95% KRAS), lung, colorectal cancer; most common oncogene in human cancer |
| MYC | Burkitt lymphoma (c-Myc overexpression via t(8;14)); small cell lung, neuroblastoma |
| HER2 (ERBB2) | Breast carcinoma (20–25%); amplification → target for trastuzumab |
| BCR-ABL | Philadelphia chromosome — t(9;22) translocation in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML); constitutively active tyrosine kinase → imatinib (Gleevec) is the targeted therapy |

### Key chromosomal translocations in cancer

| Translocation | Gene fusion | Cancer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| t(9;22) — Philadelphia chromosome | BCR-ABL | CML (90%) |
| t(14;18) | BCL2 overexpression | Follicular lymphoma |
| t(8;14) | MYC-IgH | Burkitt lymphoma |
| t(15;17) | PML-RARα | Acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL); treat with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) |

## 6.8 Epigenetics

**Epigenetics** = heritable changes in gene expression that are **not** caused by alterations in the DNA sequence itself.

| Mechanism | Description | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| DNA methylation | Addition of methyl group to cytosine at CpG sites → gene silencing (methylated promoters are switched off) | Tumour suppressor silencing in cancer; imprinting; X-inactivation |
| Histone modification | Acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation of histone tails → opens (euchromatin) or closes (heterochromatin) chromatin for transcription | Active or inactive gene expression |
| Non-coding RNA (miRNA, siRNA) | Small RNAs silence mRNA post-transcriptionally | Fine-tune gene expression; dysregulated in cancer |

Epigenetic modifications are **reversible** — unlike DNA mutations. This is the basis for epigenetic cancer drugs (demethylating agents like azacitidine; histone deacetylase inhibitors like vorinostat).

## Remember

- Multifactorial disease: many genes + environment; empirical recurrence risk; folate supplementation prevents NTD.
- Critical period for teratogens = weeks 2–8 of gestation (organogenesis).
- Thalidomide → phocomelia; rubella → congenital rubella syndrome (cataracts, deafness, PDA); alcohol → FASD; valproate → NTD; isotretinoin → CNS + cardiac defects.
- TORCH: Toxoplasma, Others, Rubella, CMV, HSV — learn foetal effect for each.
- Prader-Willi = paternal deletion 15q11; Angelman = maternal deletion 15q11.
- Huntington disease = CAG repeat expansion; anticipation; autosomal dominant.
- Fragile X = most common inherited intellectual disability; CGG repeat on X chromosome.
- TSGs: two-hit model (TP53, BRCA1/2, APC, RB1). Oncogenes: gain-of-function, dominant (RAS, MYC, HER2).
- Philadelphia chromosome t(9;22) → BCR-ABL → CML → imatinib (targeted therapy).
- Epigenetics = DNA methylation + histone modification → reversible gene silencing.
