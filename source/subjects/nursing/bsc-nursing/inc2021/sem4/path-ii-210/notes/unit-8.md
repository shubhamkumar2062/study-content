# Unit 8: Genetics — genetic services, counselling, legal and ethical issues, and the nurse's role

This final genetics unit focuses on how genetic knowledge is delivered to patients and families, the ethical challenges it raises, and the central role of the nurse throughout.

---

## 8.1 Genetic services in India

### Structure

Genetic services in India are provided at multiple levels:

| Level | Service |
| --- | --- |
| National referral centres | AIIMS New Delhi, PGI Chandigarh, SGPGI Lucknow, NIMHANS Bengaluru — comprehensive genetics including biochemical, cytogenetics, molecular, prenatal, metabolic genetics |
| Regional genetics centres | In each state under the National Health Mission (NHM); chromosomal, biochemical, prenatal diagnosis, counselling |
| District hospitals | Basic counselling, referral; newborn screening collection |
| Primary health centres (PHC) | Referral of suspected genetic cases; newborn screening collection points |

### National programmes

- **National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, CVD and Stroke (NPCDCS):** Addresses familial cancer and cardiovascular risk.
- **National Neonatal Screening Programme:** Under NHM; targets congenital hypothyroidism, PKU, G6PD deficiency, CAH and sickle cell disease in high-prevalence regions.
- **Sickle Cell Mission:** Specific programme in tribal and high-prevalence regions — screening, counselling, treatment.
- **PCPNDT Act (Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, 1994, amended 2003):** Prohibits sex determination for sex selection; mandatory registration of all prenatal diagnostic centres; strict penalty for violations.

### Referral pathways

Who should be referred to a clinical genetics service?
- Child with congenital abnormalities or dysmorphic features.
- Developmental delay or intellectual disability — unexplained.
- Positive newborn screening result.
- Two or more miscarriages.
- Family history of genetic condition or early-onset cancer.
- Consanguineous couple planning pregnancy.
- Suspected chromosomal abnormality on prenatal screen.

## 8.2 Genetic counselling

### Definition

Genetic counselling is a **communication process** by which individuals and families affected by or at risk of genetic disorders are helped to:
1. Understand the medical facts about the condition.
2. Understand how the condition is inherited and the chance of occurrence or recurrence.
3. Understand the options available for dealing with the risk.
4. Choose the course of action that is appropriate for them in view of their goals and values.
5. Adjust, as well as possible, to living with the condition or at risk of it.

**Key principles:**
- **Non-directive:** The genetic counsellor presents facts and options without telling the client what decision to make. The client's autonomy is paramount.
- **Psycho-educational:** Addresses both cognitive (information) and emotional (distress, adjustment) needs.
- **A process, not a single event:** May span several sessions over months.

### The counselling process

**Step 1 — Establish or confirm the diagnosis**
- Clinical features, family history, investigations (karyotype, molecular, enzyme assays).
- A diagnosis cannot be discussed meaningfully unless it is confirmed.

**Step 2 — Pedigree construction**

Draw a three-generation pedigree using standard symbols:
```text
Standard pedigree symbols:
 □  = Male (unaffected)        ■  = Male (affected)
 ○  = Female (unaffected)      ●  = Female (affected)
 ◯─□  = Mating (couple)
 │   = Line of descent (parent to children)
 ═══  = Consanguinity (double line between related couple)
 □ with /  = Deceased
 ◔  = Carrier (half-filled = carrier female in XLR)
Siblings drawn left to right, oldest first.
```

A three-generation pedigree includes: index patient (the **proband**, shown with an arrow), parents, grandparents, siblings of parents, and siblings and children of the proband. Record names, ages (or year of birth), affected status, and cause of death for deceased members.

**Step 3 — Calculate or estimate recurrence risk**

| Situation | Risk |
| --- | --- |
| Autosomal dominant | 50% for each child of an affected parent |
| Autosomal recessive (both parents carriers) | 25% affected; 50% carrier; 25% unaffected non-carrier |
| X-linked recessive (carrier mother) | 50% of sons affected; 50% of daughters are carriers |
| Down syndrome — trisomy 21 (maternal age 35) | Approximately 1 in 350 |
| Robertsonian translocation 14;21 (maternal carrier) | 10–15% risk of Down syndrome in offspring |
| Multifactorial (NTD, cleft) | 2–5% (approximate; use empirical family data) |

**Step 4 — Discuss options and support decision-making**

Options may include: prenatal diagnosis, preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD — test embryos before IVF transfer), adoption, using donor eggs or sperm, surveillance and risk management, preventive treatment, or choosing not to test and accepting the risk.

**Step 5 — Psychological support and follow-up**

Genetic news causes grief, guilt, anxiety, and altered family relationships. Counselling sessions address these. A written summary of the consultation is sent to the client.

## 8.3 Recurrence risk communication

Communicating probability is difficult — most people do not think naturally in percentages.

**Relative vs absolute risk:**
- "Three times the population risk" sounds alarming if the population risk is 1 in 1,000 (absolute risk = 3 in 1,000 — still small).
- Use absolute numbers: "1 in 4 of your children is likely to be affected" is clearer than "25% risk."

**What people often misunderstand:**
- A 25% risk does not mean "if the first child is unaffected, the risk is lower next time" — each pregnancy is independent.
- Carrier status is not disease; many carriers are entirely well.
- A positive predictive test for a late-onset condition does not mean disease is present now.

**Nursing application:** When giving information about tests or family risk, use clear language, avoid jargon, check understanding, and avoid inadvertently causing disproportionate anxiety or false reassurance.

## 8.4 Special counselling situations

### Late-onset conditions (Huntington's disease, BRCA1/2)

- **Predictive testing** for Huntington's disease: A person at 50% risk (parent affected) can be tested before symptoms appear. ISONG/ACMG protocol requires at minimum two pre-test counselling sessions (spaced apart) and an assessment of psychological readiness; a support person; commitment to post-result support.
- **Right not to know:** A person has an ethical right not to find out their genetic status. If a person at risk declines testing, this must be respected.
- **Third-party implications:** A positive result in one person implies that their relatives are also at risk — the obligation to disclose to family is debated; in most jurisdictions the duty to the patient takes precedence over the duty to warn relatives, but the counsellor should encourage disclosure and may assist.
- **Privacy:** Genetic information must not be disclosed to insurers or employers without explicit consent.

### Carrier testing

- For autosomal recessive conditions (cystic fibrosis, sickle cell, thalassaemia) — identifies individuals who carry one mutant allele and are healthy, but whose children (with another carrier) could be affected.
- **Population screening in India:** Sickle cell carrier screening is offered in high-prevalence states; thalassaemia screening offered in some states; pre-marital screening programmes exist in some communities.
- **Concerns:** Stigma and discrimination if carrier status is widely known; impact on marriage prospects in India; must be voluntary and confidential.

### Consanguinity

- Marriage between individuals who share common ancestors (first cousins most common).
- Increases risk of autosomal recessive conditions because related individuals are more likely to carry the same recessive allele.
- First-cousin marriage approximately doubles the background risk for severe autosomal recessive disease (from about 1–2% in the general population to about 2–4%).
- **Cultural sensitivity in India:** Consanguineous marriages are traditional in certain communities (South India, some Muslim communities); genetic counselling must be respectful and non-judgmental while giving accurate risk information.

### Prenatal diagnosis decisions

- After a positive prenatal diagnosis (e.g. Down syndrome, CF), the couple must decide whether to continue or terminate the pregnancy.
- **MTP Act (Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, amended 2021):** In India, termination is legal up to 24 weeks (20 weeks previously; now 24 weeks for specific categories, with no upper limit for foetal conditions incompatible with life or severe abnormality under a medical board). The decision is personal; the nurse's role is support, not judgment.
- **PCPNDT Act:** Prohibits sex determination and sex-selective termination; prenatal diagnosis is legal only for specified conditions.

## 8.5 Legal and ethical issues in genetics

### Genetic privacy

- Genetic information is uniquely personal — it reveals not only a person's own health but that of their biological relatives.
- Concerns: **Insurance discrimination** (insurers using genetic test results to deny cover or raise premiums) and **employment discrimination** (employers discriminating based on predisposition to disease).
- **Genetic Information Non-discrimination Act (GINA, USA):** Prohibits genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment; does not cover life or disability insurance.
- **India:** No specific comprehensive genetic discrimination law yet; legal framework developing. Data protection under IT Act is relevant.

### Ownership of genetic information

- Who owns the genetic information? The patient, the laboratory, the health system?
- When a patient's genetic result has implications for relatives, do relatives have a right to know? Does the clinician have a duty to warn?
- Current consensus: Duty is to the patient; encourage voluntary disclosure to relatives; direct disclosure without consent is not standard practice in most countries.

### Direct-to-consumer (DTC) genetic testing

- Companies (e.g. 23andMe, AncestryDNA) sell at-home genetic tests directly to consumers.
- **Limitations:** Mostly genome-wide association (GWAS) data; designed for ancestry and wellness, not clinical diagnosis; not validated for clinical decision-making; variants of uncertain significance; no genetic counselling.
- **Psychological impact:** People can receive unexpected findings (non-paternity, relatives with same genetic condition, risk estimates for serious diseases) without counselling preparation.
- **Accuracy concerns:** DTC tests may miss pathogenic variants detectable by clinical-grade tests; false reassurance.
- **Nursing response:** When patients bring DTC results to clinic, explain the limitations; refer to clinical genetics for formal testing if clinically indicated.

## 8.6 The nurse's role in genetics

The nurse is often the first health professional that a patient or family encounters when a genetic issue arises. The nurse does not replace the geneticist or genetic counsellor — the nurse refers, prepares, supports, and follows up.

### Key nursing responsibilities

| Role | Action |
| --- | --- |
| First point of contact | Recognise genetic red flags; take family history; refer appropriately |
| Preparation | Explain what a genetic consultation involves; collect pedigree information; arrange blood samples or tissue for testing |
| Support during counselling | Sit with the patient; provide emotional support; help recall information afterward; arrange follow-up |
| Education | Explain what a genetic test is and what results mean (not interpreting clinical results, but explaining general process) |
| Confidentiality | Genetic information must not be shared without explicit consent — not with insurers, employers, or even other family members without the patient's permission |
| Follow-up and adherence | Support patients on long-term genetic treatments (e.g. phenylketonuria diet, venesection for haemochromatosis, CF physiotherapy) |

### Recognising genetic red flags (refer to genetics)

- Early-onset disease (much younger than typical onset — e.g. MI at 40 in someone with FH, colon cancer at 30 with Lynch syndrome).
- Bilateral or multifocal disease (bilateral breast cancer, bilateral Wilms tumour — suggests hereditary predisposition).
- Two or more relatives on the same side with the same cancer or unusual condition.
- Unusual ethnic group association (e.g. thalassaemia in an unlikely community).
- Dysmorphic features in a child with developmental delay.
- Consanguineous parents (unusual diagnosis or multiple affected children).
- Two or more miscarriages (possible chromosomal translocation in a parent).

### Cultural sensitivity in India

- **Caste-based genetic diseases:** Certain conditions are prevalent within specific genetic communities (Bunt, Nair, Lohana communities — different thalassaemia mutations; Sickle cell in tribal communities). Screening must be delivered without reinforcing caste stereotypes.
- **Stigma around inherited conditions:** Families may feel guilty or be stigmatised; marriage prospects of a sibling may be affected if a diagnosis is shared. The nurse must balance honesty with sensitivity, and stress that genetic conditions are nobody's fault.
- **Gender dynamics:** Genetic counselling must be delivered to both partners; in patriarchal contexts, ensure the woman has space to understand, ask questions, and have her choices respected.
- **Consanguinity:** As noted above — counsel respectfully, provide accurate information, never moralize.

### Genetic counselling process — the nurse's stage-by-stage role

| Stage | Nurse's action |
| --- | --- |
| Before the appointment | Explain what will happen; ensure correct referral information is available; support the family's decision to attend |
| During the appointment | Be present for emotional support; help summarise what was said; note questions the family did not ask but needed to |
| After the appointment | Provide written information; clarify what was explained; arrange follow-up tests; link to support groups |
| Long term | Monitor adherence to treatment; support psychological adjustment; watch for new concerns as the family situation changes |

## Remember

- Genetic counselling = non-directive, psycho-educational, process (not a single event).
- Pedigree: square = male, circle = female, filled = affected, double line = consanguinity, arrow = proband.
- Confirm the diagnosis before discussing recurrence risk.
- Predictive testing for Huntington's: mandatory pre- and post-test counselling sessions; right not to know must be respected.
- PCPNDT Act: sex determination for sex selection is illegal in India.
- MTP Act (amended 2021): termination up to 24 weeks for specific categories; no upper limit for foetal conditions incompatible with life (medical board required).
- Genetic privacy: not for insurers or employers without consent; encourage but do not force disclosure to relatives.
- DTC testing: ancestry / wellness grade; not clinical quality; may need clinical follow-up.
- Nurse's key role: recognise red flags → refer → prepare → support → ensure confidentiality → follow up.
- Cultural sensitivity in India: caste-based prevalence, stigma, consanguinity, gender dynamics — always respectful and non-judgmental.
