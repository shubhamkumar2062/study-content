# Unit 4: Ethical issues, decision making, codes of ethics and patients' rights

This unit applies ethical principles to real clinical situations. Each scenario below — informed consent, refusal of treatment, end-of-life decisions, confidentiality dilemmas — is a potential examination question. Know the Indian legal context.

---

## 4.1 Informed consent

Informed consent is both a legal and an ethical requirement before any medical or nursing intervention that is not an emergency.

### Elements of valid informed consent

| Element | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| **Disclosure** | The patient must receive adequate information: diagnosis, proposed procedure, purpose, alternatives (including no treatment), material risks and benefits |
| **Comprehension** | The patient must understand the information — use simple language, an interpreter if needed, written summary |
| **Voluntariness** | The consent must be free from coercion, undue influence, or manipulation |
| **Competence (capacity)** | The patient must have decision-making capacity: able to understand, retain, reason with and communicate a decision |
| **Authorisation** | The patient explicitly agrees (signs or verbally confirms) |

### Types of consent

- **Expressed consent:** Given verbally or in writing — required for major procedures, surgery, research.
- **Implied consent:** Inferred from actions (a patient extends their arm for a blood pressure measurement); acceptable for routine nursing care.
- **Written consent:** Standard for surgery, anaesthesia, chemotherapy, clinical trials.

### Special situations

**Children:** Parents or legal guardians consent on behalf of a minor. In India, a "major" is 18 years or older. For non-emergency treatment of a child: parental consent required. Adolescents' capacity to consent (Gillick competence, used in UK; similar principles applied in India) may be recognised for certain decisions with maturity. Always seek the child's **assent** (agreement) even when parental consent is the legal requirement.

**Unconscious or incapacitated patients:** No time to obtain consent — proceed in the patient's best interest in a genuine emergency. For non-emergency treatment of a patient without capacity: use the least restrictive option; in India, the **Mental Healthcare Act 2017** provides for a **nominated representative** to consent for a person with severe mental illness.

**Language barrier:** An interpreter (not a family member where possible, to preserve confidentiality) must be provided.

**Nurse's role:** The nurse's role is to **witness** the patient's signature — not to obtain the consent (that is the treating doctor's responsibility). The nurse must verify: has the patient received the information? Does the patient appear to understand? Is the signature freely given? If not, the nurse should raise this before the procedure proceeds. Witness a consent you are not confident is valid.

## 4.2 Refusal of treatment

A **competent adult** has an absolute right to refuse any medical or surgical treatment, even one that is life-saving — this is an expression of autonomy.

**Examples:**
- A patient refusing amputation for diabetic gangrene
- A Jehovah's Witness declining a blood transfusion on religious grounds
- A patient refusing chemotherapy and choosing palliative care

**Nurse's duty when a patient refuses:**
1. **Ensure the refusal is informed** — does the patient understand the consequences, including the risk of death?
2. **Document clearly** — the refusal, the information given, the patient's stated understanding, and that the patient was of sound mind
3. **Never abandon the patient** — continue to provide comfort, pain relief and support
4. **Respect the decision** — do not coerce; override would be assault
5. **Refer to the treating team** — a senior doctor should speak with the patient; ethics committee consultation may be appropriate for complex cases

**Children refusing treatment:** A child's refusal can be overridden if parents and the court decide it is in the child's best interest. Courts in India have occasionally intervened to mandate transfusions for minors when parents refused on religious grounds.

### Advance directives

A patient may document their wishes about future care in case they lose capacity.

| Type | Contents | Indian context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Living will** | Specific wishes about treatment (e.g. no CPR, no ventilation in terminal illness, no artificial nutrition) | Legally recognised in India after the Supreme Court's **Common Cause judgment (2018)** |
| **Durable power of attorney for health care** | Appoints another person to make decisions on the patient's behalf if they lose capacity | Nominated representative system under Mental Healthcare Act 2017 |

**DNR (Do Not Resuscitate) orders:**
- A written instruction not to attempt cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) if the heart or breathing stops
- Must be: documented in the medical record, signed by a senior doctor, made after discussion with the patient (if capable) and/or family
- **Does not mean withdrawal of all care** — the patient continues to receive nursing care, comfort, nutrition, pain relief and all other treatments appropriate to their condition
- Must be clearly communicated at every handover
- Nurse's role: know which patients have DNR orders; ensure they are documented at the bedside; never attempt CPR on a patient with a valid DNR; if uncertain — start CPR and clarify immediately

## 4.3 Truth-telling and therapeutic privilege

**Veracity (truth-telling):** Patients have a right to honest information about their diagnosis, prognosis and treatment options. Without truth, informed consent is impossible.

**Breaking bad news (SPIKES protocol):**

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **S — Setting** | Private room; family present if patient wishes; no interruptions; tissues available; introduce self |
| **P — Perception** | "What have you already been told?" — find out what the patient knows and their readiness to hear |
| **I — Invitation** | "How much information would you like me to share with you today?" — allow the patient to control the pace |
| **K — Knowledge** | Give information clearly, in plain language, in small chunks; pause; watch for non-verbal cues |
| **E — Emotions** | Acknowledge and respond to the patient's emotional reaction: empathy, silence, presence |
| **S — Strategy/Summary** | Summarise the information; outline the plan; answer questions; arrange follow-up |

**Therapeutic privilege:** Withholding information because the clinician believes it will harm the patient. Modern ethics regards this as rarely, if ever, justified. Patients generally cope better with honest information than with uncertainty and hidden diagnoses.

## 4.4 End-of-life decisions

### Withdrawing vs withholding treatment

Withdrawing (stopping a treatment already started) and withholding (not starting a treatment) are **ethically equivalent** — both are legally and ethically permissible when treatment is futile or the patient refuses it. The perceived "worse feeling" about withdrawal is an emotional intuition, not an ethical distinction.

### Palliative sedation

Deep sedation to relieve intractable suffering in a dying patient who has not responded to other symptom management. The intention is relief of suffering, not hastening death (**principle of double effect**). Ethically and legally accepted when: the patient's suffering is otherwise uncontrollable; the intent is relief; the patient or family consent.

### Euthanasia

| Type | Description | Legal status in India |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Passive euthanasia** | Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment allowing natural death | **Conditionally legal in India** (Common Cause judgment 2018 — with advance directive or court approval) |
| **Active euthanasia** | Administering a drug or performing an act to intentionally end life | **Illegal in India** |
| **Physician-assisted dying (PAD)** | Doctor prescribes a lethal dose for the patient to self-administer | **Illegal in India** |
| **Voluntary euthanasia** | Patient requests death | Illegal (active form) |
| **Non-voluntary euthanasia** | Patient cannot consent (e.g. permanent vegetative state) | Passive form conditionally legal under court orders (Aruna Shanbaug case 2011) |

**Key Indian cases:**
- **Aruna Shanbaug case (2011):** Supreme Court of India conditionally allowed passive euthanasia with the High Court's approval; the case concerned a nurse who had been in a vegetative state for 37 years.
- **Common Cause v Union of India (2018):** Five-judge constitution bench recognised the right to die with dignity; advance directives (living wills) are legally valid in India; the court set out a procedure for giving effect to them.

**Role of palliative care:** Palliative care aims to relieve suffering and improve quality of life for patients with serious illness — it does not hasten or postpone death. Well-resourced palliative care makes euthanasia debates less pressing.

## 4.5 Confidentiality dilemmas

### HIV disclosure to a third party

If a patient with HIV refuses to disclose their status to a known sexual partner or needle-sharing partner who is at real risk of harm, the nurse faces a conflict between **confidentiality** and the **duty to warn** (and **beneficence** and **justice** toward the third party). Most ethics frameworks allow breach of confidentiality in this limited situation after: warning the patient, documenting the discussion, and seeking guidance from a senior or ethics committee.

### Mandatory disease reporting

Under the Epidemic Diseases Act and State-specific notifications, certain infectious diseases (cholera, plague, tuberculosis under RNTCP, COVID-19 etc.) must be reported to public health authorities without the patient's consent. This is a legal override of confidentiality in the public interest.

### Child abuse reporting

The **Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act 2012 (POCSO Act)** in India creates a **legal obligation** for every person who has knowledge or reasonable suspicion of sexual abuse of a child (under 18 years) to report it to the police or Special Juvenile Police Unit immediately. Failure to report is an offence. Nurses are covered by this duty. For physical or emotional abuse: report to the hospital child protection team or Child Welfare Committee.

## 4.6 Resource allocation and justice

### Organ donation and transplantation

The **Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act 1994 (amended 2011)** governs organ donation in India.

- **Brain death** criteria (apnoea test + clinical tests performed twice, 6 hours apart, by two certified doctors) — not to be confused with cardiac death
- **Deceased donation (cadaveric):** Opt-in consent system in India — either the deceased's prior written consent (donor card) or family consent at the time of death
- **Living donation:** Permitted for a near relative; for unrelated donors, authorisation committees scrutinise the application
- **NOTTO:** National Organ and Tissue Transplant Organisation — central coordinating body
- **Allocation criteria:** Medical urgency, blood group compatibility, waiting time, geographic proximity

**Nurse's role:** Identify potential donors (brain-dead ICU patients); approach families sensitively after they have accepted the news of brain death; provide factual information; facilitate the consent discussion with the transplant coordinator; support the family.

## 4.7 Research ethics

Medical and nursing research on human subjects requires strict ethical oversight.

| Principle | Application |
| --- | --- |
| **Informed consent** | Fully informed, voluntary; consent is ongoing — participant may withdraw at any time |
| **Vulnerability** | Extra protection for children, prisoners, the mentally ill, students — power imbalance increases coercion risk |
| **Therapeutic misconception** | Participant believes research will help them personally; must distinguish research from treatment |
| **Research vs treatment distinction** | Research aims to generate new knowledge; treatment aims to help the individual patient — sometimes both, but keep goals distinct |
| **Ethical review committees (IRB/IEC)** | Institutional Ethics Committees (IECs) review and approve all research on human participants; independent of the research team |
| **ICMR National Ethical Guidelines 2017** | Indian guidelines for biomedical and health research involving human participants |

## 4.8 Ethical issues in mental health nursing

### Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (India)

The Mental Healthcare Act (MHA) 2017 replaced the Mental Health Act 1987. Key provisions:
- Every person has the **right to access mental health care** from government facilities free of charge
- **Advance directive:** A person with a mental illness may make an advance directive about treatment they do not want
- **Nominated representative:** A person appointed by the patient to make decisions on their behalf
- **Involuntary admission:** Permitted only when: (a) the person has a mental illness, and (b) is at risk of harm to self or others, and (c) lacks capacity — must have prior approval of the Mental Health Review Board
- **Least restrictive care:** Treatment should be in the least restrictive setting possible
- **Rights during admission:** Right to information, right to legal aid, right to communication with family and legal counsel
- **Restraint and seclusion:** Permitted only as a last resort for safety; not as punishment; must be documented and reviewed

## 4.9 Privacy and dignity

- **Social media:** Never photograph patients, never post patient information or identifiable images on any social media platform, even with perceived "good intentions" — this is a breach of confidentiality and professional conduct
- **Photography in clinical settings:** Only with explicit written consent; images are patient records and subject to data protection
- **Dignity in intimate care:** Explain every step, maintain privacy (screens/curtains), expose only the area necessary, acknowledge embarrassment and reduce it, ensure the patient is warm

## 4.10 Workplace ethics

### Professional boundaries

The therapeutic relationship between nurse and patient is a professional relationship with clear boundaries:
- Personal relationships (friendship, romantic relationships) with patients are inappropriate while in a professional capacity and may cause harm
- Do not accept significant gifts (small tokens of appreciation are generally acceptable; expensive gifts should be politely declined)
- Social media with patients: accepting friend requests or following patients blurs boundaries — most professional codes advise against it

### Whistleblowing

**Definition:** Reporting unsafe practices, illegal acts or serious misconduct within the workplace through proper channels.

**Duty to report:** Nurses have a professional obligation to report practices that endanger patients — prescribing errors, unsafe staffing, abuse, unqualified practitioners, drug diversion.

**How to report:**
1. Document the concern with facts, dates, witnesses
2. Report to the immediate supervisor or charge nurse
3. If not resolved, escalate to the hospital administration or nursing director
4. If the internal system fails to act: report to the INC, NHRC (National Human Rights Commission) or the relevant state authority
5. Never ignore a patient safety concern for fear of reprisal — patient safety is the primary obligation

## 4.11 Ethical decision-making models

### MORAL model (Crisham, 1985)

| Letter | Step |
| --- | --- |
| **M** — Massage the dilemma | Gather all facts; identify the ethical issue; clarify whose problem it is |
| **O** — Outline the options | List all possible courses of action, including doing nothing |
| **R** — Review criteria and resolve | Apply ethical principles to each option; consider rights, duties, harms, benefits |
| **A** — Affirm position and act | Choose the option best supported by the ethical analysis; act on it |
| **L** — Look back and evaluate | Reflect after the event; was the decision justified? What would you do differently? |

### Thompson–Thompson model (1985)

A ten-step model that is comprehensive but less commonly taught in Indian undergraduate nursing.

## 4.12 Hospital ethics committees

**Composition:** Medical specialists, nurses, allied health, hospital administration, legal adviser, ethicist, community representative.

**Functions:**
- **Case consultation:** Advise on specific difficult cases (e.g. end-of-life decisions, refusal of treatment, DNR disputes)
- **Policy development:** Draft hospital policies on informed consent, DNR, organ donation, research
- **Education:** Train staff in ethical reasoning
- Advisory, not binding — the clinical team makes the final decision, but the committee provides a structured opinion

## Remember

- Informed consent elements: Disclosure, Comprehension, Voluntariness, Competence, Authorisation.
- Nurse's consent role: witness the signature, verify the conditions — do not obtain consent (that is the doctor's duty).
- Advance directives valid in India: Common Cause judgment 2018.
- DNR does NOT mean stop all care — only CPR is withheld; comfort care continues.
- SPIKES: Setting → Perception → Invitation → Knowledge → Emotions → Strategy/Summary.
- Active euthanasia: illegal in India. Passive euthanasia: conditionally legal (court approval or valid advance directive).
- POCSO Act 2012: mandatory reporting of child sexual abuse — a legal obligation for nurses.
- Mental Healthcare Act 2017: right to treatment, nominated representative, advance directive, least restrictive care, involuntary admission criteria.
- Whistleblowing: professional duty; document, escalate properly, never ignore patient safety.
- MORAL decision-making: Massage → Outline → Review → Affirm → Look back.
- Transplantation Act: brain death ≠ cardiac death; opt-in consent; NOTTO coordinates nationally.
