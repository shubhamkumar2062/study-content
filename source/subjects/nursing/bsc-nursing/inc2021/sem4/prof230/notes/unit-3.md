# Unit 3: Ethics, bioethics and ethical principles

Ethics provides the philosophical foundation for professional nursing practice. This unit introduces the major moral theories, the principles of bioethics, and the codes of ethics that guide nurses. Learn each concept as a definition, then be ready to apply it to a clinical scenario.

---

## 3.1 What is ethics?

**Ethics** is the systematic study of moral principles — the rules and values that govern right and wrong conduct. It asks: What should I do? What kind of person should I be? How should I treat others?

**Branches of ethics:**

| Branch | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| **Normative ethics** | Prescribes how people ought to act; includes ethical theories (utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics) |
| **Descriptive ethics** | Describes how people actually behave morally in practice |
| **Metaethics** | Examines the nature, meaning and justification of ethical claims ("Is goodness objective?") |
| **Applied ethics** | Applies ethical principles to specific practical domains — **bioethics** is applied ethics in health care |

---

## 3.2 Philosophical theories of ethics

### Consequentialism and Utilitarianism

**Core idea:** An action is right if it produces the greatest good for the greatest number of people.

- Developed by **Jeremy Bentham** (felicific calculus — measure of pleasure) and **John Stuart Mill** (refined to include quality of pleasure).
- Right and wrong depend entirely on **consequences** — the outcome determines the morality of the act.
- **Strengths:** Rational, flexible, considers everyone's interests equally.
- **Weaknesses:** Can justify harm to a minority for the majority's benefit; difficult to predict all outcomes; ignores rights.

**Nursing application:** Triage in a mass casualty event (prioritise those most likely to survive), resource allocation decisions (ventilator allocation during a pandemic), public health vaccination programmes that benefit the many at a small cost to some.

---

### Deontology (Duty-based ethics)

**Core idea:** Some actions are inherently right or wrong regardless of their consequences.

- Associated with **Immanuel Kant** (1724–1804).
- **Categorical Imperative:** "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." In other words, only do what you would want everyone to do in the same situation.
- Rights are absolute — you must not lie, even to save a life, because universal lying would destroy the very institution of communication.
- **Strengths:** Protects individual rights, provides clear duties, respects human dignity.
- **Weaknesses:** Inflexible; may produce bad outcomes; can conflict when two duties clash.

**Nursing application:** Informed consent (the duty to tell patients the truth regardless of whether it pleases them); never falsifying records; keeping promises to patients.

---

### Virtue Ethics

**Core idea:** Ethics is about cultivating a good character — the right habits and dispositions — rather than following rules or calculating outcomes.

- Originates with **Aristotle**; virtues are the golden mean between extremes (e.g. courage = between cowardice and recklessness).
- Key virtues in nursing: **compassion, honesty, courage, prudence, justice, fidelity, integrity**.
- **Florence Nightingale** as a nursing exemplar — her courage, discipline and compassion in the Crimea embody the virtuous nurse.
- **Strengths:** Focuses on the whole character of the professional, not just rule-following; captures the relational nature of nursing.
- **Weaknesses:** Does not always give clear guidance in a specific dilemma; virtues can conflict.

---

### Care Ethics

**Core idea:** Ethical relationships are built on care, context and interdependence, not abstract universal principles.

- Developed by **Carol Gilligan** and **Nel Noddings**; arose partly as a critique of Kohlberg's justice-focused moral development model.
- Emphasises the moral significance of relationships, vulnerability and responding to the particular needs of the person in front of you.
- **Most closely aligned with nursing practice** — nurses care for patients in relationship, with attention to their individual stories and needs.
- **Strengths:** Captures the moral heart of nursing; sensitive to context.
- **Weaknesses:** Can be difficult to generalise; risk of partiality (caring more for people close to us).

---

| Theory | Key thinker(s) | What makes an act right? | Nursing link |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Utilitarianism | Bentham, Mill | Greatest good for greatest number | Triage, resource allocation |
| Deontology | Kant | Following universal duties and respecting rights | Informed consent, honesty, never falsify records |
| Virtue ethics | Aristotle, Nightingale | Good character (compassion, honesty, courage) | Professional development |
| Care ethics | Gilligan, Noddings | Caring relationships and responding to need | Individual patient-centred care |

## 3.3 Bioethics

**Bioethics** applies ethical principles to health care, life sciences and research. The modern field grew from two historical events:

- **Nuremberg Code (1947):** Written after the Nuremberg Doctors' Trial (Nazi medical experiments on prisoners); established the principle that voluntary informed consent is absolutely essential for research.
- **Declaration of Helsinki (1964, World Medical Association):** Expanded principles for medical research involving human subjects; distinguishes therapeutic and non-therapeutic research.
- **Belmont Report (1979, US National Commission):** Identified three core principles for research involving humans: **respect for persons, beneficence, justice** — directly influenced Beauchamp and Childress.
- **Tom Beauchamp and James Childress, Principles of Biomedical Ethics (1979):** The four-principles framework (see below) became the dominant model in clinical bioethics worldwide.

---

## 3.4 The four principles of bioethics (Beauchamp and Childress)

### 1. Autonomy (Respect for persons)

**Definition:** Respect for an individual's right to make informed, voluntary decisions about their own life and health care.

**Key expression — Informed Consent:** A patient must receive adequate information (diagnosis, proposed treatment, alternatives, risks, benefits), understand it, and freely agree (or refuse) without coercion.

**Other expressions:** Advance directives (living will, durable power of attorney for health care) — written instructions for health care in case the patient loses decision-making capacity.

**Nurse's role:** Ensure the patient has received the information; check understanding; document consent; act as the patient's advocate if their choices are overridden.

**Limits of autonomy:** When a person lacks capacity (unconscious, severe mental illness), others act in their best interest (see substituted judgement/best-interests standard). Children — parents consent on behalf but the child's views are increasingly considered as they mature.

---

### 2. Non-maleficence (Do no harm)

**Definition:** The obligation not to inflict harm or impose unacceptable risks on patients.

**Origin:** "Primum non nocere" — first, do no harm (attributed to Hippocrates). Every medical intervention carries some risk; the duty is to ensure benefits outweigh the risks.

**Risk–benefit analysis:** Before any intervention, weigh the probability of benefit vs probability and severity of harm.

**Standard of care:** Nurses meet their non-maleficence duty by practising at or above the accepted professional standard.

**Nursing examples:** Never give a drug without checking for allergies; prevent falls and pressure sores; correct hand hygiene to prevent healthcare-associated infections; medication safety as a core non-maleficence obligation.

---

### 3. Beneficence (Do good)

**Definition:** The positive duty to act in the patient's best interest — to actively promote their health, well-being and welfare. It goes beyond merely avoiding harm.

**Distinction from non-maleficence:** Non-maleficence is a negative duty (do NOT harm); beneficence is a positive duty (DO help). Both are obligatory.

**Paternalism:** A conflict arises when a clinician believes they know what is best for the patient and overrides the patient's own wishes "for their own good." Modern ethics generally rejects paternalism in favour of autonomy, except when the patient truly lacks capacity.

**Nursing application:** Providing evidence-based wound care, early mobilisation, nutritional support, symptom management — all positive acts to promote health.

---

### 4. Justice (Fairness)

**Definition:** Fair, equitable and appropriate treatment of individuals and groups; equitable distribution of benefits, burdens and health resources.

**Distributive justice:** How should health resources be allocated?
- **Egalitarian view:** Allocate equally (everyone gets the same)
- **Libertarian view:** Allocate according to ability to pay (market-based)
- **Utilitarian view:** Allocate to maximise overall health gain (most benefit per rupee)
- **Prioritarian view:** Give priority to the worst-off

**Examples:** Organ transplant waiting list allocation criteria; pharmaceutical pricing; ensuring marginalised communities (tribal, rural, disabled) have equitable access to health care.

**Social determinants of health:** Income, education, housing and social environment shape health inequalities; justice requires addressing these upstream causes, not only treating illness.

## 3.5 Additional principles often cited in nursing

| Principle | Definition | Nursing example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fidelity** | Keeping promises and being loyal to professional obligations | Following through on a promise to return and check on a patient; completing delegated tasks |
| **Veracity** | Truth-telling; honesty with patients | Giving accurate information about a diagnosis; not falsifying records |
| **Confidentiality** | Keeping patient information private | Not discussing patients in public areas; securing records; obtaining consent before disclosure |

**Exceptions to confidentiality:**
- Serious danger to the patient or identifiable third party
- Mandatory reporting of notifiable infectious diseases (tuberculosis, HIV in some jurisdictions, cholera)
- Court order
- In a care team, sharing necessary information with colleagues caring for the same patient is not a breach of confidentiality.

## 3.6 Professional codes of ethics

### ICN Code of Ethics for Nurses (International Council of Nurses, 2021 revision)

Four fundamental elements:

| Element | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **Nurses and People** | The nurse's primary professional responsibility is to people requiring nursing care; promote health and wellbeing; respect rights; maintain confidentiality |
| **Nurses and Practice** | Maintain personal competence; uphold professional standards; promote safe practice; recognise and report unethical behaviour |
| **Nurses and the Profession** | Contribute to development of professional knowledge and practice; support professional organisations; promote safe working environments |
| **Nurses and Co-workers** | Work collaboratively; take action to protect individuals from unethical practice by co-workers |

### INC (Indian Nursing Council) Code of Professional Conduct

Governs the conduct of registered nurses and midwives in India. Covers: patient care, maintenance of competence, professional relationships, documentation standards, and disciplinary proceedings.

### ANA (American Nurses Association) Code of Ethics

Nine provisions covering: compassion, non-judgement, advocacy, responsibility for nursing practice, self-care for nurses, integrity, and social justice. Widely referenced internationally.

## 3.7 Applying ethical reasoning

A structured approach to an ethical dilemma:

1. **Recognise that there is an ethical issue** — something feels wrong or uncomfortable; two important values conflict
2. **Gather the relevant facts** — medical facts, prognosis, the patient's situation, what has been said to them
3. **Identify all stakeholders and their interests** — patient, family, nurses, doctors, hospital, community
4. **Apply the ethical principles** — which principles are relevant? Do they point in the same direction or conflict?
5. **Consider all reasonable options** — what can be done? What are the consequences of each?
6. **Reach a defensible decision** — the choice that best balances the principles; document the reasoning
7. **Reflect and learn** — after the event, did it go well? What would you do differently?

## Remember

- Utilitarianism: greatest good for greatest number (Bentham/Mill) → triage, resource allocation.
- Deontology: duty regardless of consequences (Kant) → informed consent, truth-telling, never falsify records.
- Virtue ethics: good character (Aristotle) → compassion, honesty, courage; Nightingale as the model.
- Care ethics: relationships and responding to need (Gilligan/Noddings) → patient-centred individual care.
- Four bioethical principles (Beauchamp and Childress): Autonomy, Non-maleficence, Beneficence, Justice.
- Autonomy → informed consent (voluntary, informed, competent); advance directive.
- Non-maleficence → do not harm; standard of care; risk–benefit analysis.
- Beneficence → positive duty to help; paternalism to be avoided.
- Justice → fair distribution; egalitarian vs utilitarian vs libertarian.
- ICN Code four elements: nurses and people, practice, profession, co-workers.
- Exceptions to confidentiality: danger to self/others; mandatory disease reporting; court order.
- Nuremberg Code (1947) and Helsinki Declaration (1964): foundations of research ethics.
