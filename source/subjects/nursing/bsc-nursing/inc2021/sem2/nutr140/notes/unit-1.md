# Unit 1: Introduction to nutrition, nutrients and foods

INC Unit I (2 hours): the basic ideas of nutrition and health, malnutrition, the role of nutrition in health, the factors that affect food and nutrition, the classification of nutrients and the classification of foods. Every later unit builds on the words defined here, so learn the definitions exactly.

## 1.1 Basic terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Food** | Anything eaten or drunk that nourishes the body: it gives energy, builds and repairs tissues, or regulates body processes. |
| **Nutrition** | The science of food and its relationship to health: how food is eaten, digested, absorbed, transported, used and how waste is removed, together with the social, economic and cultural factors that decide what people eat. |
| **Nutrient** | A chemical substance in food that the body needs for energy, growth, repair or regulation (carbohydrate, protein, fat, vitamins, minerals, water). |
| **Health** (WHO) | A state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. |
| **Dietetics** | The application of nutrition science to plan and give diets to healthy and sick people. |
| **Diet** | The food and drink a person usually takes. |
| **Balanced diet** | A diet with all nutrients in the right amounts and proportions to meet the needs of the body, with a small extra margin for short periods of low intake (details in Unit 4). |
| **Optimum (good) nutrition** | All nutrients are supplied in amounts that meet the body's needs and allow normal growth, work and resistance to disease. |
| **Nutritional status** | The condition of the body as a result of the intake, absorption and use of nutrients; measured by nutritional assessment (Unit 8). |
| **Therapeutic diet** | A normal diet changed in consistency, nutrients or feeding pattern to treat or support treatment of a disease (Unit 6). |

## 1.2 Malnutrition

**Malnutrition** means faulty nutrition — any condition in which the intake of nutrients is not right for the body's needs. It has two sides.

```text
                         MALNUTRITION
                 ┌─────────────┴──────────────┐
          UNDER-NUTRITION               OVER-NUTRITION
   (too little energy or nutrients)   (too much energy or a nutrient)
   • Protein energy malnutrition      • Overweight and obesity
     (wasting, stunting,              • Diet-related non-communicable
      underweight)                      diseases (type 2 diabetes,
   • Micronutrient deficiencies         hypertension, heart disease)
     (iron, iodine, vitamin A,        • Excess of a single nutrient
      vitamin D, B vitamins)            (e.g. too much salt; too much
                                        vitamin A or D from supplements)
```

Four forms are often described:

1. **Under-nutrition** — not enough food for a long time (calories and protein).
2. **Over-nutrition** — eating too much, especially energy-dense foods, for a long time.
3. **Imbalance** — wrong proportion of nutrients even when the total amount is enough (e.g. plenty of rice but very little pulse, vegetable or milk).
4. **Specific deficiency** — lack of one nutrient, e.g. iodine (goitre), vitamin A (night blindness).

**The "double burden" of malnutrition.** India now has under-nutrition (stunted, wasted and anaemic children and women) and over-nutrition (obesity, diabetes) in the same country, sometimes in the same household. Nurses meet both.

**Causes of malnutrition (short list)** — poverty and food insecurity; large family and poor spacing of births; lack of knowledge of what to feed; wrong beliefs and food taboos; repeated infections (diarrhoea, worms, measles); poor sanitation and unsafe water; low status of women; early marriage and pregnancy; sedentary life and fast food for over-nutrition.

**Effects** — poor growth and learning in children, low birth weight babies, lower working capacity and income in adults, lowered immunity and more infections, higher maternal and child deaths, and chronic disease in later life.

## 1.3 Role of nutrition in maintaining health

Good nutrition:

- **Supplies energy** for body functions (breathing, heartbeat, temperature) and for work.
- **Builds and repairs** tissues — growth in childhood, pregnancy and lactation; wound healing after surgery.
- **Regulates** body processes — enzymes, hormones, fluid and acid–base balance, nerve and muscle function (vitamins, minerals, water).
- **Protects** against infection — well-fed people have better immunity; under-nourished children get more frequent and more severe infections, which further worsen nutrition (the malnutrition–infection cycle).
- **Prevents deficiency diseases** — anaemia, goitre, rickets, night blindness, scurvy.
- **Reduces the risk of chronic disease** — a diet low in salt, sugar and saturated fat, with enough fibre, vegetables and fruit, lowers the risk of hypertension, diabetes, heart disease and some cancers.
- **Supports mental health and working capacity** — iron and iodine deficiency reduce learning and work output.
- **Speeds recovery** — correct nutrition shortens hospital stay and reduces complications such as pressure ulcers and infection.

```text
THE MALNUTRITION–INFECTION CYCLE
 Inadequate intake → weight loss, low immunity, damaged gut lining
        ↑                                          ↓
 Loss of appetite, nutrient loss,      More frequent, longer, more
 higher needs during fever  ←──────── severe infections (diarrhoea,
                                       measles, pneumonia)
```

## 1.4 Factors affecting food and nutrition

| Group | Factors | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Economic | Income, food prices, purchasing power, employment | A poor family buys cereals but cannot afford milk, pulses, fruit |
| Agricultural / geographic | What grows locally, season, climate, storage facilities | Seasonal shortage of vegetables in some regions |
| Social and cultural | Religion, caste, customs, food habits, taboos | Avoiding certain foods in pregnancy or after delivery |
| Family | Family size, birth order, who eats first, mother's education | Women and girls eating last and least |
| Personal | Age, sex, likes and dislikes, appetite, activity, physiological state (pregnancy, lactation) | Elderly with poor teeth eat less protein |
| Health | Illness, infections, worms, drugs that change appetite or absorption, oral problems | Diarrhoea reduces absorption |
| Knowledge | Nutrition awareness, cooking methods | Washing rice too much, throwing away cooking water |
| Psychological | Stress, depression, emotional eating | Depression reduces appetite; stress eating causes weight gain |
| Media and urbanisation | Advertising, fast food, eating out, working hours | More packaged and fried snacks |
| Government programmes | ICDS, PM POSHAN, public distribution system | Hot cooked meals for school children |

## 1.5 Classification of nutrients

There are about 40–50 nutrients. They are classified in three common ways.

**A. By the amount needed**

| Macronutrients (needed in grams) | Micronutrients (needed in milligrams or micrograms) |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Vitamins (fat soluble A, D, E, K; water soluble B group and C) |
| Proteins | Minerals (major: calcium, phosphorus, sodium, potassium, magnesium; trace: iron, iodine, zinc, copper, fluorine, selenium and others) |
| Fats | |
| (Water is also needed in large amounts) | |

**B. By chemical nature**

| Organic (contain carbon) | Inorganic (no carbon) |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins | Minerals, water |

**C. By function**

| Energy-yielding | Non-energy-yielding |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrate (4 kcal/g), protein (4 kcal/g), fat (9 kcal/g) | Vitamins, minerals, water, dietary fibre (fibre gives little usable energy) |

Another useful functional grouping:

- **Energy-giving foods**: cereals, roots and tubers, sugar, fats and oils.
- **Body-building foods**: milk, meat, fish, egg, pulses, nuts.
- **Protective foods**: vitamins and minerals — green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, fruits, milk, egg.

**Six classes of nutrients** (a common exam phrase): carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and water. Dietary fibre is often added as a seventh dietary component.

## 1.6 Classification of foods

**A. By origin**

- **Plant (vegetable) origin**: cereals, millets, pulses, nuts and oilseeds, vegetables, fruits, sugar, vegetable oils, spices.
- **Animal origin**: milk and milk products, eggs, meat, fish, poultry, ghee and butter.

Animal foods generally give complete (high biological value) protein, vitamin B12, haem iron and retinol; plant foods give fibre, carotenes, vitamin C, folate and many minerals.

**B. By chemical composition (predominant nutrient)**

- Carbohydrate-rich: cereals, tubers, sugar.
- Protein-rich: pulses, milk, egg, meat, fish.
- Fat-rich: oils, ghee, butter, nuts.
- Vitamin- and mineral-rich: vegetables and fruits.

**C. By function** — energy-giving, body-building and protective (above).

**D. By food groups.** Food groups help in planning a balanced diet. Two common systems are used in Indian textbooks:

| Basic four food groups (Unit 4) | Five food groups (ICMR style) |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Cereals and millets (with roots and tubers) | 1. Cereals, millets and their products |
| 2. Pulses, legumes, milk, egg, meat, fish (protein foods) | 2. Pulses and legumes |
| 3. Vegetables and fruits | 3. Milk and meat products |
| 4. Fats, oils and sugars | 4. Fruits and vegetables |
| | 5. Fats and sugars |

The newer ICMR-NIN dietary guidance (the "My Plate for the Day" idea) asks people to eat from several food groups every day — cereals and millets, pulses, milk or curd, vegetables including green leafy vegetables, fruits, and nuts and seeds — rather than mostly cereals. Check the current ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians for the exact plate proportions.

**E. By nutritive value:** cereals and millets, pulses, nuts and oilseeds, vegetables (green leafy, roots and tubers, other vegetables), fruits, animal foods, fats and oils, sugar and jaggery, condiments and spices.

## 1.7 Water

Water forms about 60% of adult body weight (more in infants). It is the medium for all chemical reactions, transports nutrients and wastes, regulates body temperature by sweating, lubricates joints and protects organs. It is lost in urine, stool, sweat and breath. Needs rise in fever, hot weather, heavy work, diarrhoea and vomiting, and lactation. Thirst is weak in the elderly, so they become dehydrated easily.

## 1.8 Nursing application

- Use simple words with patients: "energy foods", "body-building foods", "protective foods".
- Look for both under- and over-nutrition at every admission (weight, height, BMI, MUAC in children).
- Find the reason behind poor intake: money, beliefs, teeth, depression, drugs, nausea.
- Respect religious and cultural food choices; suggest local, affordable substitutes (e.g. pulses and groundnuts instead of meat).
- Break the malnutrition–infection cycle: hand washing, safe water, immunisation and continued feeding during illness.

## Remember

- Nutrition = science of food and its relation to health; nutrient = chemical in food the body needs.
- Malnutrition = under-nutrition + over-nutrition + imbalance + specific deficiency.
- Macronutrients: carbohydrate, protein, fat; micronutrients: vitamins, minerals.
- Organic: carbohydrate, protein, fat, vitamins; inorganic: minerals, water.
- Energy: carbohydrate 4, protein 4, fat 9 kcal per gram.
- Foods by function: energy-giving, body-building, protective.
- Basic four food groups: cereals; protein foods (pulses, milk, egg, meat); vegetables and fruits; fats, oils and sugar.
- India has a double burden of malnutrition.
