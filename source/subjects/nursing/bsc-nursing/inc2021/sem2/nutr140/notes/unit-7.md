# Unit 7: Cookery rules, preservation of nutrients and food safety

This notes file joins **INC Unit X** Cookery rules and preservation of nutrients (3 hours) and **INC Unit XIII** Food safety (2 hours), because both deal with handling food from market to plate. Food-borne diseases and food poisoning are taught in Community Health Nursing I (as the INC syllabus notes), so they are only mentioned here.

## 7.1 Why cook food?

Cooking: (1) makes food **easier to digest** (starch granules swell and burst, proteins become softer); (2) **kills germs and parasites**, making food safe; (3) improves **taste, flavour, colour and texture**; (4) **destroys some harmful substances** (e.g. trypsin inhibitors in pulses and soybean, goitrogens partly); (5) adds **variety**; (6) helps **keep food longer**. The cost is some **loss of nutrients**, especially vitamin C, thiamine, folate and other water-soluble vitamins and minerals.

## 7.2 Methods of cooking — advantages and disadvantages

Cooking methods are grouped by the medium of heat: moist heat (water or steam), dry heat (air or radiation), fat (frying), and microwave/combination.

| Method | How | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Boiling** | In water at 100 °C | Simple, cheap, even cooking, safe; food soft and easily digested (good for the sick) | Water-soluble vitamins and minerals leach into the water — lost if water is thrown away; flavour and colour may be lost; takes time and fuel |
| **Simmering / stewing** | Below boiling, long time in little liquid | Tough meat becomes tender; liquid is eaten, so nutrients are retained | Long cooking destroys heat-sensitive vitamins; uses fuel |
| **Steaming** | In steam over boiling water (idli, dhokla, momos) | Fewer nutrients lost than boiling; no fat; light, easily digested; shape kept | Slower; needs special vessel; bland if not seasoned |
| **Pressure cooking** | Steam under pressure — temperature above 100 °C (about 120 °C) | **Fast**, saves fuel and time; **better nutrient retention** because of short time and little water; tenderises pulses and meat; kills germs | Over-cooking makes food mushy; safety risk if the cooker is faulty; some vitamin loss at high temperature |
| **Poaching** | In a small amount of liquid just below boiling (eggs, fish) | Gentle, no fat, easily digested | Some leaching of nutrients |
| **Baking** | Dry hot air in an oven (bread, cakes) | Good flavour and texture; no fat needed for some foods | Needs oven; some loss of B vitamins (thiamine) and lysine (browning) |
| **Roasting** | Dry heat on a pan, in hot sand or an oven (groundnuts, chana, papad, corn) | Tasty, no fat, good flavour; roasted pulses and cereals are good for infant food mixes | Loss of thiamine and some protein quality with high heat; can burn |
| **Grilling / broiling / tandoor** | Direct radiant heat | Little fat, good flavour; fat drips away | Charring may form harmful compounds; dries food; needs care |
| **Shallow frying** | Little fat in a pan (paratha, dosa, cutlet) | Quick, tasty, adds energy | Adds fat and calories; fat may be overheated |
| **Deep frying** | Immersed in hot oil (puri, pakora, samosa) | Quick, crisp, very tasty, adds energy (useful for underweight) | Very high fat and calories; hard to digest; **reheated oil** forms harmful compounds and trans fats; heat-sensitive vitamins lost; bad for obesity, diabetes, heart disease |
| **Sautéing** | Tossing quickly in a little hot fat | Quick, keeps colour and crispness, retains nutrients | Adds some fat |
| **Microwave cooking** | Electromagnetic waves heat water inside food | Very quick, little water, **good nutrient retention**, less fuel | Uneven heating (cold spots may leave germs alive); needs suitable containers; no browning |
| **Solar cooking** | Sun's heat in a solar cooker | No fuel cost, eco-friendly, slow gentle cooking retains nutrients | Slow; depends on weather |

## 7.3 Cookery rules / measures to prevent loss of nutrients during preparation

**During purchase and storage**

1. Buy fresh, seasonal vegetables and fruits; avoid wilted ones (vitamin C already lost).
2. Store green leafy vegetables in a cool place or refrigerator; use them early.
3. Store cereals and pulses in clean, dry, airtight containers away from moisture and pests.

**During pre-preparation**

4. **Wash vegetables before cutting**, not after — washing cut pieces washes away water-soluble vitamins.
5. **Cut vegetables into large pieces** just before cooking — small pieces expose more surface to air and water.
6. **Peel thinly** or cook with peel where possible (many nutrients lie just under the skin).
7. **Don't wash rice and dal too many times** or rub them hard — thiamine and other B vitamins in the outer layers are lost.
8. **Don't soak cut vegetables** in water for long.

**During cooking**

9. Use the **minimum water**; cook **covered**; use pressure cooking or steaming.
10. **Don't throw away cooking water** — rice water (kanji) and vegetable water can be used in soups, dal and gravies. Cook rice by the absorption method instead of draining.
11. Add vegetables to **boiling water**, not cold, to shorten cooking time.
12. **Don't overcook**; cook till just tender.
13. **Don't add baking soda** (alkali) to pulses or vegetables to keep colour or speed cooking — it destroys thiamine and vitamin C.
14. **Avoid reheating** food many times; prepare just before serving.
15. **Don't reuse frying oil** repeatedly.
16. Add **lemon juice** after cooking (vitamin C is destroyed by heat).
17. Use **iron vessels** occasionally to increase the iron content of food (not for acidic foods stored long).

**Methods that improve nutritive value**

- **Germination (sprouting)** of pulses: increases vitamin C and B vitamins, improves digestibility and iron absorption, reduces flatulence-causing sugars and phytates.
- **Fermentation** (idli, dosa, dhokla, curd, bread): increases B vitamins, improves digestibility and mineral absorption, reduces phytates.
- **Parboiling of rice**: soaking and steaming paddy before milling drives B vitamins (thiamine) from the husk into the grain, so less is lost in milling — this protects against beriberi.
- **Combining foods**: cereal + pulse (protein quality), iron foods + vitamin C foods.
- **Fortification and enrichment** of foods (iodised salt, double-fortified salt, fortified rice, oil and milk with vitamins A and D).

## 7.4 Safe food handling and storage of foods

### General principles of food storage

- Store **raw and cooked food separately**; raw meat and fish below cooked food in the refrigerator so drips do not contaminate.
- Keep food **covered** and away from flies, rats, cockroaches and dust.
- **Cold foods cold, hot foods hot.** The **temperature danger zone** is about **5–60 °C**, where bacteria multiply fast. Do not keep cooked food at room temperature for more than about two hours.
- **First in, first out** — use older stock first; check expiry dates.
- Keep dry foods (cereals, pulses, flour, sugar, spices) in **clean, dry, airtight** containers on raised shelves away from walls and floor.
- Store chemicals, insecticides and cleaning agents **away from food** and in labelled containers.
- Don't overfill the refrigerator; clean it regularly; check its temperature.

### Storage of particular foods

| Food | How to store |
| --- | --- |
| **Milk** | Boil raw milk (or use pasteurised packets); cool quickly; keep covered in the refrigerator; use within a day or two; don't leave at room temperature; clean, scalded vessels |
| **Meat, poultry, fish** | Buy fresh from clean shops; refrigerate at once and use within a day, or freeze; thaw in the refrigerator, not on the counter; keep raw meat in sealed containers on the lowest shelf; separate chopping boards and knives |
| **Eggs** | Clean, uncracked; refrigerate; wash just before use; don't eat raw eggs |
| **Fruits and vegetables** | Cool, airy place; leafy vegetables refrigerated in a perforated bag; ripe fruits used early |
| **Cereals, pulses, flour** | Dry, airtight containers; check for weevils and fungus; sun-drying; neem leaves are a traditional pest deterrent |
| **Oils and fats** | Closed containers, away from light and heat to slow rancidity |
| **Cooked food / leftovers** | Cool quickly, refrigerate in shallow covered containers; reheat thoroughly till steaming hot once only; discard if in doubt |

## 7.5 Food preservation

**Definition**: methods of treating and handling food to stop or slow spoilage (by microbes, enzymes, insects, chemical changes) while keeping its quality and nutritive value, so food can be kept longer and transported.

**Principles**: (1) kill or remove micro-organisms; (2) prevent their growth by removing water, warmth, oxygen or by changing pH; (3) inactivate enzymes; (4) prevent contamination.

| Method | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **High temperature** | **Pasteurisation** of milk (holder method about 63 °C for 30 minutes, or HTST about 72 °C for 15 seconds, then rapid cooling) — kills disease-causing germs; **sterilisation** and **UHT** milk; **canning** (heating in sealed cans); boiling |
| **Low temperature** | **Refrigeration** (below about 5 °C) slows bacterial growth; **freezing** (about −18 °C) stops growth; cold storage of fruits and vegetables |
| **Removal of water (drying, dehydration)** | Sun-drying of grains, chillies, papad, fish; dehydrated vegetables; milk powder; freeze-drying |
| **High sugar concentration** | Jams, jellies, murabba, sweets — sugar draws water out of microbes |
| **Salt** | Salted fish, pickles — high salt prevents microbial growth |
| **Acid (pickling)** | Vinegar, lemon, fermentation (lactic acid) in pickles and sauerkraut |
| **Oil** | Covering pickles with oil keeps air out |
| **Smoking** | Smoked fish and meat |
| **Chemical preservatives** (permitted, within limits) | Sodium benzoate, potassium metabisulphite (squashes, sauces), vinegar, salt, sugar, spices |
| **Irradiation** | Controlled radiation to kill insects and microbes and delay sprouting (onion, potato, spices) |
| **Modern packaging** | Vacuum packing, modified-atmosphere packing, aseptic (tetra) packs |

## 7.6 Food additives

**Definition**: substances **added intentionally** to food in small amounts to improve its keeping quality, appearance, taste, texture or nutritive value. They must be **permitted** by FSSAI and used within the set limits.

| Type | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preservatives | Prevent spoilage | Sodium benzoate, sulphites, salt, sugar, vinegar |
| Antioxidants | Prevent rancidity of fats | BHA, BHT, vitamin E |
| Colours | Make food attractive | Natural: turmeric, saffron, caramel; permitted synthetic colours in limited foods |
| Flavours and flavour enhancers | Taste and smell | Vanilla, essences, monosodium glutamate |
| Sweeteners | Sweet taste with fewer calories | Saccharin, aspartame, sucralose (permitted foods only) |
| Emulsifiers and stabilisers | Mix oil and water, keep texture | Lecithin, gums, pectin |
| Thickeners | Body to food | Starch, gelatin, agar |
| Nutrients (fortificants) | Raise nutritive value | Iodine in salt, iron in salt and rice, vitamins A and D in oil and milk |
| Leavening agents | Make dough rise | Baking powder, yeast |

Problems: allergy or sensitivity in some people; harm when **non-permitted** colours or excess amounts are used.

## 7.7 Food adulteration

**Definition**: making food impure by adding a cheaper or harmful substance, or removing a valuable part, or using damaged/decayed food, so that the quality is lowered. An **adulterant** is the substance used. Adulteration is done for profit; it cheats the consumer and can damage health.

**Types**: intentional (adding water to milk, stones to grains, colour to spices), incidental (pesticide residues, insect matter, rodent hair), and metallic contamination (lead from pipes or containers, arsenic).

**Common adulterants and simple home tests**

| Food | Adulterant | Simple test / effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Milk | Water; starch; detergent; urea | Lactometer reading low (water); a few drops of iodine turn it **blue** (starch) |
| Turmeric, dal (arhar) | **Metanil yellow** (non-permitted dye) | A few drops of hydrochloric acid turn it **pink/magenta**; can damage nerves and is linked with cancer |
| Black pepper | **Dried papaya seeds** | Papaya seeds **float** in alcohol/spirit; pepper sinks |
| Chilli powder | **Brick powder**, sawdust, artificial colour | Brick powder **sinks** quickly and feels gritty when stirred in water |
| Mustard seeds/oil | **Argemone seeds/oil** | Argemone seeds are rough and black; argemone oil causes **epidemic dropsy** (swelling of legs, heart failure, glaucoma) |
| Arhar (tur) dal | **Khesari dal** (*Lathyrus sativus*) | Khesari is wedge-shaped; eating it in large amounts for long causes **lathyrism** (paralysis of the legs) |
| Sugar, salt | Chalk powder | Chalk settles at the bottom when dissolved in water |
| Tea leaves | Used, dried and coloured tea leaves | Colour runs on wet filter paper |
| Ghee, butter | Vanaspati, animal fat, mashed potato | Laboratory tests; starch test with iodine |
| Green vegetables, peas | Malachite green colour | Colour comes off on wet cotton |
| Food grains | Stones, mud, husk, insects | Visual inspection |
| Coffee powder | Chicory, tamarind seed powder | Chicory sinks and colours water quickly |

**Health hazards**: food poisoning, stomach upsets, epidemic dropsy, lathyrism, cancer from non-permitted colours, kidney and liver damage, nutrient loss (watered milk for children).

## 7.8 Prevention of Food Adulteration Act and food laws

- **Prevention of Food Adulteration (PFA) Act, 1954** — the earlier central law against adulteration. It defined adulterated food, laid down standards for foods, set up a **Central Food Laboratory** and public analysts, allowed **food inspectors** to take samples, and provided penalties (fine and imprisonment). It was **repealed and replaced by the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006**, which came into force with its rules and regulations in **2011**.
- **Food Safety and Standards Act (FSSA), 2006** — a single integrated food law that brought together the PFA Act and several other food orders (Fruit Products Order, Meat Food Products Order, Vegetable Oil Products Order, Milk and Milk Product Order, etc.).
  - Set up the **Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI)** under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.
  - **FSSAI** lays down science-based food standards, licenses and registers food businesses (the FSSAI licence/registration number on food packets), regulates manufacture, storage, sale and import, sets limits for additives and contaminants, notifies food safety officers and food analysts, accredits laboratories, and runs consumer campaigns (e.g. **"Eat Right India"**, fortified food "+F" logo).
  - Penalties for unsafe, sub-standard or misbranded food.
- **Other relevant measures**: Legal Metrology (packaged commodities) rules for labelling of weight and price; **Consumer Protection Act** for consumer complaints; Essential Commodities Act; state food safety departments.

**Role of the nurse / consumer**: buy from licensed sellers; check the FSSAI number, date of manufacture and expiry, and ingredients on labels; report suspected adulteration to the food safety officer; teach simple tests.

## 7.9 Food standards

Food standards fix the **minimum quality, composition and safety** of foods and the limits for additives and contaminants. Quality certification marks in India:

| Mark | For | Under |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FSSAI licence/registration number** | All packaged and sold foods | Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 |
| **AGMARK** | Agricultural produce — ghee, honey, spices, pulses, edible oils, cereals | Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act, Directorate of Marketing and Inspection |
| **ISI mark (BIS)** | Processed products and food equipment — packaged drinking water, milk powder, infant foods, food colours, pressure cookers | Bureau of Indian Standards |
| **"+F" logo** | Fortified foods (rice, wheat flour, oil, milk, salt) | FSSAI |
| **Green dot / brown dot** | Vegetarian / non-vegetarian food | FSSAI labelling rules |
| **Jaivik Bharat / organic logo** | Organic foods | FSSAI |
| **Codex Alimentarius** | International food standards (FAO/WHO) | Used as a reference for Indian standards and trade |

## 7.10 Food safety

**Definition**: the assurance that food will not cause harm to the consumer when it is prepared and eaten according to its intended use. It covers handling, preparation and storage of food to prevent food-borne illness.

**Food safety considerations**: biological hazards (bacteria, viruses, parasites, moulds and their toxins such as aflatoxin in mouldy groundnuts and maize), chemical hazards (pesticide residues, non-permitted additives, adulterants, heavy metals, cleaning agents), physical hazards (stones, glass, hair, metal pieces).

**Food safety measures**: clean source of food and water; hygienic premises and equipment; personal hygiene of food handlers; correct cooking and storage temperatures; pest control; safe waste disposal; regular inspection; food laws and their enforcement; consumer education; HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) systems in large kitchens and industry.

**Food safety regulatory measures in India**: Food Safety and Standards Act 2006 and FSSAI; state Commissioners of Food Safety, designated officers and food safety officers; FSSAI-notified laboratories; licensing and registration of food businesses; labelling rules; Eat Right India and "Safe and Nutritious Food" initiatives; hospital kitchens must follow hygiene standards.

### WHO Five keys to safer food

| Key | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Keep clean** | Wash hands before handling food and often during preparation, and after using the toilet; wash and sanitise surfaces and equipment; protect kitchen and food from insects, pests and animals |
| **2. Separate raw and cooked** | Keep raw meat, poultry and seafood apart from other foods; separate knives and cutting boards for raw foods; store in covered containers |
| **3. Cook thoroughly** | Cook food well, especially meat, poultry, eggs and seafood; bring soups and stews to boiling; reheat cooked food thoroughly |
| **4. Keep food at safe temperatures** | Don't leave cooked food at room temperature for more than 2 hours; refrigerate promptly (preferably below 5 °C); keep cooked food piping hot (above 60 °C) before serving; don't store food too long even in the refrigerator; don't thaw frozen food at room temperature |
| **5. Use safe water and raw materials** | Use safe (boiled or treated) water; choose fresh, wholesome foods; wash fruits and vegetables, especially if eaten raw; don't use food beyond its expiry date |

### Role of food handlers in food-borne diseases

Food handlers (cooks, servers, vendors, hostel and hospital kitchen staff) can spread disease through:

- **Dirty hands** and nails after using the toilet (typhoid, cholera, hepatitis A and E, amoebiasis, worm infestations).
- **Carriers** who look healthy but pass germs (typhoid carriers).
- **Skin infections**, cuts and boils on the hands (*Staphylococcus aureus* food poisoning).
- **Coughing and sneezing** over food.
- Poor practices: tasting with fingers, using the same board for raw and cooked food, keeping food warm for hours, using unclean water.

**Measures**: pre-employment and periodic **medical examination** of food handlers (including stool examination for carriers where required); exclusion of ill handlers with diarrhoea, vomiting, fever, jaundice or skin infections until fit; **hand washing** with soap; short clean nails; clean aprons, head covers, gloves when needed; no smoking, spitting or chewing in the kitchen; covering cuts with waterproof dressing; **training in food hygiene** (FSSAI food handler training); supervision.

### Essential steps in safe cooking practices

1. Wash hands with soap and water before and during food preparation.
2. Clean and sanitise utensils, surfaces and chopping boards; use separate boards for raw meat.
3. Use safe water for drinking and cooking.
4. Wash vegetables and fruits under running water before cutting.
5. Cook food thoroughly — meat until juices run clear, eggs until firm; boil milk.
6. Serve food hot and soon after cooking; cover food.
7. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate, reheat thoroughly once only.
8. Do not use food that smells bad, is mouldy, or comes from bulged or rusted cans.
9. Keep the kitchen clean, dry and free from pests; dispose of waste in covered bins.

## 7.11 Nursing application

- Teach families cooking methods that save nutrients — pressure cooking, using cooking water, no baking soda, washing before cutting.
- Promote germination and fermentation to improve low-cost diets.
- Supervise hospital diet hygiene; ensure food handlers are fit and trained.
- Teach consumers to read labels and look for the FSSAI number and "+F" logo.
- Investigate and report suspected food poisoning outbreaks (details in Community Health Nursing).

## Remember

- Pressure cooking and steaming save the most nutrients; boiling and draining lose water-soluble vitamins.
- Wash before cutting, cut big pieces just before cooking, cook covered in minimum water, don't add soda, don't discard cooking water.
- Germination raises vitamin C; fermentation raises B vitamins; parboiling saves thiamine.
- Danger zone about 5–60 °C; don't keep cooked food at room temperature over 2 hours.
- Pasteurisation: holder about 63 °C/30 min; HTST about 72 °C/15 s.
- Metanil yellow → pink with HCl; papaya seeds float in spirit; argemone oil → epidemic dropsy; khesari dal → lathyrism; starch in milk → blue with iodine.
- PFA Act 1954 replaced by Food Safety and Standards Act 2006 (in force 2011); FSSAI sets standards.
- AGMARK for agricultural produce; ISI/BIS for processed products; "+F" for fortified foods.
- WHO five keys: keep clean; separate raw and cooked; cook thoroughly; safe temperatures; safe water and raw materials.
