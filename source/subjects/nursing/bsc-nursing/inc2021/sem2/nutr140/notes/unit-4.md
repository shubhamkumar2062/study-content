# Unit 4: Balanced diet and nutrition across the life cycle

INC Unit VII (7 hours theory + 8 hours lab) — the largest unit of the course. Two parts: **balanced diet** (definition, principles, steps, food guides, RDA, food exchange system, calculating nutritive value, dietary fibre) and **nutrition across the life cycle** (meal planning, IYCF, diets for children, adolescents, elderly, pregnancy, anaemia in pregnancy and lactation). The lab sessions — preparing balanced diets and low-cost nutritious dishes — are based on this unit.

## 4.1 Balanced diet

**Definition.** A balanced diet contains a **variety of foods in such quantities and proportions** that the need for energy, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and other nutrients is fully met, with a **small margin of extra nutrients** to cover short periods of low intake.

### Principles of a balanced diet

1. **Adequacy** — meets the RDA/EAR of the person for energy and all nutrients.
2. **Balance** — right proportion among nutrients (e.g. not all energy from cereals; protein, fat and carbohydrate in reasonable shares).
3. **Variety** — foods from every food group every day; different vegetables and fruits through the week.
4. **Moderation** — limit salt, sugar, fried foods, refined foods and alcohol.
5. **Calorie control** — energy in = energy out for healthy weight.
6. **Nutrient density** — choose foods that give many nutrients per calorie (vegetables, pulses, milk) over "empty calories" (sugar, soft drinks).
7. **Individualisation** — suits age, sex, activity, physiological state, health, budget, culture, religion, likes and local availability.
8. **Safety** — clean, safe food and water.

### Steps in planning a balanced diet

1. Find the person's **age, sex, body weight, activity level and physiological state** (pregnant, lactating, ill).
2. Look up the **requirements** (ICMR-NIN 2020 RDA/EAR) for energy, protein and key nutrients.
3. Decide the **number of portions** from each food group (cereals, pulses, milk, vegetables, fruits, fats and sugar) using the food guide or exchange lists.
4. **Distribute** the foods across meals — breakfast, mid-morning, lunch, evening, dinner (and bedtime milk if needed).
5. **Select dishes** suited to the family's habits, budget and season.
6. **Calculate** the nutritive value of the planned day's diet and compare with the requirement; adjust.
7. Check variety, colour, texture, taste and cost.

## 4.2 Food guides — Basic Four Food Groups

A **food guide** groups foods with similar nutrients so a person can choose a balanced diet without calculations.

| Group | Foods | Main nutrients | Daily advice (simple) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Cereals, millets and their products** (with roots and tubers) | Rice, wheat, ragi, jowar, bajra, maize, oats, potato | Energy, carbohydrate, some protein, B vitamins, fibre (whole grains) | Main part of every meal; prefer whole grains and millets |
| **2. Protein foods** — pulses, legumes, milk, egg, meat, fish, nuts | Dal, rajma, chana, soy, milk, curd, paneer, egg, chicken, fish, groundnut | Protein, calcium (milk), iron, B12 (animal foods) | Pulses or animal foods at two meals; milk or curd daily |
| **3. Vegetables and fruits** | Green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, roots, seasonal fruits | Vitamins (A, C, folate), minerals, fibre | Several servings daily including a green leafy vegetable and a fruit |
| **4. Fats, oils and sugar** | Oils, ghee, butter, sugar, jaggery | Energy, essential fatty acids (oils) | In small amounts only |

The newer ICMR-NIN guidance ("My Plate for the Day") makes the same point with a plate: about half the plate as vegetables and fruits, the rest shared by cereals and millets, pulses/meat/egg, milk or curd, and a small amount of nuts and seeds, with limited oil, salt and sugar. Check the current ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians for the exact daily amounts.

       + a bowl of curd or glass of milk

## 4.3 RDA — definition, uses and limitations

**Definition.** The **Recommended Dietary Allowance** is the average daily intake of a nutrient that is sufficient to meet the requirement of nearly all (about 97–98%) healthy individuals of a particular age, sex and physiological group. ICMR-NIN 2020 also gives the **EAR** (meets the need of half the group) and **tolerable upper limits**.

**Uses of RDA**

- Planning diets for individuals, families and institutions (hostels, hospitals, prisons, the army).
- Planning food supply, food production and national nutrition policy.
- Planning supplementary feeding programmes (ICDS, PM POSHAN).
- Evaluating diet surveys — judging whether a population eats enough (ICMR-NIN 2020 advises using the EAR for this).
- Food labelling and fortification standards.
- Nutrition education.

**Limitations of RDA**

- Meant for **healthy** people — not for the sick, who need therapeutic diets.
- Based on **reference body weights** (65 kg man, 55 kg woman); must be adjusted for actual weight and activity.
- **Not an individual's exact requirement** — the RDA is above most people's need, so eating less than the RDA does not mean deficiency.
- Do not cover all nutrients; values change as research advances (2010 and 2020 values differ).
- Do not account for nutrient losses in cooking and differences in bioavailability in every food.
- Does not consider food habits, cost or taste.

### ICMR-NIN 2020 energy and protein for children and adolescents (RDA)

| Age group | Reference weight (kg) | Energy (kcal/day) | Protein (g/day) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1–3 years | 12.9 | 1110 | 12.5 |
| 4–6 years | 18.3 | 1360 | 15.9 |
| 7–9 years | 25.3 | 1700 | 23.3 |
| Boys 10–12 years | 34.9 | 2220 | 31.8 |
| Girls 10–12 years | 36.4 | 2060 | 32.8 |
| Boys 13–15 years | 50.5 | 2860 | 44.9 |
| Girls 13–15 years | 49.6 | 2400 | 43.2 |
| Boys 16–18 years | 64.4 | 3320 | 55.4 |
| Girls 16–18 years | 55.7 | 2500 | 46.2 |

(The 2020 summary table lists 1070 kcal for 1–3 years while the detailed table gives 1110; learn "about 1100 kcal".) Adult values are in Unit 2.

## 4.4 Food exchange system

**Definition.** Foods are grouped into lists so that one portion ("exchange") of any food in a list gives **about the same energy, carbohydrate, protein and fat** as any other in that list. Foods within a list can then be swapped without upsetting the diet plan.

**Uses**: diabetic and weight-reduction diets, renal diets, giving variety and choice to patients, simple counting of calories without tables.

Approximate exchange values commonly used in Indian dietary guidance (use the hospital's own list in practice):

| Exchange list | One portion | Approx. energy | Approx. protein |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cereals and millets | 30 g raw (e.g. 1 medium roti or ½ cup cooked rice) | 100 kcal | 3 g |
| Pulses and legumes | 30 g raw (1 small bowl dal) | 100 kcal | 6 g |
| Milk (toned) | 100 ml | 70 kcal | 3 g |
| Vegetables | 100 g | about 30 kcal | small |
| Fruits | 100 g | about 40 kcal | small |
| Fats and oils | 5 g (1 teaspoon) | 45 kcal | 0 |
| Sugar | 5 g (1 teaspoon) | 20 kcal | 0 |

Example swaps: 1 roti ↔ ½ cup cooked rice ↔ about 2 small idlis; 1 bowl dal ↔ 1 egg ↔ about 30 g paneer (roughly similar protein).

## 4.5 Calculation of the nutritive value of foods

**Tool**: the **Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT)** published by ICMR-NIN give nutrients per 100 g edible portion.

**Steps**

1. List every food eaten in the day and its **raw weight** (edible portion).
2. Find the nutrient content **per 100 g** of each food from the food composition table.
3. Calculate: **nutrient in the food = (nutrient per 100 g × weight eaten) ÷ 100**.
4. Add the values for all foods to get the day's total.
5. Compare with the RDA/EAR; work out the percentage met; adjust the diet.

**Worked example (using the exchange values above):**

| Food (raw) | Amount | Energy (kcal) | Protein (g) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cereals | 240 g = 8 exchanges | 800 | 24 |
| Pulses | 60 g = 2 exchanges | 200 | 12 |
| Milk (toned) | 400 ml | 280 | 12 |
| Vegetables | 300 g | 90 | — |
| Fruit | 100 g | 40 | — |
| Oil | 20 g | 180 | 0 |
| Sugar | 15 g | 60 | 0 |
| **Total** | | **1650** | **48** |

This meets the energy (1660 kcal) and protein (45.7 g) RDA of a sedentary woman. Vegetable and fruit protein is small and left out for simplicity.

## 4.6 Dietary fibre

**Definition.** The parts of plant foods that human digestive enzymes cannot digest (non-starch polysaccharides and lignin).

| Insoluble fibre | Soluble fibre |
| --- | --- |
| Cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin | Pectin, gums, mucilage, beta-glucan |
| Whole wheat, bran, whole grains, vegetables, skins of fruits | Fruits (apple, guava), oats, barley, pulses, fenugreek seeds, isabgol |
| Adds bulk, speeds passage through the gut, prevents constipation | Forms a gel; slows glucose absorption; binds bile acids and lowers cholesterol |

**Benefits**: prevents constipation, piles and diverticular disease; improves blood glucose control; lowers cholesterol; gives fullness and helps weight control; feeds healthy gut bacteria; linked with lower risk of colon cancer.

**Excess**: gas and bloating, reduced absorption of iron, zinc and calcium (phytates). Fibre should be increased slowly with plenty of water. (ICMR-NIN gives a fibre recommendation linked to energy intake — check the current guideline for the figure.)

## 4.7 Meal planning (menu planning)

**Definition.** Planning the meals for a day or a week for a person or family so that nutritional needs are met in an acceptable, affordable and practical way.

**Principles**: meet nutritional needs; suit the family's budget, habits and culture; use seasonal and local foods; give variety in colour, texture, flavour and cooking method; save time, fuel and effort; reduce nutrient loss; consider the needs of special members (child, pregnant woman, elderly, patient); spread food across the day.

**Steps**: (1) find family members' needs; (2) decide the meal pattern (number of meals); (3) choose the main dish and then the side dishes from each food group; (4) plan the market list and budget; (5) check the plan against the food guide; (6) evaluate acceptance and adjust.

**Low-cost nutritious ideas** (lab): khichdi with vegetables, sprouted moong or chana chaat, ragi porridge or ragi roti, vegetable upma or poha with peanuts, dal-palak, besan chilla, sattu drink, groundnut-jaggery laddoo, mixed vegetable paratha, curd rice with vegetables. Germination, fermentation and combining cereals with pulses improve value at little cost.

## 4.8 Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF)

India's national IYCF guidance (MoHFW/MWCD, in line with WHO and UNICEF) recommends:

1. **Early initiation of breastfeeding within one hour of birth** — give colostrum; no prelacteal feeds (honey, sugar water, ghutti).
2. **Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months** — no water, other milk, or foods (prescribed medicines and ORS allowed).
3. **Timely complementary feeding from six months** (completed 180 days), adequate in amount, frequency, consistency and variety, while continuing breastfeeding.
4. **Continued breastfeeding till two years of age or beyond.**
5. **Responsive feeding** and **safe, hygienic** preparation.
6. **Feeding during and after illness** — continue breastfeeding and food during illness; give extra food after recovery.
7. Special support for low birth weight babies and for feeding in difficult situations.

### Breastfeeding

**Advantages for the baby**: perfect nutrition, easily digested; colostrum rich in antibodies and vitamin A ("first immunisation"); protects against diarrhoea, pneumonia and ear infections; lower risk of obesity and diabetes later; better brain development; bonding. **For the mother**: helps the uterus contract, reduces postpartum bleeding, helps spacing of births (lactational amenorrhoea), lowers the risk of breast and ovarian cancer, saves money. **For the family and nation**: no cost, always available, safe and at the right temperature.

**Nurse's role**: help the mother start within an hour; teach correct **position and attachment** (baby's chin touching the breast, mouth wide open, lower lip turned out, more areola visible above than below); feed on demand, 8 or more times in 24 hours; signs of enough milk (6 or more wet nappies a day, steady weight gain); express and store milk for working mothers; manage sore nipples and engorgement; discourage bottles.

### Infant foods and complementary feeding (weaning)

**Complementary feeding** means giving other foods along with breast milk from six months, when breast milk alone cannot meet the baby's needs for energy, iron and other nutrients.

**Principles**: start at six months; start with small amounts of soft, mashed food and increase slowly; one new food at a time; thick consistency (food should stay on the spoon), not watery; energy-dense — add a little oil or ghee; feed from a clean bowl and spoon, not a bottle; respond to hunger cues; continue breastfeeding.

**Suggested progression (as in national IYCF guidance)**

| Age | Texture and examples | Frequency (besides breast milk) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6–8 months | Mashed, semi-solid: thick dal-rice, mashed khichdi, suji or ragi porridge with milk, mashed banana, potato | 2–3 meals a day |
| 9–11 months | Finely chopped / mashed family foods, finger foods: soft idli, chopped vegetables, egg | 3–4 meals + snacks |
| 12–24 months | Family foods, cut small | 3–4 meals + 1–2 snacks |

Home-made infant foods can be made cheaply from roasted cereal + pulse flour mixtures (e.g. wheat, ragi or rice with roasted chana or moong) with jaggery and a little oil. ICDS provides take-home ration for children 6 months to 3 years.

## 4.9 Diets for children, adolescents and the elderly

### Pre-school and school children (1–12 years)

- Rapid growth and high activity — high needs for energy, protein, calcium, iron and vitamin A relative to body size; small stomachs → **small frequent meals** (3 meals + 2 snacks).
- Include milk (calcium), pulses or egg (protein), green leafy vegetables and fruits (iron, vitamins A and C).
- Avoid junk food, sugary drinks and too much tea; establish breakfast; hand washing before meals; regular deworming as per programme.
- School children: packed lunch with cereal-pulse dish plus vegetable; PM POSHAN hot cooked meal.
- Problems: under-nutrition, anaemia, dental caries, and increasingly obesity.

### Adolescents (10–19 years)

- **Growth spurt** — energy and protein needs reach their highest (see table); **calcium** for peak bone mass; **iron** — girls lose iron in menstruation, boys gain muscle and blood volume.
- Adolescent girls need good nutrition to prepare for safe motherhood (anaemia in a girl → anaemia in pregnancy → low birth weight baby — the intergenerational cycle).
- Problems: skipping breakfast, fast food, dieting to lose weight, eating disorders, anaemia, obesity.
- Advice: three meals and healthy snacks; milk and milk products; pulses, egg, meat; green leafy vegetables and fruits; limit fried foods and sugary drinks; weekly iron-folic acid under WIFS/AMB (Unit 8).

### Elderly (60 years and above)

- **Energy need falls** (lower BMR and activity), but **protein, calcium, vitamin D, B12 and fibre needs stay the same or rise**.
- Problems: poor teeth or dentures, less taste and smell, less saliva, poor appetite, constipation, less thirst (dehydration), loneliness and depression, chronic diseases and many medicines, low income.
- Diet plan: **soft, easily chewed, easily digested** food; small frequent meals; enough protein (milk, curd, dal, egg, paneer); calcium and vitamin D (milk, ragi, sunlight); fibre and **plenty of fluids** (constipation); less salt, sugar and fat; avoid heavy fried meals at night.

## 4.10 Diet in pregnancy

### Why needs increase

Growth of the foetus, placenta, uterus and breasts; increase in the mother's blood volume and red cells; fat store for lactation; higher BMR.

### Nutritional requirements (ICMR-NIN 2020)

- **Energy**: **+ 350 kcal/day** over her normal need (second and third trimesters), adjusted for weight gain and activity.
- **Protein**: **+ 9.5 g/day in the 2nd trimester, + 22 g/day in the 3rd trimester** (RDA).
- Higher needs for **iron, folic acid, calcium, iodine, vitamin B12, vitamin A, vitamin C and zinc** (see the full ICMR-NIN 2020 report for the exact pregnancy values).

### Balanced diet plan for a pregnant woman (sample day, household measures)

| Time | Food |
| --- | --- |
| Early morning | 1 glass milk; a few soaked almonds or roasted chana |
| Breakfast | 2 vegetable-stuffed parathas or 2–3 idlis with sambar; 1 boiled egg (if eaten) |
| Mid-morning | 1 seasonal fruit (guava, orange or banana) |
| Lunch | 2–3 rotis or rice, 1 bowl dal, 1 bowl green leafy vegetable (palak, methi), salad, 1 bowl curd |
| Evening | Sprouted moong chaat or ragi laddoo; buttermilk or lemon water |
| Dinner | Rice or roti, dal or rajma or fish/chicken, mixed vegetable, salad |
| Bedtime | 1 glass milk |

**Key advice**: eat one extra small meal a day; include milk and curd, pulses, green leafy vegetables and fruit daily; use **iodised salt**; take the iron-folic acid tablets and calcium given at the antenatal clinic as advised; avoid alcohol and tobacco; limit tea and coffee; avoid unpasteurised milk and undercooked meat; small frequent meals for nausea and heartburn; fibre and water for constipation; no food taboos that cut out nutritious foods. Gain weight steadily as advised by the antenatal team.

## 4.11 Anaemia in pregnancy

**Diagnosis**: WHO defines anaemia in pregnancy as **haemoglobin below 11 g/dL**. Severity: mild 10–10.9, moderate 7–9.9, severe below 7 g/dL. Signs: pallor of conjunctiva, tongue, palms and nails; tiredness; breathlessness; palpitations; swelling of feet; spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia) in long-standing iron deficiency. Hb is checked at the first antenatal visit and repeated during pregnancy.

**Causes**: low iron, folate and B12 intake; increased need; repeated pregnancies with short gaps; worm infestation; malaria; blood loss; poor absorption (tea with meals).

**Risks**: preterm birth, low birth weight, maternal fatigue, heart failure in severe anaemia, poor tolerance of bleeding at delivery and higher maternal death.

**Diet for an anaemic pregnant woman**: iron-rich foods at every meal — green leafy vegetables (amaranth, drumstick leaves, bathua, mint), garden cress seeds (halim), ragi, bajra, pulses and sprouts, jaggery, dates, dried fruits; meat, liver, fish, egg if eaten; **a vitamin C source with each meal** (amla, guava, lemon, orange, tomato); cook in iron vessels; **avoid tea and coffee with meals**; folate-rich foods; protein-rich foods.

**Iron and folic acid supplementation**: under the **Anemia Mukt Bharat** programme, every pregnant woman receives prophylactic iron-folic acid tablets daily from the second trimester for about 180 days and also after delivery, and is treated for anaemia when detected, as per the national protocol. Deworming is given once in the second trimester as per protocol. (Doses are set by the national guideline — follow it; do not memorise from unofficial sources.)

**Counselling points for IFA**: take the tablet after food or at bedtime to reduce nausea; take it with water or lemon water, **not with tea, coffee or milk**; keep calcium tablets at a different time; stools may turn black — this is harmless; mild constipation can be eased with fibre and water; complete the full course even if feeling well; keep tablets away from children (iron overdose is dangerous for children); report severe side effects.

## 4.12 Nutrition in lactation

**Requirements (ICMR-NIN 2020)**: energy **+ 600 kcal/day (0–6 months)** and **+ 520 kcal/day (7–12 months)**; protein **+ 16.9 g/day (0–6 months)** and **+ 13.2 g/day (6–12 months)** over her normal need. Needs for calcium, iron (to replace stores), vitamin A, B vitamins, vitamin C, iodine and **fluids** are also higher.

**Diet for a lactating mother**: one extra meal plus an extra snack a day; **plenty of fluids** — water, milk, buttermilk, soups, dal water (drink a glass of water or milk at each feed); milk and milk products; pulses, egg, fish, meat; green leafy vegetables and fruits; traditional galactagogue foods (methi, garlic, dill, sesame, dry fruit laddoos) are acceptable if balanced; avoid alcohol and smoking; continue iron-folic acid and calcium as advised after delivery; avoid restricting foods because of taboos (e.g. not giving vegetables or water after delivery).

**Complementary feeding / weaning** is covered under IYCF (4.8): start at 6 months, continue breastfeeding to two years or beyond.

## 4.13 Nursing application

- Plan diets with the family's own foods and budget; use household measures (katori, glass, teaspoon) rather than grams.
- Teach "eat a rainbow": something from every food group every day.
- Weigh children monthly (growth chart) and pregnant women at every visit.
- Promote breastfeeding from the first hour; support working mothers.
- Counsel on IFA tablets and iron-rich diet; check Hb as per schedule.
- Elderly: check teeth, appetite, fluid intake and loneliness.

## Remember

- Balanced diet = variety of foods meeting all nutrient needs with a small safety margin.
- Basic four food groups: cereals; protein foods; vegetables and fruits; fats, oils, sugar.
- RDA covers nearly all healthy people; meant for healthy groups, not exact individual need; EAR for assessing groups.
- Food exchange = foods in a list interchangeable for energy and macronutrients; used in diabetic diets.
- Nutritive value = (nutrient per 100 g × amount) ÷ 100, from IFCT.
- Soluble fibre lowers glucose and cholesterol; insoluble prevents constipation.
- IYCF: breastfeed within 1 hour, exclusive 6 months, complementary from 6 months, continue 2 years and beyond.
- Pregnancy: +350 kcal; protein +9.5 g (2nd) and +22 g (3rd trimester); anaemia = Hb below 11 g/dL.
- Lactation: +600 kcal (0–6 months), +520 kcal (7–12 months); protein +16.9 g then +13.2 g; plenty of fluids.
- Elderly: fewer calories, same protein, soft food, more fluid and fibre.
