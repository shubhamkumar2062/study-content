# Unit 6: Therapeutic diets

INC Unit IX (4 hours theory + 7 hours lab): definition, objectives and principles of therapeutic diets; modifications of consistency and nutrients; feeding techniques; diets in obesity, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, underweight, renal disease, liver disorders, constipation, diarrhoea and the pre- and post-operative period. The diets below are **principles and sample meal plans**; in practice the diet is ordered by the doctor and planned with the dietitian, and it is adjusted to the patient's weight, laboratory results and medicines.

## 6.1 Definition and objectives

**Therapeutic diet**: a modification of the normal balanced diet — in consistency, nutrients, energy, or the number and timing of meals — planned to meet the needs of a patient with a particular disease.

**Objectives**

1. To **maintain or restore good nutritional status**.
2. To **correct deficiencies** (e.g. iron in anaemia, protein in hypoalbuminaemia).
3. To **rest an organ** or reduce its work (e.g. low protein in kidney failure, low fat in gall bladder disease, liquid diet after surgery).
4. To **adjust to the body's ability** to digest, absorb or use nutrients (e.g. diabetes, malabsorption).
5. To **change body weight** (reduce in obesity, increase in underweight).
6. To **prevent complications** and recurrence (e.g. heart disease, kidney stones).
7. To **support medical and surgical treatment** and speed recovery.

## 6.2 Principles of therapeutic diets

- Base it on the **normal balanced diet**; change only what the disease requires.
- **Individualise** — age, sex, weight, activity, stage of disease, laboratory values, medicines, food habits, religion, budget.
- Keep it **nutritionally adequate**; avoid unnecessary restrictions.
- Make it **acceptable and palatable** — familiar foods, attractive serving, right temperature.
- Plan **meal timing** with medicines (e.g. insulin and meals).
- **Educate** the patient and family so they can continue at home.
- **Monitor** — weight, intake-output chart, blood sugar, electrolytes, albumin — and revise the diet.
- Work as a team: doctor, dietitian, nurse, patient and family.

## 6.3 Modifications

### A. Modification of consistency

| Diet | Description | Examples | Used in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clear liquid** | Transparent fluids, leave little residue; low in nutrients — used for a short time only | Water, strained fruit juice without pulp, clear soup, rice water, coconut water, glucose water, ORS, black tea | First stage after surgery, before some investigations, acute vomiting/diarrhoea |
| **Full liquid** | All foods liquid or liquefy at body temperature | Milk, milkshakes, lassi, buttermilk, strained dal, cream soups, custard, ice cream, porridge (thin) | Step after clear liquids; difficulty chewing or swallowing; fever |
| **Soft diet** | Soft, easily chewed and digested, low fibre, mildly seasoned | Khichdi, curd rice, idli, upma, mashed potato, boiled egg, dal, stewed fruit, soft chapati soaked in dal | After liquids in post-operative period, GI disorders, fever, elderly with poor teeth |
| **Bland diet** | Mechanically, chemically and thermally non-irritating — no strong spices, fried food, coffee, alcohol | Milk, curd, rice, soft vegetables, banana | Peptic ulcer (traditional), gastritis, after GI surgery |
| **Normal (regular) diet** | Full balanced diet | Usual family meals | When no modification is needed |

Other texture modifications: **mechanical soft / minced / pureed** diets for dysphagia (e.g. after stroke) with thickened liquids, as advised by the speech/swallow assessment.

### B. Modification of nutrients

- **Energy**: high-calorie (underweight, burns, cancer, fever) or low-calorie (obesity).
- **Protein**: high-protein (burns, wounds, nephrotic syndrome, post-operative, PEM) or restricted protein (chronic kidney disease not on dialysis; short-term in hepatic encephalopathy as ordered).
- **Carbohydrate**: controlled carbohydrate (diabetes).
- **Fat**: low fat (gall bladder disease, pancreatitis, obesity, heart disease); modified fat — less saturated and trans fat (cardiovascular disease).
- **Fibre**: high fibre (constipation, diabetes) or low residue (diarrhoea, before bowel surgery, acute inflammatory bowel disease).
- **Minerals**: low sodium (hypertension, heart failure, ascites, kidney disease); low potassium and phosphorus (kidney failure); high iron (anaemia); high calcium (osteoporosis).
- **Fluid**: restricted (kidney failure, heart failure); increased (fever, diarrhoea, kidney stones, urinary infection).

### C. Modification of meal pattern

Small frequent meals (6 a day) in diabetes, liver disease, after surgery, nausea, pregnancy; regular timing with insulin; a late evening snack in cirrhosis.

## 6.4 Feeding techniques

```text
               ROUTES OF FEEDING
 Gut works?
   ├── YES → can the patient eat enough by mouth?
   │          ├── YES → ORAL feeding (normal/modified diet, supplements)
   │          └── NO  → ENTERAL tube feeding
   │                     • short term: nasogastric / nasojejunal tube
   │                     • long term: gastrostomy (e.g. PEG), jejunostomy
   └── NO  → PARENTERAL nutrition (into a vein)
              • peripheral (short term, partial)
              • central / total parenteral nutrition (TPN)
 "If the gut works, use it."
```

### Oral feeding — nurse's actions

1. Prepare the patient: offer the bedpan/urinal, hand washing, mouth care, comfortable upright position.
2. Clear the over-bed table; remove unpleasant sights and smells.
3. Check the diet tray matches the ordered diet and the patient's identity.
4. Feed dependent patients slowly, small mouthfuls, check swallowing; let them see the food; encourage self-feeding as able.
5. Record the amount eaten (intake chart) and report poor intake.

### Nasogastric (Ryle's tube) feeding — steps with rationale

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| Check the doctor's order, the feed type, amount and time; identify the patient; explain | Correct feed to the correct patient; consent and cooperation |
| Wash hands, wear gloves | Infection control |
| Sit the patient upright (Fowler's, at least 30–45°) | Reduces the risk of regurgitation and aspiration |
| **Check the tube position before every feed** — measure the external length mark, aspirate stomach contents and test the pH as per hospital policy (X-ray confirms initial placement) | Feeding into the lungs is dangerous; this is the most important safety step |
| Check the residual volume as per policy and report large residuals | Shows delayed stomach emptying; risk of vomiting and aspiration |
| Flush the tube with water before the feed | Ensures patency |
| Give the feed slowly by gravity (barrel of syringe held a short distance above the patient) or by pump at the ordered rate; feed at room temperature | Rapid feeding causes cramps, diarrhoea, vomiting; cold feeds cause discomfort |
| Flush with water after the feed and after medicines; clamp or cap the tube | Prevents blockage; gives extra water |
| Keep the patient upright for 30–60 minutes after the feed | Prevents reflux and aspiration |
| Give oral and nasal care; fix the tube securely, change tape | Prevents dry mouth, infection and nasal pressure injury |
| Record type and amount of feed and water; maintain intake-output; watch for diarrhoea, vomiting, distension, coughing during feeds | Monitoring tolerance and complications |

**Kinds of tube feeds**: blenderised kitchen feeds (milk, dal water, strained soups, fruit juice, egg, sugar, oil — strained so they pass the tube) or commercial formula feeds; given in bolus (intermittent) or continuous mode.

**Parenteral nutrition**: sterile solution of glucose, amino acids, lipid, electrolytes, vitamins and trace elements given into a vein when the gut cannot be used. Nursing care: strict asepsis of the line, infusion at the ordered rate by pump, monitoring blood glucose, electrolytes, weight and temperature, watching for line infection.

## 6.5 Diet in obesity

**Assessment**: BMI (Asian cut-offs used in India: normal 18.5–22.9, overweight 23–24.9, obese 25 and above kg/m²); waist circumference (risk at 90 cm or more in men, 80 cm or more in women in Indian guidance).

**Principles**

- **Low-calorie, balanced diet**: reduce intake by about 500 kcal a day below the requirement for a slow loss of about half a kilogram a week; avoid crash diets.
- **Adequate protein** (pulses, skimmed milk, curd, egg white, fish, chicken without skin) to preserve muscle and give fullness.
- **High fibre** — whole grains, millets, salads, vegetables, whole fruits.
- **Low fat** — limit oil, ghee, fried foods, bakery items, full-cream milk; boil, steam, grill, roast instead of frying.
- **Restrict sugar** and sugary drinks, sweets, alcohol.
- Small regular meals; no skipping breakfast; eat slowly; plenty of water.
- **Physical activity** — at least 30 minutes of brisk walking most days; behaviour change and family support.

**Sample day (about 1200–1400 kcal for an adult woman)**

| Meal | Foods |
| --- | --- |
| Early morning | Warm water or tea without sugar |
| Breakfast | Vegetable oats or moong dal chilla, 1 glass toned/skimmed milk without sugar |
| Mid-morning | 1 fruit (guava, apple, orange) |
| Lunch | 2 small multigrain rotis, 1 bowl dal, 1 bowl vegetable cooked in little oil, large salad, buttermilk |
| Evening | Roasted chana or sprouts, green tea |
| Dinner | 1–2 rotis or small bowl rice, vegetable, clear soup, salad; finish 2–3 hours before sleep |

**Avoid**: fried snacks, sweets, sugary drinks, fruit juices, bakery foods, extra ghee, alcohol, large portions of rice.

## 6.6 Diet in diabetes mellitus

**Objectives**: keep blood glucose as near normal as possible, avoid hypo- and hyperglycaemia, reach a healthy weight, control blood lipids and blood pressure, prevent complications.

**Principles**

- **Energy** to reach and keep a healthy weight (reduced in obese, adequate in thin and young diabetics).
- **Carbohydrate**: complex carbohydrates from whole grains, millets (ragi, jowar, bajra), pulses, vegetables; **low glycaemic index** foods; **avoid sugar**, jaggery, honey, sweets, sugary drinks and fruit juices; distribute carbohydrate evenly through the day.
- **High fibre** (vegetables, salads, whole grains, methi seeds, pulses) — slows glucose rise.
- **Protein** adequate (pulses, low-fat milk, curd, egg, fish, chicken).
- **Fat**: low saturated and trans fat; mixed vegetable oils in small amounts.
- **Regular meal timing** — 3 meals and 2–3 snacks; never skip meals especially if on insulin or tablets that lower sugar; match meals with medicine timing.
- **Food exchange lists** for variety.
- Fruits: whole fruit (guava, apple, orange, papaya, jamun) in measured portions instead of juice; limit mango, banana, chikoo, grapes as advised.
- Limit salt; avoid alcohol.
- Carry glucose or sugar for hypoglycaemia.

**Glycaemic index (GI)**: how fast a food raises blood glucose compared with glucose. Low GI: whole pulses, most vegetables, milk, barley, oats; high GI: glucose, white bread, polished rice, potato, cornflakes.

**Sample day**

| Meal | Foods |
| --- | --- |
| Early morning | Tea or milk without sugar; soaked methi seeds (if advised) |
| Breakfast | 2 ragi or wheat rotis/ 2 idlis with sambar, or vegetable dalia; 1 boiled egg |
| Mid-morning | 1 guava or small apple |
| Lunch | 2 multigrain rotis, small bowl brown/parboiled rice, dal, green vegetable, salad, curd |
| Evening | Sprouts chaat or roasted chana, tea without sugar |
| Dinner | 2 rotis, dal or fish/chicken curry, vegetable, salad |
| Bedtime | 1 cup toned milk (if on insulin, as advised) |

**Nurse's role**: teach meal timing with insulin/tablets, recognising and treating hypoglycaemia, foot care, exercise, reading food labels, sick-day eating.

## 6.7 Diet in cardiovascular disease (CVD)

Includes coronary heart disease, hypertension, heart failure and high cholesterol.

**Principles**

- **Calories** to reach a healthy weight.
- **Fat**: reduce **saturated fat** (ghee, butter, coconut oil, red meat, full-cream milk) and avoid **trans fat** (vanaspati, bakery and fried snacks); limit cholesterol (egg yolk, organ meats); use mixed vegetable oils in small amounts (MUFA and PUFA); fish twice a week if eaten (omega-3).
- **Salt restriction**: less than 5 g of salt a day (WHO); stricter in heart failure as ordered; avoid pickles, papad, chips, salted snacks, sauces, processed foods; no added salt at the table.
- **High fibre**: oats, whole grains, pulses, vegetables, fruits (soluble fibre lowers LDL cholesterol).
- **Potassium-rich** fruits and vegetables help lower blood pressure (unless kidney function is poor).
- The **DASH-type diet** (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension): rich in vegetables, fruits, low-fat dairy, whole grains, pulses and nuts; low in salt, saturated fat and sugar.
- **Small, light meals**; avoid heavy meals that strain the heart.
- **Fluid restriction** in heart failure as ordered; daily weight.
- Stop smoking; limit alcohol; regular exercise as advised.

**Sample day (low salt, low fat)**: morning oats porridge with toned milk; mid-morning fruit; lunch rotis, dal, vegetable cooked in a teaspoon of oil, salad with lemon instead of salt, curd; evening roasted chana and buttermilk without salt; dinner vegetable khichdi with a bowl of vegetable soup.

## 6.8 Diet for the underweight

**Causes**: inadequate intake, poverty, chronic illness (tuberculosis, cancer, HIV), hyperthyroidism, stress, eating disorders, worms.

**Principles**

- **High calorie** (about 500 kcal a day more than the requirement for steady gain) and **high protein**.
- **Small, frequent, energy-dense meals** (5–6 a day) so the volume is small.
- Add milk powder, cream, ghee, oil, nuts, groundnuts, jaggery, banana, paneer, egg to normal dishes.
- Include cereal-pulse combinations, milk and milk products, egg and meat.
- Avoid filling up on water, tea or low-calorie soups just before meals.
- Treat the underlying cause; light exercise to build muscle.

**Sample additions**: banana milkshake with nuts; peanut chikki; paneer paratha; egg bhurji; sattu drink; dry fruit laddoo.

## 6.9 Diet in renal diseases

### Acute and chronic kidney disease (CKD)

**Principles**

- **Protein**: restricted in CKD **not on dialysis** to reduce the load of urea; mostly **high biological value** protein (egg white, milk, fish, chicken, paneer) so the small amount is well used. **Patients on dialysis need more protein** because dialysis removes amino acids. The exact amount is ordered by the nephrologist and dietitian.
- **Energy**: adequate, from carbohydrates and fats, so that protein is not burnt for energy.
- **Sodium**: restricted to control oedema, blood pressure and thirst.
- **Potassium**: restricted when blood potassium is high — limit banana, orange, coconut water, potato, tomato, dried fruits, dals; **leaching** vegetables (peel, cut small, soak in warm water, boil in plenty of water and discard the water) reduces potassium.
- **Phosphorus**: restricted — limit milk products, colas, processed foods, nuts.
- **Fluid**: restricted when urine output is low — usually the previous day's urine output plus about 500 ml for insensible loss, as ordered. Count all liquids (milk, dal, soups, ice).
- **Daily weight**, intake-output chart.

### Nephrotic syndrome

Adequate protein (the patient loses protein in urine), salt restriction for oedema, low saturated fat (high cholesterol), adequate energy.

### Kidney stones

Plenty of fluids (enough to pass large volumes of urine as advised); for calcium oxalate stones limit high-oxalate foods (spinach, amaranth, chocolate, tea, nuts in excess) and salt, without cutting calcium-rich foods unless advised; limit meat for uric acid stones.

**Sample day in CKD (pre-dialysis, as ordered)**: breakfast — poha or suji upma with leached vegetables, tea (counted in fluids); lunch — rice or rotis, a small portion of dal or egg white, leached vegetable (bottle gourd, ridge gourd, cabbage), salad of cucumber; evening — puffed rice or biscuits (low salt); dinner — rice/roti, small portion paneer or fish, leached vegetable. Low-potassium fruits (apple, pear, guava) in small amounts as allowed.

## 6.10 Diet in liver (hepatic) disorders

### Hepatitis (infective)

- **High carbohydrate** (glucose drinks, rice, rotis, fruits) for energy and to spare the liver.
- **Adequate protein** for liver cell repair (milk, curd, dal, egg) unless encephalopathy develops.
- **Moderate fat**, easily digested; avoid fried and fatty foods if they cause nausea.
- Small frequent meals; plenty of fluids; **no alcohol**.
- Safe water and food hygiene (hepatitis A and E spread by contaminated food and water).

### Cirrhosis

- **Adequate energy and protein** — malnutrition is common and worsens outcome; small frequent meals and a **late evening snack** (e.g. milk with biscuits or a roti) to avoid overnight fasting.
- **Sodium restriction** when ascites and oedema are present; fluid restriction only if ordered.
- **Protein in hepatic encephalopathy**: current guidance is **not to restrict protein for long**; any short-term change is made only on medical order, with vegetable and milk protein often better tolerated.
- Soft foods if there are oesophageal varices (avoid hard, sharp foods).
- **No alcohol**; vitamin supplements as prescribed.

## 6.11 Diet in constipation

- **High-fibre diet**: whole grains (whole wheat atta with bran, daliya, oats, millets), pulses, vegetables, salads, fruits with skin (guava, papaya, pear), dried fruits (figs, raisins, prunes), isabgol as advised.
- **Plenty of fluids** — fibre needs water to work.
- **Regular meals**, especially breakfast (triggers the gastro-colic reflex), and a regular toilet time; do not ignore the urge.
- **Physical activity** — walking.
- Limit refined foods (maida, polished rice), excess tea, and constipating foods.
- Review drugs that cause constipation (iron, some pain relievers) with the doctor.

## 6.12 Diet in diarrhoea

**Principles**

1. **Prevent and correct dehydration** — **ORS** is the first line; home fluids (rice water, dal water, buttermilk, coconut water, lemon water with a pinch of salt and sugar) for prevention.
2. **Continue feeding** — **continue breastfeeding**; give the usual age-appropriate food in small frequent amounts; do not starve the patient.
3. **Easily digested, low-fibre foods** — khichdi, curd rice, rice with dal water, banana, idli, boiled potato, toast, curd.
4. **Avoid**: fatty and fried foods, spicy foods, very sweet drinks and fruit juices (sugar can worsen diarrhoea), raw vegetables, excess fibre; milk may be reduced for a short time if it worsens diarrhoea (lactose intolerance), while curd is usually well tolerated.
5. **Zinc** for children with diarrhoea as per the national protocol (with ORS).
6. **Extra meal a day for two weeks after recovery** in children (catch-up).
7. Safe water and hand washing.

## 6.13 Diet in the pre- and post-operative period

### Pre-operative

- **Correct malnutrition** before planned surgery — high calorie, high protein diet; correct anaemia; control blood glucose in diabetics.
- **Fasting before anaesthesia** as per the anaesthetist's and hospital protocol to prevent aspiration — many modern protocols allow solid food up to about 6 hours and clear fluids up to about 2 hours before surgery; follow the order given.
- **Low-residue diet** and bowel preparation before bowel surgery, as ordered.
- Carbohydrate-containing clear drinks before surgery are used in some enhanced recovery protocols.

### Post-operative

- **Early feeding** when the patient is awake, bowel sounds are present (or as per surgeon's order), and there is no vomiting — early oral feeding speeds recovery.
- **Progression**: sips of water → **clear liquids** → **full liquids** → **soft diet** → **normal diet**, as tolerated (faster in minor surgery).
- **High protein, high calorie** to heal the wound and replace losses; **vitamin C** (collagen), **zinc** and **vitamin A** for wound healing; iron if blood was lost.
- **Fluids** to replace losses; maintain intake-output chart.
- **Fibre and fluids** to prevent constipation once on solids.
- Tube feeding or parenteral nutrition when oral intake is not possible for a longer time.
- Watch for nausea, distension, vomiting, diarrhoea.

## 6.14 Nurse's role in therapeutic diets

- Check the **diet order** and that the correct tray reaches the correct patient; know what the diet allows.
- **Explain the reason** for the diet to the patient and family; this improves acceptance.
- **Assist and feed**; observe and **record intake**; report refused meals.
- **Monitor** weight, intake-output, blood sugar, signs of fluid overload or dehydration, bowel habits.
- **Prevent** aspiration in tube feeding; maintain hygiene in feed preparation.
- **Coordinate** with the dietitian; **discharge teaching** with written sample menus using home foods.

## Remember

- Therapeutic diet = modified normal diet: consistency, nutrients, energy, meal pattern.
- Consistency order: clear liquid → full liquid → soft → normal.
- "If the gut works, use it": oral → enteral (NG, gastrostomy) → parenteral.
- NG feeding: upright, check tube position every time, feed slowly, flush, keep upright 30–60 min after.
- Obesity: about 500 kcal/day deficit, high fibre, low fat, exercise; Indian BMI obese ≥ 25.
- Diabetes: complex low-GI carbohydrate, high fibre, no sugar, regular meals matched with medicines.
- CVD: low saturated and trans fat, salt below 5 g, high fibre, DASH pattern.
- CKD: protein restricted before dialysis, more protein on dialysis; restrict Na, K, P and fluid (urine output + about 500 ml).
- Cirrhosis: adequate protein, late evening snack, low salt with ascites, no alcohol.
- Diarrhoea: ORS, continue feeding and breastfeeding, zinc for children, extra meal after recovery.
- Post-operative: early feeding; clear → full → soft → normal; high protein, vitamin C, zinc.
