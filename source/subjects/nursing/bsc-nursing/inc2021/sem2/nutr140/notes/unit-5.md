# Unit 5: Nutritional deficiency disorders

INC Unit VIII (6 hours): protein energy malnutrition (PEM) including severe acute malnutrition (SAM), childhood obesity, vitamin A, B, C and D deficiency disorders, and iron, iodine and calcium deficiencies — for each the causes, signs and symptoms, management, prevention and the nurse's role. This unit is a favourite for essay questions; learn one complete answer for PEM and one for vitamin A deficiency.

## 5.1 Protein energy malnutrition (PEM)

**Definition.** A range of conditions that arise from a lack of protein and energy in varying proportions, most common in children under five, and usually worsened by infections. WHO now prefers the term **undernutrition** and measures it as stunting, wasting and underweight.

### Magnitude of the problem

- Undernutrition is a major underlying cause of child deaths in India and a cause of poor growth and learning.
- **NFHS-5 (2019–21), children under five**: **35.5% stunted** (low height-for-age), **19.3% wasted** (low weight-for-height), **32.1% underweight** (low weight-for-age); **67.1%** of children aged 6–59 months were anaemic. Check the latest National Family Health Survey fact sheet for newer figures.
- It is highest in poor, rural and tribal families, in low birth weight babies, and in the 6–24 month age group when complementary feeding begins.

### Causes

| Group | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| **Immediate** | Inadequate food intake (amount, frequency, quality); repeated **infections** — diarrhoea, measles, respiratory infections, worms |
| **Underlying** | Household food insecurity; poor IYCF practices — late initiation of breastfeeding, early or late complementary feeding, watery diluted feeds, bottle feeding; poor maternal nutrition and low birth weight; poor water, sanitation and hygiene; poor access to health care |
| **Basic** | Poverty, illiteracy (especially of the mother), large family, closely spaced births, low status of women, food taboos, social inequality, disasters |

### Classification

**1. Clinical (Wellcome Trust classification)** — uses weight-for-age as % of the standard and the presence of oedema:

| Weight-for-age (% of standard) | Oedema present | Oedema absent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60–80% | **Kwashiorkor** | Underweight |
| Below 60% | **Marasmic kwashiorkor** | **Marasmus** |

**2. Indian Academy of Paediatrics (IAP) classification** (weight-for-age, % of standard): normal above 80%; grade I 71–80%; grade II 61–70%; grade III 51–60%; grade IV 50% or less. A letter "K" is added if oedema is present.

**3. WHO classification (current, used in programmes)** — compares the child with the WHO Child Growth Standards using Z-scores (standard deviation units):

| Indicator | What it shows | Moderate | Severe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Weight-for-height (wasting)** | Acute malnutrition | −3 to < −2 SD | < −3 SD |
| **Height-for-age (stunting)** | Chronic malnutrition | −3 to < −2 SD | < −3 SD |
| **Weight-for-age (underweight)** | Composite | −3 to < −2 SD | < −3 SD |

### Marasmus and kwashiorkor compared

| Feature | Marasmus | Kwashiorkor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main deficiency | Energy (and protein) — "starvation" | Mainly protein, often with infection; energy relatively less short |
| Age | Usually under 1 year | Usually 1–3 years (after weaning) |
| Oedema | **Absent** | **Present** (pitting, feet and legs, may be general) |
| Weight | Very low (below 60%) | 60–80% (oedema hides the loss) |
| Muscle wasting | Severe — "skin and bones", old-man face | Present but masked by oedema |
| Subcutaneous fat | Absent | Present (reduced) |
| Face | Wrinkled, monkey-like | Moon face |
| Mental state | Alert, hungry | Apathetic, irritable, miserable |
| Appetite | Good | Poor |
| Hair | Usually normal or thin | Thin, sparse, easily pluckable, lighter colour ("flag sign" of alternating bands) |
| Skin | Dry, loose folds | **Flaky paint dermatosis**, ulcers |
| Liver | Not enlarged | **Enlarged (fatty liver)** |
| Serum albumin | Normal or slightly low | Very low |
| Prognosis | Better with feeding | Higher risk of death |

### Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)

In children aged **6–59 months**, SAM is diagnosed by **any one** of:

- **Weight-for-height (or length) below −3 SD** of the WHO standards, or
- **Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 11.5 cm**, or
- **Bilateral pitting oedema** of nutritional origin.

**Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM)**: weight-for-height −3 to < −2 SD, or MUAC 11.5 to < 12.5 cm.

**MUAC tape colours** used by health workers: red (below 11.5 cm) = SAM; yellow (11.5–12.5 cm) = MAM; green (12.5 cm and above) = normal.

```text
SCREENING A CHILD 6–59 MONTHS
 Bilateral pitting oedema? ──yes──► SAM
        │ no
 MUAC < 11.5 cm or WFH < −3 SD? ──yes──► SAM
        │ no
 MUAC 11.5–12.4 cm or WFH −3 to −2 SD? ──yes──► MAM
        │ no
 Normal → continue growth monitoring
 SAM + medical complication or failed appetite test → admit to NRC
 SAM, no complication, good appetite → community-based care
```

**Signs and symptoms of PEM** (general): growth failure and weight loss; muscle wasting; loss of fat under skin; oedema (kwashiorkor); hair and skin changes; apathy or irritability; anaemia; signs of vitamin deficiencies (Bitot's spots, angular stomatitis); diarrhoea and infections; hypothermia and hypoglycaemia in severe cases.

### Management of SAM

**Where**: children with SAM and **medical complications** (or no appetite, or oedema of all limbs) are admitted to a **Nutrition Rehabilitation Centre (NRC)** or hospital; uncomplicated cases with good appetite can be managed in the community (community-based management of acute malnutrition) as per state protocol.

**Facility management follows the WHO "ten steps"**, in two phases — **stabilisation** (first few days) and **rehabilitation**:

1. Treat or prevent **hypoglycaemia** — feed early and frequently, day and night.
2. Treat or prevent **hypothermia** — keep warm (kangaroo care, covered head, warm room), feed often.
3. Treat or prevent **dehydration** — with the special low-sodium rehydration solution for malnourished children (ReSoMal), given slowly; avoid fast IV fluids, which can cause heart failure.
4. Correct **electrolyte imbalance** — these children have low potassium and magnesium and excess body sodium.
5. Treat or prevent **infection** — antibiotics as per protocol, since signs of infection are often hidden.
6. Correct **micronutrient deficiencies** — vitamin A, folic acid, zinc, copper; **iron is started only in the rehabilitation phase**, once the child is gaining weight.
7. **Start cautious feeding** — small frequent feeds of starter therapeutic milk (F-75) in the stabilisation phase.
8. Achieve **catch-up growth** — catch-up formula (F-100) or ready-to-use therapeutic food in rehabilitation; aim for rapid weight gain.
9. Provide **sensory stimulation and emotional support** — play, loving care.
10. Prepare for **follow-up** after recovery — teach the mother to feed with home foods, immunise, follow up at the NRC/anganwadi.

Discharge and follow-up criteria are given in the national/state SAM guidelines.

### Prevention of PEM

- **Maternal nutrition** and care in pregnancy to prevent low birth weight.
- **Breastfeeding** within one hour, exclusive for 6 months, continued to 2 years; timely, adequate complementary feeding.
- **Growth monitoring** every month (Mother and Child Protection card) to catch growth faltering early.
- **Supplementary nutrition** through ICDS anganwadis (take-home ration, hot cooked meals), PM POSHAN in schools.
- **Immunisation** (measles), **deworming**, ORS and zinc for diarrhoea, early treatment of infections.
- Safe water, sanitation, hand washing.
- Family planning and birth spacing; women's education and empowerment.
- Nutrition education using low-cost local foods.

### Nurse's role in PEM

- **Assessment**: weigh and measure, plot on the growth chart, measure MUAC, check for bilateral pitting oedema (press both feet for 3 seconds), look for danger signs and vitamin deficiencies.
- **Referral**: SAM with complications to NRC; follow-up of discharged children.
- **Care in NRC/ward**: feeding by the clock including at night, keeping warm, monitoring weight daily, temperature, pulse, respiration, signs of over-hydration; infection control; play and stimulation; recording intake.
- **Education**: teach the mother preparation of energy-dense home foods, feeding frequency, hygiene, continued feeding in illness.
- **Community**: work with ASHA and anganwadi worker in screening, home visits and counselling.

## 5.2 Childhood obesity

**Definition**: excess body fat that harms health. In children it is judged with **WHO growth standards**: for under-five children, weight-for-height above +2 SD = overweight and above +3 SD = obese; for 5–19 years, **BMI-for-age above +1 SD = overweight and above +2 SD = obesity**.

**Causes**: energy-dense junk food and sugary drinks; large portions; low physical activity, long screen time; poor sleep; family history and parental obesity; using food as a reward; less breastfeeding; rarely, hormonal (hypothyroidism) or genetic causes or drugs.

**Signs and symptoms**: excess weight, fat around the abdomen, stretch marks, dark velvety skin in neck folds (acanthosis nigricans — a sign of insulin resistance), breathlessness on exertion, snoring and sleep apnoea, knee pain, early puberty in girls, low self-esteem and bullying.

**Assessment**: weight, height, BMI-for-age plotted on growth charts, waist circumference, diet and activity history, blood pressure, and blood sugar and lipids as advised.

**Complications**: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol, fatty liver, orthopaedic problems, polycystic ovary syndrome, depression; obesity in childhood tends to continue into adult life.

**Management and prevention**: involve the whole family; no crash diets — aim to slow weight gain while the child grows; regular meals with breakfast; more vegetables, fruits, whole grains and pulses; water and milk instead of sugary drinks; fewer fried and packaged snacks; at least an hour of active play a day; limit screen time; enough sleep; healthy school canteens; positive praise, not shaming.

**Nurse's role**: screening in schools and clinics, plotting BMI-for-age, counselling the family, school health education, encouraging sports, referral for complications.

## 5.3 Vitamin A deficiency (VAD)

**Causes**: low intake of vitamin A and carotene-rich foods; poor fat intake; malabsorption; repeated infections (diarrhoea, measles) that use up stores; PEM; worm infestation; lack of breastfeeding.

**Signs — WHO classification of xerophthalmia**:

| Code | Sign |
| --- | --- |
| XN | **Night blindness** — earliest symptom; child cannot see in dim light |
| X1A | Conjunctival xerosis — dry, wrinkled, dull conjunctiva |
| X1B | **Bitot's spots** — foamy, cheesy, triangular white spots on the conjunctiva, usually lateral |
| X2 | Corneal xerosis — dry, hazy cornea |
| X3A | Corneal ulceration/keratomalacia covering less than one third of the cornea |
| X3B | Keratomalacia covering one third or more — the cornea softens and melts; permanent blindness |
| XS | Corneal scar |
| XF | Xerophthalmic fundus — white spots on the retina |

Other signs: dry rough skin (follicular hyperkeratosis), poor growth, frequent infections and higher deaths from measles and diarrhoea.

**Management**: high-dose vitamin A given as per the national treatment schedule on diagnosis (doctor's order); treat infections and PEM; eye care with antibiotic eye drops as ordered; protect the eye; vitamin A-rich diet.

**Prevention**: (1) **Short term** — periodic vitamin A supplementation for children 9 months to 5 years under the national programme (Unit 8); (2) **Medium term** — food fortification (vitamin A-fortified oil and milk); (3) **Long term** — diet: green leafy vegetables, yellow-orange fruits and vegetables, milk, egg, liver, cooked with a little oil; breastfeeding (colostrum); measles immunisation; deworming; kitchen gardens.

**Nurse's role**: screen children's eyes for Bitot's spots and ask about night blindness; give vitamin A as per the programme schedule and record it; educate mothers on cheap carotene sources; refer corneal cases urgently.

## 5.4 Vitamin B deficiency disorders

| Disorder | Cause | Signs and symptoms | Management and prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Beriberi (B1, thiamine)** | Diet of highly polished rice; washing rice repeatedly; alcoholism | **Dry beriberi**: peripheral neuritis — tingling, numbness, calf tenderness, foot drop, difficulty getting up from squatting. **Wet beriberi**: oedema, breathlessness, palpitations, heart failure. **Infantile beriberi** in breastfed babies of deficient mothers. **Wernicke–Korsakoff** syndrome in alcoholics | Thiamine as prescribed; parboiled or unpolished rice, whole grains, pulses, nuts; don't wash rice too much or discard cooking water; reduce alcohol |
| **Riboflavin (B2) deficiency** (ariboflavinosis) | Low milk and green vegetable intake | **Angular stomatitis** (cracks at the mouth corners), **cheilosis**, **glossitis** (magenta, sore tongue), scaly skin around nose, eye watering and burning | B-complex as prescribed; milk, curd, green leafy vegetables, egg, pulses |
| **Pellagra (niacin)** | Maize- or jowar-based diet (jowar has excess leucine which interferes with niacin), alcoholism | **Dermatitis** (dark rough skin on sun-exposed parts — "Casal's necklace" on the neck), **diarrhoea**, **dementia** — and death if untreated (4 D's) | Niacin as prescribed; groundnut, pulses, meat, fish, milk; varied cereals |
| **Pyridoxine (B6) deficiency** | Long isoniazid treatment, alcoholism | Peripheral neuritis, convulsions in infants, anaemia, glossitis | Pyridoxine as prescribed alongside isoniazid as per protocol; varied diet |
| **Folate deficiency** | Low green vegetable intake, pregnancy, overcooking, some drugs, alcohol | **Megaloblastic anaemia**, glossitis; in pregnancy — **neural tube defects** in the baby | Folate-rich food; IFA supplementation as per programme; periconceptional folic acid as advised |
| **Vitamin B12 deficiency** | Strict vegetarian (vegan) diet, lack of intrinsic factor (pernicious anaemia), gastric surgery, tapeworm, elderly | **Megaloblastic anaemia**, sore tongue, numbness and tingling, loss of position sense, memory loss; nerve damage can be permanent | B12 as prescribed (injections when absorption fails); milk, curd, egg, meat, fish |

## 5.5 Vitamin C deficiency — scurvy

**Causes**: diets lacking fresh fruits and vegetables; overcooking and long storage; infants fed only boiled animal milk without fruit; elderly living alone; alcoholism.

**Signs and symptoms**: fatigue and weakness; **swollen, spongy, bleeding gums**; loose teeth; small bleeds around hair follicles (perifollicular haemorrhages) and bruising; **poor wound healing** and reopening of old wounds; joint and bone pain; anaemia. In infants (infantile scurvy): painful swollen legs, the baby lies with legs flexed and cries when touched ("pseudoparalysis"), bleeding gums once teeth erupt.

**Management**: vitamin C as prescribed; daily fresh fruits and vegetables — amla, guava, orange, lemon, tomato, sprouts; gentle handling of painful limbs; oral hygiene.

**Prevention**: fresh fruit daily; eat vegetables raw (salads) or lightly cooked; cut vegetables just before cooking; sprouting pulses; avoid soda in cooking.

## 5.6 Vitamin D deficiency — rickets and osteomalacia

**Causes**: little sunlight exposure (indoor life, covered clothing, pollution, dark skin, swaddled babies); low dietary vitamin D and calcium; malabsorption; liver or kidney disease; prolonged exclusive breastfeeding of babies of deficient mothers without sun exposure.

**Rickets (children)**: delayed closure of the anterior fontanelle, soft skull bones (craniotabes), **frontal bossing**; **rickety rosary** (beading at the rib–cartilage junctions); **Harrison's sulcus**; **pigeon chest**; widened wrists and ankles; **bow legs** or knock knees; delayed teething and walking; muscle weakness, pot belly; tetany in some.

**Osteomalacia (adults)**: bone pain and tenderness, muscle weakness, waddling gait, fractures; common in women with repeated pregnancies and long lactation who stay indoors.

**Management**: vitamin D and calcium as prescribed; sunlight exposure; calcium-rich diet; orthopaedic care for deformities.

**Prevention**: sunlight on arms and face daily; milk, egg, fish; fortified foods; supplements to at-risk groups as advised.

## 5.7 Iron deficiency anaemia

**Anaemia**: haemoglobin below the normal level for age, sex and physiological state (WHO cut-offs: children 6–59 months below 11 g/dL; non-pregnant women below 12 g/dL; pregnant women below 11 g/dL; men below 13 g/dL). It is the **most common nutritional deficiency in India** — NFHS-5 found about two-thirds of young children and more than half of women of reproductive age anaemic.

**Causes**: low intake of iron (mainly non-haem iron of poor absorption); inhibitors (tea, coffee, phytates); increased need (growth, adolescence, pregnancy); **blood loss** — heavy periods, **hookworm**, bleeding piles, frequent pregnancies, peptic ulcer; malaria; poor absorption.

**Signs and symptoms**: pallor (conjunctiva, tongue, palms, nails); tiredness and weakness; breathlessness and palpitations on exertion; headache, dizziness; poor concentration and school performance; reduced work output; **koilonychia** (spoon nails); angular stomatitis; pica (eating mud, chalk, ice); oedema and heart failure in severe anaemia.

**Diagnosis**: haemoglobin (digital haemoglobinometer used in Anemia Mukt Bharat "Test, Treat, Talk"), peripheral smear (microcytic hypochromic cells), serum ferritin.

**Management**: iron and folic acid treatment as per the national protocol (oral; injectable iron or blood transfusion for severe cases under medical order); treat the cause — deworming, malaria treatment, control of bleeding; iron-rich diet with vitamin C; avoid tea and coffee with meals.

**Prevention**: Anemia Mukt Bharat (prophylactic IFA to six beneficiary groups, deworming, behaviour change communication, testing and treatment, fortified foods — Unit 8); iron-rich diet; cooking in iron vessels; birth spacing; sanitation and footwear (hookworm).

## 5.8 Iodine deficiency disorders (IDD)

**Causes**: low iodine in soil and water (hilly, Himalayan and flood-prone areas, now known to be present all over India); not using iodised salt; goitrogens in large amounts.

**Spectrum of IDD (by life stage)**:

| Stage | Effects |
| --- | --- |
| Foetus | Abortions, stillbirths, congenital anomalies, **cretinism** (dwarfism, intellectual disability, deaf-mutism, squint), higher perinatal deaths |
| Newborn | Neonatal hypothyroidism, goitre |
| Child and adolescent | Goitre, poor school performance, retarded mental and physical growth |
| Adult | **Goitre** and its complications, hypothyroidism (lethargy, weight gain, cold intolerance), reduced work capacity |

**Management**: thyroid hormone or iodine as prescribed; surgery for large goitres causing pressure.

**Prevention**: **universal use of iodised salt** (National Iodine Deficiency Disorders Control Programme, Unit 8); store iodised salt in a closed container away from heat and moisture; add salt after cooking where possible; screening newborns for hypothyroidism where available.

## 5.9 Calcium deficiency

**Causes**: low intake of milk and other calcium foods; vitamin D deficiency; high phytate and oxalate intake; repeated pregnancies and lactation; menopause (falling oestrogen); old age; low parathyroid hormone; kidney disease; long steroid use.

**Signs and symptoms**: in children — rickets, poor growth, delayed teething; in adults — osteomalacia; **osteoporosis** in the elderly and post-menopausal women (back pain, loss of height, stooped back, fractures of hip, wrist and spine after minor falls); **tetany** with low blood calcium — tingling around the mouth and fingers, muscle cramps, carpopedal spasm, convulsions.

**Management**: calcium and vitamin D as prescribed; calcium-rich diet; weight-bearing exercise; fall prevention in the elderly.

**Prevention**: milk, curd, paneer, ragi, sesame, green leafy vegetables; sunlight; regular exercise; avoid smoking and excess alcohol; adequate calcium in adolescence to build peak bone mass.

## 5.10 Nurse's role in deficiency disorders — summary

1. **Identify** at-risk groups (under-fives, adolescent girls, pregnant and lactating women, elderly, poor families).
2. **Assess** — anthropometry, clinical signs, Hb, diet history (Unit 8).
3. **Treat and refer** as per protocol; give supplements and record them.
4. **Educate** on low-cost foods, cooking methods that save nutrients, hygiene and sanitation.
5. **Implement programmes** — ICDS, AMB, vitamin A supplementation, NIDDCP, deworming.
6. **Follow up** and evaluate (weight gain, Hb rise).

## Remember

- WHO: wasting = WFH < −2 SD (acute); stunting = HFA < −2 SD (chronic); underweight = WFA < −2 SD.
- SAM (6–59 months): WFH < −3 SD, MUAC < 11.5 cm, or bilateral pitting oedema. MAM: MUAC 11.5–12.5 cm.
- Kwashiorkor = oedema, flaky paint skin, fatty liver, apathy; marasmus = severe wasting, no oedema, alert, hungry.
- Ten steps for SAM: hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, dehydration, electrolytes, infection, micronutrients (iron later), cautious feeding (F-75), catch-up growth (F-100/RUTF), stimulation, follow-up.
- Night blindness = earliest VAD sign; Bitot's spots = X1B; keratomalacia = blindness.
- Beriberi (B1), angular stomatitis (B2), pellagra 4 D's (niacin), megaloblastic anaemia (folate, B12), scurvy (C), rickets/osteomalacia (D).
- Anaemia cut-offs: pregnant women and young children below 11 g/dL; non-pregnant women below 12; men below 13.
- IDD: goitre, cretinism; prevention by iodised salt.
- Childhood obesity: BMI-for-age above +2 SD (5–19 years).
