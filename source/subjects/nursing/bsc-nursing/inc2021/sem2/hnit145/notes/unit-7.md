# Unit 7: Information law and governance, healthcare quality and evidence-based practice

This notes file joins the last two INC units: **Unit IX, Information Law & Governance in Clinical Practice** (4 hours theory) and **Unit X, Healthcare Quality & Evidence Based Practice** (3 hours theory). The first part covers the ethical and legal issues of health information and of digital health in nursing, with the Indian laws and guidelines a nurse must know. The second explains how scientific evidence and informatics standards improve the quality of care.

Laws and rules change. The facts below were checked against official sources; always confirm the current position on government websites (MeitY, MoHFW, NMC, INC) before relying on them.

## Part A: Information law and governance

## 7.1 Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Privacy** | The person's right to control who collects and sees information about them |
| **Confidentiality** | The professional's duty not to disclose information shared in trust, except when allowed |
| **Security** | The technical, physical and administrative measures that protect data (passwords, encryption, locked rooms) |
| **Information governance** | The framework of policies, roles and controls that makes sure information is handled legally, securely, efficiently and ethically |
| **Data integrity** | Data are accurate and have not been changed without authority |
| **Availability** | Authorised people can get the data when they need it |

Security aims for **CIA**: Confidentiality, Integrity and Availability.

## 7.2 Ethical principles applied to health information

| Principle | Application to information |
| --- | --- |
| **Autonomy** | Patients decide who sees their data; informed consent for sharing and for telehealth |
| **Beneficence** | Use data to improve care (alerts, continuity) |
| **Non-maleficence** | Avoid harm from leaks, wrong data or wrong-patient entries |
| **Justice** | Fair access to digital services; avoid the digital divide and biased software |
| **Veracity** | Honest, accurate documentation; never alter records falsely |
| **Fidelity** | Keep promises of confidentiality |
| **Accountability** | Every entry is traceable to a user; nurses answer for what they record |

## 7.3 Ethical-legal issues in contemporary clinical practice

1. **Confidentiality breaches**: looking up records of neighbours, celebrities or colleagues without a care reason; discussing patients in lifts or on WhatsApp groups; leaving screens open.
2. **Unauthorised access and sharing of passwords**: makes it impossible to know who made an entry.
3. **Accuracy and alteration of records**: late entries must be marked as late; never delete or back-date; electronic systems keep an **audit trail**.
4. **Copy-paste documentation**: may record care that was not given, which is false documentation.
5. **Consent**: for treatment, for teleconsultation, for sharing records (ABDM consent), for photographs, for research use of data.
6. **Ownership and access**: the facility holds the record; the patient has a right to information and copies under hospital policy and the law.
7. **Retention and destruction**: records must be kept for the period required by law and policy, then destroyed securely. (The medical ethics regulations of 2002 required indoor records to be kept for 3 years; hospitals often keep them longer. Follow your hospital's policy.)
8. **Special categories**: HIV status (HIV and AIDS (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017) and mental health information (Mental Healthcare Act, 2017) carry strong confidentiality duties.
9. **Cyber-security incidents**: ransomware, phishing e-mails, lost pen drives; must be reported at once.
10. **Social media**: posting photos or details of patients, even without names, can identify them and is a breach.
11. **Electronic evidence**: electronic records can be produced as evidence in court (now under the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which replaced the Indian Evidence Act), so they must be complete and genuine.

## 7.4 Indian laws and guidelines

### Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended 2008)

The main cyber law of India. Provisions relevant to health care include:

| Section | Subject |
| --- | --- |
| 43 | Penalty and compensation for unauthorised access, damage to a computer or data |
| 43A | Compensation by a body corporate that is negligent in protecting **sensitive personal data** |
| 66 | Computer-related offences (dishonest or fraudulent acts under section 43) |
| 66C | Identity theft (misuse of passwords, electronic signatures) |
| 66E | Violation of privacy by capturing or publishing images of a person's private areas |
| 72 / 72A | Breach of confidentiality and privacy; disclosure of information in breach of a lawful contract |

The **IT (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011** listed physical, physiological and mental health condition and medical records and history as sensitive personal data. When the relevant provisions of the DPDP Act are fully in force, they take over this role; check the current position.

### Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023

The **DPDP Act, 2023** received Presidential assent on **11 August 2023**. The **DPDP Rules, 2025** were notified in **November 2025**, and the duties come into force in phases. It applies to **digital** personal data (including paper data later digitised).

| Term in the Act | Meaning | Health example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Data principal** | The person the data are about (for a child or a person with a disability, includes the parent or lawful guardian) | The patient |
| **Data fiduciary** | Decides why and how data are processed | The hospital |
| **Data processor** | Processes data for a fiduciary | A software or cloud company running the HMIS |
| **Consent manager** | A registered entity through which a person can give, manage and withdraw consent | |

**Main rules**

- Process personal data only for a **lawful purpose**, with **consent** or for certain **legitimate uses**.
- Consent must be **free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous**, given by a **clear affirmative action**, preceded by a **notice** explaining what data and why, and can be **withdrawn** as easily as it was given.
- Legitimate uses without consent include responding to a **medical emergency** involving a threat to life, and measures during epidemics or disasters.
- **Children** (under 18): verifiable consent of a parent or lawful guardian is needed.
- Fiduciaries must keep data **accurate**, use **reasonable security safeguards**, **erase** data when the purpose is over (unless law requires keeping it), and report a **personal data breach** to the **Data Protection Board of India** and to each affected person.
- **Rights of the data principal**: information about processing, correction and erasure, grievance redressal, and nominating someone to act in case of death or incapacity.
- **Duties of the data principal**: no false information, no impersonation, no frivolous complaints.
- **Penalties** are monetary and can reach **₹250 crore** (the highest level, for failing to take reasonable security safeguards to prevent a breach).

**For nurses**: collect only the data needed, tell the patient why, protect devices and passwords, share only for care or with consent, and report a suspected breach to the hospital immediately.

### Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, 2020

Issued on **25 March 2020** by the Board of Governors in supersession of the Medical Council of India (with NITI Aayog), now under the National Medical Commission. They apply to **registered medical practitioners (RMPs)** and describe how teleconsultation must be done:

- **Modes**: video, audio and text (chat, e-mail, messages), each with its strengths and limits.
- **Consultations** may be patient to RMP, caregiver to RMP, **health worker to RMP** (a nurse, ANM or other health worker may connect the patient and help with the examination; the RMP remains responsible for advice), and RMP to RMP.
- Both sides must **identify** themselves: the patient's name, age and contact details; the RMP's name and registration number.
- **Consent**: implied if the patient starts the teleconsultation; **explicit** consent needed if the health worker or RMP starts it.
- The RMP decides whether teleconsultation is enough or the patient must be seen in person; **emergencies** are referred for in-person care.
- Prescribing follows medicine lists in the guidelines; certain medicines (such as narcotic and psychotropic drugs listed as prohibited) cannot be prescribed through telemedicine.
- **Records**: keep consultation records, reports and prescriptions; protect privacy and confidentiality.

### Other relevant guidance

- **INC Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct** for nurses: confidentiality, respect, accountability.
- **EHR Standards for India (2016)**: privacy, security and audit requirements for record systems.
- **ABDM Health Data Management Policy**: consent-based sharing within ABDM.

## 7.5 Ethical-legal issues in digital health applied to nursing

| Issue | Nursing implication |
| --- | --- |
| **Telenursing** | Verify patient identity; document every call; know your scope; refer when you cannot assess safely |
| **Licensure and scope** | Work only within the scope of your registration (State Nursing Council) and your institution's policy |
| **Documentation** | Electronic notes are legal records; timely, accurate, signed with your own login |
| **Consent** | Explain telehealth, recording, and data sharing in language the patient understands |
| **Use of mobile phones** | No patient photos or data on personal phones or messaging apps unless the hospital approves a secure method |
| **Decision-support software** | The nurse stays accountable even when software suggests an action |
| **Equity** | Help patients who cannot use technology; do not exclude them |
| **Professional boundaries online** | Do not "friend" patients or give advice through personal social media |

## 7.6 Elements of information governance in a hospital

- Policies on access, passwords, e-mail, internet, mobile devices and social media.
- **Role-based access** (each user sees only what the job needs) and **audit trails**.
- Staff training and signed confidentiality agreements.
- Data-sharing agreements with other organisations.
- Backup, downtime and disaster recovery plans.
- Incident reporting and breach management.
- Records retention and secure destruction schedules.

## Part B: Healthcare quality and evidence-based practice

## 7.7 Quality of health care

**Quality** is the degree to which health services increase the likelihood of desired outcomes and are consistent with current professional knowledge.

**Dimensions of quality** (Institute of Medicine): **safe, effective, patient-centred, timely, efficient and equitable**.

**Donabedian's model** evaluates quality by **structure** (staff, equipment, IT systems) → **process** (what is done: hand hygiene, timely medicines) → **outcome** (infection rates, satisfaction). Informatics supplies the data for all three.

**PDSA cycle** for improvement: **Plan** a change, **Do** it on a small scale, **Study** the data, **Act** to adopt, adapt or abandon.

## 7.8 Evidence-based practice (EBP)

**EBP** is the integration of the **best research evidence** with **clinical expertise** and **patient values and preferences** to make decisions about care (after Sackett and colleagues).

```text
          Best research evidence
                   ▲
                   │
       Clinical ───┼─── Patient values
       expertise   │    and preferences
                   ▼
           Clinical decision
     (within the available resources and context)
```

**Steps (the 5 A's)**

1. **Ask** a focused clinical question (PICO).
2. **Acquire** the evidence: search databases (PubMed, CINAHL, Cochrane).
3. **Appraise** the evidence for validity, results and relevance.
4. **Apply** it, combined with expertise and patient preferences.
5. **Assess** (evaluate) the outcome and share it.

**Levels of evidence** (highest to lowest)

```text
   1  Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs
   2  Randomised controlled trials (RCTs)
   3  Controlled trials without randomisation
   4  Cohort and case-control studies
   5  Systematic reviews of descriptive/qualitative studies
   6  Single descriptive or qualitative studies
   7  Opinion of authorities and expert committees
```

**How informatics supports EBP**: online databases and full-text journals; point-of-care references; evidence built into order sets, care plans and decision support; EHR data for audits and research; e-learning for updating knowledge.

**Barriers to EBP**: lack of time, limited access to journals, poor search and appraisal skills, resistance to change, lack of managerial support. **Facilitators**: library and internet access, journal clubs, EBP mentors, protocols that are kept up to date.

**Nursing examples**: chlorhexidine skin preparation for central lines, regular repositioning and pressure-relieving surfaces to prevent pressure injury, early mobilisation after surgery, kangaroo mother care for low-birth-weight babies.

## 7.9 Technical and professional informatics standards

| Type | Examples | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Technical standards** | HL7 v2 and FHIR, DICOM, SNOMED CT, LOINC, ICD, ISO security standards, India's EHR Standards, ABDM specifications | Make systems interoperable, safe and secure |
| **Professional standards** | Nursing informatics scope and standards of practice (American Nurses Association); TIGER informatics competencies for nurses; INC curriculum competencies; NABH standards on management of information | Define what nurses and organisations should know and do with information |

Using standards is itself evidence-based: research shows that standardised, well-designed systems reduce errors and improve outcomes, while poorly designed ones can cause harm. Health IT should therefore be **evaluated** with data before and after it is introduced.

## 7.10 Nurse's role

- Protect privacy and confidentiality in every form of record and communication.
- Follow the law and hospital policy on consent, records and telehealth.
- Base care on current evidence; question routine practices that lack evidence.
- Use quality data to improve care in your unit.

## Remember

- Privacy = patient's right; confidentiality = professional's duty; security = protective measures. CIA = confidentiality, integrity, availability.
- IT Act 2000 (amended 2008): sections 43, 43A, 66, 66C, 66E, 72, 72A.
- DPDP Act 2023: assent 11 August 2023; data principal, data fiduciary, data processor, consent manager; breach reported to the Data Protection Board; penalty up to ₹250 crore.
- Telemedicine Practice Guidelines: 25 March 2020; video, audio, text; health worker may connect patient to RMP; explicit consent if health worker or RMP starts.
- Quality dimensions: safe, effective, patient-centred, timely, efficient, equitable; Donabedian: structure–process–outcome.
- EBP = best evidence + clinical expertise + patient preferences; 5 A's: ask, acquire, appraise, apply, assess.
- Systematic reviews of RCTs are the top level of evidence; expert opinion is the lowest.
