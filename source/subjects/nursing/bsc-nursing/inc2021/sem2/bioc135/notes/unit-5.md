# Unit 5: Acid–base balance

INC Unit V (3 hours): pH — definition and normal value; regulation of blood pH by blood buffers, the lungs and the kidneys; arterial blood gas (ABG) normal values; acid–base disorders — types, definitions and causes. The INC assessment for this unit is short and very short answers, but this topic is used every day in wards and ICUs.

> ABG values below are approximate ("about") adult arterial values at sea level; laboratories and analysers print their own ranges.

## 5.1 Acids, bases and pH

- An **acid** gives up hydrogen ions (H⁺) — a proton donor (e.g. carbonic acid H2CO3, lactic acid, ketone acids).
- A **base** accepts hydrogen ions — a proton acceptor (e.g. bicarbonate HCO3⁻).
- **pH** is the **negative logarithm (to base 10) of the hydrogen ion concentration**:

```text
pH = −log10 [H⁺]
```

Because the scale is logarithmic and negative:

- a **lower pH = more H⁺ = more acidic**; a higher pH = fewer H⁺ = more alkaline;
- a change of **1 pH unit = a tenfold change** in [H⁺]; even a fall from 7.4 to 7.1 roughly doubles [H⁺].

**Normal blood pH: about 7.35–7.45 (mean 7.4)** — slightly alkaline.

| pH | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Below 7.35 | **Acidaemia** (the process causing it is acidosis) |
| 7.35–7.45 | Normal |
| Above 7.45 | **Alkalaemia** (the process is alkalosis) |
| Below about 6.8 or above about 7.8 | Usually not compatible with life |

**Why pH must be held steady**: enzymes, ion channels, oxygen carriage by haemoglobin, heart rhythm and nerve function all depend on pH. Acidosis depresses the brain (confusion, coma) and the heart; alkalosis makes nerves irritable (tingling, tetany, fits).

**Where the acid comes from**: the body produces acid constantly — a very large amount of CO2 (volatile acid, removed by the lungs) from metabolism, and a smaller amount of **fixed (non-volatile) acids** such as sulphuric acid (from sulphur amino acids), phosphoric acid, lactic acid and ketone acids, which must be removed by the kidneys.

## 5.2 Three lines of defence

```text
Acid load
   ↓
1. BLOOD BUFFERS   — act in seconds; limit the change but do not remove acid
   ↓
2. LUNGS           — act in minutes; blow off or retain CO2 (carbonic acid)
   ↓
3. KIDNEYS         — act over hours to days; excrete H⁺ and make/reabsorb HCO3⁻;
                     the only route that removes fixed acid permanently
```

## 5.3 Blood buffers

A **buffer** is a mixture of a weak acid and its salt (conjugate base) that **resists a change in pH** when an acid or base is added.

| Buffer system | Where | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bicarbonate buffer** (H2CO3 / NaHCO3) | Plasma, ECF | **Most important buffer of blood/ECF** — both parts can be adjusted: CO2 by the lungs, HCO3⁻ by the kidneys |
| **Phosphate buffer** (NaH2PO4 / Na2HPO4) | Inside cells and in **urine (renal tubules)** | Main buffer of intracellular fluid and of tubular fluid |
| **Protein buffer** | Plasma proteins and cell proteins | Side chains (especially histidine) take up or release H⁺ |
| **Haemoglobin buffer** | Red blood cells | **Most important buffer of the H⁺ produced from CO2 carriage**; deoxygenated Hb is a better buffer, so it takes up H⁺ in tissues |

**How the bicarbonate buffer works**

```text
CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H⁺ + HCO3⁻          (carbonic anhydrase speeds the first step)

Strong acid added:  HCl + NaHCO3  →  NaCl + H2CO3  →  H2O + CO2 (breathed out)
Strong base added:  NaOH + H2CO3  →  NaHCO3 + H2O   (excess HCO3⁻ excreted by kidney)
```

**Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** (for the bicarbonate buffer):

```text
pH = pKa + log [HCO3⁻] / [H2CO3]
   = 6.1 + log [HCO3⁻] / (0.03 × pCO2)

Normal: HCO3⁻ 24 mmol/L ; H2CO3 = 0.03 × 40 = 1.2 mmol/L
Ratio = 24 / 1.2 = 20 : 1  →  log 20 = 1.3  →  pH = 6.1 + 1.3 = 7.4
```

**Key idea**: blood pH depends on the **ratio of bicarbonate to carbonic acid (20 : 1)**, not on their absolute amounts. HCO3⁻ is the **metabolic (kidney) component**; pCO2 is the **respiratory (lung) component**. If one changes, the body tries to change the other in the same direction to bring the ratio back towards 20 : 1 — this is **compensation**.

## 5.4 Respiratory regulation

- CO2 is an acid-forming gas (it forms H2CO3). The **respiratory centre** in the medulla (and chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies and the brain stem) responds to a rise in pCO2 or H⁺.
- **Acidosis → faster and deeper breathing** → more CO2 blown off → pH rises (seen as **Kussmaul breathing** in diabetic ketoacidosis).
- **Alkalosis → slower, shallower breathing** → CO2 retained → pH falls.
- Fast (minutes), but can only remove volatile acid and can only partly correct a metabolic problem (limited, because slowing breathing too far causes hypoxia).

## 5.5 Renal regulation

The kidney is slow (hours to 2–3 days) but is the only organ that can **excrete fixed acid** and **regenerate bicarbonate**. Tubular cells contain carbonic anhydrase, which makes H⁺ and HCO3⁻ from CO2 and water.

| Mechanism | Where | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate** | Mainly proximal tubule | H⁺ secreted into tubular fluid combines with filtered HCO3⁻ → CO2 + H2O → reabsorbed; a new HCO3⁻ returns to blood — **no net acid excreted, but bicarbonate is saved** |
| 2. **Excretion of titratable acid** | Distal tubule | Secreted H⁺ is buffered by **phosphate** in urine (HPO4²⁻ + H⁺ → H2PO4⁻); a new HCO3⁻ is added to blood for each H⁺ |
| 3. **Excretion of ammonium (NH4⁺)** | Proximal and distal tubules | Tubular cells make **ammonia (NH3) from glutamine**; NH3 traps H⁺ as NH4⁺ in urine — the **main way to increase acid excretion in chronic acidosis** |
| 4. Urine acidification | Collecting ducts | Urine pH can range from about 4.5 to 8 |

In acidosis the kidney excretes more H⁺ (more acid urine) and returns more bicarbonate to blood; in alkalosis it excretes bicarbonate (alkaline urine).

## 5.6 Arterial blood gas (ABG) — normal values

| Parameter | Normal (arterial, about) | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH | **7.35–7.45** | Overall acid–base state |
| PaCO2 | **35–45 mmHg** | Respiratory component (lungs) |
| HCO3⁻ (bicarbonate) | **22–26 mmol/L (mEq/L)** | Metabolic component (kidneys) |
| PaO2 | **80–100 mmHg** (on room air) | Oxygenation (not an acid–base value) |
| SaO2 | **95–100%** | Saturation of haemoglobin with oxygen |
| Base excess | **about −2 to +2 mmol/L** | Negative = metabolic acidosis; positive = metabolic alkalosis |
| Anion gap | **about 8–16 mmol/L** (method-dependent) | Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO3⁻); see 5.8 |

Venous blood has a slightly lower pH, higher pCO2 and much lower pO2 — ABG must be **arterial**.

## 5.7 Acid–base disorders — types, definitions, causes

| Disorder | Definition | Primary change | pH | Compensation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | Fall in pH due to a primary **fall in HCO3⁻** (gain of fixed acid or loss of bicarbonate) | HCO3⁻ ↓ | ↓ | Lungs: hyperventilation → PaCO2 ↓ |
| **Metabolic alkalosis** | Rise in pH due to a primary **rise in HCO3⁻** (loss of H⁺ or gain of bicarbonate) | HCO3⁻ ↑ | ↑ | Lungs: hypoventilation → PaCO2 ↑ (limited) |
| **Respiratory acidosis** | Fall in pH due to a primary **rise in PaCO2** (under-ventilation, CO2 retention) | PaCO2 ↑ | ↓ | Kidneys: retain HCO3⁻ → HCO3⁻ ↑ |
| **Respiratory alkalosis** | Rise in pH due to a primary **fall in PaCO2** (over-ventilation) | PaCO2 ↓ | ↑ | Kidneys: excrete HCO3⁻ → HCO3⁻ ↓ |

Memory aid: **ROME — Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal.** In respiratory disorders pH and PaCO2 move in **opposite** directions; in metabolic disorders pH and HCO3⁻ move in the **same** direction.

### Causes

**Metabolic acidosis**

- *High anion gap* (an extra acid is added): **diabetic ketoacidosis**, starvation ketosis, **lactic acidosis** (shock, severe hypoxia, cardiac arrest, sepsis), **renal failure** (retained sulphate, phosphate), poisoning (e.g. methanol, salicylate)
- *Normal anion gap* (bicarbonate lost, chloride retained): **severe diarrhoea**, intestinal fistula or ileostomy loss, renal tubular acidosis, ureterosigmoid diversion, carbonic anhydrase inhibitor drugs

**Metabolic alkalosis**

- Loss of H⁺: **prolonged vomiting**, **continuous nasogastric aspiration**, pyloric stenosis (loss of stomach HCl)
- Diuretics (thiazide and loop), **hypokalaemia**
- Excess mineralocorticoid: Cushing's syndrome, hyperaldosteronism
- Excess alkali intake: antacids/bicarbonate, massive transfusion (citrate → bicarbonate)

**Respiratory acidosis** (CO2 retention)

- Lung disease: **COPD** (chronic bronchitis, emphysema), severe asthma, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema
- Depressed respiratory centre: overdose of opioids or sedatives, general anaesthesia, head injury, stroke
- Weak breathing muscles or chest wall: Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis, polio, spinal cord injury, chest injury, severe obesity
- Airway obstruction, inadequate mechanical ventilation

**Respiratory alkalosis** (CO2 washed out)

- **Hyperventilation** due to anxiety, panic, pain, fever
- High altitude, hypoxia, severe anaemia
- Early salicylate poisoning, liver failure, sepsis, pregnancy (mild, normal), brain lesions
- Excess mechanical ventilation

### Main clinical features

| Disorder | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | Deep rapid (Kussmaul) breathing, headache, confusion, drowsiness, low BP, arrhythmia, hyperkalaemia |
| Metabolic alkalosis | Slow shallow breathing, muscle cramps, tingling, tetany, arrhythmia, hypokalaemia |
| Respiratory acidosis | Breathlessness, headache, flushed warm skin, drowsiness ("CO2 narcosis"), confusion |
| Respiratory alkalosis | Light-headedness, tingling around the mouth and fingers, carpopedal spasm (low ionised calcium), palpitations |

## 5.8 Anion gap

```text
Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO3⁻)
Example:   140 − (104 + 24) = 12 mmol/L  (normal, about 8–16)
```

It represents unmeasured anions (mainly albumin, phosphate, sulphate, organic acids). A **raised anion gap** in metabolic acidosis means an extra acid has been added (ketones, lactate, uraemic acids, poisons); a **normal anion gap** means bicarbonate has been lost (diarrhoea) and replaced by chloride (hyperchloraemic acidosis).

## 5.9 Reading an ABG — simple steps

1. **pH**: below 7.35 = acidaemia; above 7.45 = alkalaemia.
2. **PaCO2**: does it explain the pH? (High PaCO2 with low pH → respiratory acidosis; low PaCO2 with high pH → respiratory alkalosis.)
3. **HCO3⁻**: does it explain the pH? (Low HCO3⁻ with low pH → metabolic acidosis; high HCO3⁻ with high pH → metabolic alkalosis.)
4. **Compensation**: the other value moving in the same direction as the primary one = compensation. Partly compensated if pH is still abnormal; fully compensated if pH is back in the normal range (usually on the side of the primary problem). Compensation never over-corrects.
5. **Oxygenation**: PaO2 and SaO2.
6. In metabolic acidosis, calculate the **anion gap**.

**Worked examples**

| pH | PaCO2 | HCO3⁻ | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7.25 | 30 | 14 | Metabolic acidosis, partly compensated (e.g. DKA) |
| 7.52 | 46 | 36 | Metabolic alkalosis, partly compensated (e.g. vomiting) |
| 7.28 | 60 | 27 | Acute respiratory acidosis (e.g. opioid overdose) |
| 7.36 | 58 | 32 | Respiratory acidosis, compensated (e.g. chronic COPD) |
| 7.50 | 28 | 22 | Acute respiratory alkalosis (e.g. anxiety hyperventilation) |

## 5.10 Nursing points

**Collecting an arterial blood sample (steps with reasons)**

1. Check the order, explain to the patient, note whether on oxygen (FiO2) and the temperature. *Reason*: results are read against oxygen given.
2. For the radial artery, do the **modified Allen test** (compress both radial and ulnar arteries, ask the patient to clench and open the hand, release the ulnar — colour should return within about 5–15 seconds). *Reason*: confirms ulnar circulation to the hand if the radial artery is damaged.
3. Use a **heparinised** ABG syringe with the right needle; follow aseptic technique. *Reason*: prevents clotting.
4. After the sample, **expel air bubbles at once** and cap the syringe. *Reason*: air changes PaO2 and PaCO2.
5. **Apply firm pressure for at least 5 minutes** (longer if on anticoagulants). *Reason*: prevents haematoma.
6. Label and send **immediately** (or on ice if a delay is unavoidable, as the lab advises). *Reason*: cells keep using O2 and making CO2.

**Other nursing links**: count respiratory rate and depth (Kussmaul breathing suggests metabolic acidosis); be careful with high-flow oxygen in chronic CO2 retainers (COPD) and watch for drowsiness; record vomiting, NG aspirate and diarrhoea losses (they cause alkalosis and acidosis respectively); calm and coach a hyperventilating patient to breathe slowly; watch potassium, which moves out of cells in acidosis and into cells in alkalosis.

## Remember

- pH = −log [H⁺]; normal blood pH about 7.35–7.45; one pH unit = tenfold change in [H⁺].
- Buffers act in seconds, lungs in minutes, kidneys in hours to days.
- Bicarbonate buffer is the main blood buffer; haemoglobin buffers H⁺ from CO2; phosphate buffers urine and cells.
- HCO3⁻ : H2CO3 ratio 20 : 1 keeps pH 7.4 (pH = 6.1 + log 20).
- Kidney: reabsorbs bicarbonate, excretes titratable acid (phosphate) and ammonium (main response in chronic acidosis).
- ABG (about): pH 7.35–7.45, PaCO2 35–45 mmHg, HCO3⁻ 22–26 mmol/L, PaO2 80–100 mmHg, SaO2 95–100%.
- ROME: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal.
- Metabolic acidosis: DKA, lactic acidosis, renal failure, diarrhoea. Metabolic alkalosis: vomiting, NG aspiration, diuretics, hypokalaemia.
- Respiratory acidosis: COPD, respiratory depression. Respiratory alkalosis: hyperventilation, high altitude.
- Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO3⁻), about 8–16; high in ketoacidosis and lactic acidosis.
