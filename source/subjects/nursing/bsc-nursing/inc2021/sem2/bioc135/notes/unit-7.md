## 7.1 Basic terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Antigen** | A substance that the body recognises as foreign and that provokes an immune response (bacteria, viruses, toxins, foreign proteins, mismatched blood cells) |
| **Epitope (antigenic determinant)** | The small part of an antigen that an antibody actually binds |
| **Hapten** | A small molecule that is not antigenic alone but becomes so when attached to a carrier protein (e.g. some drugs causing allergy) |
| **Antibody (immunoglobulin, Ig)** | A glycoprotein made by **plasma cells** (activated B lymphocytes) that binds a specific antigen |
| **Humoral immunity** | Immunity carried by antibodies in body fluids |
| **Cell-mediated immunity** | Immunity carried by T lymphocytes |

Immunoglobulins are the **γ-globulins** of serum electrophoresis (Unit 3) and make up about 20% of plasma proteins.

## 7.2 Structure of an immunoglobulin

The basic unit is a **Y-shaped monomer** made of **four polypeptide chains**:

- **2 identical heavy (H) chains** (larger, about 440 amino acids) — the heavy chain type decides the **class**: γ (IgG), α (IgA), μ (IgM), δ (IgD), ε (IgE).
- **2 identical light (L) chains** (smaller, about 220 amino acids) — of either **kappa (κ) or lambda (λ)** type; one antibody has only one type.
- The chains are held together by **disulphide (–S–S–) bonds** (inter-chain and intra-chain) and non-covalent forces.

```text
   Antigen-binding site                     Antigen-binding site
           \                                       /
     [V_L][V_H]                             [V_H][V_L]      ← VARIABLE region
     [C_L][C_H1]                           [C_H1][C_L]      ← constant region
        \    \                             /    /
  light  \    \  ← Fab arm      Fab arm →  /    /  light
  chain   \    \                         /    /   chain
           \    \____ -S-S- HINGE -S-S-/____/
                       |  C_H2  |  C_H2 |
                       |  C_H3  |  C_H3 |      ← Fc stem (two heavy chains only)
                       |________|_______|        binds complement, phagocytes,
                         heavy    heavy          mast cells, placental receptors
                         chain    chain

 -S-S- = disulphide bonds; V = variable domain; C = constant domain;
 carbohydrate is attached to the heavy chains in the Fc part.
```

**Regions**

| Region | Where | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Variable (V) region** | N-terminal ends of both light and heavy chains (tips of the arms) | Different in every antibody; contains **hypervariable regions (complementarity-determining regions)** that form the **antigen-binding site** → specificity |
| **Constant (C) region** | Rest of the chains | Same within a class; decides the **biological (effector) functions** |
| **Hinge region** | Between Fab arms and Fc | Flexible — the arms can open to bind antigens at different distances |
| **Fab fragment** | Each arm | Binds antigen (each monomer has **2 binding sites** — bivalent) |
| **Fc fragment** | Stem | Activates complement, binds Fc receptors on phagocytes (opsonisation), NK cells, mast cells (IgE), crosses the placenta (IgG) |

**Enzyme digestion (classic experiments)**: **papain** splits an IgG into **2 Fab + 1 Fc** fragments; **pepsin** gives one **F(ab')2** fragment (both arms joined) and small Fc pieces.

## 7.3 Classes of immunoglobulins

| Feature | **IgG** | **IgA** | **IgM** | **IgD** | **IgE** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy chain | γ | α | μ | δ | ε |
| Form | Monomer | Monomer in serum; **dimer** in secretions (with J chain + secretory component) | **Pentamer** (with J chain) — largest ("macroglobulin") | Monomer | Monomer |
| Share of serum Ig (about) | **75–80%** (most abundant) | 10–15% | 5–10% | below 1% | Trace (lowest) |
| Crosses placenta | **Yes — the only one** | No | No | No | No |
| Main functions | Main antibody of the **secondary (memory) response**; neutralises toxins and viruses; opsonisation; activates complement; gives **passive immunity to the foetus/newborn** | **Mucosal (local) immunity** in gut, respiratory and urinary tracts; present in **colostrum, breast milk**, saliva, tears | **First antibody made** in the primary response and by the foetus; best activator of complement; agglutinates; natural antibodies to blood groups (anti-A, anti-B) | **Antigen receptor on the surface of B cells**; role in B-cell activation | **Allergy** (hay fever, asthma, anaphylaxis) — binds **mast cells and basophils**, releasing histamine; defence against **worms (helminths)** |
| Half-life (about) | about 3 weeks (longest) | about 6 days | about 5 days | about 3 days | about 2 days |

**Clinical significance**

- **IgM in a newborn's blood** means the baby made it itself → a **congenital (intrauterine) infection** (e.g. rubella, toxoplasma, CMV), because maternal IgM cannot cross the placenta.
- **IgM antibody** to a microbe = recent/current infection; **IgG** = past infection, immunity or later stage (e.g. hepatitis A IgM vs IgG; hepatitis B anti-HBc IgM).
- **Breastfeeding** gives secretory IgA → protects the baby's gut from infection.
- **Raised IgE** in allergy and worm infestation.
- **Monoclonal Ig** (M band) in multiple myeloma; light chains in urine = **Bence Jones protein** (Unit 3).
- Immunoglobulin **deficiency** (e.g. agammaglobulinaemia) → repeated bacterial infections.
- **Passive immunisation**: ready-made antibodies (immunoglobulin preparations, antitoxins) for immediate short-term protection — e.g. after exposure to rabies, tetanus, hepatitis B; anti-D for Rh-negative mothers.

**Functions of antibodies in general**: neutralisation of toxins and viruses, agglutination of bacteria, precipitation of soluble antigens, opsonisation (coating microbes for phagocytosis), complement activation (lysis), antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, and mast-cell activation (IgE).

## 7.4 Primary and secondary immune response

```text
Antibody
level      Secondary response: faster, much higher, longer-lasting, mainly IgG
  ↑                                    ┌─────────────────
  │                                   /
  │      Primary response:           /
  │      slow, low, mainly IgM      /
  │         ┌──┐                    │
  │________/    \___________________│__________________→ time
         1st exposure            2nd exposure (memory cells)
       (lag about 1–2 weeks)     (lag a few days)
```

This is the basis of **booster doses** of vaccines.

## 7.5 ELISA — investigations and interpretation

**ELISA = Enzyme-Linked ImmunoSorbent Assay.**

**Principle**: an **antigen–antibody reaction** takes place on a solid surface (wells of a plastic microtitre plate). One of the reactants is linked to an **enzyme** (commonly **horseradish peroxidase** or **alkaline phosphatase**). After washing away unbound material, a **colourless substrate** is added; the enzyme converts it into a **coloured product**. The intensity of colour, read as **optical density (OD)** in an **ELISA reader (spectrophotometer)**, is proportional to the amount of antigen or antibody in the patient's sample. It is very sensitive (detects tiny amounts) and can test many samples at once.

**Main types**

| Type | What is coated on the well | Detects | Example uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct ELISA** | Antigen | Antigen, using an enzyme-labelled primary antibody | Research; simple antigen detection |
| **Indirect ELISA** | Known **antigen** | **Antibody** in patient serum (detected by an enzyme-labelled anti-human Ig) | **HIV antibody**, hepatitis C antibody, dengue/typhoid serology |
| **Sandwich ELISA** | Known **antibody** (capture) | **Antigen** in patient sample, "sandwiched" between two antibodies | **HBsAg** (hepatitis B), dengue **NS1 antigen**, hormones (hCG, TSH) |
| **Competitive ELISA** | Antigen or antibody | Patient's antigen competes with labelled antigen — **less colour = more antigen** in sample | Small molecules, some hormones, drug levels |

**Steps of an indirect ELISA (for antibody detection)**

```text
1. Wells coated with known antigen; free sites blocked (e.g. with albumin/milk protein)
2. Add diluted patient serum → incubate → antibody (if present) binds antigen
3. WASH → remove unbound serum proteins
4. Add enzyme-labelled anti-human immunoglobulin (conjugate) → incubate
5. WASH → remove unbound conjugate
6. Add colourless substrate (e.g. TMB with peroxidase) → colour develops
7. Add stop solution (acid) → read OD in the ELISA reader at the set wavelength
8. Compare with positive and negative controls and the CUT-OFF value
```

**Interpretation**

| Result | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| OD **above the cut-off** | **Reactive (positive)** — antigen/antibody present |
| OD **below the cut-off** | **Non-reactive (negative)** |
| OD near the cut-off (grey zone) | **Equivocal** — repeat the test or test a fresh sample later |

Points to remember when reading results:

- ELISA is mainly a **screening test** with high sensitivity; a reactive result is **confirmed** by further tests (another assay or a confirmatory method such as Western blot or nucleic acid testing), following the national testing strategy — for HIV in India, follow the current NACO guideline.
- **False negative**: in the **window period** (infection present but antibodies not yet formed), in immunosuppression, or poor technique.
- **False positive**: cross-reacting antibodies, autoimmune disease, pregnancy, recent vaccination, contamination or poor washing.
- Results are valid only if the controls give their expected values.

**Advantages**: highly sensitive and specific, safe (no radioactivity, unlike radioimmunoassay), many samples at once, can be automated, reagents stable, quantitative. **Limitations**: needs equipment and trained staff, takes a few hours, false results as above.

**Common clinical uses**: HIV, hepatitis B (HBsAg) and C screening (including blood bank screening), dengue (NS1, IgM), typhoid, tuberculosis-related tests, rubella/TORCH antibodies, hormones (hCG for pregnancy, TSH, T4), tumour markers, troponin and PSA assays (variants of the same immunoassay principle), allergy (specific IgE).

## 7.6 Application in nursing

- Collect blood for serology with standard precautions; label clearly; ensure **pre-test counselling and informed consent** for HIV testing and keep results **confidential**; post-test counselling follows.
- Explain the **window period** — a negative test soon after exposure may need repeating.
- Encourage **exclusive breastfeeding** (secretory IgA) and **colostrum** feeding.
- Know which infections in pregnancy are detected by IgM/IgG testing (TORCH) and why IgM in a newborn matters.
- Give immunoglobulin preparations (e.g. anti-D, rabies or tetanus immunoglobulin) exactly as prescribed and watch for reactions; follow cold-chain storage for vaccines and immunoglobulins.
- Observe for allergic reactions (IgE-mediated): itching, rash, wheeze, low BP — follow the anaphylaxis protocol.
- Follow biomedical waste rules for used plates, tips and sharps in the laboratory.

## Remember

- Immunoglobulin = Y-shaped glycoprotein: 2 heavy + 2 light (κ or λ) chains joined by disulphide bonds.
- Variable region (tips) binds antigen; constant region (Fc) gives effector functions; hinge gives flexibility.
- Papain → 2 Fab + 1 Fc; pepsin → F(ab')2.
- IgG: most abundant, crosses placenta, secondary response, longest half-life.
- IgA: secretory dimer in colostrum, milk, mucosa. IgM: pentamer, largest, first in primary response; IgM in newborn = intrauterine infection.
- IgD: B-cell surface receptor. IgE: allergy and worms, binds mast cells, lowest level.
- ELISA: antigen–antibody reaction + enzyme-linked antibody + substrate → colour read as OD.
- Indirect ELISA detects antibody (HIV antibody); sandwich ELISA detects antigen (HBsAg, NS1).
- OD above cut-off = reactive; screening test — confirm positives; window period gives false negatives.
