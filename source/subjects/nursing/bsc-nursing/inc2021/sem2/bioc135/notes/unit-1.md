# Unit 1: Carbohydrates, blood glucose and diabetes mellitus

INC Unit I (8 hours): digestion, absorption and metabolism of carbohydrates and related disorders; regulation of blood glucose; diabetes mellitus; investigations of diabetes (OGTT and its variants, HbA1c); hypoglycaemia. This is the heaviest unit of the course and the most common source of essay-type and short questions.

> **About laboratory values.** All normal values in these notes are approximate ("about"). Each laboratory sets its own reference range according to its method and machine, so always read a report against the range printed on it. Diagnostic cut-offs for diabetes are the widely used ADA/WHO figures; your teacher or hospital may use a slightly different guideline.

## 1.1 Carbohydrates in brief

Carbohydrates are aldehyde or ketone derivatives of polyhydroxy alcohols. They are the main fuel of the body, especially for the brain and red blood cells.

| Class | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monosaccharides | Single sugar unit | Glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose |
| Disaccharides | Two units | Sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), maltose (glucose + glucose) |
| Oligosaccharides | 3–10 units | Raffinose |
| Polysaccharides | Many units | Starch and glycogen (storage), cellulose (dietary fibre), heparin, hyaluronic acid |

Glucose is the key sugar: it is the form in which carbohydrate travels in blood and is used by every cell.

## 1.2 Digestion of carbohydrates

```text
MOUTH      Salivary amylase (ptyalin) → starch → dextrins + some maltose
           (short contact time; stopped by stomach acid)
STOMACH    No carbohydrate-digesting enzyme of its own
DUODENUM   Pancreatic amylase → starch/dextrins → maltose, isomaltose,
           limit dextrins
SMALL      Brush-border enzymes:
INTESTINE    Maltase      maltose  → glucose + glucose
             Isomaltase   isomaltose → glucose
             Sucrase      sucrose  → glucose + fructose
             Lactase      lactose  → glucose + galactose
```

- Cellulose cannot be digested by humans (no enzyme to break its β-1,4 links). It stays as fibre, adds bulk to stool and helps prevent constipation.
- The end products of carbohydrate digestion are **monosaccharides**: glucose, fructose and galactose.

## 1.3 Absorption

| Sugar | Mechanism | Carrier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glucose, galactose | Secondary active transport, linked with sodium | SGLT-1 on the brush border |
| Fructose | Facilitated diffusion (no energy) | GLUT-5 |
| All three | Leave the cell into blood | GLUT-2 on the basal side |

The absorbed sugars travel by the **portal vein to the liver**, which takes up a large share after a meal.

**Nursing link: oral rehydration solution (ORS).** Glucose and sodium are absorbed together by SGLT-1, and water follows. This is why ORS containing both glucose and sodium works even in diarrhoea, when other absorption is disturbed.

## 1.4 Metabolism of glucose — overview

```text
                       Glycogen
              glycogenesis ↑   ↓ glycogenolysis
Dietary   →   GLUCOSE  ──glycolysis──→  Pyruvate ──→ Acetyl-CoA ──→ TCA cycle → CO2 + H2O + ATP
glucose        ↑   │                     │  (no O2)                  │
   gluconeogenesis │ HMP shunt           ↓                           ↓
 (lactate, amino   ↓                   Lactate                    Fatty acids (excess)
  acids, glycerol) NADPH + ribose
```

| Pathway | Where | What it does | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Glycolysis** | Cytoplasm of all cells | Glucose → 2 pyruvate (with O2) or 2 lactate (without O2) | Net 2 ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation; only pathway that gives energy without oxygen; RBCs depend on it (no mitochondria) |
| **Link reaction** | Mitochondria | Pyruvate → acetyl-CoA | Pyruvate dehydrogenase needs thiamine (vitamin B1) and other B vitamins |
| **TCA (citric acid / Krebs) cycle** | Mitochondria | Acetyl-CoA oxidised to CO2 | Final common pathway for carbohydrate, fat and protein; produces NADH and FADH2 for the electron transport chain |
| **Glycogenesis** | Liver, muscle | Glucose → glycogen | Stores glucose after meals; stimulated by insulin |
| **Glycogenolysis** | Liver, muscle | Glycogen → glucose | Liver glycogen keeps blood glucose up between meals; muscle glycogen is used only by muscle (muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase) |
| **Gluconeogenesis** | Liver (mainly), kidney | New glucose from non-carbohydrates: lactate, glycerol, glucogenic amino acids | Keeps blood glucose up during fasting and starvation; stimulated by glucagon and cortisol |
| **HMP shunt (pentose phosphate pathway)** | Liver, RBC, adipose, adrenal | Makes NADPH and ribose-5-phosphate | NADPH needed for fat synthesis and to protect RBCs from oxidation |

**Energy yield**: complete oxidation of one glucose gives about 30–32 ATP by current figures (older textbooks give 36–38). Anaerobic glycolysis gives only 2.

**Cori cycle**: lactate made by working muscle and RBCs goes to the liver, is changed back to glucose (gluconeogenesis) and returns to muscle.

## 1.5 Disorders of carbohydrate metabolism

| Disorder | Defect | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lactose intolerance** | Low lactase in the gut wall | Bloating, cramps and diarrhoea after milk; common in adults; manage with less milk, curd |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | Lack of insulin or resistance to it | Hyperglycaemia (see 1.7) |
| **Galactosaemia** | Enzyme deficiency in galactose use (commonly galactose-1-phosphate uridyl transferase) | Infant vomits, fails to thrive, jaundice, cataract, liver damage, mental retardation; remove milk sugar from diet |
| **Hereditary fructose intolerance** | Aldolase B deficiency | Hypoglycaemia and vomiting after fruit/sucrose |
| **Glycogen storage diseases** | Enzymes of glycogen breakdown, e.g. glucose-6-phosphatase (von Gierke disease) | Large liver, fasting hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis |
| **G6PD deficiency** | Low NADPH in RBCs | Haemolysis after oxidant drugs, infections or fava beans; neonatal jaundice |
| **Lactic acidosis** | Excess lactate (shock, hypoxia) | Metabolic acidosis (see Unit 5) |
| **Hypoglycaemia** | Low blood glucose | See 1.10 |

## 1.6 Regulation of blood glucose

Blood glucose is kept within a narrow range because the brain uses glucose almost exclusively under normal conditions.

**Approximate normal values (venous plasma)**

| Test | About |
| --- | --- |
| Fasting plasma glucose | 70–100 mg/dL |
| 2 hours after a meal (post-prandial) | below 140 mg/dL |
| Random | below about 140 mg/dL in a healthy person |

**Renal threshold for glucose**: about 180 mg/dL. Above this, the kidney tubules cannot reabsorb all filtered glucose and glucose appears in urine (glycosuria).

**Hormones that act on blood glucose**

| Hormone | Source | Effect on blood glucose | How |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Insulin** | β cells of pancreatic islets | **Lowers** (the only hormone that lowers it) | ↑ glucose entry into muscle and fat (GLUT-4), ↑ glycogenesis, ↑ fat synthesis, ↓ gluconeogenesis, ↓ glycogenolysis |
| Glucagon | α cells of islets | Raises | ↑ glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in liver |
| Adrenaline | Adrenal medulla | Raises (quickly, in stress) | ↑ glycogenolysis in liver and muscle |
| Cortisol | Adrenal cortex | Raises | ↑ gluconeogenesis; ↓ glucose use in tissues |
| Growth hormone | Anterior pituitary | Raises | Anti-insulin action |
| Thyroxine | Thyroid | Raises | ↑ absorption from gut, ↑ glycogenolysis |

```text
After a meal: glucose ↑ → insulin ↑ → glucose enters cells, stored as glycogen and fat → glucose ↓
During fasting: glucose ↓ → glucagon ↑ (and cortisol, adrenaline) → liver releases glucose
                (glycogenolysis first, gluconeogenesis later) → glucose ↑
```

**Organs**: the **liver** is the glucose "buffer" — it stores glucose after meals and releases it in fasting; muscle and adipose tissue take up glucose under insulin; the kidney excretes glucose above the threshold and contributes to gluconeogenesis in prolonged fasting.

## 1.7 Diabetes mellitus

**Definition**: a group of metabolic disorders marked by chronic hyperglycaemia due to a defect in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both, with disturbed metabolism of carbohydrate, fat and protein.

**Types**

| Feature | Type 1 | Type 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Autoimmune destruction of β cells → absolute insulin deficiency | Insulin resistance with relative insulin deficiency |
| Usual age | Children, young adults | Adults (now seen in young people too) |
| Body build | Usually thin | Often overweight/obese, central obesity |
| Onset | Sudden | Gradual; often found on a routine test |
| Ketoacidosis | Common | Uncommon (hyperosmolar state more typical) |
| Family history | Less strong | Strong |
| Treatment | Insulin always needed | Diet, exercise, oral drugs; insulin later in some |

Other types: **gestational diabetes** (first found in pregnancy), and diabetes due to other causes (pancreatitis, drugs such as steroids, endocrine disorders).

**Biochemical basis of the symptoms**

| Symptom | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| **Polyuria** (passing much urine) | Glucose above the renal threshold spills into urine and pulls water with it (osmotic diuresis) |
| **Polydipsia** (excess thirst) | Water lost in urine → dehydration |
| **Polyphagia** (excess hunger) | Cells are "starved" of glucose despite high blood glucose |
| Weight loss | Fat and protein broken down for energy |
| Tiredness, weakness | Poor glucose use by cells |
| Blurred vision, infections (boils, thrush, UTI), slow wound healing | High glucose damages lens function, immunity and healing |

**Complications**

| Acute | Chronic — small vessels (microvascular) | Chronic — large vessels (macrovascular) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): high glucose, ketone bodies, metabolic acidosis, dehydration, Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath | Retinopathy (can lead to blindness) | Coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction |
| Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (very high glucose, severe dehydration, little ketosis) | Nephropathy (microalbuminuria → proteinuria → kidney failure) | Stroke |
| Hypoglycaemia (from treatment) | Neuropathy (numb feet, autonomic problems) | Peripheral vascular disease → diabetic foot, gangrene |

**Biochemistry of DKA**: no insulin → fat breakdown ↑ → fatty acids flood the liver → excess acetyl-CoA → **ketone bodies** (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone) → metabolic acidosis (Unit 5); glucose high → osmotic diuresis → dehydration and loss of sodium and potassium.

**Management in brief**

1. **Diet**: regular meals, complex carbohydrates and fibre, limited sugar and saturated fat, portion control.
2. **Exercise**: regular moderate activity improves insulin sensitivity.
3. **Drugs**: oral glucose-lowering drugs (for example metformin, sulphonylureas and newer groups) in type 2; insulin in type 1 and when needed in type 2. Doses are decided by the doctor.
4. **Monitoring**: self blood glucose testing with a glucometer, HbA1c every few months, yearly eye, kidney (urine albumin, creatinine) and foot check, lipid profile and blood pressure.
5. **Education**: recognising and treating hypoglycaemia, sick-day care, foot care, insulin storage and injection technique, stopping smoking.

## 1.8 Investigations of diabetes mellitus

| Test | What it is | Diabetes if (about) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) | After 8 hours with no calories | ≥ 126 mg/dL |
| 2-hour plasma glucose in OGTT | 2 h after a 75 g glucose drink | ≥ 200 mg/dL |
| Random plasma glucose | Any time | ≥ 200 mg/dL with classic symptoms |
| HbA1c | Glycated haemoglobin | ≥ 6.5% |

"Prediabetes" (increased risk): FPG about 100–125 mg/dL (impaired fasting glucose) or 2-h OGTT value about 140–199 mg/dL (impaired glucose tolerance). (WHO uses 110–125 mg/dL for impaired fasting glucose.)

Other tests: urine glucose and ketones (Benedict's test, dipsticks, Rothera's test for ketones), urine microalbumin, serum creatinine, lipid profile, C-peptide (to judge the patient's own insulin output).

## 1.9 Glucose tolerance test (OGTT)

**Definition**: a test of the body's ability to handle a standard glucose load; blood glucose is measured fasting and at fixed times after drinking glucose.

**Indications**

- Borderline fasting or random glucose values (doubtful diabetes)
- Glucose in urine without symptoms (to separate diabetes from renal glycosuria)
- Screening and diagnosis of gestational diabetes
- People at high risk: strong family history, obesity, a previous large baby
- Unexplained recurrent infections, retinopathy or neuropathy

**Procedure (the steps a student learns, with reasons)**

1. **Preparation for 3 days**: normal diet with adequate carbohydrate (about 150 g/day) and normal activity. *Reason*: a low-carbohydrate diet reduces tolerance and gives a falsely "diabetic" curve.
2. **Overnight fast of 8–14 hours** (water allowed). *Reason*: a true fasting baseline.
3. Avoid smoking, tea/coffee and exercise on the test morning; the patient sits quietly throughout. Note drugs that affect glucose (steroids, diuretics) for the doctor. *Reason*: these change glucose values.
4. Collect a **fasting blood sample** (in a fluoride tube) and a **fasting urine sample**. *Reason*: fluoride stops RBCs using glucose in the tube.
5. Give **75 g anhydrous glucose dissolved in about 250–300 mL water**, to be drunk within 5 minutes (children: 1.75 g per kg body weight, not more than 75 g). Note the time.
6. Collect blood (and urine) samples at **30-minute intervals for 2 hours** (classic test), or at least at **2 hours** (WHO standard test).
7. Label each tube with name, time and sample number; send promptly.
8. Watch for faintness, nausea or vomiting (vomiting spoils the test); report it.

**Interpretation (venous plasma, about)**

| Result | Fasting | 2 hours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | below 100 mg/dL | below 140 mg/dL |
| Impaired glucose tolerance | below 126 | 140–199 |
| Diabetes mellitus | ≥ 126 | ≥ 200 |

In a normal curve the value peaks within 30–60 minutes (usually below about 160–180 mg/dL), returns to the fasting level by about 2 hours, and no glucose appears in urine.

**Types of GTT curve**

```text
Plasma glucose (mg/dL), sampled at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120 min

            0 min    30–60 min (peak)    120 min     Urine glucose
Normal      <100     <160–180            <140        absent
Impaired    <126     raised              140–199     usually absent
Diabetic    ≥126     very high           ≥200        present
Lag curve   normal   >180 (sharp peak)   normal      present briefly
Flat curve  normal   small rise only     normal      absent

Draw it as a graph in the exam: time on the x-axis, glucose on the y-axis,
a dotted line at about 180 mg/dL for the renal threshold.
```

| Curve | Pattern | Seen in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Peak within 1 h below renal threshold; fasting level by 2 h | Healthy person |
| Diabetic | High fasting, high peak, still high at 2 h; glycosuria | Diabetes mellitus |
| Impaired | In between normal and diabetic | Prediabetes |
| **Lag (alimentary / "oxyhyperglycaemic") curve** | Quick high peak above renal threshold at 30–60 min, normal at 2 h, brief glycosuria | After gastrectomy (fast emptying), hyperthyroidism, some liver disease |
| **Renal glycosuria** | Normal blood curve but glucose in urine | Low renal threshold (harmless tubular defect, pregnancy) |
| **Flat curve** | Very small rise | Malabsorption, hypothyroidism, Addison's disease, hypopituitarism |

## 1.10 Variants of the GTT

| Variant | How it differs | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mini GTT** | Only two blood (and urine) samples: fasting and 2 hours after 75 g glucose | Routine screening; quicker and cheaper; the form used most in practice |
| **Extended GTT** | Sampling continued every 30–60 min up to 4–6 hours | Suspected reactive (post-meal) hypoglycaemia, where glucose falls low 3–5 h after the load |
| **Glucose challenge test (GCT)** | 50 g glucose given at any time of day without fasting; one sample at 1 hour | Screening for gestational diabetes; a raised value (commonly ≥ 140 mg/dL) is followed by a full OGTT |
| **Intravenous GTT** | Glucose is given into a vein and blood sampled at intervals | When oral absorption is unreliable: malabsorption, after gastric surgery, persistent vomiting |

**Gestational diabetes in India**: national guidelines use a single-step 75 g oral glucose test in pregnancy with a 2-hour value. Check the current Ministry of Health guideline for the exact timing and cut-off used in your hospital.

## 1.11 HbA1c (definition only)

**HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin)** is haemoglobin A to which glucose has attached itself non-enzymatically (glycation). Because red cells live about 120 days, the percentage of HbA1c reflects the **average blood glucose over the previous 2–3 months**. A value of about 6.5% or above supports a diagnosis of diabetes; below about 5.7% is usually normal. It does not need fasting. It can be misleading in anaemia, haemoglobin variants and after blood loss or transfusion.

## 1.12 Hypoglycaemia

**Definition**: a blood glucose low enough to cause symptoms or harm; in practice a plasma glucose below about **70 mg/dL** is treated as hypoglycaemia (alert value), and below about 54 mg/dL is significant. Classic proof is **Whipple's triad**: symptoms of hypoglycaemia, a low measured glucose at that time, and relief when glucose is raised.

**Causes**

- **Drug-induced** (commonest): excess insulin or sulphonylurea, a missed or late meal, unusual exercise
- Alcohol (blocks gluconeogenesis), especially on an empty stomach
- Insulinoma (insulin-secreting tumour)
- Liver failure (cannot release or make glucose), severe sepsis
- Adrenal insufficiency, hypopituitarism (lack of counter-regulatory hormones)
- Starvation, severe malnutrition
- Inborn errors: glycogen storage disease, galactosaemia, hereditary fructose intolerance
- Newborns: infants of diabetic mothers, preterm and small-for-date babies
- Reactive hypoglycaemia after meals, after gastric surgery

**Features**: sweating, tremor, palpitation, hunger, anxiety (adrenaline response); then confusion, odd behaviour, slurred speech, fits and coma (brain deprived of glucose).

## 1.13 Application in nursing

- Check capillary blood glucose with a glucometer: clean and dry the fingertip (alcohol left on the skin alters the reading), prick the side of the finger, wipe the first drop, record time and relation to meals.
- Collect glucose samples in **fluoride (grey-top) tubes**; delay in plain tubes lowers the value as cells keep using glucose.
- Know the signs of hypoglycaemia and the hospital protocol to give quick-acting sugar to a conscious patient; recheck glucose.
- Watch a diabetic patient for DKA: rising glucose, ketones in urine, deep rapid breathing, vomiting, dehydration.
- Teach foot care, regular meals with insulin, sick-day rules and the meaning of HbA1c.
- Prepare patients correctly for OGTT; the result is only as good as the preparation.

## Remember

- End products of carbohydrate digestion: glucose, fructose, galactose; glucose and galactose absorbed with sodium (SGLT-1).
- Glycolysis: cytoplasm, net 2 ATP, works without oxygen; RBCs depend on it.
- Insulin is the only hormone that lowers blood glucose.
- Renal threshold for glucose is about 180 mg/dL.
- Diabetes (about): FPG ≥ 126, 2-h OGTT ≥ 200, random ≥ 200 with symptoms, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%.
- OGTT: 3 days normal carbohydrate diet, overnight fast, 75 g glucose in about 250–300 mL water, samples fasting to 2 h.
- Lag curve = early high peak with brief glycosuria (gastrectomy, hyperthyroidism); flat curve = malabsorption, hypothyroidism, Addison's.
- Mini GTT = fasting + 2 h samples; GCT = 50 g, non-fasting, 1 h sample.
- HbA1c = glycated haemoglobin, average glucose over 2–3 months.
- Hypoglycaemia below about 70 mg/dL; Whipple's triad; commonest cause is diabetes treatment.
