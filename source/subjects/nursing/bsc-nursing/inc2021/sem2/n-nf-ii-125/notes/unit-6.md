# Unit 6: Fluid, electrolyte and acid–base balance, IV therapy and blood transfusion

INC Unit VIII (5 hours theory + 10 hours skill lab): review of how the body regulates fluids, electrolytes and acid–base balance; factors affecting them; fluid volume deficit and excess; electrolyte and acid–base imbalances; intravenous therapy (venipuncture sites, types of IV fluids, calculations, complications); intake–output measurement; blood and blood components; restricting and enhancing fluid intake.

## 6.1 Review: body fluids

- Water is about **60% of body weight** in a young adult man (less in women and the elderly, more — about 75–80% — in newborns).
- **Intracellular fluid (ICF)**: about two-thirds of body water.
- **Extracellular fluid (ECF)**: about one-third — interstitial fluid (around cells), intravascular fluid (plasma), and a small transcellular part (CSF, pleural, synovial, digestive juices).

```text
Total body water (≈ 60% of body weight)
├── ICF ≈ 2/3 (≈ 40% of body weight)
└── ECF ≈ 1/3 (≈ 20% of body weight)
    ├── Interstitial ≈ 15%
    └── Plasma ≈ 5%
```

**Movement of fluids and solutes**: diffusion (high to low concentration), osmosis (water moves towards higher solute concentration), filtration (pushed by hydrostatic pressure), active transport (needs energy — the sodium–potassium pump).

**Regulation of fluid balance**

| Mechanism | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Thirst | Triggered by raised plasma osmolality or low volume |
| Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) | From the posterior pituitary; kidneys keep water |
| Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system | Low BP/volume → aldosterone → kidneys keep sodium and water, lose potassium |
| Atrial natriuretic peptide | Released when the heart is stretched; increases loss of sodium and water |
| Kidneys | Final regulator of water and electrolytes |

**Normal daily intake and output (adult, approximate)**: intake about 2,500 mL (drinks about 1,500 mL, food about 750 mL, metabolism about 250 mL); output about 2,500 mL (urine about 1,500 mL, skin and lungs about 900 mL insensible loss, faeces about 100 mL).

## 6.2 Review: electrolytes

| Electrolyte | Main site | Approximate normal serum level | Key roles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium (Na+) | ECF | 135–145 mEq/L | Water balance, nerve and muscle function |
| Potassium (K+) | ICF | 3.5–5.0 mEq/L | Heart rhythm, muscle contraction |
| Calcium (Ca2+) | ECF, bone | About 8.5–10.5 mg/dL | Bones, clotting, muscle and nerve |
| Magnesium (Mg2+) | ICF | About 1.5–2.5 mEq/L | Enzymes, nerve and muscle |
| Chloride (Cl–) | ECF | About 96–106 mEq/L | Follows sodium |
| Bicarbonate (HCO3–) | ECF | 22–26 mEq/L | Buffer for acid–base balance |
| Phosphate | ICF, bone | About 2.5–4.5 mg/dL | Bones, energy (ATP) |

## 6.3 Review: acid–base balance

Normal arterial **pH 7.35–7.45**. Regulation:
1. **Buffers** (immediate): bicarbonate–carbonic acid, phosphate and protein (haemoglobin) buffers.
2. **Lungs** (minutes): blow off or keep CO2 (CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3–).
3. **Kidneys** (hours to days): excrete H+ and reabsorb or make bicarbonate.

Arterial blood gas normals: pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO2 35–45 mmHg; HCO3– 22–26 mEq/L; PaO2 80–100 mmHg.

## 6.4 Factors affecting fluid, electrolyte and acid–base balance

Age (infants and elderly at greater risk), body size and fat, environmental heat, diet and fluid intake, stress, illness (fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, kidney and heart failure, diabetes), surgery, medicines (diuretics, laxatives, steroids), and loss of fluid through drains, NG suction and wounds.

## 6.5 Disturbances in fluid volume

### Fluid volume deficit
- **Hypovolaemia**: loss of water and electrolytes together from the ECF (bleeding, vomiting, diarrhoea, burns).
- **Dehydration**: loss of water alone, so serum sodium rises (fever, too little intake, diabetes insipidus).

**Signs**: thirst, dry mouth and tongue, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes (and depressed fontanelle in infants), weight loss, low and concentrated urine (high specific gravity), rapid weak pulse, low BP and postural drop, flat neck veins, confusion; raised haematocrit and blood urea.

**Nursing care**: oral fluids (ORS for diarrhoea), IV fluids as prescribed, intake–output, daily weight at the same time with the same scale, vital signs, mouth care, safety (fall risk from postural hypotension).

### Fluid volume excess
- **Fluid overload (hypervolaemia)**: too much isotonic fluid in the ECF (heart failure, kidney failure, too much IV fluid).
- **Oedema**: excess fluid in the interstitial space (raised capillary pressure, low plasma protein, blocked lymph drainage, leaky capillaries).

**Signs**: weight gain, pitting oedema, distended neck veins, bounding pulse, raised BP, breathlessness, crackles in the lungs, low haematocrit.

**Nursing care**: fluid and salt restriction as ordered, diuretics as prescribed, semi-Fowler's position, intake–output and daily weight, skin care over oedematous areas, monitor breathing.

**Grading pitting oedema (common scale)**: press for a few seconds over the shin — 1+ (about 2 mm, disappears quickly) to 4+ (about 8 mm, lasts several minutes).

## 6.6 Electrolyte imbalances

| Imbalance | Common causes | Key signs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hyponatraemia** (Na below 135) | Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics, excess water, SIADH | Headache, confusion, nausea, muscle cramps, seizures |
| **Hypernatraemia** (Na above 145) | Water loss, too little water, diabetes insipidus | Thirst, dry sticky mucosa, restlessness, fever, confusion |
| **Hypokalaemia** (K below 3.5) | Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics, NG suction | Muscle weakness, leg cramps, decreased bowel sounds, arrhythmias, flat T waves |
| **Hyperkalaemia** (K above 5.0) | Kidney failure, crush injuries, burns, haemolysis | Muscle weakness, tall peaked T waves, dangerous arrhythmias, cardiac arrest |
| **Hypocalcaemia** | Removal of parathyroids, pancreatitis, vitamin D deficiency | Tingling around mouth and fingers, muscle spasms, **Chvostek's** and **Trousseau's** signs, tetany |
| **Hypercalcaemia** | Hyperparathyroidism, cancer of bone, immobility | Weakness, constipation, kidney stones, confusion |
| **Hypomagnesaemia** | Alcoholism, malnutrition, diarrhoea | Tremors, increased reflexes, arrhythmias |
| **Hypermagnesaemia** | Kidney failure, excess magnesium | Drowsiness, low reflexes, low BP, respiratory depression |

Nursing points: potassium is never given as an undiluted IV push — it is diluted and given by controlled infusion as prescribed; monitor ECG in severe potassium imbalance; watch urine output before potassium-containing fluids.

## 6.7 Acid–base imbalances

| Imbalance | pH | Primary change | Common causes | Signs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Respiratory acidosis | Low | PaCO2 raised | Hypoventilation: COPD, opioid overdose, chest injury | Drowsiness, headache, confusion |
| Respiratory alkalosis | High | PaCO2 low | Hyperventilation: anxiety, pain, high altitude | Light-headedness, tingling, carpopedal spasm |
| Metabolic acidosis | Low | HCO3– low | Diabetic ketoacidosis, kidney failure, severe diarrhoea, lactic acidosis | Kussmaul breathing, headache, confusion |
| Metabolic alkalosis | High | HCO3– high | Vomiting, NG suction, excess antacids | Slow shallow breathing, muscle twitching, tetany |

**Memory aid — "ROME"**: in **R**espiratory problems the pH and PaCO2 move in **O**pposite directions; in **M**etabolic problems the pH and HCO3– move in the same (**E**qual) direction. The body **compensates** — lungs for metabolic problems, kidneys for respiratory problems.

## 6.8 Intravenous therapy

**Purposes**: replace fluid and electrolytes, give medicines and blood, provide nutrition, keep a vein open in emergencies.

### Peripheral venipuncture sites
- **Preferred**: veins of the forearm and back of the hand — cephalic, basilic, median antebrachial veins and dorsal metacarpal veins; start distally and move upward.
- **Antecubital fossa** (median cubital vein) — good for blood sampling and emergencies; avoid for long infusions (bending blocks the flow).
- **Avoid**: an arm with an AV fistula, the side of mastectomy with lymph node removal, paralysed limbs, areas of infection, phlebitis, oedema or bruising, legs in adults (risk of thrombosis).
- **Infants**: scalp, hand, foot veins.

**IV cannula sizes (common colour code)**

| Gauge | Colour | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 14 G | Orange | Rapid large-volume transfusion, trauma |
| 16 G | Grey | Trauma, surgery, rapid blood |
| 18 G | Green | Blood transfusion, surgery |
| 20 G | Pink | Most adult infusions |
| 22 G | Blue | Small veins, children, elderly |
| 24 G | Yellow | Infants, children, very fragile veins |
| 26 G | Violet | Neonates |

The larger the gauge number, the smaller the cannula.

**Main steps of cannulation**: check the order; explain; hand hygiene and gloves; apply a tourniquet 10–15 cm above the site; choose a vein; clean with antiseptic and let it dry; anchor the vein; insert bevel up at about 15–30° angle; look for flashback; lower the angle and advance the cannula off the needle; release the tourniquet; remove the needle and dispose in the sharps container; connect and flush; secure with a transparent dressing; label with date, time and gauge.

### Types of IV fluids

| Type | Examples | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Isotonic** (same osmolality as plasma) | 0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline), Ringer's lactate, 5% dextrose (isotonic in the bag but behaves as free water once the glucose is used) | Expands ECF volume; used for blood loss, dehydration, maintenance |
| **Hypotonic** | 0.45% sodium chloride | Water moves into cells; used for cellular dehydration — avoid in raised intracranial pressure |
| **Hypertonic** | 3% sodium chloride, 10% dextrose, dextrose 5% in normal saline (DNS) | Draws water out of cells into vessels; watch for fluid overload |
| **Colloids** | Albumin, dextran, gelatin and starch solutions | Stay in the vessels and draw fluid in; volume expanders |

### Calculation for making an IV fluid plan

**1. Rate in mL per hour** = total volume (mL) ÷ total time (hours)

**2. Drops per minute** = volume (mL) × drop factor (drops/mL) ÷ time (minutes)

The **drop factor** is printed on the infusion-set packet — commonly 15 or 20 drops/mL for adult (macro-drip) sets and 60 drops/mL for paediatric micro-drip sets.

**Worked examples (made-up orders)**

- Order: 1,000 mL normal saline over 8 hours with a 20 drops/mL set.
  mL/hour = 1,000 ÷ 8 = **125 mL/h**.
  drops/min = 1,000 × 20 ÷ (8 × 60) = 20,000 ÷ 480 = 41.7 ≈ **42 drops/min**.
- Order: 500 mL over 4 hours with a 15 drops/mL set.
  drops/min = 500 × 15 ÷ 240 = 7,500 ÷ 240 = 31.25 ≈ **31 drops/min**.
- With a micro-drip set (60 drops/mL), drops per minute = mL per hour. Example: 50 mL/h = 50 micro-drops/min.
- Time to finish: 1,000 mL at 125 mL/h = 8 hours.
- A 24-hour plan: if 3 litres are ordered in 24 hours, give 1 litre every 8 hours (125 mL/h), labelling each bag with start time, rate and expected finish time.

### Complications of IV therapy

| Complication | Signs | Nursing action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Infiltration** (non-irritant fluid into tissue) | Swelling, coolness, pallor, slow flow | Stop, remove, elevate limb, restart at another site |
| **Extravasation** (irritant/vesicant drug into tissue) | Pain, burning, blistering, tissue damage | Stop, inform at once, follow the extravasation protocol |
| **Phlebitis** (vein inflammation) | Redness, warmth, pain, red streak along the vein | Remove cannula, warm compress as ordered, record using a phlebitis score; resite |
| **Thrombophlebitis** | Phlebitis with a clot — hard cord-like vein | Remove, report, do not flush |
| **Haematoma** | Bruising, swelling at site | Pressure, remove |
| **Local infection** | Pus, redness | Remove, swab culture, report |
| **Septicaemia** | Fever, chills, low BP | Report urgently; cultures |
| **Fluid overload (circulatory overload)** | Breathlessness, crackles, raised BP, distended neck veins | Slow the infusion, sit up, report |
| **Air embolism** | Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, low BP, cyanosis | Clamp the line, place on **left side with head down**, oxygen, call for help |
| **Speed shock** (drug given too fast) | Flushing, headache, chest tightness, collapse | Stop and report; prevent by correct rates |
| **Catheter embolism** | Piece of cannula lost | Tourniquet above site as advised, report |

**Prevention**: aseptic technique, change sites and dressings as per policy, check the site every hour (or as per policy), use the correct rate, label all lines, do not let air in, use pumps for drugs where needed.

## 6.9 Measuring fluid intake and output

**Intake**: oral fluids (including semi-solids that melt such as ice cream, jelly), tube feeds, IV fluids, blood, irrigation fluid that is not returned.
**Output**: urine, vomitus, diarrhoea, drainage from wounds, drains and NG tubes, bleeding; insensible loss is estimated.

Use a measuring jug and the 24-hour intake–output chart; know the volume of hospital cups and glasses; total every shift and every 24 hours; report output below about 0.5 mL/kg/hour (about 30 mL/hour in an adult) or a large positive or negative balance.

## 6.10 Administering blood and blood components

**Components**: whole blood, packed red cells (anaemia, bleeding), platelet concentrate (low platelets), fresh frozen plasma (clotting factors), cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen, factor VIII), albumin.

**Before transfusion**
1. Valid prescription and **written informed consent**.
2. Correct sample for grouping and cross-matching, labelled at the bedside.
3. Baseline temperature, pulse, respiration, BP.
4. Wide-bore cannula (18–20 G for adults).
5. **Bedside check by two qualified persons**: patient's name, hospital number, blood group and Rh, bag number, expiry date, compatibility label against the request form and the patient's wristband.
6. Inspect the bag: no clots, discolouration, leaks or bubbles.

**During**
1. Use a **blood administration set with a filter**; prime with the blood or normal saline. Use **only normal saline** in the same line — dextrose causes clumping and haemolysis; calcium-containing fluids such as Ringer's lactate can cause clotting; never add medicines to blood.
2. Start slowly for the first 15 minutes and **stay with the patient** — most severe reactions appear early.
3. Check vital signs at the start, after 15 minutes, as per policy during, and at the end.
4. Complete a unit of red cells within the time limit in the policy (usually within 4 hours of removal from the refrigerator); do not warm blood except with an approved warmer.

**Transfusion reactions**

| Reaction | Signs |
| --- | --- |
| Acute haemolytic (ABO mismatch) | Fever, chills, back or loin pain, chest pain, low BP, dark urine, bleeding — can be fatal |
| Febrile non-haemolytic | Fever, chills |
| Allergic | Itching, urticaria, rarely anaphylaxis |
| Circulatory overload | Breathlessness, crackles, raised BP |
| Bacterial contamination | High fever, shock |
| Delayed (days later) | Jaundice, falling haemoglobin; infections |

**If a reaction occurs**: **stop the transfusion immediately**; keep the vein open with normal saline through a **new** set; inform the doctor and blood bank; check vital signs often; recheck the identification; send the blood bag, set and fresh blood and urine samples to the blood bank as per protocol; give treatment as ordered; document.

## 6.11 Restricting and enhancing fluid intake

**Restricting** (heart failure, kidney failure, SIADH): explain why; divide the allowed amount over the day (more during waking hours and with medicines); use small cups; offer ice chips (counted as fluid), frequent mouth care and lip moisturiser to relieve thirst; record accurately.

**Enhancing** (dehydration, fever, UTI, kidney stones, constipation): explain why; set hourly targets; offer preferred drinks within the diet; keep water within reach; offer fluids each time you visit; record intake.

## Remember

- Body water ≈ 60% of weight; ICF two-thirds, ECF one-third.
- Na+ 135–145; K+ 3.5–5.0 mEq/L; arterial pH 7.35–7.45; PaCO2 35–45 mmHg; HCO3– 22–26 mEq/L.
- Hypovolaemia: weak fast pulse, low BP, poor turgor, concentrated urine; overload: crackles, neck veins, weight gain.
- Potassium never as an IV push.
- ROME: respiratory — opposite; metabolic — equal.
- mL/h = volume ÷ hours; drops/min = volume × drop factor ÷ minutes.
- 1,000 mL in 8 h at 20 drops/mL ≈ 42 drops/min; micro-drip: drops/min = mL/h.
- Air embolism: clamp, left side head down, oxygen.
- Blood: two-person bedside check, normal saline only, start slow and stay 15 minutes, stop at once if a reaction occurs and keep the vein open with saline through a new set.
