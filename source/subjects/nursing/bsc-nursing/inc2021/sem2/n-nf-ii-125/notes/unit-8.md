# Unit 8: Sensory needs, care of the unconscious patient, and death and dying

INC Unit X (5 hours theory + 6 hours skill lab) — sensory experience, sensory alterations and their management, and care of the unconscious patient (with the Glasgow Coma Scale) — joined with INC Unit XI (4 hours theory + 6 hours skill lab) — loss, grief, theories of grief and dying, death, care of the dying, last office, mortuary procedures, and an overview of medico-legal cases, advance directives, DNR, organ donation and euthanasia.

## Part A: Sensory needs

## 8.1 Introduction and components of sensory experience

People need a steady, meaningful flow of sensory input to stay oriented and well. Illness, hospital environments and ageing can disturb it.

**Components of the sensory experience**

```text
Reception  →  Perception  →  Reaction
(stimulus     (brain gives    (the person
 reaches a     it meaning)     responds)
 receptor and
 nerve impulse
 travels)
```

- **Reception**: a stimulus (light, sound, touch, smell, taste, position) excites a sense receptor and is conducted to the brain.
- **Perception**: the brain integrates and interprets the stimulus, using memory and attention.
- **Reaction**: the person responds; the brain also filters out unimportant stimuli so that attention goes only to meaningful ones.

## 8.2 Arousal mechanism

The **reticular activating system (RAS)** in the brainstem receives sensory input and keeps the cerebral cortex awake and alert. Too little input lowers arousal; too much floods it. The RAS also controls the sleep–wake cycle — this is why noise and lights at night disturb patients.

## 8.3 Factors affecting sensory function

Age (presbyopia and hearing loss in the elderly; developing senses in infants), meaningful stimuli in the environment, amount of stimuli (ICU noise), social interaction, environment (isolation, darkness), culture, illness (diabetes affects vision and touch, stroke affects perception), medicines (some damage hearing; sedatives dull perception), and lifestyle (smoking dulls taste and smell, loud music damages hearing).

## 8.4 Assessment of sensory alterations

| Alteration | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sensory deficit** | Impaired reception or perception in one or more senses | Blindness, deafness, loss of touch after stroke, peripheral neuropathy |
| **Sensory deprivation** | Too little meaningful stimulation | Isolation rooms, bed rest, patching both eyes, elderly living alone |
| **Sensory overload** | Too many stimuli to process | ICU noise, alarms, constant procedures, pain |
| **Sensory poverty** | Long-term lack of varied, meaningful stimuli in daily life | Monotonous environment of long-stay institutions |

**Signs of deprivation**: boredom, restlessness, drowsiness, poor concentration, confusion, hallucinations, depression.
**Signs of overload**: anxiety, irritability, racing thoughts, poor sleep, disorientation, fatigue.

**Assessment**: history of sensory loss and aids used; mental status; vision, hearing, touch, taste, smell tests; the environment; ability to communicate; risk of injury.

## 8.5 Management of sensory alterations

- **Promote health**: screening (vision, hearing), protection from noise, eye protection at work, control of diabetes and blood pressure.
- **Prevent deprivation**: meaningful stimulation — conversation, radio, newspapers, clocks and calendars, family visits, pictures, touch, a window view; group activities.
- **Prevent overload**: reduce noise and light at night, cluster care to allow rest, explain procedures, limit visitors when needed, quiet room.
- **Safety**: orientation to the room, call bell within reach, night light, rails as needed, clear pathways, check bath water temperature for those with reduced touch.
- **Use and care of aids**: glasses, contact lenses, hearing aids (Unit 3).

### Promoting meaningful communication

| Patient | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| **Aphasia** (expressive — knows what to say but cannot say it; receptive — cannot understand speech; global — both) | Simple short sentences, one question at a time, yes/no questions, gestures, picture boards, give time, do not shout, do not finish sentences for them |
| **Artificial airway** (endotracheal tube, tracheostomy — cannot speak) | Writing board, letter or picture boards, call bell, yes/no questions with nods or blinks, lip-reading; speaking valves as ordered; explain that speech will return when the tube is out |
| **Visual impairment** | Announce yourself and say when you leave, speak in a normal tone, describe surroundings, use the clock method for meals, keep furniture in the same place, guide with the patient holding your elbow |
| **Hearing impairment** | Face the person in good light, get attention first, speak clearly and slightly slower (do not shout), reduce background noise, check the hearing aid, use writing or gestures, sign language interpreter if needed |

## Part B: Care of the unconscious patient

## 8.6 Definition, causes and risk factors

**Consciousness** is awareness of self and the environment with the ability to respond. **Unconsciousness** is an abnormal state in which the person is not aware of self or surroundings and cannot respond normally to stimuli; **coma** is the deepest state, from which the patient cannot be aroused even by painful stimuli.

**Causes (memory aid "AEIOU TIPS")**

| Letter | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| A | Alcohol, acidosis |
| E | Epilepsy (post-seizure), electrolyte imbalance, endocrine (thyroid, adrenal) |
| I | Insulin — hypoglycaemia or diabetic coma |
| O | Opioids and other overdose, oxygen lack (hypoxia) |
| U | Uraemia (kidney failure), liver failure |
| T | Trauma (head injury), temperature extremes |
| I | Infection (meningitis, encephalitis, sepsis) |
| P | Poisoning, psychiatric |
| S | Stroke, shock, space-occupying lesions (tumour, abscess) |

## 8.7 Pathophysiology

Consciousness needs the **RAS** in the brainstem and both cerebral hemispheres working. Unconsciousness results from (1) damage or pressure on the brainstem (bleeding, herniation from raised intracranial pressure), (2) widespread damage to both hemispheres, or (3) metabolic disturbances (low glucose, low oxygen, toxins) that depress neuronal function. Rising intracranial pressure reduces cerebral blood flow and can push brain tissue downward, compressing the brainstem.

## 8.8 Stages (levels) of consciousness

| Level | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Alert (conscious) | Awake, oriented, responds appropriately |
| Lethargy (drowsy) | Sleepy but wakes easily to voice; slow responses |
| Obtundation | Wakes with difficulty to loud voice or shaking; confused |
| Stupor (semi-coma) | Responds only to strong or painful stimuli; little speech |
| Coma | No response to any stimulus; deep coma — reflexes absent |

**Clinical manifestations**: reduced or absent response, abnormal breathing patterns (Cheyne–Stokes), changes in pupils (unequal, fixed, dilated in serious brain injury), abnormal posturing (decorticate — arms flexed; decerebrate — arms extended, worse sign), loss of protective reflexes (cough, gag, swallow, blink), incontinence, signs of raised intracranial pressure (headache, vomiting, rising BP with slow pulse and irregular breathing — **Cushing's triad**).

## 8.9 Assessment: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

| Response | Score |
| --- | --- |
| **Eye opening (E)** | |
| Spontaneously | 4 |
| To speech (sound) | 3 |
| To pain (pressure) | 2 |
| None | 1 |
| **Verbal response (V)** | |
| Oriented | 5 |
| Confused | 4 |
| Inappropriate words | 3 |
| Incomprehensible sounds | 2 |
| None | 1 |
| **Motor response (M)** | |
| Obeys commands | 6 |
| Localises pain | 5 |
| Withdraws from pain (normal flexion) | 4 |
| Abnormal flexion (decorticate) | 3 |
| Extension (decerebrate) | 2 |
| None | 1 |

Total 3–15. **15** = fully conscious; **13–15** minor, **9–12** moderate, **8 or less** severe impairment (coma — the airway is at risk). Record each part separately (for example E2 V2 M4 = 8). A **fall of 2 or more points** is reported urgently. Also check pupils, limb movements and vital signs at each neurological check.

## 8.10 Nursing management of the unconscious patient

| Need | Nursing measures (with reasons) |
| --- | --- |
| **Airway** | Lateral or semi-prone position with the head slightly down/turned (tongue falls forward, secretions drain); oropharyngeal airway; suction as needed; never give anything by mouth |
| **Breathing** | Monitor rate, pattern and SpO2; oxygen as prescribed; chest physiotherapy; turn regularly |
| **Circulation** | Vital signs; fluid balance; prevent deep vein thrombosis — limb exercises, stockings as ordered |
| **Neurological** | GCS and pupils at ordered intervals; head end raised about 30° if raised intracranial pressure (and no contraindication); avoid neck flexion |
| **Nutrition and fluids** | IV fluids, NG feeds (check position each time), record intake–output and weight |
| **Elimination** | Indwelling catheter or condom drainage as ordered, catheter care; bowel care to prevent constipation |
| **Skin** | Turn 2-hourly, pressure-relieving mattress, keep dry, inspect pressure points (Braden score) |
| **Hygiene** | Daily bed bath, oral care every 2–4 hours (side-lying, swabs), hair and nail care |
| **Eyes** | Keep lids closed (tape or moist pads as ordered), artificial tears as prescribed — blink reflex is lost and the cornea dries and ulcerates |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Passive range-of-motion exercises, correct alignment, foot support to prevent foot drop, splints as advised |
| **Safety** | Side rails, padding, seizure precautions, temperature control |
| **Communication** | Talk to the patient and explain every action (hearing may remain), use the name, encourage family to talk and touch |
| **Family** | Information, emotional support, involve in care |

**Complications to prevent**: aspiration pneumonia, airway obstruction, pressure ulcers, contractures and foot drop, deep vein thrombosis, urinary infection, corneal ulcer, constipation, malnutrition, dehydration.

## Part C: Care of the terminally ill, death and dying

## 8.11 Loss, grief, bereavement and mourning

**Loss**: being without something one had and valued.

| Type of loss | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Actual | Death of a loved one, amputation |
| Perceived | Loss of self-confidence, status |
| Maturational (developmental) | Children leaving home, retirement, menopause |
| Situational | Job loss, divorce, disaster, illness |
| Anticipatory | Grieving before an expected loss (terminal diagnosis) |

- **Grief**: the emotional response to loss.
- **Bereavement**: the state of having lost someone to death.
- **Mourning**: the outward, culturally shaped expression of grief (rituals, customs, period of mourning).

**Types of grief responses**

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Normal (uncomplicated) | Common reactions that ease with time |
| Anticipatory | Grief before the actual loss |
| Complicated (dysfunctional) | Prolonged or intense grief that disrupts life — chronic, delayed, exaggerated or masked |
| Disenfranchised | Grief not openly accepted by society (miscarriage, death of a partner in a hidden relationship, pet) |

**Manifestations of grief**: crying, sadness, anger, guilt, anxiety, loneliness, disbelief; loss of appetite, sleep disturbance, fatigue, tightness in the chest; poor concentration, preoccupation with the deceased; social withdrawal.

**Factors influencing loss and grief responses**: age and development, relationship with the deceased, nature of the death (sudden, violent, suicide), personality and coping, social support, culture and religion, previous losses, socio-economic status.

## 8.12 Theories of grief and loss

**Kübler-Ross — five stages of dying**

| Stage | Typical words or behaviour | Nursing approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Denial | "Not me — the report must be wrong." | Accept without arguing or reinforcing; listen |
| Anger | "Why me?" — anger at staff, family, God | Do not take it personally; let feelings be expressed |
| Bargaining | "If I get better I will…" | Listen; support realistic hopes |
| Depression | Withdrawn, sad, crying | Be present, allow grief, touch, avoid false cheerfulness |
| Acceptance | Calm, making plans, saying goodbyes | Support wishes, involve family, ensure comfort |

The stages do not always follow this order; people move back and forth.

**Rando's "R" process model** — six processes of mourning:
1. **Recognise** the loss (acknowledge and understand the death).
2. **React** to the separation (feel and express the pain).
3. **Recollect** and re-experience the deceased and the relationship.
4. **Relinquish** old attachments to the deceased and the old assumptions about the world.
5. **Readjust** to move adaptively into the new world without forgetting the old.
6. **Reinvest** emotional energy in new relationships and goals.

## 8.13 Death

**Definition**: the permanent end of life.

- **Circulatory (cardiopulmonary) death**: irreversible stopping of heartbeat and breathing.
- **Brain death (brainstem death)**: irreversible loss of all brain function including the brainstem, while breathing and circulation may be kept going by machines. In India, brainstem death for organ donation is certified by a board of medical experts under the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act and Rules, with tests repeated after a set interval (check the current Rules for the board's composition and the interval).

**Signs of impending death**: cold, mottled, bluish extremities; weak, rapid, irregular pulse and falling BP; noisy breathing from secretions ("death rattle"), Cheyne–Stokes breathing, periods of apnoea; decreased urine output; reduced consciousness; loss of swallowing; relaxed jaw; staring or glazed eyes; hearing often remains till late.

## 8.14 The dying patient's Bill of Rights (in summary)

The dying person has the right to: be treated as a living human being until death; keep hope; be cared for by those who maintain hope; express feelings and emotions about death in their own way; take part in decisions about care; expect continuing medical and nursing attention even when cure is no longer possible; not die alone; be free from pain; have honest answers to questions; not be deceived; receive help from and for the family in accepting death; die in peace and dignity; keep their individuality and not be judged for decisions; discuss and enlarge religious or spiritual experiences; expect that the body will be respected after death; and be cared for by caring, sensitive, knowledgeable people.

## 8.15 Care of the dying patient

- **Physical comfort**: pain relief as prescribed (regular, not only "when needed"), position change, mouth care (dry mouth), eye care, skin care, managing breathlessness (upright position, fan, oxygen if helpful), managing noisy secretions (positioning, gentle suction if needed), small sips and favourite foods, elimination care.
- **Psychological**: be present, listen, allow expression of fear and sadness, honest answers.
- **Spiritual**: arrange prayers, religious leader and rituals as wished.
- **Family**: open visiting, allow them to help in care, explain changes, prepare them for death, privacy.
- **Palliative care approach**: improves quality of life for patients and families facing life-limiting illness, through relief of pain and other physical, psychological, social and spiritual problems.

## 8.16 Physiological changes after death

| Change | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Algor mortis** | Gradual cooling of the body to room temperature |
| **Livor mortis** (post-mortem hypostasis) | Purple discolouration of dependent parts as blood settles by gravity; becomes fixed after some hours |
| **Rigor mortis** | Stiffening of muscles that begins a few hours after death in small muscles (eyelids, jaw), spreads to the whole body and later passes off; timing varies with temperature |
| Tissue softening, decomposition | Later changes by bacterial action, faster in heat |

Last office is therefore done soon after death, **before rigor mortis sets in**, so the body can be positioned naturally.

## 8.17 Death declaration, certification, autopsy and embalming

- **Declaration** of death is made by a doctor after examination (absence of pulse, heart sounds, breathing, fixed dilated pupils); the time is recorded.
- **Certification**: the doctor issues the medical certificate of cause of death (MCCD) under the Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969; the death must be registered with the local registrar (the hospital reports institutional deaths).
- **Autopsy (post-mortem examination)**: examination of the body after death to find the cause. **Medico-legal (police) autopsy** is compulsory in unnatural or suspicious deaths and is ordered by police or magistrate; **clinical (hospital) autopsy** needs the consent of the next of kin. For medico-legal cases, do not remove tubes, lines or dressings and preserve all evidence.
- **Embalming**: preserving the body with chemicals to delay decomposition, done by trained persons — for example when the body must be transported over a long distance or kept for a longer period.

## 8.18 Last office (death care)

**Purposes**: to keep the body clean and in a natural position; to prevent discharge and odour; to show respect; to identify the body correctly; to support the relatives.

**Main steps**
1. Confirm that death has been declared and recorded; inform the nurse in charge; check for any religious or cultural wishes and whether it is a medico-legal case or organ/eye donation (eye donation must happen within a few hours).
2. Allow the family time with the body in privacy.
3. Wear gloves and apron (standard precautions).
4. Lay the body flat, one pillow under the head (prevents blood pooling and discolouring the face); close the eyes gently with moist cotton; close the mouth (support the jaw with a rolled towel or bandage); replace dentures if possible.
5. Remove tubes, catheters and lines (unless medico-legal); cover wounds with clean dressings; pack orifices only where policy requires.
6. Bathe as needed, comb hair, put on a clean gown or as the family wishes.
7. List valuables and belongings with a witness and hand them over to the relatives against signature; leave the wedding ring or religious articles if the family wishes, taped and recorded.
8. Attach **identification labels** (name, age, sex, hospital number, ward, date and time of death) — usually to the wrist or ankle and on the outer wrap.
9. Wrap in a clean sheet (and a body bag if infectious), straighten the limbs, hands by the side.
10. Shift to the mortuary as per policy; clean and disinfect the unit; complete the records.

## 8.19 Counselling and supporting grieving relatives

Inform with sensitivity in a private room; use simple, clear words ("He has died"); allow expression of grief; stay with them; answer questions honestly; help with practical matters (formalities, belongings, transport); respect cultural and religious practices; give information about support services; recognise signs of complicated grief and refer.

## 8.20 Mortuary: placing and releasing the body

- **Placing**: the body is shifted on a covered trolley through a route that avoids public areas; identity checked and entered in the mortuary register with time; kept in a cold chamber.
- **Releasing**: verify the identity of the body and the person receiving it (relationship, ID proof); check that the death certificate is issued and, in medico-legal cases, police clearance and post-mortem are complete; hand over belongings; record the time and signatures of the receiving person and the staff.

## 8.21 Overview: medico-legal and ethical issues at the end of life

- **Medico-legal cases (MLC)**: injuries from road accidents, assault, burns, poisoning, suicide attempts, unnatural or suspicious deaths. Inform the police through the hospital's MLC procedure, record accurately, preserve evidence (clothes, bullets, poison samples, gastric contents), keep records safely.
- **Advance directive (living will)**: a written statement made by a competent adult about the treatment they want or refuse if they later lose the capacity to decide. In India the Supreme Court recognised living wills and passive euthanasia in *Common Cause v. Union of India* (2018) with procedural safeguards (later simplified by the court in 2023).
- **DNR / DNI (do not resuscitate / do not intubate)**: a written medical order, after discussion with the patient or family, that CPR or intubation will not be done if the heart or breathing stops. All other care continues.
- **Organ donation**: governed by the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act; donors may be living (close relatives, with authorisation committees in other cases) or deceased (after brainstem death, with consent of the family). Eye (cornea) donation is possible after circulatory death within a few hours. NOTTO is the national body co-ordinating organ donation. The nurse identifies potential donors, informs the transplant co-ordinator, and supports the family.
- **Euthanasia**: deliberately ending life to relieve suffering. **Active euthanasia** (giving a substance to cause death) is illegal in India. **Passive euthanasia** (withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment) is permitted only under the procedure laid down by the Supreme Court (Aruna Shanbaug case, 2011, and Common Cause, 2018).

## Remember

- Sensory experience: reception → perception → reaction; the RAS keeps us alert.
- Deficit (organ problem), deprivation (too little), overload (too much), poverty (long-term lack of variety).
- Aphasia: short simple sentences, yes/no questions, picture boards, give time.
- GCS: E4 V5 M6 = 15; 8 or less = coma; a fall of 2 points is urgent.
- Unconscious patient: lateral position, nothing by mouth, eyes closed and moist, turn 2-hourly, talk to the patient.
- Kübler-Ross: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance.
- Rando: recognise, react, recollect, relinquish, readjust, reinvest.
- Algor (cooling), livor (pooling), rigor (stiffening) — do last office before rigor sets in.
- MLC: do not remove tubes or dressings; preserve evidence.
- Active euthanasia is illegal in India; passive euthanasia and living wills only under Supreme Court guidelines.
