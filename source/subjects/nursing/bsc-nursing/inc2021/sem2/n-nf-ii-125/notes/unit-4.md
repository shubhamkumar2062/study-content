# Unit 4: Elimination needs and diagnostic testing

INC Unit V (10 hours theory + 10 hours skill lab) — urinary and bowel elimination, catheter care, enemas, suppositories, bowel wash, digital evacuation and ostomy care — joined with INC Unit VI (3 hours theory + 4 hours skill lab) — phases of diagnostic testing, common investigations with normal values, specimen collection and an overview of radiological and endoscopic procedures.

## Part A: Urinary elimination

## 4.1 Review: physiology and characteristics of urine

The kidneys filter blood, reabsorb what the body needs and excrete waste as urine. Urine passes through the ureters to the bladder. When about 200–400 mL collects, stretch receptors send signals to the spinal cord (S2–S4); the detrusor contracts and the internal sphincter relaxes; voluntary relaxation of the external sphincter allows **micturition**.

| Feature | Normal |
| --- | --- |
| Amount | About 1–2 litres a day in adults (about 0.5 mL/kg/hour or more) |
| Colour | Pale yellow to amber (from urochrome) |
| Clarity | Clear when fresh |
| Odour | Faintly aromatic; ammonia smell on standing |
| Reaction (pH) | Slightly acidic, average about 6 (range about 4.5–8) |
| Specific gravity | About 1.005–1.030 |
| Abnormal constituents | No glucose, protein, ketones, blood or pus cells |

## 4.2 Factors influencing urination

Fluid intake, diet (caffeine, alcohol increase urine), muscle tone, psychological factors (anxiety, lack of privacy), posture (men usually stand), age (enuresis in children, frequency in the elderly), pregnancy, surgery and anaesthesia, pain, medicines (diuretics increase, some sedatives cause retention), and diseases of kidney, bladder, prostate or nerves.

## 4.3 Alterations in urinary elimination

| Alteration | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Polyuria | Large volume (more than about 2.5–3 L a day) |
| Oliguria | Low output (less than about 400 mL a day) |
| Anuria | Almost no urine (less than about 100 mL a day) |
| Frequency | Voiding often in small amounts |
| Urgency | Sudden strong need to void |
| Dysuria | Painful or burning micturition |
| Nocturia | Waking at night to void |
| Haematuria | Blood in urine |
| Urinary retention | Bladder full but cannot empty |
| Enuresis | Involuntary voiding, often at night in children |
| Urinary incontinence | Loss of control: **stress** (with coughing, sneezing), **urge** (cannot hold after urge), **overflow** (dribbling from an over-full bladder), **functional** (cannot reach toilet in time — mobility or confusion), **reflex** (no sensation, as in spinal injury) |

**Nursing measures to help a patient void (before catheterising)**: privacy, normal position (sitting or standing if allowed), warm bedpan, sound of running water, pouring warm water over the perineum, warm drinks if allowed, relieving pain and anxiety. Catheterise only when these fail and it is ordered.

## 4.4 Facilitating urine elimination

### Providing a bedpan or urinal
1. Explain, screen, wash hands, wear gloves; warm the bedpan.
2. Ask the patient to raise the hips (or roll to the side, place the pan against the buttocks and roll back); place it with the broad end under the buttocks.
3. Raise the head end (unless contraindicated) — a more natural position.
4. Give toilet paper and the call bell; leave if safe.
5. Remove, clean the perineum front to back, offer hand washing, observe and measure the contents, clean the pan and record.

### Condom (external) catheter
For incontinent men with normal bladder emptying. Clean and dry the penis, roll the sheath on leaving 2–5 cm space at the tip, secure without being tight (risk of cutting off circulation), connect to a drainage bag, change daily and inspect the skin.

### Intermittent catheterisation
The bladder is drained with a straight catheter that is then removed — used for retention, residual urine measurement and sterile specimens, and as **clean intermittent self-catheterisation** in neurogenic bladder (spinal injury) at regular intervals. Lower infection rate than an indwelling catheter.

### Indwelling (retention) catheter — insertion

**Equipment**: sterile catheterisation tray, Foley catheter of the right size (commonly 14–16 Fr for adults), sterile gloves, antiseptic or cleansing solution, sterile lubricant (anaesthetic gel as prescribed for men), syringe with sterile water to inflate the balloon (amount printed on the catheter), closed drainage bag, drapes, light.

**Steps with reasons**

1. Check the order, identify, explain, consent, privacy — dignity and co-operation.
2. Position: female — dorsal recumbent with knees flexed and apart; male — supine with legs slightly apart.
3. Perineal cleaning with soap and water, then hand hygiene.
4. Open the tray; sterile gloves; drape; test the balloon (if policy); lubricate.
5. **Female**: separate the labia with the non-dominant hand (which now stays there), clean the meatus front to back with one swab per stroke, insert about 5–7.5 cm until urine flows, then a little further.
   **Male**: hold the penis upright at about 90° to straighten the urethra, retract the foreskin, clean in circles from the meatus outward, insert about 17–22 cm until urine flows, then advance to the bifurcation; replace the foreskin.
6. Inflate the balloon only after urine flows (inflating in the urethra causes injury); gently pull back until resistance is felt.
7. Connect to a closed drainage system; secure the catheter to the thigh (female) or lower abdomen/thigh (male) — prevents pulling on the bladder neck.
8. Hang the bag below bladder level, off the floor — prevents backflow and infection.
9. Record date, size, balloon volume, amount and nature of urine.

### Care of a patient with an indwelling catheter and urinary drainage
- Keep the drainage **closed** — do not disconnect.
- Bag below bladder level; empty when two-thirds full using a clean container for each patient; no kinks.
- Meatal care daily and after bowel movements.
- Encourage fluids (unless restricted) — flushes the bladder.
- Measure output; observe colour, clarity, sediment, blood.
- Take specimens from the sampling port with a sterile syringe after cleaning the port.
- Remove as soon as no longer needed — **catheter-associated urinary tract infection** risk rises each day.
- Removal: deflate the balloon fully with a syringe, withdraw gently, record the time, and check that the patient passes urine within about 6–8 hours.

### Urinary diversions
Surgical re-routing of urine when the bladder is removed or diseased — **ileal conduit** (ureters into a segment of ileum that opens as a stoma), **ureterostomy**, **nephrostomy** (tube from the kidney through the skin), **suprapubic catheter**. Care: clean, well-fitting appliance; protect the skin; urine will be continuous; watch stoma colour (pink-red, moist) and urine output; encourage fluids.

### Bladder irrigation
Washing the bladder with sterile solution through a catheter to remove clots, debris or to instil medicine as prescribed. **Continuous bladder irrigation** (common after prostate surgery) uses a three-way catheter: one channel for the balloon, one for inflow, one for outflow. Use strict asepsis; keep inflow running at the ordered rate; output must exceed inflow — **true urine = total output − irrigation fluid**; watch for clots, bladder spasm and blocked outflow.

## Part B: Bowel elimination

## 4.5 Review: physiology and characteristics of faeces

Peristalsis moves contents through the colon, where water is absorbed. Mass movements (often after meals — the gastrocolic reflex) carry faeces into the rectum; stretch causes the urge to defaecate; relaxation of the anal sphincters and abdominal straining (Valsalva manoeuvre) expel the stool.

**Normal faeces**: brown (stercobilin), formed and soft, about 100–200 g a day in adults on a mixed diet, characteristic odour. Frequency varies from about three times a day to three times a week. Colour changes: clay-coloured (bile obstruction), black tarry (upper GI bleeding or iron tablets), red streaks (lower bleeding, haemorrhoids), mucus or pus (inflammation).

## 4.6 Factors affecting bowel elimination
Age, diet (fibre), fluid intake, activity, privacy and routine, psychological factors, pain (haemorrhoids, surgery), pregnancy, medicines (opioids constipate; antibiotics may cause diarrhoea), surgery and anaesthesia, diagnostic tests (barium), and disease.

## 4.7 Alterations in bowel elimination
- **Constipation**: infrequent, hard, dry stools passed with difficulty.
- **Faecal impaction**: a hard mass that cannot be passed; liquid stool may leak around it.
- **Diarrhoea**: frequent loose watery stools — risk of dehydration and skin breakdown.
- **Faecal incontinence**: loss of control over passage of stool.
- **Flatulence**: excess gas causing distension and discomfort.
- **Haemorrhoids**: dilated veins in the anal canal.

The **Bristol Stool Chart** (types 1–7) helps describe stool form.

## 4.8 Facilitating bowel elimination

### Enemas
An **enema** is the introduction of fluid into the rectum and colon through the anus.

| Type | Purpose | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cleansing — large volume (tap water, normal saline) | Empty the bowel before surgery, tests; constipation | Normal saline is safest; tap water repeated many times can upset fluid balance |
| Cleansing — small volume, ready-made (hypertonic) | Constipation, bowel preparation | Draws water into the bowel |
| Retention (oil) | Softens hard stool; retained 30 minutes or more | Followed by a cleansing enema |
| Carminative | Relieves flatulence | |
| Medicated | Gives a drug as prescribed | |
| Return-flow (Harris flush) | Removes flatus | Fluid in and out repeatedly |

**Procedure: cleansing enema (main steps)**

1. Check the order, explain, privacy, keep a bedpan and commode ready.
2. Position in the **left lateral (Sims') position** with the right knee flexed — the sigmoid colon and rectum lie on the left, so fluid flows in by gravity.
3. Warm the solution to about body temperature or slightly above (around 40 °C, checked with a thermometer) — cold fluid causes cramps; hot fluid burns.
4. Lubricate the tip; expel air from the tubing.
5. Ask the patient to breathe slowly through the mouth (relaxes the sphincter); insert the tube gently towards the umbilicus about 7.5–10 cm in adults.
6. Raise the can about 30–45 cm above the anus — low height gives slow flow and less cramping.
7. Let fluid flow slowly; if cramps occur, lower the can or clamp briefly.
8. Remove the tube; ask the patient to hold the fluid for 5–15 minutes as tolerated.
9. Help to the toilet, commode or bedpan; observe and record the result.

**Contraindications**: suspected appendicitis or intestinal obstruction, recent rectal or bowel surgery (unless ordered), severe bleeding, some cardiac conditions; use care in the elderly and children.

### Suppository
A cone-shaped drug form that melts at body temperature.
1. Left lateral position; gloves; unwrap and lubricate the tip.
2. Ask the patient to breathe through the mouth.
3. Insert with the index finger, tapered end first, along the rectal wall beyond the internal sphincter (about a finger's length in adults, about 2–3 cm deeper than the sphincter); not into a mass of stool, because it must touch the mucosa.
4. Ask the patient to retain it for the time stated (often 15–30 minutes for laxative suppositories).

### Bowel wash
Repeated washing of the large bowel with fluid until the return is clear — before bowel surgery or investigations, or to remove poisons as ordered. Done with a rectal tube and funnel: fluid poured in by gravity, then the funnel lowered over a bucket to siphon it out. Record the amount in and out.

### Digital evacuation of impacted faeces
Removing hard faeces with a gloved, lubricated finger, done only with an order. Insert one finger, gently break up the mass and remove pieces. Stop if the patient becomes faint or the pulse falls — rectal stimulation can trigger the **vagal reflex** (bradycardia). Follow with an enema if ordered.

### Care of patients with ostomies (bowel diversions)
An **ostomy** is a surgical opening; the **stoma** is the part seen on the abdomen.

- **Colostomy**: opening of the colon; stool from formed (sigmoid) to semi-liquid (ascending).
- **Ileostomy**: opening of the ileum; liquid, continuous, enzyme-rich output that irritates the skin.
- **Temporary** (loop) or **permanent** (end).

**Care**
1. A healthy stoma is moist and pink-red; report a dark, purple or black stoma (poor blood supply).
2. Change the pouch when it leaks or as recommended; empty it when one-third to half full.
3. Clean the skin with warm water, dry; measure the stoma and cut the flange opening about 2–3 mm larger than the stoma — protects skin without pressing on the stoma.
4. Use skin barrier; watch for excoriation, prolapse, retraction, stenosis.
5. Diet: chew well; for ileostomy, plenty of fluids and salt as advised, avoid high-fibre foods that can block the stoma.
6. Teach self-care and support body image; refer to support groups.

## Part C: Diagnostic testing

## 4.9 Phases of diagnostic testing

| Phase | Nurse's role |
| --- | --- |
| **Pre-test** | Check the order and consent; explain the test; preparation (fasting, diet, bowel preparation, full or empty bladder); check allergies (iodine contrast), pregnancy, medicines; correct container and label |
| **Intra-test** | Position and support the patient; maintain asepsis; collect the specimen correctly; observe for reactions |
| **Post-test** | Label and send the specimen promptly with the request form; care of the puncture site; observe for complications; resume diet when allowed; record and report results, and tell the doctor about abnormal values |

## 4.10 Common investigations and their normal values

Laboratory ranges differ slightly between laboratories; always read the reference range printed on the report.

| Test | Approximate adult normal value | Clinical meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemoglobin | Men 13–17 g/dL; women 12–15 g/dL | Low = anaemia |
| Total WBC count | 4,000–11,000 per mm³ | High = infection, leukaemia; low = marrow suppression |
| Platelets | 1.5–4.5 lakh per mm³ | Low = bleeding risk |
| Serum sodium | 135–145 mEq/L | Fluid and electrolyte status |
| Serum potassium | 3.5–5.0 mEq/L | Too high or too low affects the heart |
| Serum chloride | About 96–106 mEq/L | |
| Total bilirubin (LFT) | About 0.3–1.2 mg/dL | High = jaundice |
| ALT and AST (LFT) | Up to about 40 U/L (varies) | High = liver cell injury |
| Serum albumin | 3.5–5.0 g/dL | Low = malnutrition, liver or kidney disease |
| Total cholesterol | Desirable below 200 mg/dL | Heart disease risk |
| LDL cholesterol | Optimal below 100 mg/dL | "Bad" cholesterol |
| HDL cholesterol | Above 40 (men), above 50 (women) mg/dL | "Good" cholesterol |
| Triglycerides | Below 150 mg/dL | |
| Fasting blood glucose (AC) | 70–99 mg/dL; 126 or above on two occasions suggests diabetes | |
| 2-hour post-meal glucose (PC) | Below 140 mg/dL; 200 or above suggests diabetes | |
| HbA1c | Below 5.7%; 5.7–6.4% prediabetes; 6.5% or above diabetes | Average sugar over about 3 months |
| Serum creatinine | About 0.6–1.2 mg/dL | Kidney function |

### Monitoring capillary blood glucose (glucometer random blood sugar — GRBS)
1. Check the meter, strip code and expiry; explain.
2. Wash the patient's hands with warm water and dry (sugar on fingers gives a false high; warmth increases blood flow).
3. Use the side of the fingertip (fewer nerve endings than the pad); rotate sites.
4. Prick with a single-use lancet; wipe away the first drop if policy says so; apply the drop to the strip.
5. Press the site with cotton; read and record; dispose of the lancet in the sharps container.
6. Report values outside the ordered range; follow the sliding-scale order if one exists.

### Stool routine examination
Collect a small fresh sample (about the size of a walnut, or a few spoonfuls if liquid) in a clean, dry container with a spoon; avoid mixing with urine or water; send promptly (warm, fresh specimen for amoebae). Tests: ova and cysts, occult blood, colour, consistency, mucus.

### Urine testing
- **Routine specimen**: a fresh sample, preferably the first morning (concentrated) in a clean container.
- **Midstream clean-catch specimen** (for culture): clean the meatus, pass the first part into the toilet, collect the middle part in a **sterile** container without touching the inside, send within about an hour or refrigerate.
- **Catheter specimen**: from the sampling port with a sterile syringe — never from the bag.
- **Timed (24-hour) specimen**: discard the first void at the start time, collect all urine for 24 hours including the last void at the end, keep the container cool and with any preservative supplied, label times.
- **Reagent strip (dipstick) tests**: dip a fresh sample, remove excess, read each pad against the colour chart at the stated time — glucose, albumin (protein), acetone (ketones), pH, specific gravity, blood. Ketones suggest uncontrolled diabetes or starvation; albumin suggests kidney disease or pre-eclampsia.

### Sputum culture
Early morning specimen (secretions collect overnight), before antibiotics. Rinse mouth with water (not antiseptic), take deep breaths and cough from deep in the chest into a sterile container; sputum, not saliva. Send promptly. For tuberculosis, follow the programme's instructions on the number of samples.

## 4.11 Overview of radiological and endoscopic procedures

| Procedure | Purpose | Nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain X-ray | Bones, chest, abdomen | Remove metal objects; ask about pregnancy |
| Contrast studies (barium swallow, meal, enema; IVP) | Outline hollow organs | Fasting and bowel preparation as ordered; check allergy for iodine contrast; after barium, fluids and laxative to clear it (white stools) |
| Ultrasound | Organs, pregnancy | Abdomen — fasting; pelvis — full bladder |
| CT scan | Cross-section images | Contrast allergy and kidney function check; remain still |
| MRI | Soft tissues, brain, spine | No metal (pacemakers, implants, oxygen cylinders); claustrophobia; noisy |
| Endoscopy (gastroscopy, colonoscopy, bronchoscopy, cystoscopy) | Look inside, biopsy | Consent; nil by mouth before upper endoscopy (usually 6–8 h); bowel preparation for colonoscopy; after upper GI endoscopy or bronchoscopy, nothing by mouth until the gag reflex returns; watch for bleeding, pain, perforation |

## Remember

- Normal urine 1–2 L/day; oliguria under 400 mL; anuria under 100 mL; SG 1.005–1.030.
- Try nursing measures before catheterising; catheterise with strict asepsis; inflate the balloon only after urine flows.
- Closed drainage, bag below bladder, remove early — prevents CAUTI.
- Bladder irrigation: true urine = output − irrigant.
- Enema: left lateral, fluid warm (about 40 °C), can 30–45 cm high, tube 7.5–10 cm.
- Digital evacuation can slow the heart (vagal reflex).
- Stoma healthy = moist and pink-red; flange 2–3 mm larger than the stoma.
- Diagnostic testing has pre-, intra- and post-test phases.
- Fasting glucose 70–99 mg/dL; HbA1c 6.5% or more = diabetes; K+ 3.5–5.0; Na+ 135–145 mEq/L.
- Midstream specimen in a sterile container; catheter specimen from the port, never the bag; sputum early morning before antibiotics.
