# Unit 5: Oxygenation needs

INC Unit VII (11 hours theory + 10 hours skill lab): review of cardiovascular and respiratory physiology, factors and conditions affecting breathing, alterations in oxygenation, and nursing interventions — airway care, oxygen administration, suctioning, chest physiotherapy, chest drainage, pulse oximetry, and restorative care (hydration, humidification, coughing, breathing exercises, incentive spirometry).

## 5.1 Review of cardiovascular and respiratory physiology

Oxygenation depends on four processes working together:

```text
1. Ventilation       air moves in and out of the lungs
2. Diffusion         O2 and CO2 cross the alveolar–capillary membrane
3. Transport         O2 carried by haemoglobin (about 97%) and dissolved in plasma
4. Perfusion         the heart pumps oxygenated blood to the tissues
```

- **Ventilation** needs a clear airway, a working diaphragm and intercostal muscles, elastic lungs and **surfactant** (keeps alveoli open), and a normal respiratory centre (medulla and pons).
- **Control**: the main stimulus to breathe is a rise in CO2 (detected by central chemoreceptors); low oxygen is sensed by peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies. In some people with long-standing CO2 retention, the low-oxygen drive becomes more important — high oxygen must then be given carefully as prescribed.
- **Diffusion** depends on the surface area and thickness of the membrane and the pressure difference of gases.
- **Transport**: each haemoglobin molecule carries up to four O2 molecules; anaemia reduces carrying capacity even when SpO2 is normal.
- **Cardiac output** = stroke volume × heart rate; a failing heart reduces delivery of oxygen.

Normal values: respiratory rate 12–20/min in adults; SpO2 95–100% (often 88–92% is the prescribed target in chronic CO2-retaining lung disease); arterial PaO2 about 80–100 mmHg; PaCO2 35–45 mmHg.

## 5.2 Factors affecting respiratory functioning

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Age | Infants have small airways; the elderly have stiffer chest walls and weaker cough |
| Environment | Altitude (less oxygen), pollution, heat, allergens |
| Lifestyle | Smoking damages cilia and airways; exercise improves capacity |
| Obesity, pregnancy | Limit diaphragm movement |
| Posture and immobility | Lying flat reduces lung expansion; secretions pool |
| Anxiety and pain | Rapid shallow breathing; post-operative pain stops deep breathing |
| Medicines | Opioids and sedatives depress breathing |
| Anaemia, heart disease | Reduce transport and perfusion |
| Nutrition | Weak respiratory muscles in malnutrition |

## 5.3 Alterations in respiratory functioning — conditions affecting

| Process | Conditions |
| --- | --- |
| **Airway** | Tongue falling back in unconscious patients, secretions, foreign body, asthma (bronchospasm), tumour, oedema of larynx |
| **Movement of air** | Weak or paralysed muscles (spinal injury, Guillain–Barré syndrome), chest injury, pain, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, obesity, CNS depression |
| **Diffusion** | Pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, pulmonary fibrosis, emphysema (loss of surface area) |
| **Oxygen transport** | Anaemia, carbon monoxide poisoning, low cardiac output, shock |

## 5.4 Alterations in oxygenation

- **Hypoxaemia**: low oxygen in arterial blood.
- **Hypoxia**: inadequate oxygen at the tissue level. Early signs — restlessness, anxiety, confusion, rapid pulse and breathing, raised BP; late signs — cyanosis, falling BP, slow pulse, drowsiness.
- **Cyanosis**: bluish colour of skin and mucous membranes; a late sign and may be absent in severe anaemia.
- **Hypercapnia**: raised CO2 — headache, flushing, drowsiness, confusion.
- **Hyperventilation, hypoventilation**.
- **Dyspnoea**: difficult breathing; **orthopnoea**: breathless when lying flat (relieved by sitting up — common in heart failure).
- **Breathing patterns**: tachypnoea, bradypnoea, apnoea, Cheyne–Stokes (cycles of deepening then shallowing breaths with apnoea — seen near death and in brain injury), Kussmaul (deep, rapid — metabolic acidosis such as diabetic ketoacidosis).

## 5.5 Nursing assessment for oxygenation
History of cough (dry or productive), sputum (amount, colour, blood), breathlessness (when it occurs, how many pillows), chest pain, smoking, occupation; inspection of rate, rhythm, depth, chest movement, accessory muscles, flaring of nostrils, clubbing, cyanosis; auscultation; SpO2; investigations (ABG, chest X-ray, sputum, pulmonary function tests).

## 5.6 Maintenance of a patent airway
- **Positioning**: Fowler's or semi-Fowler's for conscious breathless patients (lets the diaphragm descend); **lateral (recovery) position** for unconscious patients (tongue falls forward, secretions drain out).
- **Head-tilt chin-lift** (or **jaw thrust** if neck injury is suspected) opens the airway.
- **Airway adjuncts**: oropharyngeal (Guedel) airway — size from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw, only in unconscious patients without a gag reflex; nasopharyngeal airway.
- Suction of secretions, coughing and hydration.
- Advanced airways (endotracheal tube, tracheostomy) are cared for with suctioning, humidification, cuff care and stoma care.

## 5.7 Oxygen administration

Oxygen is a medicine: it is given **on prescription** (except in an emergency as per protocol), with flow rate or target saturation written.

**Indications**: hypoxaemia, respiratory distress, shock, heart attack with low saturation, carbon monoxide poisoning, during and after anaesthesia.

**Sources**: central (piped) supply, oxygen cylinders (in India, medical oxygen cylinders are black with a white shoulder), oxygen concentrators.

**Equipment**: flow meter, humidifier with sterile water (filled to the mark), delivery device, tubing, "No smoking" sign.

**Delivery devices (typical device ranges)**

| Device | Typical flow | Approximate oxygen delivered | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nasal cannula (prongs) | 1–6 L/min | About 24–44% | Comfortable, patient can eat and talk |
| Simple face mask | 5–10 L/min | About 40–60% | At least 5 L/min to flush exhaled CO2 |
| Venturi mask | Set by colour-coded valve | Fixed, precise (about 24–60%) | Preferred where an exact concentration is needed (chronic lung disease) |
| Non-rebreather mask (with reservoir bag) | 10–15 L/min | High (about 60–90%) | Bag must stay inflated |
| Partial rebreather mask | 6–10 L/min | About 40–70% | |
| Oxygen tent, hood, head box | Varies | | Children and infants |

**Procedure (nasal cannula/mask) — main steps with reasons**

1. Check the prescription; identify the patient; explain; place in semi-Fowler's.
2. Check SpO2, respiration and colour before starting — baseline.
3. Fill the humidifier with sterile water to the mark — dry oxygen dries and irritates the mucosa (humidification is especially needed at higher flows).
4. Connect the device and set the prescribed flow; check that oxygen is flowing.
5. Fit the prongs into the nostrils (curve downward) or the mask over the nose and mouth; adjust the elastic.
6. Pad the ears and cheeks — prevents pressure sores.
7. Recheck SpO2 and breathing; record time, device, flow and response.
8. Give mouth and nose care; check the humidifier water level, tubing for kinks.

**Safety precautions**
- No smoking, open flames, lighted lamps or sparks near oxygen — oxygen supports combustion.
- Avoid oil, grease or alcohol-based products near the equipment.
- Secure cylinders upright on a stand; keep away from heat; check the content before transport.
- Use cotton linen and avoid static electricity.
- Do not give more oxygen than prescribed; watch for **oxygen toxicity** (prolonged high concentrations) and, in premature infants, retinopathy of prematurity.

## 5.8 Suctioning

**Purpose**: remove secretions the patient cannot cough out, to keep the airway clear.

**Types**: oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal (clean technique), tracheal through an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy (sterile technique), open or closed (in-line) systems.

**Main steps with reasons**
1. Assess the need (gurgling, crackles, falling SpO2, visible secretions); explain; position semi-Fowler's (conscious) or lateral (unconscious).
2. Pre-oxygenate as prescribed (especially for tracheal suction) — suction removes oxygen too.
3. Check suction pressure — commonly about 100–150 mmHg for adults on wall suction (lower for children); higher pressure damages the mucosa.
4. Choose a catheter not wider than half the internal diameter of the tube; sterile gloves for tracheal suction.
5. Insert **without** applying suction; apply suction only while withdrawing with a rotating movement.
6. Limit each pass to about 10–15 seconds — longer passes cause hypoxia and bradycardia.
7. Let the patient rest and re-oxygenate between passes; maximum two to three passes.
8. Flush the tubing; observe and record amount, colour and consistency of secretions; check SpO2.

**Complications**: hypoxia, mucosal trauma and bleeding, infection, cardiac arrhythmias (vagal stimulation), raised intracranial pressure, laryngospasm.

## 5.9 Chest physiotherapy

Used to loosen and drain secretions — bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, lung abscess, patients unable to clear secretions.

- **Postural drainage**: positions that use gravity to drain particular lung segments (for example head down for lower lobes, sitting upright for upper lobes). Done before meals or at least 1–2 hours after meals (prevents vomiting); each position about 10–15 minutes as tolerated.
- **Percussion (clapping)**: striking the chest wall over the affected area with **cupped hands** to loosen secretions. Never over the spine, breastbone, kidneys, breasts, fractures or bare skin (use a towel).
- **Vibration**: a fine shaking pressure applied with flat hands while the patient breathes out slowly.
- Follow with coughing or suctioning.

**Contraindications**: unstable patients, haemoptysis, raised intracranial pressure, recent chest surgery or trauma, rib fractures, severe osteoporosis, bleeding disorders, pulmonary embolism.

## 5.10 Care of chest drainage

**Principles**: the pleural space normally has negative pressure that keeps the lungs expanded. Air (pneumothorax), fluid (effusion), blood (haemothorax) or pus (empyema) collapse the lung. A chest tube connected to an **underwater seal** lets air and fluid leave but not return.

**Purposes**: remove air, fluid or blood from the pleural space; re-expand the lung; restore negative pressure; after chest surgery.

```text
Chest tube ─► tubing ─► long tube under ~2 cm of water in the drainage bottle
                         (water acts as a one-way valve)
                         short tube open to air (or to suction as ordered)
```

**Nursing care**
1. Keep the drainage bottle **below the chest level** at all times — prevents backflow.
2. Check that the long tube stays under water; mark the fluid level and time.
3. Observe **tidalling** (water level rises and falls with breathing) — shows the system is patent; continuous bubbling in the water seal may mean an air leak.
4. Keep tubing free of kinks and loops; do not clamp routinely (clamping can cause tension pneumothorax) — clamp only as per policy, for example briefly when changing the bottle or if it breaks.
5. Keep two artery forceps (padded) at the bedside; if the tube disconnects, place the end in sterile water; if it comes out, cover the site with an occlusive dressing and call for help.
6. Encourage deep breathing and coughing; give pain relief as prescribed.
7. Record amount, colour and character of drainage; report sudden increase or bright red blood.

## 5.11 Pulse oximetry

A non-invasive method of measuring **oxygen saturation of haemoglobin (SpO2)** using red and infrared light passed through a finger, toe, earlobe or nose.

**Factors affecting accuracy**
- Poor peripheral circulation (cold, shock, vasoconstriction) — weak signal.
- Movement and shivering.
- Nail polish, artificial nails, dark-coloured dyes.
- Bright external light.
- Carbon monoxide poisoning (gives a falsely normal reading).
- Severe anaemia (the reading may look normal though oxygen content is low).
- Darker skin pigmentation can make readings overestimate saturation.
- Wrong probe size or position.

**Interpretation (adults, as a guide)**: 95–100% normal; 91–94% mild hypoxaemia; 90% or below needs urgent attention. Always look at the patient and the waveform/pulse reading together, not just the number. Rotate the probe site to avoid pressure injury.

## 5.12 Restorative and continuing care

- **Hydration**: adequate fluids (unless restricted) keep secretions thin and easy to cough out.
- **Humidification**: humidified oxygen, steam inhalation (with safety from burns), room humidifiers, nebulisation as prescribed.
- **Coughing techniques**:
  - *Deep cough*: sit upright, take a deep breath, hold for 2–3 seconds, cough forcefully twice.
  - *Huff coughing*: short forced breaths out with an open glottis ("huff") — less tiring.
  - *Splinting*: support an abdominal or chest incision with a pillow while coughing.
- **Breathing exercises**:
  - *Deep breathing*: slow breath in through the nose, hold, slow breath out through the mouth.
  - *Pursed-lip breathing*: breathe out slowly through lips pursed as if whistling — keeps airways open longer (chronic lung disease).
  - *Diaphragmatic (abdominal) breathing*: hand on the abdomen should rise on breathing in.
- **Incentive spirometry**: a device that shows how deep a breath the patient takes, encouraging slow, sustained maximal inspiration. Sit upright, seal the lips around the mouthpiece, breathe in slowly to raise the ball or piston, hold for 3–5 seconds, breathe out; repeat about 10 times each hour while awake. Prevents atelectasis and pneumonia after surgery.
- **Positioning and early ambulation** improve lung expansion.

## Remember

- Oxygenation = ventilation + diffusion + transport + perfusion.
- Early hypoxia: restlessness, confusion, tachycardia; cyanosis is late.
- Orthopnoea = breathless lying flat; Kussmaul = deep rapid breathing of metabolic acidosis.
- Oxygen is a prescribed drug; humidify; no smoking or flames; Venturi mask gives a fixed concentration.
- Nasal cannula 1–6 L/min; simple mask at least 5 L/min; non-rebreather 10–15 L/min with bag inflated.
- Suction: apply only on withdrawal, 10–15 seconds per pass, pre-oxygenate, sterile for tracheal suction.
- Postural drainage before meals; percussion with cupped hands, never over spine or kidneys.
- Chest drain: bottle below chest, tube under water, watch tidalling, avoid routine clamping.
- SpO2 normally 95–100%; poor circulation, movement, nail polish, CO poisoning affect accuracy.
- Incentive spirometer: slow deep breath, hold 3–5 s, about 10 times an hour.
