# Unit 9: Psychosocial needs — self-concept, sexuality, stress and adaptation, culture and spirituality

INC Units XII–XV, grouped in the syllabus as "Psychosocial needs (A–D)": A. Self-concept (3 hours), B. Sexuality (2 hours), C. Stress and adaptation (2 hours theory + 4 hours skill lab), D. Cultural diversity and spirituality (6 hours). Holistic care means meeting these needs as well as physical ones.

## Part A: Self-concept

## 9.1 Introduction

**Self-concept** is the mental picture a person has of himself or herself — the beliefs and feelings about who one is. It develops from birth through interaction with family, friends, school and society, and it changes with life events, illness and ageing. A healthy self-concept helps a person cope with illness; a damaged one can lead to anxiety, depression and poor co-operation with treatment.

## 9.2 Components of self-concept

| Component | Meaning | Threats in illness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Personal identity** | The sense of being a unique individual — name, gender, roles, values, beliefs | Confusion, dementia, loss of independence |
| **Body image** | The mental picture of one's body — appearance, size, function | Amputation, mastectomy, colostomy, burns scars, hair loss, obesity, paralysis |
| **Role performance** | How a person carries out expected roles (parent, worker, student) | Hospital stay, disability, job loss, retirement |
| **Self-esteem** | The value a person places on himself or herself — feelings of self-worth | Failure, criticism, chronic illness, dependence, stigma |

**Role terms**: role conflict (two roles with incompatible demands — a nurse-mother with a sick child at home), role ambiguity (unclear expectations), role strain (feeling unable to fulfil a role), role overload (too many roles).

## 9.3 Factors affecting self-concept

Developmental stage, family relationships and early experiences, culture, illness and disability, surgery affecting appearance or function, trauma, abuse, success and failure, relationships, ageing, media images, and stressors.

## 9.4 Nursing management

1. **Assessment**: ask how the patient feels about self and the illness ("How do you feel about the change in your body?"), observe behaviour — avoiding looking at a wound, negative self-talk, withdrawal, excessive dependence, poor grooming.
2. **Nursing diagnoses**: disturbed body image; situational low self-esteem; ineffective role performance; disturbed personal identity.
3. **Interventions**:
   - Accept the patient warmly; show respect; call by name.
   - Encourage expression of feelings; listen without judging.
   - Help the patient look at and touch the changed body part gradually, when ready.
   - Teach self-care skills (stoma care, use of prosthesis) — independence builds self-esteem.
   - Point out strengths and give honest praise for achievements.
   - Involve family; prepare them for the patient's changed appearance.
   - Arrange contact with support groups and people who have adapted to similar changes.
   - Respect privacy and modesty during care.
4. **Evaluation**: patient talks about the change, takes part in care, resumes roles, expresses positive statements about self.

## Part B: Sexuality

## 9.5 Sexual development throughout life

**Sexuality** includes a person's sexual feelings, identity, attitudes, relationships and behaviour, and is part of total well-being.

| Stage | Development |
| --- | --- |
| Infancy and early childhood | Develops gender identity (awareness of being a boy or girl) by about 2–3 years; curiosity about body parts |
| School age | Learns gender roles; same-sex friendships; questions about reproduction |
| Adolescence | Puberty — secondary sexual characteristics, menarche, nocturnal emissions; strong sexual feelings; sexual identity and orientation become clearer; risk of unsafe sexual behaviour |
| Young adulthood | Intimate relationships, marriage or partnership, family planning |
| Middle age | Menopause in women; slower sexual response in men; changes in body image |
| Older adulthood | Sexual interest often continues; physical changes (vaginal dryness, slower erection) and illness or medicines may affect function; need for intimacy and touch remains |

## 9.6 Sexual health and sexual orientation

- **Sexual health** (WHO): a state of physical, emotional, mental and social well-being in relation to sexuality — not only the absence of disease; it needs a positive, respectful approach and the possibility of pleasurable and safe sexual experiences free of coercion, discrimination and violence.
- **Sexual orientation**: the pattern of emotional and sexual attraction — heterosexual, homosexual, bisexual and others. **Gender identity** is the inner sense of being male, female, both or neither, which may differ from the sex assigned at birth (transgender persons). In India the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 protects against discrimination, and consensual same-sex relations between adults are not a crime since the Supreme Court judgment of 2018 (Navtej Singh Johar). The nurse gives non-judgemental care to every person.

## 9.7 Factors affecting sexuality

Physical illness (diabetes, heart disease, spinal injury), surgery (mastectomy, hysterectomy, prostate surgery, stoma), medicines (some antihypertensives and antidepressants), pregnancy and post-partum period, ageing, psychological factors (stress, depression, anxiety, body image, past abuse), relationship problems, culture and religion, alcohol and drug use, and lack of knowledge.

## 9.8 Prevention of STIs, unwanted pregnancy, sexual harassment and abuse

**Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)**: health education about abstinence, mutual faithfulness, correct and consistent condom use, avoiding multiple partners; early treatment of STIs for both partners; screening in pregnancy; hepatitis B and HPV vaccination as per national schedules; reducing stigma so that people seek care.

**Unwanted pregnancy**: information on family planning methods (temporary — condoms, oral pills, IUCD, injectables; permanent — tubectomy, vasectomy) and emergency contraception as available under the national family planning programme; counselling of adolescents and couples; safe abortion services within the law (the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, as amended).

**Sexual harassment and abuse**
- Teach children about "safe and unsafe touch" and to tell a trusted adult.
- In India, sexual offences against children are dealt with under the **POCSO Act, 2012**, which makes reporting compulsory; workplace sexual harassment is covered by the **Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013** (internal complaints committees).
- Nurses: recognise signs of abuse (injuries in different stages of healing, fear, withdrawal, STIs in children), give care with privacy and a female attendant, document accurately, preserve evidence and follow the hospital's medico-legal procedure.

## 9.9 Dealing with inappropriate sexual behaviour

Patients may make sexual remarks, touch staff inappropriately or expose themselves (sometimes because of confusion, dementia, brain injury or medicines).

1. Stay calm and professional; do not laugh it off or encourage it.
2. Set clear limits firmly: "Please do not touch me. That is not acceptable."
3. Redirect the patient to the task; leave if the behaviour continues and get help.
4. Assess for causes (confusion, medicines, unmet need for intimacy).
5. Report to the nurse in charge and record the facts; plan consistent responses in the team (care in pairs if needed).
6. Nurses have the right to work without harassment; use the institution's complaint mechanism.

## Part C: Stress and adaptation

## 9.10 Introduction and types of stress

- **Stress**: a state produced by a change in the environment that is perceived as challenging, threatening or damaging to the person's balance.
- **Stressor**: any event or stimulus that causes stress.
- **Adaptation**: the process of adjusting to stressors to keep balance (**homeostasis**).

**Types of stress**: **eustress** (positive, motivating — exam preparation, a new job) and **distress** (negative, harmful); **acute** (short-term) and **chronic** (long-lasting, as in caring for a chronically ill family member).

**Sources of stress**: internal (illness, fear, low self-esteem) and external (work, family, finances, environment); developmental (normal life changes — adolescence, retirement) and situational (accident, hospitalisation, divorce).

**Types of stressors**: **physical** (heat, cold, noise, trauma), **physiological** (pain, infection, hunger, fatigue), **psychological** (fear, loss, conflict), **social and cultural** (poverty, unemployment, discrimination), **chemical** (drugs, pollutants), **microbiological**.

**Effects and indicators of stress**: in mild amounts stress improves alertness and performance; excessive or prolonged stress lowers immunity, raises BP, and contributes to headache, peptic ulcer, heart disease, sleep problems, anxiety and depression.

## 9.11 Stress adaptation: General and Local Adaptation Syndromes (Hans Selye)

**General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)** — the whole-body response:

```text
1. ALARM REACTION        Stressor recognised; "fight or flight":
   (shock → countershock) sympathetic nervous system and adrenal medulla
                          release adrenaline; cortisol rises;
                          heart rate, BP, breathing, blood sugar rise;
                          pupils dilate
            ↓
2. RESISTANCE (adaptation) Body tries to adapt; hormone levels
                          stay raised but stabilise; if the stressor
                          is removed, the body returns to normal
            ↓
3. EXHAUSTION             Adaptive energy runs out if the stressor
                          continues; resistance falls; illness or
                          even death may follow
```

**Local Adaptation Syndrome (LAS)** — a localised response of a tissue, organ or body part; short-term and aimed at restoring balance. Examples:
- **Reflex pain response**: withdrawing a hand from a hot object.
- **Inflammatory response**: redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function around an injury to limit damage and promote healing.

If the local response is not enough, the GAS is activated.

## 9.12 Manifestations of stress

| Physical | Psychological / behavioural |
| --- | --- |
| Rapid pulse, raised BP, rapid breathing | Anxiety, fear, irritability, anger |
| Dilated pupils, sweating, cold clammy hands | Poor concentration, forgetfulness |
| Dry mouth, nausea, loss of appetite or overeating | Crying, withdrawal, restlessness |
| Headache, muscle tension, back pain | Sleep disturbance |
| Raised blood sugar | Use of alcohol, tobacco or drugs |
| Fatigue, lowered immunity | Loss of interest, depression |

## 9.13 Coping strategies and mechanisms

**Coping** is the way a person deals with stress.

- **Problem-focused coping**: acting on the problem (seeking information, planning, making changes).
- **Emotion-focused coping**: managing the feelings (talking to a friend, prayer, relaxation, crying).
- **Adaptive** coping reduces stress and helps (exercise, support, problem solving); **maladaptive** coping gives short relief but causes harm (alcohol, avoidance, aggression).

**Ego defence mechanisms** (unconscious ways of reducing anxiety) — useful in small amounts, harmful if overused:

| Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Denial | Refusing to believe a diagnosis |
| Projection | Blaming others for one's own feelings |
| Rationalisation | Giving acceptable reasons for failure |
| Displacement | Shouting at the nurse when angry with the doctor |
| Regression | Returning to childlike behaviour when ill |
| Repression | Pushing painful memories out of awareness |
| Sublimation | Channelling feelings into acceptable activity (sport, art) |
| Compensation | Excelling in one area to make up for weakness in another |

## 9.14 Stress management

**Assisting with coping and adaptation**
1. Assess stressors, stress level, coping methods and support.
2. Give information about the illness and procedures — reduces fear of the unknown.
3. Encourage expression of feelings; listen actively.
4. Teach relaxation techniques: deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, meditation, yoga as appropriate.
5. Encourage problem solving, time management, adequate sleep, a balanced diet, regular exercise, and avoidance of alcohol and tobacco.
6. Mobilise family and social support; refer to counselling or support groups when needed; crisis intervention when coping fails.

**Creating a therapeutic environment**
- Physical: clean, quiet, well-lit, ventilated, safe, with privacy and some personal belongings; reduce noise at night.
- Psychological and social: warm, accepting attitude; respect and dignity; consistent staff; clear routines; family involvement; opportunity to make choices.

**Recreational and diversion therapies**: reading, music, television or radio, games (carrom, chess, cards), art and craft, gardening, group activities, outings for long-stay patients, play therapy for children, occupational therapy. They reduce boredom, anxiety and pain perception and improve mood and social interaction.

## Part D: Cultural diversity and spirituality

## 9.15 Cultural concepts

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Culture** | The shared, learned values, beliefs, customs and ways of life of a group, passed from generation to generation |
| **Subculture** | A smaller group within a larger culture with its own distinctive patterns (tribal communities, a religious sect, adolescents) |
| **Multicultural** | A society or setting in which many cultures live together |
| **Diversity** | Differences among people — ethnicity, religion, language, gender, age, ability, economic status |
| **Race** | A grouping of people by shared physical (biological) characteristics such as skin colour |
| **Ethnicity** | Belonging to a group with a shared cultural heritage, language and origin |
| **Acculturation** | Adopting some practices of another (dominant) culture while keeping one's own |
| **Assimilation** | Gradually taking on the dominant culture's patterns so fully that the original culture is largely lost |
| **Ethnocentrism** | Believing one's own culture is superior and judging others by it — a barrier to care |
| **Stereotyping** | Assuming all members of a group are the same |

## 9.16 Transcultural nursing and cultural competence

**Transcultural nursing** (founded by **Madeleine Leininger**, theory of culture care diversity and universality, illustrated by the **Sunrise model**) is the study and practice of nursing that compares cultures' health beliefs and practices, and gives care that fits the person's culture. Leininger's three modes of action: **culture care preservation/maintenance**, **culture care accommodation/negotiation**, and **culture care repatterning/restructuring**.

**Cultural competence** is the ability to give effective care to people of different cultures. Campinha-Bacote describes five constructs:
1. **Cultural awareness** — examining one's own biases.
2. **Cultural knowledge** — learning about other cultures' beliefs and health practices.
3. **Cultural skill** — collecting cultural data in assessment.
4. **Cultural encounters** — interacting directly with people of different cultures.
5. **Cultural desire** — genuine motivation to become culturally competent.

## 9.17 Providing culturally responsive care

- Assess cultural beliefs: language, religion, diet (vegetarian, halal, fasting), modesty and touch, family decision-making, beliefs about illness causes, use of traditional medicine (AYUSH remedies, home remedies), birth and death customs.
- Use a trained interpreter, not children of the family, for important information.
- Respect modesty — same-sex caregiver where requested; expose only what is needed.
- Allow religious articles (threads, amulets) unless they interfere with treatment; discuss when removal is necessary.
- Adjust diet and timing of medicines during fasting where safe, after consulting the doctor.
- Include the family as the patient wishes; explain in simple words; check understanding.
- Avoid stereotyping — ask each person what matters to them.

## 9.18 Spirituality

| Concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Spirituality** | A person's search for meaning and purpose in life and connection with self, others, nature and a higher power; broader than religion |
| **Faith** | Belief in or commitment to something or someone (often a higher power) that cannot be proven |
| **Hope** | Expectation of a good future; gives energy to cope |
| **Religion** | An organised system of beliefs, practices, rituals and worship shared by a community |
| **Spiritual well-being** | A sense of harmony, meaning and peace — vertical (relationship with God or a higher power) and horizontal (relationships with self, others and the world) |

**Factors affecting spirituality**: age and development, family and culture, life experiences, illness and suffering, hospitalisation (separation from usual practices and community), crisis and loss, the nurse's own attitudes.

**Spiritual problems in illness**
- **Acute illness**: sudden illness may make a person question beliefs ("Why has God done this to me?") or turn to prayer for strength.
- **Chronic illness**: long dependence and suffering can lead to hopelessness, anger with God, or growth in faith.
- **Terminal illness**: fear of death, regrets, need for forgiveness and reconciliation, search for meaning.
- **Near-death experience**: people who survive a close brush with death may describe seeing a light, leaving the body or peace; they need to be listened to without judgement and may change their values afterwards.

**Spiritual distress**: disturbance in the person's belief or value system that gives strength, hope and meaning. Signs: questioning the meaning of life or suffering, anger at God, feeling abandoned, guilt, inability to pray or join religious practices, hopelessness, sleep disturbance.

## 9.19 Dealing with spiritual distress

1. Assess spiritual needs (beliefs, practices, sources of strength — for example the FICA questions: Faith, Importance, Community, Address in care).
2. Be present; listen with empathy; accept the person's beliefs even if different from your own; do not impose your beliefs.
3. Support religious practices — prayer time, scriptures, prayer beads, a quiet place, dietary rules, rituals before surgery or death.
4. Arrange visits by a priest, pandit, maulvi, granthi or other spiritual leader as the patient wishes.
5. Encourage hope (realistic goals, meaningful activities, life review, contact with loved ones).
6. Help the patient find meaning — talk about life events and achievements, forgiveness and reconciliation.
7. Respect cultural and religious customs around death and the care of the body.

## Remember

- Self-concept components: personal identity, body image, role performance, self-esteem.
- Body-image threats: amputation, mastectomy, stoma, burns, alopecia — encourage gradual looking and self-care.
- Sexual health is well-being, not only absence of disease; give non-judgemental care to all orientations and gender identities.
- POCSO Act 2012 — mandatory reporting of child sexual abuse.
- Inappropriate sexual behaviour: calm, firm limits, redirect, report, record.
- Selye's GAS: alarm → resistance → exhaustion; LAS: reflex pain and inflammatory responses.
- Eustress positive; distress harmful; coping problem-focused or emotion-focused.
- Acculturation keeps the old culture; assimilation largely replaces it.
- Leininger = transcultural nursing (Sunrise model); Campinha-Bacote = awareness, knowledge, skill, encounters, desire.
- Spirituality is broader than religion; support practices, listen, involve spiritual leaders as wished.
