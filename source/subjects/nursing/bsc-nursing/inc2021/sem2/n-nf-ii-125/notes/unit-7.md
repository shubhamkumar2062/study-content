# Unit 7: Administration of medications

INC Unit IX (20 hours theory + 22 hours skill lab) — the largest unit: definitions, nomenclature, effects, forms, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics; factors affecting drug action; medication orders; systems of measurement and dose calculation; the 10 rights; medication errors; routes; storage and nurse's responsibility; abbreviations; developmental considerations; oral, sublingual and buccal routes; parenteral routes, equipment, vials and ampoules, safe disposal and needle-stick prevention; topical, instillation, irrigation, spraying and inhalation; and other parenteral routes.

> All numbers in the calculation examples below are invented for practice. Real doses always come from the prescription and the drug reference used by your hospital.

## 7.1 Introduction and definitions

- **Medication (drug)**: a substance used to prevent, diagnose, treat or relieve disease, or to change a body function.
- **Administration of medication**: giving a prescribed medicine to a patient by the right route, at the right time, safely, and recording it.
- **Drug nomenclature**:
  - **Chemical name** — describes the chemical structure.
  - **Generic (non-proprietary) name** — the official name, used in prescriptions and pharmacopoeias (for example paracetamol).
  - **Trade (brand, proprietary) name** — given by the manufacturer; one generic drug may have many brands.
  - **Official name** — the name listed in the pharmacopoeia (Indian Pharmacopoeia).
- **Purposes of medication**: prevent (vaccines), diagnose (contrast media), treat or cure (antibiotics), relieve symptoms (analgesics), replace deficiencies (insulin, thyroxine), support life functions.

## 7.2 Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics

**Pharmacokinetics** — what the body does to the drug ("ADME"):

```text
Absorption → Distribution → Metabolism (mainly liver) → Excretion (mainly kidneys)
```

- **Absorption**: passage from the site of administration into the blood; affected by route, blood flow, solubility, food and stomach acidity. IV route has no absorption phase.
- **Distribution**: carried by the blood to tissues; affected by circulation, protein binding, blood–brain and placental barriers.
- **Metabolism (biotransformation)**: usually makes the drug inactive and water-soluble; reduced in liver disease, the very young and the old. Oral drugs pass through the liver first (**first-pass effect**), so some oral drugs lose much of their effect.
- **Excretion**: mainly in urine; also bile, faeces, lungs, sweat, breast milk. Kidney disease causes accumulation.
- Useful terms: **onset** (time to start acting), **peak**, **duration**, **half-life** (time for the blood level to fall by half), **therapeutic range**, **trough**.

**Pharmacodynamics** — what the drug does to the body: drugs act mainly by binding to **receptors** (agonists activate, antagonists block), by altering enzymes, by physical or chemical action (antacids neutralise acid), or by acting on ion channels.

## 7.3 Effects of drugs

| Effect | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Therapeutic | The intended effect |
| Side effect | Predictable unwanted effect at normal dose (drowsiness with antihistamines) |
| Adverse effect | Harmful, unintended effect needing action |
| Toxic effect | Harmful effect from accumulation or overdose |
| Allergic reaction | Immune response — rash, itching, up to anaphylaxis |
| Idiosyncratic | Unusual, unexpected response peculiar to the person |
| Tolerance | Larger amounts needed for the same effect |
| Dependence | Physical or psychological need for the drug |
| Cumulative effect | Drug builds up when it is given faster than it is removed |
| Drug interaction | One drug changes the effect of another (synergism, antagonism) |
| Iatrogenic | Disease caused by treatment |

## 7.4 Forms of medications

| Route | Forms |
| --- | --- |
| Oral | Tablets (plain, enteric-coated, sustained-release), capsules, caplets, lozenges, powders, syrups, elixirs, suspensions, emulsions |
| Topical | Ointments, creams, lotions, pastes, gels, powders, transdermal patches |
| Body cavities | Suppositories, pessaries, drops, sprays |
| Inhalation | Metered-dose inhalers, dry-powder inhalers, nebuliser solutions |
| Parenteral | Solutions and suspensions in ampoules, vials, prefilled syringes, infusion bags |

Enteric-coated and sustained-release tablets must **not be crushed or chewed**.

## 7.5 Factors influencing medication action

Age (infants and elderly: immature or slowed metabolism and excretion), body weight and composition, sex, genetics, pregnancy and breast-feeding, disease (liver, kidney, heart), psychological factors (placebo effect), diet and food–drug interactions, timing in relation to meals, route, environment (heat, cold), tolerance, and other drugs taken.

## 7.6 Medication orders and prescriptions

**Parts of a complete order**: patient's full name and identification; date and time written; drug name (generic preferred); dose; route; frequency/time; duration where relevant; prescriber's signature (and registration number on prescriptions).

**Types of orders**

| Type | Meaning | Example (made-up) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routine (standing) | Given regularly until stopped or for a set period | Drug A tablet, twice daily for 5 days |
| PRN (as needed) | Given when the patient needs it, within set limits | Analgesic when pain score is above 4 |
| Single (one-time) | Given once at a stated time | Before surgery |
| STAT | Given immediately, once | In an emergency |
| Verbal / telephone | Spoken orders — only in emergencies or as per policy | Write it down, **read it back**, and get it signed within the time set by policy |

Do not carry out an order that is incomplete, illegible or seems wrong — clarify with the prescriber first.

## 7.7 Systems of measurement

- **Metric system** (standard): weight — kilogram (kg), gram (g), milligram (mg), microgram (µg or mcg); volume — litre (L), millilitre (mL).
- **Household measures**: teaspoon (about 5 mL), tablespoon (about 15 mL), cup, glass — inaccurate; use an oral syringe or medicine cup for exact measurement.
- **Units**: biological activity, for example insulin and heparin (write "units" in full).
- **Milliequivalents (mEq)**: electrolytes.
- **Apothecary system** (grains, minims, drams): old, mostly out of use.

**Conversions to know**

| From | To |
| --- | --- |
| 1 kg | 1,000 g |
| 1 g | 1,000 mg |
| 1 mg | 1,000 µg |
| 1 L | 1,000 mL |
| 1 kg | about 2.2 lb |
| 1 mL of water | 1 g; 1 mL = 1 cc |

To change a large unit to a smaller one, **multiply** (0.5 g × 1,000 = 500 mg). To change a small unit to a larger one, **divide** (250 mg ÷ 1,000 = 0.25 g).

## 7.8 Medication dose calculation

**Basic formula**

```text
Amount to give = (Desired dose ÷ Dose on hand) × Quantity it comes in
              = (D ÷ H) × Q
```

Always convert to the **same units first**, then estimate whether the answer is sensible.

**Practice examples (invented drugs and numbers)**

1. *Tablets*: Ordered Drug X 500 mg. Available: 250 mg tablets.
   (500 ÷ 250) × 1 tablet = **2 tablets**.
2. *Liquids*: Ordered Drug Y 150 mg. Available: 250 mg in 5 mL.
   (150 ÷ 250) × 5 mL = **3 mL**.
3. *Different units*: Ordered Drug Z 0.2 g. Available: 100 mg per tablet.
   0.2 g = 200 mg → (200 ÷ 100) × 1 = **2 tablets**.
4. *Injection*: Ordered Drug P 40 mg. Available: 80 mg in 2 mL.
   (40 ÷ 80) × 2 mL = **1 mL**.
5. *Reconstitution*: a vial contains 1 g of powder; the label says add 4 mL diluent to make 5 mL in total. Concentration = 1,000 mg ÷ 5 mL = 200 mg/mL. Ordered 300 mg → 300 ÷ 200 = **1.5 mL**.
6. *Weight-based*: Ordered 5 mg per kg for a 12 kg child (made-up drug). 5 × 12 = 60 mg. Available 120 mg in 5 mL → (60 ÷ 120) × 5 = **2.5 mL**.
7. *Percentage solutions*: a 1% solution contains 1 g in 100 mL (10 mg/mL). Preparing 500 mL of a 2% lotion from pure solute needs 2 × 5 = 10 g (or 10 mL if liquid) made up to 500 mL.
8. *Ratio strength*: 1:1,000 means 1 g in 1,000 mL (1 mg/mL).
9. *Dilution from a stronger solution*: C1 × V1 = C2 × V2. To make 1,000 mL of a 1% solution from a 5% stock: V1 = (1 × 1,000) ÷ 5 = 200 mL of stock made up to 1,000 mL.

(For IV drip rates see Unit 6.)

Double-check high-alert drug calculations (insulin, heparin, potassium, opioids, paediatric doses) with a second nurse.

## 7.9 Principles and the 10 rights of medication administration

**General principles**: know the drug (action, usual range, side effects, nursing points); give only what you have prepared yourself; never give from an unlabelled container; check expiry; do not return a prepared medicine to the stock bottle; stay with the patient until an oral medicine is swallowed; maintain asepsis for parenteral routes; record immediately after giving, never before.

**The 10 rights**

1. **Right patient** — check two identifiers (name and hospital number/wristband); ask the patient to state their name.
2. **Right drug** — compare the label with the order **three times**: when taking it from storage, while preparing, and before giving (or returning the container).
3. **Right dose** — calculate carefully; double-check high-risk drugs.
4. **Right route** — and the right form for that route.
5. **Right time** — and frequency; give within the time window set by policy.
6. **Right documentation** — record after giving: drug, dose, route, time, site, signature.
7. **Right client education** — tell the patient what the drug is for and what to expect.
8. **Right to refuse** — explain the consequences, inform the prescriber, record the refusal.
9. **Right assessment** — check what is needed before giving (allergy history, pulse before a cardiac drug, BP before an antihypertensive, swallowing ability).
10. **Right evaluation** — check the response and side effects afterwards.

(Some books list "right reason" or "right response" in place of one of these.)

## 7.10 Errors in medication administration

**Types**: wrong patient, drug, dose, route, time or form; omission; giving a drug to which the patient is allergic; giving a drug twice; wrong rate of infusion; wrong preparation.

**Common causes**: illegible or incomplete orders, look-alike and sound-alike names, calculation errors, misread abbreviations, distractions and interruptions, workload and fatigue, failing to check identity, poor communication at handover.

**Prevention**: follow the rights and three checks; clarify doubtful orders; use generic names and approved abbreviations; avoid interruptions during medicine rounds; double-check high-alert drugs; separate look-alike drugs; know the drugs you give.

**What to do after an error**: check the patient immediately and take vital signs; inform the nurse in charge and the doctor at once; give any ordered treatment; record the facts in the patient's notes; complete an incident report as per hospital policy; never hide an error — reporting protects the patient and improves the system.

## 7.11 Routes of administration

| Route | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral | Safe, cheap, convenient, painless | Slow; first-pass loss; not for vomiting, unconscious or NPO patients; taste |
| Sublingual (under the tongue) / buccal (against the cheek) | Rapid absorption, avoids first-pass effect | Only for small amounts of suitable drugs; must not be swallowed |
| Topical (skin, mucous membrane) | Local effect, few systemic effects | Messy, may irritate |
| Inhalation | Rapid local action in lungs | Needs correct technique |
| Rectal / vaginal | When oral is not possible; local effect | Uncomfortable, irregular absorption |
| Intradermal | Small amount for tests (tuberculin, sensitivity tests) | Very small volume |
| Subcutaneous | Slow, steady absorption (insulin, heparin, some vaccines) | Small volumes; irritation |
| Intramuscular | Faster absorption than subcutaneous; larger volumes than SC | Pain, nerve injury, abscess |
| Intravenous | Immediate action, 100% availability, for large volumes | Irreversible once given; infection, phlebitis, speed shock |

## 7.12 Storage and maintenance of drugs; nurse's responsibility

- Store in a locked cupboard or trolley; keys with the nurse in charge.
- **Narcotics and psychotropic substances** (controlled under the NDPS Act, 1985) in a separate double-locked cupboard, with a register counted and signed at every shift change.
- Keep internal and external medicines on separate shelves; poisonous and high-alert drugs separate and labelled.
- Store as the label says: room temperature, cool place, **refrigerator at 2–8 °C** for vaccines, insulin and some injections (not in the freezer or door); protect light-sensitive drugs in amber containers.
- Check expiry dates; use the "first expiry, first out" rule; return expired or damaged drugs to the pharmacy.
- Never transfer drugs between containers or relabel them.
- Keep labels clean and readable; discard if unreadable.
- Keep the emergency (crash) trolley checked and stocked.
- Keep records of stock, indents and controlled drugs.

**Error-prone forms to avoid**: "U" or "IU" (write "units"), "µg" handwritten (write "mcg"), a trailing zero (write 5 mg, not 5.0 mg) and a missing leading zero (write 0.5 mg, not .5 mg), "OD" when it might be read as "right eye".

## 7.14 Developmental considerations

- **Infants and children**: doses by weight or body surface area; use an oral syringe or dropper, give slowly into the side of the mouth; do not add medicine to a full bottle of milk (the baby may not finish it); involve parents; the vastus lateralis is the IM site of choice in infants; use age-appropriate explanations and praise.
- **Pregnancy and lactation**: many drugs cross the placenta or enter milk; check with the prescriber.
- **Older adults**: slower metabolism and excretion, many medicines at once (polypharmacy), memory problems, poor vision, swallowing difficulty, fragile skin and less muscle mass; use large-print labels, pill organisers, review medicines regularly, watch for falls and confusion.

## 7.15 Oral, sublingual and buccal administration

**Equipment**: medicine trolley, medication record, medicine cups, oral syringes, spoon, water, pill crusher (only when the drug may be crushed), straws, tissue.

**Procedure — main steps with reasons**
1. Hand hygiene; check the order against the medication record — avoids transcription errors.
2. Check allergies and any assessment needed (pulse, BP, swallowing).
3. Prepare one patient's medicines at a time; check the label three times; pour tablets into the cap and then the cup without touching them; pour liquids with the label towards the palm (keeps the label clean) and read the level at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
4. Identify the patient with two identifiers; explain.
5. Sit the patient up — eases swallowing and prevents aspiration.
6. Give with a full glass of water (unless restricted); stay until all are swallowed — the patient may otherwise hoard or discard them.
7. **Sublingual**: place under the tongue and let it dissolve — no swallowing, eating or drinking until it dissolves. **Buccal**: place between the cheek and gum, alternate cheeks.
8. Document immediately; observe the effect.

**Points**: do not give oral medicines to patients who are vomiting, unconscious, NPO or unable to swallow; give medicines through an NG tube only in liquid form or crushed if allowed, one at a time, flushing before, between and after.

## 7.16 Introduction to parenteral administration

| Route | Sites | Needle (adult guide) | Angle | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Intradermal (ID)** | Inner forearm, upper back, upper arm | 26–27 G, about 1 cm | 10–15° (almost flat), bevel up | Raise a small bleb (wheal); do not massage |
| **Subcutaneous (SC)** | Outer upper arm, abdomen (about 5 cm away from the umbilicus), front of thighs, upper back | 25–27 G, short | 45° (90° if enough fat or with a short pen needle) | Pinch the skin; rotate sites; do not massage after heparin or insulin |
| **Intramuscular (IM)** | **Ventrogluteal** (preferred for adults — no major nerves or vessels), **vastus lateralis** (preferred in infants), **deltoid** (small volumes, vaccines), dorsogluteal (avoided — risk of sciatic nerve injury) | 21–23 G, 2.5–4 cm depending on muscle and fat | 90° | Z-track technique for irritating drugs; small volume in deltoid |
| **Intravenous (IV)** | Veins of forearm and hand | Cannula 18–22 G | 15–30° for cannulation | Bolus, intermittent infusion or continuous infusion |

**Locating IM sites**
- **Ventrogluteal**: place the palm on the greater trochanter (left hand on the right hip), index finger on the anterior superior iliac spine, spread the middle finger towards the iliac crest; inject in the centre of the "V".
- **Vastus lateralis**: middle third of the outer thigh, between a hand's breadth below the greater trochanter and a hand's breadth above the knee.
- **Deltoid**: 2.5–5 cm (about 2–3 finger widths) below the acromion process, in the middle of the lateral arm.
- **Dorsogluteal**: upper outer quadrant of the buttock — taught for recognition, but many guidelines now advise using the ventrogluteal site instead.

**Contraindications for a site**: infection, bruising, scars, oedema, paralysis, lumps; for IM generally — bleeding disorders and anticoagulant therapy (needs care), poor muscle mass, shock (poor absorption).

**Aspiration**: aspiration before IM injection is no longer advised for vaccines, and practice for other drugs varies — follow your institution's policy.

**General steps for any injection**: check order and rights; hand hygiene; prepare at a clean surface; identify the patient; explain and give privacy; select and clean the site with alcohol swab and let it dry; insert with a quick dart-like motion at the right angle; inject slowly; withdraw at the same angle; press gently with dry cotton; discard the needle-syringe immediately into the sharps container without recapping; record the site, time and response.

**Z-track technique**: pull the skin and tissue laterally by about 2.5–3 cm before inserting; inject, wait about 10 seconds, withdraw, then release the skin — this seals the track and prevents leakage of irritant drugs into the tissue.

## 7.17 Equipment: syringes, needles, cannulas and infusion sets

- **Syringe parts**: tip (luer slip or luer lock), barrel with markings, flange, plunger with rubber bung. Sizes: 1 mL (tuberculin, insulin syringes marked in units), 2, 3, 5, 10, 20 and 50 mL.
- **Needle parts**: hub, shaft (cannula), bevel (slanted tip). **Gauge** = diameter — a higher number means a finer needle; length chosen by route and the patient's size.
- **IV cannula parts**: needle (stylet), catheter, hub, wings, injection port (in some types), cap.
- **Infusion set**: spike, drip chamber, roller clamp, tubing, injection port, luer connector. Macro-drip (adult) and micro-drip (60 drops/mL); blood sets have a filter; burette sets (measured chamber) for children.

## 7.18 Vials and ampoules

- **Ampoule**: sealed glass container with a neck; single use; contents liquid.
  Steps: tap the top to bring liquid down; protect the fingers with gauze or an ampoule opener and snap away from you; withdraw using a **filter needle** where available (prevents drawing glass particles); change to a fresh needle to inject; discard the glass in the sharps/glass container.
- **Vial**: glass or plastic bottle with a rubber stopper; single-dose or multi-dose; liquid or powder.
  Steps: remove the cap; clean the rubber top with alcohol; draw into the syringe air equal to the volume to be withdrawn and inject it into the vial — prevents a vacuum; invert and withdraw; remove air bubbles; for powders, add the stated diluent, roll gently to mix (do not shake vigorously); label a multi-dose vial with date, time, concentration and initials after opening and store as directed.
- **Mixing two drugs in one syringe** only when known to be compatible and as per policy.

## 7.19 Care of equipment, disposal and prevention of needle-stick injuries

**Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016 (colour coding, as amended)**

| Container | Waste |
| --- | --- |
| Yellow | Anatomical waste, items soiled with blood or body fluids, expired or discarded medicines, chemical waste |
| Red | Contaminated recyclable plastics — tubing, IV sets, catheters, urine bags, syringes without needles, gloves |
| White (translucent, puncture-proof) | Sharps — needles, syringes with fixed needles, blades, scalpels |
| Blue (cardboard box with blue marking) | Glassware — broken glass, ampoules, vials (after decontamination as per rules) |

**Prevention of needle-stick injuries**
- **Never recap** used needles; if recapping is unavoidable, use the one-handed scoop method.
- Do not bend, break or remove needles by hand.
- Dispose immediately into a puncture-proof sharps container kept at the point of use; close it when three-quarters full.
- Use safety-engineered devices where available; get hepatitis B vaccination.
- Concentrate, keep good light, and get help with restless patients.

**If a needle-stick injury occurs**: let the wound bleed freely; wash with soap and running water (do not scrub, squeeze or apply bleach); splashes into eyes or mouth — rinse with plenty of water; report immediately to the in-charge; the source and exposed person are assessed; **post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)** is decided by the doctor and should be started as early as possible — ideally within 2 hours and not later than 72 hours; record and follow up.

## 7.20 Topical administration

**Application to skin and mucous membranes**: clean the area, wear gloves, apply a thin layer of cream or ointment with a spatula or gloved hand in the direction of hair growth; transdermal patch — remove the old one, apply to a clean, hairless site, rotate sites, date it.

**Direct application of liquids**: painting with swabs (for example antiseptic on the throat or a wound), **gargle** (the patient tilts the head back and gargles the prescribed solution, then spits out), **swabbing the throat** (use a tongue depressor and good light, swab the area with the prescribed solution without causing gagging).

**Insertion into body cavities**: rectal suppository (see Unit 4); **vaginal pessary or medicated packing** — lithotomy or dorsal recumbent position, insert with an applicator or gloved finger along the posterior wall about 5–7.5 cm, ask the patient to lie down for 10–15 minutes; give a perineal pad.

**Instillations (drops)**

| Site | Key steps |
| --- | --- |
| **Eye** | Head tilted back, look up; pull the lower lid down; drop into the **lower conjunctival sac**, not on the cornea; do not touch the eye with the dropper; ask the patient to close the eye gently; press the inner canthus for about 1 minute (reduces systemic absorption); separate dropper for each eye if infected; ointment applied from inner to outer canthus |
| **Ear** | Warm drops to room/body temperature (cold drops cause vertigo); patient lies with the affected ear up; **adult — pull the pinna up and back**; **child under 3 — down and back**; drop along the canal wall; stay on the side for 2–3 minutes |
| **Nose** | Blow the nose first; head tilted back (or position for the sinus to be reached); insert the dropper just inside the nostril without touching; stay in position for a few minutes |
| **Bladder** | Through a catheter with strict asepsis; retain for the ordered time |
| **Rectal** | Small-volume medicated enema or retention solution in left lateral position |

**Irrigations (washing out a cavity)**

| Site | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| Eye | Patient's head turned towards the affected side; flow from **inner to outer canthus** so fluid does not enter the other eye; for chemical splashes irrigate copiously |
| Ear | Warm solution; direct the stream along the roof of the canal, gently; not if the eardrum is perforated |
| Bladder | See Unit 4 |
| Vaginal (douche) | Only on order; lithotomy; low pressure, with the container held only a little above the hips |
| Rectal | Bowel wash (Unit 4) |

**Spraying**: nose (patient sitting, head slightly forward, nozzle into the nostril pointed away from the septum, spray while breathing in) and throat (with good light, tongue depressed, spray onto the back of the throat).

## 7.21 Inhalation

**Purposes**: deliver medicine directly to the airways, loosen secretions, moisten the airways, relieve congestion.

**Types**
- **Steam inhalation** — moist heat; simple bowl-and-towel method or a steam inhaler; keep the face about 20–30 cm away; never leave children unattended near hot water (scald risk).
- **Oxygen** (Unit 5).
- **Metered-dose inhaler (MDI)**: shake; breathe out fully; place the mouthpiece in the mouth (or use a **spacer**); press once as you begin a slow deep breath; hold the breath for about 10 seconds; wait before the next puff as instructed; **rinse the mouth after steroid inhalers** (prevents oral thrush).
- **Dry-powder inhaler**: load, breathe out away from the device, then breathe in quickly and deeply.
- **Nebuliser**: converts liquid medicine into a fine mist using compressed air or oxygen; patient sits upright and breathes normally through a mouthpiece or mask until the chamber is empty; clean and dry the parts after use.
- **Endotracheal or tracheal inhalation**: medicines or humidified air given through an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy, by in-line nebuliser or MDI adapter.

**Recording and reporting**: drug, method, time, the patient's breathing, pulse and SpO2 before and after, any side effects (tremor, rapid heart rate), and the effect on breathlessness.

## 7.22 Other parenteral routes (meaning only)

| Route | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Epidural | Into the epidural space outside the dura of the spinal cord (pain relief in labour or after surgery) |
| Intrathecal | Into the subarachnoid space (CSF) through a lumbar puncture |
| Intraosseous | Into the bone marrow cavity (usually the tibia) — emergency access in children and adults when veins cannot be found |
| Intraperitoneal | Into the peritoneal cavity (peritoneal dialysis, some chemotherapy) |
| Intrapleural | Into the pleural space |
| Intra-arterial | Into an artery (some chemotherapy, angiography) |
| Intracardiac | Into the heart (rarely used) |
| Intra-articular | Into a joint |

These are given by doctors or specially trained staff; the nurse prepares, assists, monitors and records.

## Remember

- ADME = absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion; first-pass effect for oral drugs.
- 10 rights: patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, education, refusal, assessment, evaluation; check the label three times.
- Amount = (Desired ÷ Have) × Quantity; same units first.
- 1 g = 1,000 mg; 1 mg = 1,000 µg; 1% = 1 g per 100 mL.
- Verbal orders: write, read back, get signed.
- ID 10–15°, SC 45°, IM 90°; ventrogluteal preferred, vastus lateralis for infants, avoid dorsogluteal.
- Never recap; sharps into white puncture-proof container; PEP ideally within 2 hours, not after 72 hours.
- Ear drops: adult pinna up and back; child under 3 down and back. Eye drops into the lower sac.
- Rinse the mouth after steroid inhalers; refrigerated drugs at 2–8 °C; controlled drugs double-locked with a register.
- Report and record every medication error; never hide it.
