# Unit 2: The nursing process and introduction to nursing theories

INC Unit II (13 hours theory + 8 hours skill lab) — critical thinking and the five steps of the nursing process — joined with INC Unit XVI (6 hours) — meaning, purposes and types of nursing theories, with Nightingale, Orem and Roy. They are studied together because theories give the framework within which the nursing process is used.

## 2.1 Critical thinking in nursing

**Critical thinking** is purposeful, reasoned thinking: questioning information, looking at it from different angles, and reaching a sound judgement about what to do. In nursing it turns data into safe decisions.

**Critical thinking competencies**

1. **General critical thinking**: scientific method, problem solving, decision making.
2. **Specific critical thinking in clinical situations**: diagnostic reasoning, clinical inference, clinical decision making.
3. **Specific to nursing**: the nursing process.

**Attitudes for critical thinking**

| Attitude | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Confidence | Trusts own sound judgement, knows own limits |
| Independent thinking | Considers different ideas, does not just copy others |
| Fairness | Listens to all views without bias |
| Responsibility and authority | Follows standards and accepts accountability |
| Risk taking | Willing to question a doubtful order |
| Discipline | Thorough and systematic |
| Perseverance | Keeps looking for the answer to a difficult problem |
| Creativity | Finds new ways to meet a patient's needs |
| Curiosity | Asks "why?" |
| Integrity | Honest, admits mistakes |
| Humility | Recognises the need for more knowledge |

**Levels of critical thinking in nursing**

1. **Basic**: the learner follows rules and procedures exactly and trusts experts to have the right answer.
2. **Complex**: the nurse begins to analyse and weigh options, recognising that there may be more than one solution.
3. **Commitment**: the nurse chooses an action, takes responsibility for it and learns from its outcome.

## 2.2 Nursing process: overview

The **nursing process** is a systematic, scientific, problem-solving method used to give individualised, holistic care. It has five steps, remembered as **ADPIE**:

```text
      ┌──────────────► Assessment ──────────────┐
      │                                          ▼
  Evaluation                               Nursing Diagnosis
      ▲                                          │
      │                                          ▼
 Implementation ◄──────────────────────── Planning
        (cyclic and continuous — evaluation leads back to reassessment)
```

**Characteristics**: cyclic and dynamic, client-centred, goal-directed, systematic, interpersonal, universally applicable, based on critical thinking.

**Benefits**: individualised care, continuity, better communication in the team, legal record, patient participation, professional growth.

## 2.3 Step 1: Assessment

**Collection of data**

- **Types of data**: subjective (stated by the patient — "I feel dizzy") and objective (observed or measured — BP 90/60 mmHg).
- **Sources**: primary (the patient — the best source) and secondary (family, other health team members, records, investigation reports, literature).
- **Methods**: interview (nursing history), observation, physical examination, review of records and tests.

**Organising data**: grouping data into a framework — Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Gordon's functional health patterns, body systems or a nursing theory.

**Validating data**: double-checking that data are accurate and complete — re-measure an abnormal reading, compare subjective and objective data (a patient who says "no pain" but grimaces), ask another nurse.

**Documenting data**: record promptly and accurately, using the patient's words for subjective data.

## 2.4 Step 2: Nursing diagnosis

A **nursing diagnosis** is a clinical judgement about the person's (or family's or community's) response to actual or potential health problems or life processes; it is the basis for choosing nursing interventions for which the nurse is accountable (NANDA International).

**Identification**: analyse the data, find cues and patterns, identify **problems**, **risks** and **strengths**.

**Parts of a diagnostic statement (PES format)**

| Part | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P — Problem | NANDA-I diagnostic label | Acute pain |
| E — Etiology | Related factor ("related to") | related to surgical incision |
| S — Signs and symptoms | Defining characteristics ("as evidenced by") | as evidenced by pain score 7/10, grimacing, guarding of abdomen |

**Types of nursing diagnosis (NANDA-I)**

| Type | Statement | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem-focused (actual) | Three parts (P, E, S) | Impaired skin integrity related to immobility as evidenced by stage 2 sacral pressure injury |
| Risk | Two parts (problem + risk factors), no signs yet | Risk for infection related to indwelling urinary catheter |
| Health promotion | Readiness to improve | Readiness for enhanced nutrition |
| Syndrome | Cluster of diagnoses | Disuse syndrome |

**Guidelines for formulating a nursing diagnosis**

1. Write it in terms of the patient's response, not a medical diagnosis ("Ineffective airway clearance", not "pneumonia").
2. Use "related to" rather than "due to" or "caused by".
3. The etiology must be something nursing can change.
4. Do not write a legally risky statement ("related to nurse not turning the patient").
5. Do not write a nursing need, a test or an equipment problem as a diagnosis ("needs bed bath", "catheter blocked").
6. Do not repeat the same idea in both parts.
7. Use NANDA-approved labels where possible.

**Examples of NANDA-I approved diagnoses**: Ineffective breathing pattern, Impaired gas exchange, Deficient fluid volume, Excess fluid volume, Imbalanced nutrition: less than body requirements, Constipation, Urinary retention, Impaired physical mobility, Self-care deficit (bathing), Risk for falls, Anxiety, Disturbed sleep pattern, Deficient knowledge, Grieving.

**Medical diagnosis versus nursing diagnosis**

| Medical diagnosis | Nursing diagnosis |
| --- | --- |
| Names a disease or disorder | Describes the person's response to the disease or situation |
| Made by the doctor | Made by the nurse |
| Stays the same while the disease is present | Changes as the patient's response changes |
| Treatment is medical (drugs, surgery) | Treatment is by nursing interventions |
| Example: Diabetes mellitus | Example: Risk for unstable blood glucose level related to lack of knowledge of diet |

## 2.5 Step 3: Planning

**Types of planning**

1. **Initial planning**: by the admitting nurse, after the first assessment.
2. **Ongoing planning**: done during each shift as new data come in.
3. **Discharge planning**: begins on admission; prepares patient and family for care at home.

**Establishing priorities**: rank problems so that the most urgent are dealt with first.

- **High**: threat to life (airway obstruction, bleeding, shock).
- **Medium**: not life-threatening but may cause harm (acute pain, immobility).
- **Low**: needs that can wait or need little support.
- Use Maslow (physiological needs first) and the **ABC** rule (airway, breathing, circulation), and consider the patient's own priorities.

**Establishing goals and expected outcomes**

- **Purposes**: give direction to care, are the yardstick for evaluation, motivate patient and nurse.
- **Types**: **short-term** (hours to days, usually within the hospital stay) and **long-term** (weeks to months, often after discharge).
- **Components of a goal/outcome statement**: **Subject** (the patient), **Verb** (measurable action — states, walks, demonstrates), **Conditions** (with the help of a walker), **Performance criteria** (how much, how well — 50 metres without breathlessness), **Target time** (by the third post-operative day).
- **Guidelines** — goals should be **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Realistic, Time-bound; patient-centred, derived from the diagnosis, agreed with the patient.

Example: "The patient will walk 50 metres along the corridor with a walker, without breathlessness, by the third post-operative day."

**Types of nursing interventions**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent | Nurse decides and acts on own judgement | Turning 2-hourly, health teaching, positioning |
| Dependent | Need a doctor's order | Giving medicines, IV fluids |
| Collaborative (interdependent) | With other team members | Chest physiotherapy with the physiotherapist, diet plan with the dietitian |

**Selecting interventions**: choose actions that are safe, evidence-based, acceptable to the patient, achievable with available resources, and directed at the etiology.

- **Protocols**: written plans for a particular situation (for example care after a cardiac catheterisation).
- **Standing orders**: a doctor's pre-signed orders the nurse may carry out in defined situations (for example in an ICU or a primary health centre).

**Nursing Intervention Classification (NIC)** is a standardised list of nursing actions (each with a label, definition and activities). **Nursing Outcome Classification (NOC)** is a standardised list of patient outcomes with measurement scales. With NANDA-I they give a common language (**NNN linkage**) for care plans and electronic records.

**Guidelines for writing a care plan**

1. Date and sign the plan.
2. Use standard medical terms and approved abbreviations.
3. Be specific — "turn 2-hourly from back to left side to right side".
4. Refer to procedure manuals instead of writing every step.
5. Make it individual and include the patient's preferences.
6. Include collaborative and discharge aspects.
7. Keep it current — revise as the patient changes.

**Format of a care plan**

| Assessment | Nursing diagnosis | Goal/expected outcome | Interventions | Rationale | Evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "I feel breathless." RR 28/min, SpO2 91% on room air, crackles at bases | Impaired gas exchange related to fluid in alveoli as evidenced by SpO2 91% and breathlessness | SpO2 ≥ 95% and RR 16–20/min within 24 h | Fowler's position; oxygen as prescribed; deep breathing; monitor SpO2 hourly | Upright position lets the lungs expand; oxygen raises arterial oxygen | Goal partly met; continue plan |

## 2.6 Step 4: Implementation

**Process of implementing the plan of care**

1. **Reassess** the patient — the condition may have changed.
2. **Review and revise** the plan if needed.
3. **Organise resources** — equipment, staff, time, environment.
4. **Anticipate and prevent complications**.
5. **Implement** the interventions with skill and safety.
6. **Document and communicate**.

**Skills needed**: cognitive (knowledge, judgement), interpersonal (communication, teaching), technical (procedures).

**Types of care**

- **Direct care**: interventions carried out through contact with the patient — feeding, bathing, giving medicines, teaching, counselling, emergency measures.
- **Indirect care**: actions away from the patient but on the patient's behalf — co-ordinating with other departments, writing records, managing the environment, infection control, arranging referrals.

## 2.7 Step 5: Evaluation

**Evaluation process**

1. Identify the expected outcomes (from the plan).
2. Collect data about the patient's response.
3. Compare the response with the outcome: **met**, **partly met** or **not met**.
4. Relate the nursing actions to the outcome.
5. Draw conclusions: continue, modify or end the plan; reassess if the goal was not met (was the diagnosis wrong? was the goal unrealistic? were interventions not carried out?).

**Types of evaluation**: **structure** (setting, equipment, staff), **process** (how care was given), **outcome** (change in the patient).

**Documentation and reporting**: record the evaluation statement with the data ("Goal met: patient walked 50 m with walker without breathlessness on day 3"), and report changes at handover.

## 2.8 Nursing theories: introduction

**Meaning and definition**: a **theory** is a set of related concepts, definitions and statements that describe, explain, predict or prescribe a phenomenon. A **nursing theory** is an organised framework of concepts that explains what nursing is, what nurses do and why.

**The four metaparadigm concepts** found in every nursing theory: **person**, **environment**, **health** and **nursing**.

**Purposes of nursing theories**

1. Give a scientific base and identity to the profession.
2. Guide practice, education and research.
3. Help nurses describe, explain and predict patient responses.
4. Improve the quality and consistency of care and communication.

**Types of theories**

| Type | Scope | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grand theories | Broad, abstract, apply to all nursing | Orem's self-care deficit theory, Roy's adaptation model |
| Middle-range theories | Narrower, testable, a specific phenomenon | Peplau's interpersonal relations, Pender's health promotion model, Mishel's uncertainty in illness |
| Practice (situation-specific) theories | Very narrow, one clinical situation | A theory of pain relief after surgery |

They can also be grouped by purpose — **descriptive** (describe a phenomenon) and **prescriptive** (direct actions to achieve outcomes).

## 2.9 Selected nursing theories

### Florence Nightingale — Environmental theory
- Nursing is putting the patient in the best condition for nature to act upon him.
- Focus on the **environment**: fresh air (ventilation), light, warmth, cleanliness, quiet, proper drainage and sanitation, pure water, diet, and observation of the patient.
- Use today: infection control, hand hygiene, ward ventilation, noise control, nutrition.

### Dorothea Orem — Self-care deficit theory
Three linked theories:

1. **Theory of self-care**: people perform activities to maintain life and health. Self-care requisites are **universal** (air, water, food, elimination, activity and rest, solitude and social interaction, prevention of hazards, normal functioning), **developmental** (needs at each life stage) and **health-deviation** (needs arising from illness or injury).
2. **Theory of self-care deficit**: nursing is needed when the demand for self-care is greater than the person's ability (self-care agency).
3. **Theory of nursing systems**:

| Nursing system | When | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wholly compensatory | Patient can do nothing | Unconscious patient |
| Partly compensatory | Nurse and patient share care | Post-operative patient who can wash face but not walk |
| Supportive-educative | Patient can do it with teaching and support | New diabetic learning self-monitoring |

### Sister Callista Roy — Adaptation model
- The person is an **adaptive system** responding to stimuli from the environment.
- **Stimuli**: **focal** (the immediate one — surgery), **contextual** (other factors present — noisy ward), **residual** (unclear past beliefs and experiences).
- **Coping mechanisms**: regulator (physiological) and cognator (thinking and emotion).
- **Four adaptive modes**: **physiological**, **self-concept**, **role function**, **interdependence**.
- The goal of nursing is to promote adaptation in all four modes.

## 2.10 Use of theories in nursing practice

- **Assessment** is organised by the theory (Orem: what self-care can the patient do? Roy: which mode shows ineffective response?).
- **Diagnosis and planning** are framed by its concepts.
- **Education**: curricula built on theory.
- **Research**: theories are tested and refined.
- **Administration**: nursing models shape policies and documentation.

## Remember

- Nursing process: Assessment, Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation — cyclic, client-centred, goal-directed.
- Data: subjective/objective; sources primary/secondary; methods interview, observation, examination, records.
- PES = Problem, Etiology ("related to"), Signs and symptoms ("as evidenced by").
- NANDA-I types: problem-focused, risk, health promotion, syndrome.
- Goals: SMART; short-term and long-term; components subject, verb, condition, criteria, time.
- Interventions: independent, dependent, collaborative; care: direct and indirect.
- NIC = interventions; NOC = outcomes.
- Theories: grand, middle-range, practice; metaparadigm = person, environment, health, nursing.
- Nightingale = environment; Orem = self-care deficit, three nursing systems; Roy = adaptation, four modes.
