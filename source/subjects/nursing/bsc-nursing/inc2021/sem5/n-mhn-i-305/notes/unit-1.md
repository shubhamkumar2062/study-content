# Unit 1: Introduction to mental health nursing

This unit covers INC Unit I (6 hours). It sets the scene: what mental health and mental illness mean, how care of the mentally ill grew from chains and asylums into a team-based service, who works in that team, and what the mental health nurse actually does.

## 1.1 Mental health, mental illness and the continuum

**Mental health** is a state of well-being in which a person realises their own abilities, copes with the ordinary stresses of life, works productively, and contributes to the community. It is not merely the absence of a disorder.

**Mental illness (mental disorder)** is a clinically significant disturbance of a person's thinking, mood, perception or behaviour, linked with distress or impaired functioning, that is not just an expected response to a loss or a culturally approved reaction.

Health and illness are better seen as the two ends of a **continuum**, not two boxes. A person moves along it depending on stress, coping, support and biology.

```text
  MENTAL HEALTH  <------------------------------------>  MENTAL ILLNESS
  coping well        mild distress     moderate         severe, functioning
  good function      still functioning  symptoms          badly impaired
```

**Characteristics of a mentally healthy person** (commonly taught set):
- accepts self and others, has a positive self-concept
- is in touch with reality; perceives the world as it is
- controls and expresses emotions appropriately
- forms and keeps satisfying relationships
- works, loves and plays; is reasonably independent
- can meet the demands of life and adapt to change
- finds meaning and purpose in life

## 1.2 Concepts of normal and abnormal behaviour

There is no single test for "normal". Judgement uses several yardsticks together:

| Criterion | What it asks |
| --- | --- |
| Statistical | Is the behaviour common or rare in the population? (rare is not always abnormal) |
| Social / cultural norms | Does it fit what this culture accepts? (what is normal in one culture may not be in another) |
| Subjective distress | Does the person feel distressed or suffer? |
| Impaired functioning (maladaptive) | Does it stop the person working, studying, relating or self-caring? |
| Deviation from ideal mental health | How far is it from the healthy picture above? |

A behaviour is more likely to be called **abnormal** when it is distressing, disabling, deviant from norms, and a danger to self or others ("the four Ds"). Nurses avoid labelling a person too quickly; context, culture and duration all matter.

## 1.3 Evolution of mental health services and nursing

**Early and medieval period.** Mental illness was blamed on evil spirits, sin or the moon ("lunacy"). "Treatment" was exorcism, confinement, chains and neglect.

**Moral treatment era (late 18th century).** Philippe Pinel in France famously unchained the mentally ill and argued for humane care. In England, William Tuke set up the York Retreat. Care became kinder, with routine, work and respect.

**Reform of asylums (19th century).** Dorothea Dix in the USA campaigned for proper institutions instead of jails. Large mental hospitals were built, but many later became overcrowded and custodial.

**Birth of modern psychiatric nursing (20th century).** **Hildegard Peplau** is called the mother of psychiatric nursing. Her 1952 work on interpersonal relations made the **nurse-patient relationship itself a tool of treatment**. The arrival of effective drugs (first antipsychotic and antidepressant medicines from the 1950s) allowed many patients to live outside hospital — the start of **deinstitutionalisation** and community care.

**In India** (broad outline a student should know):
- Older British laws governed "lunatic asylums"; the Indian Lunacy Act of 1912 was in force for most of the last century.
- The Bhore Committee (1946) drew attention to the poor state of mental hospitals.
- Centres such as NIMHANS (Bengaluru) and the Central Institute of Psychiatry (Ranchi) developed training, treatment and research.
- The **National Mental Health Programme (NMHP) was launched in 1982** to make basic mental health care available to all, especially the under-served, and to join mental health with general health services.
- The **District Mental Health Programme (DMHP)** was added from the 1990s to take care to the district and primary-care level.
- The **Mental Health Act 1987** replaced the old Lunacy Act and was itself replaced by the rights-based **Mental Healthcare Act 2017** (studied in Unit 2).

The overall shift has been: **custody → treatment → rights, recovery and community care.**

## 1.4 Nature and scope of mental health nursing

**Mental health (psychiatric) nursing** is the specialised field of nursing that uses the nursing process and the purposeful use of self to promote mental health, prevent mental illness, care for people with mental disorders, and help them recover and reintegrate into society.

Its **scope** runs across:
- **Promotion** of mental health in schools, workplaces, families and the community
- **Prevention** of mental illness and early identification
- **Curative care** of acutely ill people in hospitals and psychiatric units
- **Rehabilitation** and long-term support for chronic illness
- **Settings**: psychiatric hospitals and units in general hospitals, de-addiction centres, child guidance clinics, community mental health centres, outreach camps, schools, old-age homes, prisons, disaster sites, and the patient's own home.

## 1.5 The mental health team

Mental health care is team work. Each member has a distinct role but they plan care together.

| Member | Main role |
| --- | --- |
| Psychiatrist | Medical doctor; diagnoses, prescribes drugs and physical treatments (e.g. ECT), leads treatment |
| Clinical psychologist | Psychological testing, psychotherapy and behaviour therapy |
| Psychiatric nurse | 24-hour care, therapeutic relationship, gives and monitors treatment, applies the nursing process, health teaching |
| Psychiatric social worker | Family and social assessment, links with community resources, rehabilitation, welfare schemes |
| Occupational therapist | Uses purposeful activity and work to restore skills and confidence |
| Counsellor / psychotherapist | Talking therapies for specific problems |
| Others | Pharmacist, dietitian, special educator, recreation/art/music therapist, and importantly the **patient and family** themselves |

The nurse is often the member who is **with the patient around the clock**, so the nurse's observations and the therapeutic relationship are central.

## 1.6 Role and functions of the mental health nurse

The psychiatric nurse wears several "hats":

- **Caregiver** — meets physical and emotional needs, keeps the patient safe.
- **Therapist / counsellor** — builds a therapeutic relationship, listens, supports.
- **Teacher (health educator)** — teaches patient and family about the illness, medicines and relapse signs.
- **Manager / coordinator** — plans and organises care, runs the ward, coordinates the team.
- **Advocate** — protects the patient's rights, dignity and consent.
- **Socialising agent / role model** — helps the patient relearn social skills.
- **Technician** — gives medicines safely, assists in ECT, monitors for side effects.
- **Researcher** — uses and adds to evidence for better care.

**Factors that affect the level of nursing practice** (how much a nurse can actually do):
- the nurse's own education, experience and competence
- the law and professional standards (INC)
- the setting and resources available
- the practice environment, staffing and supervision
- the nurse's attitudes and freedom from stigma
- the patient's condition and the degree of autonomy allowed

## 1.7 Attitudes, stigma and self-awareness

**Stigma** — negative labels and discrimination against people with mental illness — is a major barrier to care; it makes people hide symptoms and avoid treatment. Nurses challenge stigma by their own respectful, non-judgemental behaviour and by educating families and the public.

Because the nurse's personality is a treatment tool, **self-awareness** matters. The nurse must know their own feelings, prejudices and reactions so these do not harm the relationship.

## 1.8 Application and implication in nursing

- Treat every patient as a person with rights and dignity, not as a diagnosis.
- Use yourself therapeutically: a calm, consistent, accepting nurse is itself part of treatment.
- Watch your own attitudes; stigma can hide inside caring staff too.
- Know the team and refer appropriately; no single person manages mental illness alone.
- Promotion and prevention are nursing work, not only hospital cure — health teaching in a family is mental health nursing.

## Remember

- Mental health is well-being and good functioning, not just absence of illness; health and illness lie on a continuum.
- Abnormal behaviour is judged by the "four Ds": distress, disability (maladaptive), deviance from norms, danger.
- Pinel unchained patients (moral treatment); Dorothea Dix reformed asylums; **Peplau is the mother of psychiatric nursing** (nurse-patient relationship as a tool).
- India: NMHP launched 1982; DMHP added from the 1990s; Mental Health Act 1987 → Mental Healthcare Act 2017.
- The shift in care: custody → treatment → rights and community care.
- The team includes psychiatrist, psychologist, psychiatric nurse, psychiatric social worker, occupational therapist — and the patient and family.
- Nurse roles: caregiver, counsellor, teacher, manager, advocate, socialising agent, technician, researcher.
- Stigma blocks care; self-awareness protects the therapeutic relationship.
