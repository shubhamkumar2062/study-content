# Unit 5: Nursing management of schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders

This unit covers INC Unit V (10 hours). Schizophrenia is the prototype psychotic disorder and a cornerstone of the whole subject. Understand the biology, recognise the symptoms, know the antipsychotic drugs and their side effects, and be confident with nursing diagnoses and care plans.

---

## 5.1 Review: What is a psychotic disorder?

A **psychotic disorder** is characterised by a loss of contact with reality — the person cannot tell what is real from what is not. The defining features are **hallucinations** (false perceptions) and **delusions** (fixed false beliefs), and in schizophrenia, also **disorganised thinking** and **negative symptoms**.

## 5.2 Schizophrenia: overview and epidemiology

- Prevalence: approximately 1% of the world population; affects all cultures.
- Onset: typically late teens to mid-30s; males tend to present earlier (early 20s) than females (late 20s).
- Course: episodic with incomplete remission (many patients have residual symptoms between episodes); a minority recover fully; some deteriorate.
- Schizophrenia is a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide.

## 5.3 Aetiology and dopamine hypothesis

No single cause is established; schizophrenia is best explained by a **biopsychosocial model**.

| Factor | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Genetic | Twin studies: 50% concordance in identical twins; 10–15% in first-degree relatives (vs ~1% general population) |
| Neurodevelopmental | Prenatal events (maternal infection, malnutrition, hypoxia); minor brain structure differences |
| Neurotransmitter | Dopamine hypothesis: excess dopaminergic activity in mesolimbic pathway → positive symptoms; hypodopaminergia in prefrontal cortex → negative symptoms |
| Glutamate | NMDA receptor hypofunction also implicated |
| Psychosocial | High expressed emotion in family, urban living, social adversity can trigger relapse |
| Substance use | Cannabis use (especially adolescent onset) is a risk factor |

**Dopamine hypothesis in brief:**

```text
  MESOLIMBIC pathway overactive → POSITIVE symptoms (hallucinations, delusions)
  MESOCORTICAL pathway underactive → NEGATIVE symptoms (flat affect, poverty of speech)
  NIGROSTRIATAL pathway blocked by drugs → EXTRAPYRAMIDAL side effects
  TUBEROINFUNDIBULAR pathway blocked → HYPERPROLACTINAEMIA
```

## 5.4 Types of symptoms

### Positive symptoms (added to normal experience)

| Symptom | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Hallucinations | Auditory most common: third-person commentary, voices discussing the patient, command hallucinations |
| Delusions | Persecutory, grandiose, reference, passivity (thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcasting/control) |
| Disorganised speech | Loosening of associations, tangentiality, incoherence, word salad |
| Disorganised behaviour | Unpredictable, socially inappropriate actions; catatonia |

### Negative symptoms (taken away from normal experience)

| Symptom | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Affective flattening | Reduced range and intensity of emotional expression |
| Alogia | Poverty of speech; brief, empty replies |
| Avolition | Lack of motivation to initiate or sustain activity |
| Anhedonia | Inability to feel pleasure |
| Social withdrawal (asociality) | Loss of interest in social contact |
| Attentional impairment | Difficulty focusing on tasks |

Negative symptoms are more difficult to treat and are most responsible for long-term disability.

### Cognitive symptoms

Impaired working memory, attention, executive function and processing speed — present before the first episode and persist throughout.

## 5.5 Subtypes of schizophrenia (DSM-IV; still asked in exams)

| Subtype | Key feature |
| --- | --- |
| Paranoid | Prominent delusions and/or hallucinations; relatively preserved functioning |
| Disorganised (hebephrenic) | Disorganised speech and behaviour, flat or inappropriate affect; poor prognosis |
| Catatonic | Marked psychomotor disturbance — stupor, negativism, rigidity, waxy flexibility, excitement |
| Undifferentiated | Criteria for schizophrenia met but not fitting one specific subtype |
| Residual | No prominent positive symptoms but negative symptoms and mild positive features persist |

(Note: DSM-5 removed formal subtypes from the diagnostic criteria but they remain in teaching and older INC references.)

## 5.6 Other psychotic disorders

| Disorder | Key features |
| --- | --- |
| **Brief psychotic disorder** | Psychotic symptoms lasting 1 day to 1 month, then full recovery; often after stress |
| **Schizophreniform disorder** | Same features as schizophrenia but lasting 1–6 months |
| **Schizoaffective disorder** | Schizophrenia symptoms PLUS major mood episode (depression or mania) occurring together and at different times |
| **Delusional disorder** | Fixed, non-bizarre delusion; functioning otherwise relatively preserved; no prominent hallucinations |
| **Substance-induced psychosis** | Psychotic symptoms caused directly by a substance (cannabis, amphetamine) or its withdrawal (alcohol) |
| **Psychosis due to medical condition** | Systemic lupus, thyroid disorders, delirium, epilepsy, etc. |

## 5.7 Assessment of a patient with schizophrenia

**MSE findings typically seen in schizophrenia:**
- Appearance: dishevelled, poor hygiene
- Behaviour: disorganised, catatonic posturing or agitated
- Speech: poverty of speech or disorganised; echolalia; neologisms
- Mood: flat or blunted affect; incongruent affect (laughing while expressing a sad thought)
- Thought form: loosening of associations, tangentiality, thought blocking
- Thought content: persecutory, reference, passivity delusions
- Perception: auditory hallucinations (third-person, commanding, arguing voices)
- Cognition: impaired attention and concentration
- Insight: Grade 1 or 2 in acute phase
- Judgement: impaired

**Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 simplified):** At least one clear first-rank symptom (or two second-rank) for at least one month, not explained by organic illness or substances.

Schneider's **first-rank symptoms** (highly characteristic of schizophrenia):
- Thought insertion, withdrawal, broadcasting
- Passivity feelings (made feelings, made impulses, made actions)
- Delusional perception
- Third-person auditory hallucinations (voice commentary, two voices discussing)

## 5.8 Medical management

**First-line:** Atypical antipsychotic (risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole) for 2 or more years after first episode.

**Second-line:** Clozapine for treatment-resistant schizophrenia (failed two adequate antipsychotic trials).

**Adjuncts:** Benzodiazepine in acute agitation; mood stabiliser in schizoaffective disorder.

**Acute phase care:** May need involuntary admission under MHCA 2017 if patient is dangerous or unable to care for themselves; manage agitation safely.

**Maintenance:** Long-acting injectable antipsychotics for poor adherence.

## 5.9 Nursing diagnoses and care

### Common nursing diagnoses

1. Disturbed sensory perception (auditory/visual hallucinations)
2. Disturbed thought processes (delusional thinking)
3. Risk for violence (self-directed or other-directed)
4. Self-care deficit (bathing, grooming, feeding, toileting)
5. Impaired social interaction
6. Disturbed sleep pattern
7. Non-compliance with medication
8. Ineffective coping
9. Deficient knowledge (patient and family about illness and treatment)

### Nursing interventions

**For hallucinations:**
- Do not argue with or deny the hallucinations but do not reinforce them: "I know the voices are real to you, but I am not hearing them."
- Help the patient identify when hallucinations are worst (triggers: stress, fatigue, isolation).
- Offer distraction (music through headphones, activity, conversation).
- Assess for command hallucinations instructing harm; report at once.
- Ensure medicines are given and taken (observe swallowing).

**For delusions:**
- Do not argue, agree, or challenge the delusional belief directly.
- Do not play along ("That must be terrible to have people following you") — this reinforces the belief.
- Redirect to reality-based topics calmly.
- Document the content precisely using the patient's words.
- Assess whether the delusion poses a risk to self or others.

**For disorganised behaviour:**
- Keep the environment calm and predictable with clear structure.
- Use short, simple sentences.
- Break tasks into small steps.
- Anticipate needs rather than waiting for the patient to become agitated.

**For self-care deficit:**
- Assist with hygiene, dressing, meals and toileting without taking over.
- Provide prompts and step-by-step guidance.
- Use positive reinforcement; never mock or shame.

**For risk of violence:**
- Monitor for warning signs: pacing, clenched fists, raised voice, intense staring.
- De-escalate early: calm tone, personal space, offer choice ("Would you like to walk with me?").
- Remove potential weapons from the environment (sharps, breakable objects).
- Call for help before the patient becomes unmanageable.
- Document all incidents and near-misses.

**For medication non-adherence:**
- Educate about the importance of maintenance therapy.
- Identify reasons for stopping (side effects, lack of insight, stigma, cost).
- Address side effects proactively (e.g. use depot injection for forgetting; anticholinergic for EPS).
- Involve family in supervision of medications.

### Safety considerations

- **Ligature points:** assess the ward environment; remove cords, belts, extension cables from areas accessible to suicidal patients.
- **Absconding:** know the patient's level of leave and supervise accordingly.
- **Self-neglect:** some patients may not eat, drink or manage fluid balance.

## 5.10 Health education for patient and family

| Topic | Key messages |
| --- | --- |
| Nature of illness | Schizophrenia is a brain illness, not a moral failure or spiritual problem; it is treatable |
| Medicines | Take every day, even when feeling well; never stop suddenly; report side effects |
| Early warning signs | Sleep disturbance, increasing suspicion, hearing voices — come back to clinic early |
| Stress | High stress and high expressed emotion can trigger relapse; keep life stable |
| Substances | Avoid cannabis and alcohol — these make symptoms worse and cause relapse |
| Follow-up | Regular clinic visits; crisis plan if relapse begins |
| Stigma | Help family understand and explain to neighbours; rights under MHCA 2017 |

## 5.11 Application and implication in nursing

- Always document the content of delusions and hallucinations exactly — this helps track improvement.
- Reward reality-based interaction; build on healthy parts of the person's functioning.
- Engage the family as partners; reducing expressed emotion reduces relapse rates.
- Recovery is possible; hold hope even when the patient cannot.

## Remember

- Schizophrenia: onset 18–35 years; affects 1% of population; genetic risk in first-degree relatives ~10–15%.
- Positive symptoms: hallucinations (auditory most common), delusions (persecutory, passivity), disorganised speech and behaviour.
- Negative symptoms: flat affect, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, social withdrawal — harder to treat.
- Dopamine hypothesis: mesolimbic excess → positive symptoms; mesocortical deficit → negative symptoms.
- Schneider's first-rank symptoms include thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcasting, passivity phenomena, third-person hallucinations.
- Subtypes: paranoid (best prognosis), hebephrenic/disorganised (worst), catatonic, undifferentiated, residual.
- Schizoaffective = schizophrenia features + major mood episode.
- Brief psychotic disorder: 1 day–1 month then full recovery.
- Nursing: do not argue with delusions or deny hallucinations; use short sentences; supervise self-care; monitor for violence warning signs; educate family about expressed emotion and early warning signs.
- Clozapine: treatment-resistant schizophrenia; weekly WBC monitoring mandatory.
