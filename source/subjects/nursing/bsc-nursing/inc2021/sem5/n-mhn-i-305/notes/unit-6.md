# Unit 6: Nursing management of mood disorders

This unit covers INC Unit VI (10 hours). Mood disorders — depression and bipolar disorder — are among the most common, most disabling and most treatable of all psychiatric conditions. Suicide risk assessment runs through every section. This is a high-scoring unit.

---

## 6.1 Overview of mood disorders

**Mood disorders** are characterised by a primary, clinically significant disturbance of mood — either low (depression), high (mania), or both at different times (bipolar disorder). They cause marked distress and impaired functioning.

```text
  SPECTRUM OF MOOD
  ←─────────────────────────────────────────────────────→
  Severe      Moderate   Mild      Euthymia    Hypomania   Mania
  Depression  Depression Depression (normal)   (mild high)  (severe high)
```

## 6.2 Depression: types and classification

| Type | Key feature |
| --- | --- |
| Major depressive disorder (MDD) | One or more major depressive episodes; most common diagnosis |
| Dysthymia (persistent depressive disorder) | Low-level, chronic depression lasting at least 2 years |
| Postpartum (postnatal) depression | Onset within 4 weeks (up to 1 year) after childbirth |
| Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) | Depressive episodes with a seasonal pattern (usually winter) |
| Premenstrual dysphoric disorder | Severe mood changes in the week before menstruation |
| Psychotic depression | Major depression with delusions or hallucinations (mood-congruent) |
| Recurrent depressive disorder | Multiple episodes, with normal mood between episodes |

## 6.3 Clinical features of depression

### Diagnostic criteria (simplified DSM-5/ICD-10)

For a major depressive episode: **five or more** of the following symptoms, present for at least **two weeks**, including either (1) depressed mood or (2) loss of interest/pleasure:

| Feature | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Depressed mood | Sad, empty, hopeless; tearfulness; in some patients irritability |
| Loss of interest/pleasure (anhedonia) | Cannot enjoy activities that were once pleasurable |
| Weight change | Significant weight loss or gain; change in appetite |
| Sleep disturbance | Insomnia (most common) or hypersomnia |
| Psychomotor change | Retardation (slowed movement, speech) or agitation |
| Fatigue | Low energy nearly every day |
| Worthlessness/guilt | Excessive, inappropriate guilt; self-blame |
| Cognitive impairment | Reduced concentration, indecisiveness |
| Suicidal ideation | Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, plan or attempt |

### Beck's cognitive triad

Aaron Beck described the negative thinking pattern of depression as three interlocking negative beliefs:

```text
  BECK'S COGNITIVE TRIAD:
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │  1. Negative view of SELF              │
  │     "I am worthless, a failure"        │
  │                                        │
  │  2. Negative view of the WORLD         │
  │     "Everything is against me"         │
  │                                        │
  │  3. Negative view of the FUTURE        │
  │     "Nothing will ever get better"     │
  └────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

These thinking patterns are the target of cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT).

### Biological features ("somatic" or "vegetative" symptoms — especially examined)

- **Early morning awakening** (wakes 2–3 hours earlier than normal; cannot return to sleep) — classic
- Mood worse in the morning (diurnal variation — improves slightly during the day)
- Marked weight loss and appetite loss
- Markedly decreased libido
- Psychomotor retardation

## 6.4 Aetiology of depression

| Factor | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Genetic | Twin concordance; family history doubles risk |
| Neurobiological | Monoamine hypothesis: deficiency of serotonin and/or noradrenaline; HPA axis dysregulation (cortisol excess) |
| Psychological | Beck's cognitive model (learned negative thinking); learned helplessness (Seligman) |
| Life events | Loss (bereavement, job, relationship), trauma, chronic stress are precipitants |
| Medical | Hypothyroidism, Cushing's, cancer, stroke, Parkinson's disease, chronic pain |
| Drugs | Corticosteroids, beta-blockers, reserpine, oral contraceptives |
| Social | Social isolation, poverty, domestic violence |

## 6.5 Suicide: assessment and nursing care

Suicide is a major risk in depression. All depressed patients must be assessed for suicide risk.

### Why depressed patients are at risk

- Hopelessness is the strongest predictor (even more than depression severity).
- Early in recovery (when energy improves before mood fully lifts) is a particularly dangerous time.
- Late evening and early morning are higher-risk periods.

### Risk assessment

```text
  Assess: Ideation → Plan → Intent → Means available
  "Are you having thoughts that life is not worth living?"
  "Have you thought about how you might do it?"
  "Have you taken any steps?"
```

| High-risk features | Low-risk features |
| --- | --- |
| Previous attempt | No previous attempt |
| Specific, lethal plan | No specific plan |
| Access to means (medication, weapon) | No access to means |
| Male sex, older age | Female sex, younger age |
| Social isolation, no support | Strong social support |
| Hopelessness, intent to die | Ambivalence, reason for living |
| Alcohol or drug use | No substance use |
| Recent discharge from hospital | Community contact maintained |

### Nursing care for the suicidal patient

- **Never leave the patient alone** when suicidal risk is assessed as high.
- Remove all potential means from the environment (medications, sharps, belts, cords, breakable glass).
- Carry out **safety contract** (ask patient to agree not to act on suicidal thoughts without informing staff) — note: this is a clinical tool, not a legal safeguard.
- Do not make false promises of confidentiality; suicidal plans must be shared with the team.
- Establish a therapeutic relationship: listen empathically without minimising.
- Increase observation level as ordered (15-minute, 30-minute or continuous special).
- Notify the physician and document fully.
- Provide psychoeducation to family about warning signs and safe storage of medications.
- After a suicidal attempt: post-attempt care — medical treatment of injuries, psychological assessment, do not leave alone.

**Warning signs of imminent suicide:**
- talks about suicide or death
- gives away prized possessions
- says goodbye to people, ties up affairs
- sudden calmness after a period of severe depression (may have made a decision)

## 6.6 Management of depression

### Pharmacotherapy (reviewed briefly here; full detail in Unit 4)

| Class | First choice | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SSRI | Fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram | First-line; safe in overdose |
| SNRI | Venlafaxine, duloxetine | For depression with pain |
| TCA | Amitriptyline, imipramine | Effective but dangerous in overdose (cardiac) — caution in suicidal patients |
| MAOI | Phenelzine | Used rarely; dietary tyramine restriction |
| Mirtazapine | Mirtazapine | Useful when sleep and appetite loss are prominent |

**Key nursing point:** Antidepressants take 2–4 weeks; warn patient not to stop early because they "feel nothing is happening." Close monitoring in the first 2 weeks of a new prescription.

### Psychotherapy

- **CBT** — first-line for mild-to-moderate depression; targets Beck's cognitive triad.
- **Interpersonal therapy (IPT)** — focuses on relationships and social roles.
- **Behavioural activation** — schedule pleasant activities; overcomes withdrawal.

### ECT for depression

Fastest and most effective treatment for severe depression with:
- suicidal risk so high the patient cannot wait for drugs
- refusal to eat or drink (risk of physical deterioration)
- severe psychotic depression
- failure to respond to two or more adequate drug trials
- severe depression in pregnancy (avoid drugs)

(Procedure and nursing care: see Unit 4.)

## 6.7 Nursing care in depression

| Problem | Nursing interventions |
| --- | --- |
| Low mood and anhedonia | Accept feelings without minimising; encourage small pleasures; behavioural activation |
| Self-neglect | Assist with hygiene and meals; offer preferred foods; nutritional assessment |
| Sleep disturbance | Sleep hygiene; warm milk, relaxation; avoid caffeine and screens |
| Psychomotor retardation | Gentle encouragement; keep tasks very simple; do not rush |
| Suicidal ideation | (See 6.5 above) constant vigilance, observation, remove means |
| Non-adherence | Education about delayed onset; involve family; simplify regimen |
| Social withdrawal | Gradually increase social contact; group activities |
| Hopelessness | Help identify reasons for living; set tiny, achievable goals |

**Nurse's self-care:** Working with depressed and suicidal patients is emotionally draining. Seek supervision; recognise vicarious trauma.

## 6.8 Bipolar disorder

**Bipolar disorder** is characterised by episodes of mania (or hypomania) alternating with episodes of depression, with periods of relatively normal mood (euthymia) in between.

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Bipolar I | At least one full manic episode (may also have depressive episodes) |
| Bipolar II | Recurrent depressive episodes + hypomania (not full mania) |
| Cyclothymia | Numerous periods of hypomanic and mild depressive symptoms over at least 2 years |
| Rapid cycling | Four or more mood episodes in 12 months |

### Features of mania (the manic episode)

| Feature | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Elevated or irritable mood | Euphoric, expansive, or intensely irritable |
| Grandiosity | Inflated self-esteem; belief in special powers, wealth, identity |
| Decreased need for sleep | Rested after 3 hours; does not feel tired |
| Pressured speech | Rapid, loud, difficult to interrupt |
| Flight of ideas | Racing thoughts; jumps from one topic to another |
| Distractibility | Attention drawn easily by irrelevant stimuli |
| Increased goal-directed activity / psychomotor agitation | Many projects, hypersexuality, overspending |
| Reckless behaviour | Excessive spending, risky sexual behaviour, dangerous driving, unwise business decisions |
| Psychotic features (in severe mania) | Grandiose or persecutory delusions, hallucinations |

A **manic episode** lasts at least 1 week. **Hypomania** is similar but less severe and shorter (at least 4 days); does not cause marked impairment or require hospitalisation.

### Management of mania

**Acute:**
- **Hospitalisation** often needed (poor insight, impulsive behaviour, dangerous spending or sexual behaviour).
- **Mood stabilisers:** lithium, sodium valproate, carbamazepine (onset takes 1–2 weeks).
- **Atypical antipsychotics:** olanzapine, quetiapine — fast sedation and control of agitation.
- **Benzodiazepines:** short-term for agitation and sleep.

**Maintenance (prevention of further episodes):**
- Lithium is the gold standard for bipolar prevention; also reduces suicide risk.
- Sodium valproate, lamotrigine (especially for bipolar depression).
- Psychoeducation and mood charting.

### Nursing care in mania

| Problem | Interventions |
| --- | --- |
| Hyperactivity and poor rest | Low-stimulation environment; structured timetable; encourage sleep and rest |
| Nutritional deficit (too busy to eat) | Finger foods, frequent snacks; high-calorie drinks |
| Impulsive, reckless behaviour | Limit access to phone, credit cards, car keys; set firm limits consistently |
| Grandiosity and poor insight | Do not challenge beliefs aggressively; redirect, set limits, give information |
| Pressured speech | Remain calm; use brief, clear responses; allow venting to a limited extent |
| Hypersexuality | Private room if possible; redirect; protect other patients; non-judgemental manner |
| Risk of exhaustion or medical complications | Monitor vitals, hydration, nutrition, skin, joints; encourage rest between activities |
| Non-adherence (doesn't believe ill) | Education; involve family; depot or supervised meds; explain consequences |

## 6.9 Postpartum depression and postpartum psychosis

| Condition | Onset | Features | Management |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Baby blues | Days 3–5 | Tearfulness, anxiety, mood swings; self-limiting | Reassurance, support |
| Postpartum depression | Within 4 weeks to 1 year | Low mood, anhedonia, guilt, fear of harming baby, bonding difficulty | SSRI, psychotherapy, support |
| Postpartum psychosis | Within 2 weeks | Delusions, hallucinations, severe agitation, confusion; psychiatric emergency | Hospitalisation, antipsychotic, mood stabiliser; protect baby |

**Nursing priority in postpartum psychosis:** assess risk to the baby — command hallucinations to harm the baby require immediate action; protect the infant.

## 6.10 Seasonal affective disorder (SAD)

- Depressive episodes occur at the same time each year, typically autumn/winter.
- Features: increased sleep, increased appetite (carbohydrate craving), weight gain, fatigue, low mood.
- Management: **light therapy** (bright-light lamp 10,000 lux for 30 minutes in the morning), SSRI antidepressants, CBT.
- Remits spontaneously in spring.

## Remember

- Major depressive episode: five features for ≥2 weeks; must include depressed mood or anhedonia.
- Beck's cognitive triad: negative view of self, world and future — target for CBT.
- Biological features of depression: early morning awakening, diurnal variation (worse in AM), weight loss, decreased libido, psychomotor retardation.
- Suicide risk: assess ideation → plan → intent → means; hopelessness is the strongest predictor; previous attempt is the biggest risk factor.
- Antidepressants take 2–4 weeks; monitor for suicidal ideation in early weeks; TCAs are dangerous in overdose.
- ECT: fastest for severe depression with suicidal risk or refusal to eat.
- Mania: elevated/irritable mood, grandiosity, reduced sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, impulsive reckless behaviour.
- Bipolar I: full manic episode; Bipolar II: hypomania + depression.
- Lithium: gold standard for bipolar prevention; narrow therapeutic window; monitor levels; dehydration raises levels.
- Postpartum psychosis: psychiatric emergency within 2 weeks of birth; protect the baby.
- SAD: winter depression; treat with morning bright-light therapy.
