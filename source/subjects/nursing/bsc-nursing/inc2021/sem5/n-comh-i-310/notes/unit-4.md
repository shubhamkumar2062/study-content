# Unit 4: Behaviour change communication, health education, and community health nursing approaches

Health behaviour change is at the heart of community health nursing. This unit covers the science and practice of health education, BCC strategies, theories of behaviour change, and the approaches community health nurses use to work with individuals, families, and communities.

---

## 4.1 Health education

**Health education** is the process of helping people acquire knowledge, attitudes, and practices that promote and protect health. It differs from health information, which is one-way communication; health education is interactive and aims for behaviour change.

**Goals of health education**:
1. Enable people to make informed choices about health.
2. Modify unhealthy behaviours (smoking, alcohol, poor diet, unsafe sex).
3. Promote healthy behaviours (vaccination, breastfeeding, hand hygiene, safe sanitation).
4. Develop life skills (communication, decision making, refusal skills).
5. Empower communities to address their own health problems.

**Principles of health education**:
- Start from where people are (knowledge, attitudes, practices).
- Use simple, local language; avoid medical jargon.
- Base content on felt needs of the community.
- Involve participants actively (not lecture alone).
- Repeat key messages consistently.
- Use multiple methods and channels.
- Evaluate whether behaviour change has occurred.

## 4.2 Behaviour Change Communication (BCC)

**BCC** is a systematic, evidence-based approach to changing health behaviours and social norms through communication strategies tailored to specific audiences.

**BCC vs IEC**:
- **IEC** (Information, Education, Communication): traditional approach; provides information and educates — often one-way.
- **BCC**: builds on IEC; emphasises social and behavioural factors; uses participatory methods; targets specific barriers to change.

**Key elements of effective BCC**:
- **Audience analysis**: understand the target audience's knowledge, attitudes, practices, barriers, and motivators before designing messages.
- **Message design**: clear, simple, action-oriented messages (not "eat nutritious food" but "eat one palm-sized portion of dal every day").
- **Channel selection**: appropriate media for the audience (community meeting, radio, mobile SMS, street play, women's self-help group).
- **Pretesting**: test materials on a sample of the target audience before wide distribution.
- **Feedback and evaluation**: measure whether behaviour changed, not just knowledge.

## 4.3 Theories and models of behaviour change

### Health Belief Model (HBM)

Developed in the 1950s to explain why people do not use preventive health services. A person will take a health action if they believe:
1. **Perceived susceptibility**: "I can get this disease."
2. **Perceived severity**: "This disease is serious."
3. **Perceived benefits**: "This action will protect me."
4. **Perceived barriers**: "The barriers to action are manageable."
5. **Cues to action**: a trigger event (symptom, reminder, media message) prompts action.
6. **Self-efficacy**: "I am able to do this."

**Application**: designing messages that increase perceived susceptibility (risk communication) and reduce perceived barriers (address cost, access, side effects of vaccines).

### Transtheoretical Model (Stages of Change)

Developed by Prochaska and DiClemente. Behaviour change is a process, not a single event. People move through stages:

| Stage | Description | Communication strategy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-contemplation | Not thinking about change; not aware of the problem | Raise awareness; personalise risk |
| Contemplation | Aware; thinking about change; ambivalent | Explore pros and cons; motivational interviewing |
| Preparation | Intending to change soon; planning | Help make a concrete plan; build confidence |
| Action | Actively making behaviour change | Support; encourage; reinforce |
| Maintenance | Sustaining the change for > 6 months | Prevent relapse; build social support |
| Relapse | Returns to old behaviour | Normalise; encourage to try again; analyse triggers |

### Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura)

**Self-efficacy** (confidence to perform a behaviour) is the key predictor of behaviour change. Learning occurs through **observation** (watching others succeed) and **modelling** (seeing someone like yourself do it). Use peer educators, community champions, and role-play demonstrations.

### PRECEDE-PROCEED model

A community diagnosis framework:
- **PRECEDE** (planning): identify the health problem, its behavioural and environmental determinants, and the predisposing/enabling/reinforcing factors.
- **PROCEED** (implementation and evaluation): implement the programme and evaluate at multiple levels.

**Predisposing factors**: knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, values, perceptions.
**Enabling factors**: availability, accessibility, affordability of health services; skills.
**Reinforcing factors**: social support, peer norms, media, family influence.

## 4.4 Communication methods and media

### Individual communication
- **Counselling**: one-to-one interaction; most effective for sensitive issues (HIV, family planning, substance abuse).
- **Home visits**: nurse visits the family in their own home; most effective for follow-up, care of bedridden or housebound patients, and for assessing home environment.

### Group communication
- **Group discussion**: 8–15 persons; interactive; promotes shared problem solving.
- **Lecture**: 10–50 persons; efficient for information delivery; less interactive.
- **Demonstration**: showing a skill (ORS preparation, breastfeeding technique, hand hygiene); always do a return demonstration.
- **Role play**: acting out scenarios; effective for skill development and stigma reduction (e.g. HIV disclosure, negotiating condom use).
- **Street play (Nukkad Natak)**: public drama; reaches low-literacy audiences; uses entertainment-education.

### Mass communication
- **Print media**: posters, pamphlets, flip charts, newspapers; effective for literate audiences.
- **Radio**: reaches rural areas with low literacy; local language; health programmes on community radio.
- **Television and digital**: video demonstrations; social media campaigns; WhatsApp groups for health messages; effective for broader audiences.
- **Mobile phone SMS**: reminders for immunisation, ANC visits, medication adherence.

**Selection of communication channel**: consider literacy level, language, accessibility, cost, reach, feedback potential.

## 4.5 Community health nursing approaches

### Visiting the community

**Community health survey**: a systematic assessment of a defined community's health status, resources, and needs. Components:
- Demographic data: population, age-sex composition, vital events.
- Health status data: morbidity (disease burden), mortality rates, nutritional status.
- Environmental data: water, sanitation, housing, air pollution.
- Health services data: facilities, utilisation rates, immunisation coverage.
- Socioeconomic data: income, education, occupations.
- Community resources: women's groups, NGOs, local government.

### Home visit

A **home visit** is a professional visit by a community health nurse to a family at their residence.

**Purposes**:
- Assessment of health problems in the natural environment.
- Delivery of nursing care (wound dressing, injection, health education).
- Follow-up of communicable disease contacts.
- Maternal and child health services.
- Assessment of environmental hazards.

**Principles of a home visit**:
1. Plan the visit in advance (purpose, materials needed).
2. Identify yourself and obtain consent.
3. Respect the family's privacy and cultural practices.
4. Maintain confidentiality.
5. Keep records; report any concerns to the team.

**Bag technique**: the community health nurse's bag must be maintained clean (clean outside, clean inside technique). Lay a protective paper on a surface; take out only what is needed; wash hands before and after procedures; contaminated materials go into a separate bag for disposal or sterilisation.

### Community diagnosis

**Community diagnosis** is the process of identifying health problems and their determinants in a defined community, in order to plan and prioritise interventions.

Steps:
1. Define the community and its boundaries.
2. Collect data (survey, review of records, focus groups, key informant interviews).
3. Analyse data: identify leading causes of death, morbidity, risk factors.
4. Set priorities (severity, frequency, feasibility of intervention, community concern).
5. Plan, implement and evaluate interventions.

### Community organisation

**Community organisation** is the process of helping a community to identify its needs, develop confidence to act, find resources, and take action.

**Key principles**: participatory; community-led; builds on existing strengths; respects culture; promotes self-reliance; avoids dependency.

**Community participation levels** (Arnstein's ladder): from manipulation/therapy (lowest — token) through consultation, partnership, to citizen control (highest — genuine participation).

**Self-Help Groups (SHGs)**: in India, SHGs (especially of women) are powerful community platforms for health promotion — ASHA workers often link with SHGs to address reproductive health, nutrition, sanitation, and domestic violence.

### Epidemiological approach in community nursing

Community health nurses use epidemiological thinking to identify patterns of disease:
- **Host-Agent-Environment triad** (Epidemiological triad): all three must interact for disease to occur.
- **Levels of prevention**: primordial (address upstream determinants), primary (prevent disease before it occurs), secondary (early detection and treatment), tertiary (rehabilitation).
- **Surveillance**: ongoing collection and analysis of health data to detect trends and outbreaks; notify communicable diseases to district health authorities.
