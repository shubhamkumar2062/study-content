# Unit 6: Introduction to epidemiology — approaches, processes and investigation of an epidemic

Epidemiology is the scientific foundation of public health. It tells us who gets sick, where, when, and why — essential information for designing and evaluating community health interventions.

---

## 6.1 Definitions and scope

**Epidemiology** is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to control health problems. (Last, John M.)

**Uses of epidemiology**:
- Determine the burden of disease (frequency, distribution).
- Identify risk factors for disease (determinants).
- Evaluate the effectiveness of interventions (clinical trials, programme evaluation).
- Plan and allocate health services.
- Monitor changes in disease patterns over time (surveillance).

**Epidemiological triad**: every disease requires interaction between:
- **Host**: the person who gets the disease (age, sex, immunity, genetics, behaviour).
- **Agent**: the cause (bacteria, virus, chemical, nutrient deficiency, physical force).
- **Environment**: the conditions that facilitate transmission (water quality, temperature, vector presence, social conditions).

For disease to occur, the agent must be present in sufficient quantity, the host must be susceptible, and the environment must permit exposure.

## 6.2 Measures of disease frequency

### Rates

A **rate** is the frequency of a health event in a population over a specified time period.

| Measure | Formula | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crude Birth Rate | (Births ÷ Midyear population) × 1,000 | Measure of fertility |
| Crude Death Rate (CDR) | (Deaths ÷ Midyear population) × 1,000 | Overall mortality |
| Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) | (Deaths < 1 year ÷ Live births) × 1,000 | Key indicator of child health and health system performance |
| Neonatal Mortality Rate | (Deaths < 28 days ÷ Live births) × 1,000 | Early neonatal care quality |
| Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) | (Maternal deaths ÷ Live births) × 100,000 | Obstetric care quality |
| Under-5 Mortality Rate (U5MR) | (Deaths < 5 years ÷ Live births) × 1,000 | Child survival |
| Proportional Mortality Ratio | (Deaths from cause X ÷ All deaths) × 100 | Relative importance of a cause |
| Attack Rate | (New cases during outbreak ÷ Population at risk) × 100 | Used in outbreak investigation |
| Secondary Attack Rate (SAR) | (New cases among contacts ÷ Susceptible contacts) × 100 | Transmissibility of disease in households |

### Incidence vs Prevalence

| Measure | Definition | Formula | Answers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Incidence** | New cases of a disease in a population over a specified time period | New cases ÷ Population at risk × constant | How fast is the disease occurring? |
| **Prevalence** | All existing cases (new + old) at a point in time (point prevalence) or during a period (period prevalence) | All existing cases ÷ Total population × constant | How much disease is in the population? |

**Relationship**: Prevalence ≈ Incidence × Duration of disease. A chronic disease (long duration) has high prevalence relative to incidence. A short, acute disease (brief duration) has prevalence ≈ incidence.

**Uses**: incidence is used for acute diseases and to measure risk; prevalence is used for chronic diseases and health service planning.

## 6.3 Study designs in epidemiology

| Study type | Description | Use | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Descriptive** (surveys, case reports, ecological) | Describe distribution of disease in terms of person, place, time | Generate hypotheses; plan interventions | Cannot prove causation |
| **Case-control** | Compare cases (with disease) to controls (without) for past exposures | Study rare diseases; test hypotheses; quick and inexpensive | Recall bias; cannot calculate incidence |
| **Cohort (prospective)** | Follow exposed and unexposed groups over time for disease development | Calculate incidence and relative risk; multiple outcomes | Expensive; long follow-up; loss to follow-up |
| **Cross-sectional** | Measure exposure and disease at the same point in time | Prevalence; screening; planning | Cannot determine temporal relationship |
| **Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT)** | Randomly allocate participants to intervention or control; gold standard | Causation; intervention efficacy | Ethical constraints; expensive; may not reflect real world |

**Relative risk (RR)**: risk in exposed ÷ risk in unexposed; used in cohort studies. RR > 1 means increased risk; RR < 1 means protective.

**Odds ratio (OR)**: used in case-control studies; approximate RR for rare diseases. OR = (cases exposed × controls unexposed) ÷ (cases unexposed × controls exposed).

## 6.4 Epidemiological causation

**Association vs causation**: a statistical association between two variables does not necessarily mean one causes the other.

**Bradford Hill criteria** for causality (evidence that an association is causal):
1. Strength of association (high OR or RR).
2. Consistency (same result in different populations and settings).
3. Specificity (exposure linked to one disease).
4. Temporality (exposure precedes disease — essential criterion).
5. Biological gradient (dose-response relationship).
6. Plausibility (biologically plausible mechanism).
7. Coherence (consistent with known biology and natural history).
8. Experiment (disease decreases when exposure is removed).
9. Analogy (similar agent causes similar disease).

## 6.5 Screening

**Screening** is the application of a test to identify individuals at increased risk of a condition so they can be referred for further investigation and treatment.

**Criteria for a good screening programme** (Wilson and Jungner):
- The condition must be an important health problem.
- Effective treatment must be available.
- Facilities for diagnosis and treatment must be available.
- There must be a recognisable latent or early symptomatic stage.
- A suitable screening test must exist.
- The test must be acceptable to the population.
- The natural history of the condition must be understood.
- An agreed policy on who to treat must exist.
- The benefit must outweigh the harm.
- The cost must be balanced against benefits.

**Sensitivity and specificity**:

| Measure | Formula | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sensitivity | TP ÷ (TP + FN) | Ability to detect true cases; high sensitivity → few false negatives; use for serious diseases where missing a case is dangerous |
| Specificity | TN ÷ (TN + FP) | Ability to identify true non-cases; high specificity → few false positives; use when false positives cause harm (unnecessary treatment, anxiety) |
| Positive predictive value (PPV) | TP ÷ (TP + FP) | Proportion of positive tests that are true cases; depends on prevalence |
| Negative predictive value (NPV) | TN ÷ (TN + FN) | Proportion of negative tests that are true non-cases |

**India's national screening programmes**: CPHC screening for 30 conditions including cancer (oral, cervical, breast), diabetes, hypertension, tuberculosis; cervical cancer screening with VIA (Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid).

## 6.6 Disease surveillance

**Surveillance** is the ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and timely dissemination of health data for public health action.

**Types**:
- **Passive surveillance**: health workers report cases spontaneously (using case report forms, weekly returns); depends on health workers' initiative; most common system.
- **Active surveillance**: public health staff actively seek out cases by visiting health facilities, reviewing records, or surveying communities; more sensitive but expensive.
- **Sentinel surveillance**: designate specific health facilities as sentinel sites for intensive monitoring of specific diseases (e.g. influenza-like illness, HIV sentinel surveillance in antenatal clinics).

**IDSP (Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme)**: India's national disease surveillance network. Weekly reporting using three forms:
- **S-form (suspected)**: reported by community health workers and community members.
- **P-form (probable)**: reported by clinicians.
- **L-form (laboratory confirmed)**: reported by laboratories.
Data flows from facility → district → state → national (NCDC — National Centre for Disease Control).

**Notifiable diseases**: diseases that health facilities are legally required to report to health authorities. Examples: cholera, plague, smallpox, yellow fever (international); tuberculosis, malaria, dengue, kala-azar, viral encephalitis (national/state notification in India).

## 6.7 Outbreak investigation

An **outbreak** (epidemic) is the occurrence of disease cases in excess of what would normally be expected in a defined community, geographical area, or season.

A **pandemic** is a worldwide epidemic.

### Steps in outbreak investigation

```text
1. Verify the diagnosis (confirm cases are real; not a reporting artifact).
2. Confirm the existence of an outbreak (compare with baseline; are numbers unusual?).
3. Define a case definition (clinical + epidemiological criteria for inclusion).
4. Count cases (apply case definition to find all cases).
5. Determine descriptive epidemiology: person (who), place (where), time (when — epidemic curve).
6. Develop hypotheses about the source, mode of transmission, and risk factors.
7. Test hypotheses (analytical epidemiology — case-control or cohort study within the outbreak).
8. Implement control measures (should start early, even before all steps are complete).
9. Communicate findings to health authorities.
10. Write a report.
```

### Epidemic curve

An **epidemic curve** (epi curve) is a histogram of cases over time. The shape provides clues to the source and mode of spread:

```text
   Point source (all cases exposed at one time, e.g. contaminated food at a party):
   → Sharp peak, narrow curve (bell-shaped or single peak), cases within one incubation period
   
   Propagated (person-to-person spread, e.g. measles):
   → Progressive rise with multiple waves, each one incubation period apart
   
   Continuous source (ongoing exposure to contaminated water):
   → Extended plateau without a clear peak
```

**Incubation period** from the epidemic curve: measure from the exposure point to the peak of the curve; this indicates the most likely causative agent.

**Attack rate table**: list all persons exposed; mark who became ill; calculate attack rate by food item (or other exposure) — highest attack rate in those who ate that food item identifies the vehicle.

### Control measures

| Type | Measures |
| --- | --- |
| Source | Remove or treat the source (condemn contaminated food, treat infected persons, chlorinate water supply) |
| Route of transmission | Interrupt transmission (boil water, improve sanitation, quarantine/isolation) |
| Susceptible host | Reduce susceptibility (mass vaccination, prophylactic treatment, education) |

**Quarantine**: restriction of movement of well persons who may have been exposed to a communicable disease, for the duration of the incubation period.

**Isolation**: separation of persons known to be infected with a communicable disease from those who are not infected.
