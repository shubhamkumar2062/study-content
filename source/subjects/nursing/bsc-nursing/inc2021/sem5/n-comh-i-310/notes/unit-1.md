# Unit 1: Concepts of community health, levels of prevention, health care planning and the delivery system in India

This unit builds the base for the whole subject. It joins the INC syllabus Unit I (concepts of community health and community health nursing) and Unit II (health care planning and the delivery system) because both describe the "big picture" a community nurse works inside: what health is, how disease develops, how prevention works, and how the country is organised to deliver care.

## 1.1 Public health, community health and community health nursing

- **Public health** is the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society. It works on whole populations, not one patient at a time.
- **Community health** is the health status of a defined group of people and the actions taken to protect and improve it. A community can be a village, an urban ward, a tribal block or a group sharing a common feature.
- **Community health nursing** is a field of nursing that blends nursing practice with public health. The family and community are the unit of service. The nurse promotes and protects health, prevents disease, and gives care at home, at health centres and in field areas, working with people rather than only for them.

**Scope of community health nursing:** health promotion and health education; maternal and child health; family planning; immunisation; nutrition; control of communicable and non-communicable diseases; school health; occupational health; care of the elderly, the disabled and the dying; environmental sanitation; and participation in national health programmes.

## 1.2 Evolution of public health in India

Public health in India grew in clear steps:

- Early sanitary work under colonial rule (sanitary commissions, control of plague and cholera outbreaks).
- The **Bhore Committee (1946)** — the Health Survey and Development Committee — gave the blueprint for a three-tier rural health service, the primary health centre idea and an "integrated" preventive-and-curative approach. Its recommendations still shape the system.
- After independence, a series of committees (see 1.7) refined the plan.
- The launch of the **National Health Mission** and, more recently, **Ayushman Bharat** with Health and Wellness Centres marks the shift towards comprehensive primary health care.

## 1.3 Concepts of health and disease

**Health** (WHO definition): a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. Later thinking adds spiritual and other dimensions and sees health as a resource for daily living, not a goal in itself.

**Dimensions of health:**

| Dimension | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Body works well; organs and systems are in order |
| Mental | Able to think clearly, cope with stress, feel balanced |
| Social | Good relationships, able to take part in society |
| Spiritual | Sense of purpose, values, meaning |
| Others | Emotional, vocational, and (for a population) environmental well-being |

Health is not a fixed point. People move along a **wellness–illness continuum**, from best possible health through reduced health to serious illness and death. The nurse's aim is to move the person and family towards the wellness end.

**Determinants of health** — the factors that decide how healthy a person or group is:

- **Biological** — age, sex, genetics.
- **Behavioural / lifestyle** — diet, physical activity, tobacco, alcohol, hygiene.
- **Environmental** — safe water, clean air, housing, sanitation, workplace.
- **Socio-economic** — income, education, occupation, food security.
- **Health services** — how available, reachable and acceptable the care is.
- **Other** — gender, culture, social support, and the wider "social determinants" such as poverty and discrimination.

## 1.4 Natural history of disease

This is the full course a disease runs in a person if nothing is done. It has two phases.

```text
   PRE-PATHOGENESIS                 PATHOGENESIS
   (before the person is ill)       (disease is present in the body)
   Agent + Host + Environment  -->  Entry/stimulus --> Early changes
   interact (the "triad")           --> Clinical disease --> Outcome
                                          (recovery / disability / death)
```

- **Pre-pathogenesis phase:** the person is still healthy, but the agent, host and environment are interacting. This is where health promotion and specific protection act.
- **Pathogenesis phase:** the agent has entered or acted on the host; tissue changes begin, signs and symptoms appear, and the disease moves to its outcome.

Knowing the natural history tells the nurse **where** to intervene — the earlier, the better.

## 1.5 Levels of prevention

Prevention is matched to the phase of disease (Leavell and Clark's model).

| Level | When it acts | What the nurse does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primordial** | Before risk factors appear | Stop risk factors from developing — healthy habits in childhood, policy for clean environment |
| **Primary** | Pre-pathogenesis | Health promotion (education, nutrition, lifestyle) and specific protection (immunisation, safe water, use of mosquito nets) |
| **Secondary** | Early pathogenesis | Early diagnosis and prompt treatment — screening camps, finding cases early |
| **Tertiary** | Late disease | Disability limitation and rehabilitation — prevent complications, restore function |

A single disease shows all levels. For diabetes: healthy diet and activity (primary), blood-sugar screening (secondary), foot care and physiotherapy after complications (tertiary).

## 1.6 Health problems (profile) of India

India faces a **double burden of disease**. The nurse should be able to name the main groups:

- **Communicable diseases** — tuberculosis, vector-borne diseases (malaria, dengue), diarrhoeal diseases, respiratory infections, HIV/AIDS, viral hepatitis.
- **Non-communicable diseases (NCDs)** — rising fast: diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, stroke, cancers, chronic lung disease, mental illness.
- **Maternal and child health problems** — anaemia, under-nutrition, maternal and newborn deaths.
- **Nutritional problems** — under-nutrition in children, anaemia, and now obesity.
- **Environmental problems** — unsafe water, poor sanitation, air pollution.
- **Demographic change** — a large young population and a growing elderly population.

Exact rates (IMR, MMR, under-five mortality) change every year; look up the **latest Sample Registration System (SRS)** and **National Family Health Survey (NFHS)** figures rather than memorising old numbers.

## 1.7 Health planning and its steps

**Health planning** is the orderly process of deciding health goals and the best way to reach them with the resources available.

Steps of health planning (a simple cycle):

1. Analysis of the health situation (assess needs and problems).
2. Setting priorities among problems.
3. Setting objectives and targets.
4. Assessing resources and constraints.
5. Choosing the best strategy / plan of action.
6. Putting the plan into action (implementation).
7. Monitoring and evaluation, then feeding results back into the next cycle.

**Community participation** is central: people and local stakeholders (panchayats, self-help groups, village health committees) help identify problems and run the plan, which makes it more acceptable and lasting.

### Committees and commissions on health (know the main ones)

| Committee / plan | Main contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Bhore Committee (1946) | Three-tier rural health service; primary health centre concept |
| Mudaliar Committee (1962) | Strengthen district hospitals; improve the quality of PHCs |
| Chadha Committee (1963) | Linked malaria and family planning work; basic health workers |
| Mukherjee Committee (1965) | Separate staff for family planning and health programmes |
| Kartar Singh Committee (1973) | Multipurpose health worker (male and female) scheme |
| Srivastava Committee (1975) | Community health workers; led to the "health for all" direction |
| Five Year Plans | The country's main tool for funding and expanding health services over successive planning periods |

## 1.8 Health care delivery system in India

The system is built in levels, from the village up to the nation, so that care gets steadily more specialised as the patient is referred upward.

```text
              NATIONAL LEVEL  (Ministry of Health & Family Welfare, policy)
                     |
              STATE LEVEL     (State health department, directorates)
                     |
              DISTRICT LEVEL  (District Hospital, CMO, district programmes)
                     |
       -----------------------------------------
       |                  |                     |
   CHC (block)        PHC (cluster of           SC / HWC (nearest
   First Referral     villages)                 to the people)
   Unit, specialists  Medical officer, first    ANM/MLHP, first contact
                      referral and supervision  care, home visits
                     |
              COMMUNITY: ASHA, Anganwadi worker, family
```

**Sub-Centre (SC):** the most peripheral contact point; staffed by the ANM (and a male health worker). It gives maternal-child health, family planning, immunisation and basic care.

**Primary Health Centre (PHC):** a referral unit for sub-centres; has a medical officer and staff; provides curative and preventive care and supervises field work.

**Community Health Centre (CHC):** a block-level First Referral Unit (FRU) with specialists (medicine, surgery, obstetrics, paediatrics) and beds.

**District, State and National levels** provide more specialised hospitals, programme management, policy and funding.

> Population norms (per the Indian Public Health Standards) have long been used as roughly **one SC per 5,000 people (3,000 in hilly/tribal areas), one PHC per 30,000 (20,000 hilly/tribal), one CHC per about 80,000–1,20,000.** These norms are revised from time to time, so quote the current IPHS figure in an exam and say you would confirm it.

## 1.9 Primary health care, CPHC and the health and wellness centre

- **Primary health care (PHC as a concept)** was defined at the **Alma-Ata Declaration (1978)**, with the goal of **"Health for All"**. It means essential health care, made universally accessible, at a cost the community and country can afford, with full community participation.
- **Four principles:** equitable distribution, community participation, intersectoral coordination, and appropriate technology.
- **Comprehensive Primary Health Care (CPHC)** widens this to cover the whole range of services across the lifespan — not just mother-and-child care but also NCDs, mental health, elderly care, palliative care and more.
- Under **Ayushman Bharat**, sub-centres and some PHCs are being upgraded to **Health and Wellness Centres (HWCs/Ayushman Arogya Mandir)** to deliver CPHC close to home. A **Mid-Level Health Provider (MLHP) / Community Health Provider (CHP)** leads the HWC team and provides an expanded package of services and screening.

## 1.10 National health policies and programmes (overview)

- **National Health Policy (1983, 2002, 2017):** successive statements of the country's health goals. The 1983 policy aimed for "Health for All"; the 2017 policy stresses universal access, reducing out-of-pocket spending and strengthening primary care.
- **National Health Mission (NHM):** the umbrella programme that combines the **National Rural Health Mission (NRHM, launched 2005)** and the **National Urban Health Mission (NUHM, launched 2013)**. It strengthens health systems, the ASHA scheme, maternal-child health and disease programmes.
- **Ayushman Bharat (2018):** has two parts — (1) **Health and Wellness Centres** for comprehensive primary care, and (2) **PM-JAY (Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana)**, a health-insurance scheme that gives eligible families cover of up to ₹5 lakh per family per year for hospital care.
- **Universal Health Coverage (UHC):** the goal that all people get the health services they need, of good quality, without financial hardship. SDG target 3.8.
- **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs):** 17 global goals adopted in 2015, to be reached by 2030; **SDG 3 ("Good health and well-being")** is the main health goal, with targets on maternal and child deaths, communicable and non-communicable diseases and UHC.

## Remember

- Community health nursing = nursing + public health; the **family and community** are the unit of service.
- WHO health = complete physical, mental and social well-being; dimensions also include spiritual.
- Determinants: biological, behavioural, environmental, socio-economic, health services.
- Natural history: **pre-pathogenesis** (promotion, protection) → **pathogenesis** (early diagnosis, then rehabilitation).
- Prevention levels: **primordial, primary, secondary, tertiary** — match them to the disease phase.
- Delivery tiers: **SC/HWC → PHC → CHC (FRU) → District → State → National.**
- PHC concept = **Alma-Ata 1978, "Health for All"**, four principles; **CPHC** through **HWCs** led by the MLHP/CHP.
- Policies/programmes: **NHP 1983/2002/2017; NHM (NRHM 2005, NUHM 2013); Ayushman Bharat 2018 (HWC + PM-JAY); SDGs 2015, goal 3.**
- Quote current IMR/MMR and IPHS norms from the **latest SRS/NFHS and IPHS** rather than old figures.
