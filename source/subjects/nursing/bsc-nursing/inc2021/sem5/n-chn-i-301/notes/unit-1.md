# Unit 1: Modern concepts of child-care; national policy, programmes and child rights

This unit is the first part of INC Unit I. It explains what "child health" means today, how child-care has changed over time, the national and international framework that protects children, the main welfare programmes in India, and how we measure the health of children with morbidity and mortality rates. These are the ideas every later unit builds on.

## 1.1 Who is a "child"? Some common terms

| Term | Age |
| --- | --- |
| Neonate (newborn) | Birth to 28 days |
| Infant | Birth to 1 year |
| Toddler | 1 to 3 years |
| Pre-school child | 3 to 6 years |
| School-age child | 6 to 12 years |
| Adolescent | 10 to 19 years (WHO) |
| Under-five | Birth to below 5 years |

**Paediatrics** is the branch of medicine dealing with children; **child health nursing** is the care of children in health and illness, with the family at the centre.

## 1.2 Historical development of child health

- In early times child-care was a purely family and religious matter; sickness and death in childhood were accepted as fate, and infant death was very common.
- Organised child health began with the recognition that most childhood deaths are **preventable** — from infection, poor feeding and unsafe birth.
- Milestones in the modern era: the founding of child welfare societies, maternal and child health (MCH) clinics, the start of **immunization**, the spread of **oral rehydration therapy (ORT)** for diarrhoea, and the worldwide push to control vaccine-preventable diseases.
- In India, child health moved from scattered charity to a planned, government-led service through the Five-Year Plans, the primary health centre (PHC) network, the **Reproductive and Child Health** programme, and today's National Health Mission.

## 1.3 Philosophy and modern concept of child-care

The modern approach can be summed up in a few principles:

- **The child is not a small adult.** Children differ in body, mind and needs, so care, drug amounts and communication must suit the age.
- **Family-centred care.** The family is the constant in a child's life; parents are partners in care, not visitors. Care supports the whole family.
- **Atraumatic care.** Reduce the physical and emotional distress of illness and treatment — prevent or minimise separation, pain and fear.
- **Growth and development focus.** Every contact is a chance to promote normal growth, development and good habits, not only to treat disease.
- **Prevention first.** Promotion of health and prevention of disease (immunization, nutrition, safe environment) come before cure.
- **Rights-based and holistic.** The child has rights; care covers physical, emotional, social and spiritual needs.

**Changing trends** in child-care: a shift from hospital cure to community prevention and promotion; shorter hospital stays and more day-care and home-based care; parents staying with the child; play and school continued during illness; team care; and strong national programmes reaching the village through the ASHA and Anganwadi worker.

## 1.4 Cultural and religious considerations in child-care

Beliefs and customs strongly shape how families feed, name, protect and treat their children. A nurse must learn the family's practices and work with them.

- **Feeding customs:** when breastfeeding is started, whether **colostrum** is given or discarded, "hot" and "cold" food beliefs, prelacteal feeds (honey, water, ghutti). The nurse gently corrects harmful practices (discarding colostrum, early water) while respecting helpful ones.
- **Naming and birth rituals**, head-shaving, ear-piercing, applying kajal, threads and amulets.
- **Illness beliefs:** some families first seek traditional or faith healers; the nurse keeps a non-judgemental manner and guides the family to safe, timely care.
- **Religious needs** around diet, prayer, and care of the dying child must be respected.

Culturally sensitive care builds trust and improves follow-up and immunization.

## 1.5 National policy and legislation for child health and welfare

India protects children through policy and law:

- **National Health Policy 2017** — aims to raise life expectancy, lower infant and under-five mortality, achieve universal immunization and strengthen primary care.
- **National Policy for Children (2013)** — declares every person below 18 a child and lists survival, health, nutrition, education, development and protection as priorities.
- **The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education (RTE) Act, 2009** — free, compulsory education for children aged 6–14 years.
- **Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015** — care, protection and rehabilitation of children in need and children in conflict with law.
- **Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012** — protects children from sexual abuse and exploitation.
- **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act** — prohibits employment of children below 14 years.
- **Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006**, the **PC-PNDT Act** (bans sex determination to prevent female foeticide), and the **Infant Milk Substitutes (IMS) Act** (restricts advertising of breast-milk substitutes and protects breastfeeding).

> Laws are updated from time to time; confirm the current title and provisions before using them in practice.

## 1.6 National programmes and agencies for child welfare

| Programme / agency | Main purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) | Through the **Anganwadi centre**: supplementary nutrition, pre-school education, immunization, health check-up, referral and nutrition/health education for children under 6 and mothers |
| Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child, Adolescent Health (RMNCH+A) | The umbrella strategy linking care across the life stages |
| Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) | Child health screening for the **4 D's — Defects at birth, Diseases, Deficiencies, and Development delays/disabilities** — with free treatment |
| Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (RKSK) | Adolescent health |
| Janani Suraksha Yojana (JSY) & Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (JSSK) | Promote safe institutional delivery and free care for mother and sick newborn/child |
| Home-Based Newborn Care (HBNC) & Facility-Based Newborn Care (FBNC) | ASHA home visits to newborns; sick newborn care units (SNCU), newborn stabilisation and care corners |
| Universal Immunization Programme (UIP) & Mission Indradhanush | Free vaccines to all children and pregnant women (covered in Unit 2) |
| POSHAN Abhiyaan | National nutrition mission to reduce stunting, wasting, anaemia and low birth weight |

**Agencies:** WHO and **UNICEF** (United Nations Children's Fund) support programmes worldwide; in India, the Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP), the Indian Council for Child Welfare, and state health departments deliver services; the ASHA, ANM and Anganwadi worker are the front-line team.

## 1.7 Internationally accepted rights of the child

The **United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC, 1989)** is the main international charter; India ratified it in 1992. Its rights are grouped into four:

- **Survival** — life, health, nutrition, a name and nationality.
- **Development** — education, play, leisure, and freedom of thought.
- **Protection** — from abuse, neglect, exploitation, child labour and harmful work.
- **Participation** — the right to be heard and to express views on matters affecting the child.

The guiding idea is the **best interests of the child**.

## 1.8 Changing trends in hospital, preventive, promotive and curative care

| Aspect | Older approach | Modern approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where care happens | Mostly in hospital | Community and home, with hospital for the seriously ill |
| Family | Kept away | Stays with the child; a partner in care |
| Focus | Cure of disease | Prevention and promotion first, then cure |
| Procedures | Routine, often distressing | Atraumatic; play, preparation and pain control |
| Record and follow-up | Paper, clinic-based | Growth charts, immunization cards, digital records, home follow-up by ASHA |

## 1.9 Differences between an adult and a child that affect the response to illness

A child reacts to illness differently from an adult. The nurse must keep these differences in mind.

| Area | How the child differs |
| --- | --- |
| **Physiological** | Larger body-surface-area to weight ratio → faster heat and fluid loss; higher metabolic rate and fluid turnover → dehydrates quickly; smaller, softer airway → blocks easily; immature liver and kidneys → handle drugs differently; faster heart and breathing rates; bones are growing |
| **Psychological** | Depends on parents; cannot express feelings in words; shows **separation anxiety** (especially 6 months–3 years); has **magical thinking** and fears (of the dark, of procedures, of mutilation); may see illness as punishment |
| **Social** | The whole family is affected; illness disrupts school and play; the child needs the family present |
| **Immunological** | Immature immune system; passive immunity from the mother wanes by a few months; so the young child catches infections easily and needs immunization |

These differences explain why children fall ill fast, deteriorate quickly, but also recover quickly with the right early care.

## 1.10 Child morbidity and mortality rates

Rates let us measure and compare the health of children and plan services.

| Rate | What it counts (per 1000) |
| --- | --- |
| **Neonatal mortality rate (NMR)** | Deaths of infants in the first 28 days, per 1000 live births |
| **Infant mortality rate (IMR)** | Deaths of infants below 1 year, per 1000 live births |
| **Under-five (child) mortality rate (U5MR)** | Deaths of children below 5 years, per 1000 live births |
| **Perinatal mortality rate** | Stillbirths plus deaths in the first week, per 1000 total births |
| **Maternal mortality ratio (MMR)** | Maternal deaths per 100,000 live births |
| **Morbidity** | How much illness there is in the child population (incidence and prevalence of disease) |

- **IMR is the most sensitive index of a country's health and living standards.** India's IMR, NMR and U5MR have fallen steadily over the years. Confirm the latest figures from the **Sample Registration System (SRS)** and **National Family Health Survey (NFHS)**, as they change each year.
- **Leading causes of under-five death** in India: prematurity and low birth weight, birth asphyxia, **pneumonia**, **diarrhoea**, neonatal infections (sepsis), and congenital anomalies. Most are preventable — which is why prevention, immunization, breastfeeding and early treatment are the heart of this subject.

## 1.11 Application and implication in nursing

- Treat the family as partner and the child as a growing person, not a small adult.
- Learn and respect the family's culture; correct only the harmful practices, and do so gently.
- Know the national programmes so you can refer families to ICDS, RBSK, JSY/JSSK and immunization services.
- Use rights-based, atraumatic, family-centred care in every contact.
- Understand the rates so you can read district data, find high-risk groups and explain to families why prevention matters.

## Remember

- The child is not a small adult: body, mind and needs differ.
- Modern care = family-centred + atraumatic + prevention-first + rights-based.
- Age terms: neonate <28 days, infant <1 yr, toddler 1–3, pre-school 3–6, school-age 6–12.
- CRC 1989, India ratified 1992: survival, development, protection, participation; best interests of the child.
- RBSK screens the **4 D's**: Defects at birth, Diseases, Deficiencies, Development delays/disabilities.
- ICDS works through the **Anganwadi centre**; ASHA gives Home-Based Newborn Care.
- IMR = deaths below 1 year per 1000 live births; **most sensitive index of health status**. NMR <28 days; U5MR <5 years.
- Top under-five killers: prematurity/LBW, birth asphyxia, pneumonia, diarrhoea, neonatal sepsis — nearly all preventable.
- Separation anxiety is strongest between about 6 months and 3 years.
