# Unit 7: Nursing management of common childhood diseases — respiratory and endocrine

This unit covers INC Unit VII: acute respiratory illnesses from upper respiratory infection to severe asthma, and endocrine conditions including congenital hypothyroidism, type 1 diabetes, and growth disorders. Use the comparison tables to distinguish conditions that look alike in examinations.

---

## PART A — RESPIRATORY DISEASES

## 7.1 Upper respiratory tract infections (URTI)

### Common cold (viral rhinitis)

- **Cause**: Rhinovirus (most common), coronavirus, RSV, adenovirus.
- **Features**: nasal congestion, watery discharge (becoming mucopurulent), sneezing, mild fever, sore throat; lasts 7–10 days.
- **Management**: supportive — saline nasal drops, adequate fluids, paracetamol for fever; no antibiotics; no antihistamines or decongestants in children < 6 years (risk of side effects).
- **Nurse**: teach hand hygiene to prevent spread; reassure parents that most colds resolve without antibiotics.

### Pharyngitis and tonsillitis

- **Viral** (80%): adenovirus, EBV — red throat, mild fever, cervical lymphadenopathy; no antibiotics.
- **Bacterial — Group A Streptococcus (GAS)**: sudden onset, severe throat pain, fever > 38.5°C, tonsillar exudate, petechiae on palate, anterior cervical lymphadenopathy; no cough (distinguishes from viral URTI). **Rapid antigen detection test (RADT/rapid Strep test)** is positive.
  - **Treatment**: oral penicillin V or amoxicillin × 10 days (prevents rheumatic fever); azithromycin if penicillin allergic.
  - If untreated GAS → rheumatic fever → rheumatic heart disease.
- **Indications for tonsillectomy**: ≥ 7 episodes of proven strep tonsillitis in 1 year; or ≥ 5 episodes/year for 2 years; sleep-disordered breathing with tonsillar obstruction.

### Acute otitis media (AOM)

- **Cause**: often follows URTI; bacteria — Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis.
- **Features**: ear pain (otalgia), fever, hearing loss, irritability; otoscopy shows red bulging tympanic membrane.
- **Management**: amoxicillin × 5–7 days; analgesia; most resolve spontaneously; **myringotomy** (surgical incision) + grommets for recurrent AOM or otitis media with effusion (OME, "glue ear").
- **Nurse**: reassure that most cases resolve; advise completing the antibiotic course; re-examine if no improvement in 48 hours.

## 7.2 Laryngotracheobronchitis (Croup)

**Croup** is inflammation of the larynx, trachea, and bronchi causing subglottic narrowing.

- **Cause**: Parainfluenza virus type 1 (most common); RSV, influenza, adenovirus.
- **Age**: 6 months – 3 years (peak); usually in autumn/winter.
- **Features**: 1–3 days of URTI, then:
  - **Inspiratory stridor** (harsh, high-pitched noise on breathing in due to subglottic narrowing)
  - **Barking (seal-like) cough** — the classic sign
  - Hoarse voice
  - Respiratory distress (recession, tachypnoea) in moderate/severe cases
  - Low-grade fever
  - CXR: "steeple sign" (subglottic narrowing)
- **Westley Croup Score**: scores 0–17 for stridor, retractions, air entry, cyanosis, consciousness; 0–2 mild, 3–7 moderate, ≥ 8 severe.

**Management**:
- **Mild** (barking cough, no stridor at rest): humidified air (cool mist), reassurance, oral dexamethasone 0.15–0.6 mg/kg (single dose).
- **Moderate**: dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg orally/IM + nebulised adrenaline 0.5 mL/kg (max 5 mL) of 1:1000 solution; monitor SpO₂.
- **Severe** (stridor at rest, significant retractions, cyanosis): nebulised adrenaline + IV/IM dexamethasone; consider intubation; admit to ICU.

**Nursing care**: nurse upright (sitting), calm and reassuring (crying worsens obstruction), monitor respiratory rate and SpO₂, keep the airway clear, do not force the child to lie flat, have emergency airway equipment ready, avoid unnecessary disturbance.

### Croup vs Epiglottitis

| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Parainfluenza virus (viral) | Haemophilus influenzae type B (bacterial) — now rare with Hib vaccine |
| Age | 6 months – 3 years | 2–6 years |
| Onset | Gradual (1–3 days URTI then cough) | Rapid (hours) |
| Temperature | Low grade | High (> 38.5°C) |
| Cough | Barking (classic) | No/minimal |
| Voice | Hoarse | Muffled ("hot potato") |
| Stridor | Inspiratory | Inspiratory |
| Drooling | Absent | Present (cannot swallow secretions) |
| Posture | Any | Tripod (leaning forward on hands) |
| Examine throat? | Yes | **NO** — may precipitate sudden obstruction |
| CXR | Steeple sign | "Thumbprint sign" (enlarged epiglottis) |
| Treatment | Dexamethasone, nebulised adrenaline | Emergency airway (intubation in OR), IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone) |

## 7.3 Acute bronchiolitis

- **Cause**: RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus) in 80%; also parainfluenza, human metapneumovirus.
- **Age**: < 2 years (peak 2–6 months); epidemic in winter.
- **Pathophysiology**: RSV infects bronchiolar epithelium → inflammation, oedema, mucus plugging → air trapping and atelectasis.
- **Features**: 2–3 days of URTI then: **wheeze, crepitations, tachypnoea, subcostal recession, feeding difficulty**; SpO₂ may fall. CXR: hyperinflation, patchy atelectasis.
- **Severity assessment**: mild (feeding well, SpO₂ > 95%); moderate (tachypnoea, some recession, SpO₂ 92–95%); severe (marked recession, apnoeas, SpO₂ < 92%, unable to feed).
- **Management**: supportive — nasal suctioning before feeds, small frequent feeds (NG if unable to feed), supplemental O₂ for SpO₂ < 92%; **bronchodilators and steroids are NOT recommended** in mild/moderate bronchiolitis (Cochrane evidence shows no benefit). Hypertonic saline nebulisation may modestly reduce admission duration.
- **Hospital admission**: SpO₂ < 92%, unable to feed, apnoeas, age < 3 months or premature, underlying lung/heart disease.

## 7.4 Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)

- **Cause**: Streptococcus pneumoniae most common (bacterial); viral CAP also common in young children.
- **Features**: fever, cough, tachypnoea (most sensitive sign), chest in-drawing, reduced breath sounds, dullness on percussion, crackles.
- **Diagnosis**: clinical (WHO IMNCI threshold); CXR shows consolidation; blood culture rarely positive in outpatients.
- **Management**:
  - **Outpatient (mild)**: oral amoxicillin 40–90 mg/kg/day × 5–7 days.
  - **Inpatient (moderate)**: IV ampicillin or crystalline penicillin; switch to oral when tolerating.
  - **Severe (ICU/ventilator)**: IV cefotaxime ± vancomycin.
- **Supportive care**: O₂ for SpO₂ < 90% (via nasal prongs); adequate fluids; encourage feeds; physiotherapy (older children).
- **Nurse**: monitor respiratory rate every 2–4 hours, SpO₂, temperature, feeding; ensure completion of antibiotics; teach parents warning signs.

## 7.5 Asthma in children

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible airways obstruction. It is the most common chronic disease of childhood.

### Classification by control (GINA)

| Level of control | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Controlled | No daytime symptoms, no night waking, no activity limitation, no reliever use, normal peak flow |
| Partially controlled | 1–2 of the above features |
| Uncontrolled | ≥ 3 of the above features |

### Triggers

Viral infections (most common in children), house dust mites, pet dander, cockroach, mould, tobacco smoke, cold air, exercise, strong emotions.

### Stepwise management (GINA paediatric steps)

| Step | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| 1 (mild intermittent) | SABA (salbutamol inhaler) as needed only |
| 2 (mild persistent) | Low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) daily + SABA as reliever |
| 3 (moderate) | Medium-dose ICS or low-dose ICS + LTRA (montelukast) |
| 4 (severe) | Medium-high ICS + LABA (salmeterol, ≥ 5 years) |
| 5 | Add-on therapy (tiotropium, biologics); refer specialist |

**Preferred reliever**: SABA (salbutamol metered dose inhaler with spacer — the most effective and safest reliever at all ages).

### Acute severe attack

```text
Step 1: Salbutamol 2.5–5 mg nebulised every 20 minutes × 3 doses (1st hour)
Step 2: Ipratropium bromide 0.25 mg added to nebuliser (reduces admission risk)
Step 3: Systemic corticosteroid — prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg orally (max 40 mg) × 3–5 days
         OR IV hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg 4-hourly if unable to swallow
Step 4 (life-threatening): IV magnesium sulfate 25–75 mg/kg (max 2 g) over 20 minutes
Step 5: Intubation and ventilation if failing
```

**Nurse's role**:
- Upright position, supplemental O₂ for SpO₂ < 92%.
- Administer nebulisers/MDI with spacer as prescribed; monitor response (RR, SpO₂, work of breathing).
- Educate: inhaler technique (MDI + spacer; correct pMDI-spacer assembly and cleaning); trigger avoidance; written asthma action plan; peak flow diary for children ≥ 5 years.
- Advise on allergen reduction: mattress and pillow covers, frequent bedding washing, avoid tobacco smoke.

### Cystic fibrosis (CF)

- **Cause**: CFTR gene mutation → thick sticky mucus in lungs, pancreas, sinuses, bowel, sweat glands.
- **Features**: chronic productive cough, recurrent Pseudomonas colonisation, bronchiectasis; pancreatic insufficiency → malabsorption, fatty stools, poor growth; raised sweat chloride (> 60 mmol/L — gold standard test).
- **Management**: daily chest physiotherapy (airway clearance techniques — Flutter, PEP); DNase (dornase alfa) thins mucus; azithromycin (anti-inflammatory); CFTR modulators (ivacaftor, elexacaftor-tezacaftor-ivacaftor) for specific mutations; enzyme replacement (Creon) with every meal; high-calorie diet; multidisciplinary team (paediatrician, physiotherapist, dietitian, pharmacist, CF nurse, psychologist).

### Foreign body aspiration

- **Peak age**: 1–3 years; most common in right main bronchus (larger, more vertical).
- **Objects**: nuts (peanut most common), seeds, small toys, coins, button batteries.
- **Features**: sudden coughing fit, unilateral wheeze or reduced air entry, hyperlucency on the affected side CXR (ball-valve effect causes air trapping).
- **First aid (child conscious, coughing effectively)**: encourage to cough; do not perform Heimlich/back blows if coughing is effective.
- **If choking (unable to cry/cough, cyanosis)**:
  - < 1 year: 5 back blows (between shoulder blades) alternating with 5 chest thrusts.
  - ≥ 1 year: 5 back blows alternating with 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich).
- **Definitive treatment**: rigid bronchoscopy under general anaesthesia.

## PART B — ENDOCRINE DISEASES

## 7.6 Congenital hypothyroidism (CH)

- **Cause**: thyroid dysgenesis (most common, 85%), thyroid dyshormonogenesis, maternal iodine deficiency.
- **Screening**: neonatal TSH (blood spot) on day 3–5 of life under National Programme (RBSK). TSH > 10 mIU/L → confirmatory serum TSH and free T₄.
- **Features if undetected**: prolonged neonatal jaundice, hypotonia, large tongue (macroglossia), umbilical hernia, constipation, dry skin, wide posterior fontanelle, hoarse cry, poor feeding, delayed development → cretinism (irreversible intellectual disability if untreated beyond 1–2 months).
- **Treatment**: thyroxine (levothyroxine) **10–15 mcg/kg/day** starting as early as possible (within first 2 weeks). Lifelong treatment — dose adjusted for growth.
- **Nurse's role**: stress strict compliance; weigh and measure at every visit; monitor for signs of overtreatment (irritability, rapid heartbeat, poor weight gain); advise not to give thyroxine with calcium, iron, or soy — these reduce absorption.

## 7.7 Type 1 diabetes mellitus in children (T1DM)

- **Pathophysiology**: autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells → absolute insulin deficiency; child must use insulin for life.
- **Age**: typically childhood/adolescence; can occur at any age.
- **Onset**: often acute — polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss, fatigue; may present in **DKA** (diabetic ketoacidosis) with vomiting, Kussmaul breathing, acetone breath.

### Insulin regimens in children

- **Multiple daily injections (MDI)**: long-acting basal insulin once/twice daily + rapid-acting bolus at mealtimes — the standard intensive regimen.
- **Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII/insulin pump)**: used in young children and those with hypoglycaemia unawareness.
- **Honeymoon period**: brief partial remission after diagnosis; insulin requirements transiently fall.

### HbA1c targets (paediatric)

- Target ≤ 7.0% (53 mmol/mol) — balance between tight control and hypoglycaemia risk.
- Measured every 3 months.

### Hypoglycaemia management in a child

- **Symptoms**: sweating, shaking, pallor, irritability, hunger, confusion; in young children — behavioural change.
- **Treatment (conscious)**: 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate (3 glucose tablets, 150 mL orange juice); recheck blood glucose in 15 minutes; if no improvement, repeat; then give a snack.
- **Treatment (unconscious)**: do NOT give anything by mouth; if IV access — 2 mL/kg 10% dextrose; if no IV — glucagon 0.5–1 mg SC/IM.
- **School management**: teacher must know the signs and have glucagon kit; child must carry fast sugar; no skipped meals or extra exercise without extra carbohydrate.

**Nurse's role**:
- Teach the child and family insulin injection technique (SC — abdomen, thigh, arm, buttock; **rotate sites** to prevent lipohypertrophy).
- Insulin storage: opened vial at room temperature up to 28 days; unopened in fridge 2–8°C.
- Blood glucose monitoring: target pre-meal 4–7 mmol/L, 2-h post-meal < 10 mmol/L.
- Sick-day rules: continue insulin even when not eating; check glucose 2-hourly; check ketones; give extra fluids; seek medical help if glucose persistently > 14 mmol/L or ketones moderate/large.
- Psychological support for child (fear of injections, peer anxiety, rebellion in adolescence) and family (caregiver burnout, overprotection).

## 7.8 Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) and short stature

- **Cause**: idiopathic (most common), pituitary tumour, cranial irradiation, structural brain malformation.
- **Features**: short stature (height < −2 SD or height velocity < −1 SD per year), delayed bone age (wrist X-ray), pudgy appearance, small genitalia in boys.
- **Diagnosis**: GH stimulation tests (insulin-induced hypoglycaemia, arginine, clonidine) under specialist supervision — single random GH is unreliable.
- **Treatment**: recombinant human GH injections SC daily until growth is complete; response best when started early.
- **Nurse**: teach injection technique; monitor height every 3 months (plot on growth chart); check IGF-1 (surrogate for GH effect); advise on compliance.

## 7.9 Precocious puberty

- **Definition**: onset of secondary sexual characteristics before age 8 years in girls or 9 years in boys.
- **Central (true)**: activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis; pulsatile LH/FSH secretion; causes: idiopathic (most common in girls), brain tumour, CNS radiation, McCune-Albright syndrome.
- **Peripheral**: sex hormone excess independent of GnRH; causes: adrenal tumour, ovarian cyst, McCune-Albright.
- **Management (central)**: GnRH agonist (leuprolide, triptorelin) — given as monthly or 3-monthly depot injection; suppresses LH/FSH; slows bone age advancement and preserves final adult height.
- **Nurse**: psychosocial support (child may be teased; explain that puberty will pause); privacy during examination; parent education about the treatment; monitor for injection site reactions.

## Comparison table: common childhood respiratory illnesses

| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis | Bronchiolitis | Asthma |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Age | 6 m – 3 y | 2–6 y | < 2 y | Any; often > 2 y |
| Cause | Parainfluenza | H. influenzae | RSV | Allergic/viral |
| Season | Autumn/winter | Any | Winter | Any |
| Main sound | Barking cough + inspiratory stridor | Muffled voice + drooling | Wheeze + crackles | Wheeze |
| CXR | Steeple sign | Thumbprint sign | Hyperinflation | May be normal |

## Remember

- Croup: barking cough, inspiratory stridor; treat with dexamethasone + nebulised adrenaline; never examine throat in suspected epiglottitis.
- Epiglottitis: drooling, tripod posture, toxic, emergency airway.
- Bronchiolitis: RSV, < 2 years, wheeze + crackles; supportive care only; no bronchodilators in mild/moderate.
- Fast breathing thresholds for pneumonia: < 2 m = 60; 2–12 m = 50; 1–5 y = 40 /min.
- Asthma: SABA as reliever; low-dose ICS as preventer (Step 2); salbutamol every 20 min + ipratropium + steroids for acute attack.
- Choking infant (< 1 year): 5 back blows + 5 chest thrusts.
- CH: neonatal TSH screening; thyroxine 10–15 mcg/kg/day started within 2 weeks; lifelong treatment.
- T1DM: insulin-dependent; teach MDI technique, site rotation, hypoglycaemia treatment (15 g glucose), sick-day rules.
- Precocious puberty: GnRH agonist to suppress early puberty and preserve final height.
