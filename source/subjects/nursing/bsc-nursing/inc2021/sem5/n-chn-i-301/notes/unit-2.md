# Unit 2: Preventive paediatrics and immunization

This unit covers INC Unit II: levels of prevention in child health, national programmes for child welfare, the National Immunisation Schedule, cold chain management, and adverse events following immunisation. Immunisation is one of the highest-probability examination topics in this subject.

---

## 2.1 Levels of prevention in child health

Prevention is classified into three levels. The goal in paediatrics is to shift every child from the later levels back to primary prevention.

| Level | Aim | Examples in child health |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary prevention** | Stop disease before it starts | Immunisation, exclusive breastfeeding, safe water and sanitation, vitamin A supplementation, iodised salt |
| **Secondary prevention** | Early detection and prompt treatment | Newborn screening (TSH for congenital hypothyroidism, G6PD), growth monitoring with WHO charts, IMNCI assessment, vision screening |
| **Tertiary prevention** | Limit disability and rehabilitate | Early intervention for developmental delay, special education, physiotherapy for cerebral palsy, corrective surgery for cleft palate |

**Primary prevention is always cheaper and more effective than treatment.** Nurses spend most of their child health work at this level.

## 2.2 Infant and young child feeding (IYCF)

Malnutrition in India is closely linked to poor feeding practices. WHO and UNICEF IYCF recommendations:

- **Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months** — only breast milk; no water, no other milk, no food.
- **Initiate breastfeeding within one hour of birth** — the first milk (colostrum) is rich in antibodies (secretory IgA) and must not be discarded.
- **Complementary feeding from 6 months** — introduce semi-solid foods while continuing breastfeeding; start with soft mashes, gradually increase texture.
- **Continue breastfeeding up to 2 years or beyond.**

No infant formula substitutes for the immunological factors in breast milk: secretory IgA, lactoferrin, lysozyme, macrophages, and T-lymphocytes. Formula-fed infants have higher rates of diarrhoea, respiratory infection, and otitis media.

## 2.3 Growth monitoring

**Growth is the best single indicator of child health and nutrition.**

- Measure weight, length/height, and head circumference at every visit and plot on a WHO Child Growth Standards chart.
- WHO charts use **Z-scores** (standard deviations from the median):

| Z-score | Nutritional status |
| --- | --- |
| Above +2 | Overweight (weight-for-height) |
| −1 to +2 | Normal |
| Below −2 | Underweight (or wasted/stunted) |
| Below −3 | Severely underweight |

- **Gomez classification** (weight-for-age): Grade I 76–90% of median (mild); Grade II 61–75% (moderate); Grade III ≤60% (severe).
- **MUAC (mid-upper arm circumference)**: quick screening tool for SAM — MUAC < 11.5 cm = severe acute malnutrition; 11.5–12.5 cm = moderate acute malnutrition (MAM).

**Nurse's role**: weigh the child accurately (remove clothes, calibrate scales), plot on the chart, recognise faltering growth, counsel the mother on feeding, refer SAM/MAM as needed.

## 2.4 IMNCI overview

**Integrated Management of Neonatal and Childhood Illness (IMNCI)** is a WHO/UNICEF strategy integrated into India's National Health Mission to reduce child mortality.

- **Target age**: 0–59 months (young infant chart 0–2 months; child chart 2–59 months).
- **Integrated approach**: a single child health worker (ASHA, ANM, or nurse) assesses all major causes of child death (ARI, diarrhoea, malaria, measles, malnutrition, anaemia) in one visit rather than by organ system.
- **Simplified guidelines**: only the signs needed to identify children requiring referral, specific treatment, or home care — not a full clinical examination.

**The IMNCI five steps for the sick child (2–59 months):**

```text
ASK → CHECK → CLASSIFY → IDENTIFY TREATMENT → TREAT / REFER / COUNSEL / FOLLOW-UP
```

**Danger signs** — refer any child with even one:
- Cannot drink or breastfeed
- Vomits everything
- Convulsions (now or in the last illness)
- Lethargic or unconscious
- Severe malnutrition with oedema

**Colour coding of classification**:
- **Pink**: urgent referral to hospital
- **Yellow**: specific treatment at clinic (antibiotics, ORS, zinc)
- **Green**: home treatment and counselling

**Role of community workers**: ASHA counsels on IYCF, identifies sick children and refers, records births and deaths. AWW (anganwadi worker) provides nutrition supplementation under ICDS, monitors growth, identifies SAM.

## 2.5 National child health programmes

### National Health Mission (NHM)

The NHM merges the NRHM (rural) and NUHM (urban) missions. Its Reproductive and Child Health (RCH) component covers:
- Antenatal care, skilled birth attendance, postnatal visits.
- Child immunisation (Universal Immunisation Programme — UIP).
- IMNCI.
- FBNC (Facility Based Newborn Care) — at health centres for sick neonates.
- HBNC (Home Based Newborn Care) — ASHAs make 7 home visits in the first 42 days.

### RBSK — Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram

Screens children 0–18 years for the **4 Ds**:
1. **Defects at birth** (congenital hypothyroidism, CHD, cleft lip/palate, neural tube defects)
2. **Diseases** (anaemia, dental caries, skin conditions, ear infections)
3. **Deficiencies** (vitamin D, iron, iodine)
4. **Developmental delays and disabilities** (vision, hearing, locomotor, learning, autism, cerebral palsy)

Mobile Health Teams (medical officer + 2 RBSK health workers) screen children at anganwadis and government schools.

### ICDS — Integrated Child Development Services

Targets children under 6 and pregnant/lactating women. Provides **six services**:
1. Supplementary nutrition (take-home rations)
2. Immunisation (through the AWW/ANM link)
3. Health check-up
4. Referral services
5. Pre-school education (3–6 years)
6. Nutrition and health education to mothers

### Mid-Day Meal Scheme

Hot cooked meal to children in government schools; reduces dropout, improves nutrition status, especially protein-energy malnutrition and iron-deficiency anaemia.

### POSHAN Abhiyaan (National Nutrition Mission)

India's flagship multi-ministry programme to reduce stunting, undernutrition, anaemia and low birth weight. Uses a technology-based real-time monitoring system; targets are measured against baseline.

## 2.7 Cold chain

Vaccines are biological products destroyed by heat or freezing. The cold chain is the system of refrigerated storage and transport from manufacturer to child.

**Temperature requirement**: most vaccines must be kept at **+2°C to +8°C**.

**Key equipment**:
- **Walk-in cold rooms** at national/state stores (−15° to −25°C for OPV)
- **Ice-lined refrigerators (ILR)** at district/PHC level — liner of ice keeps vaccines cold even during power failure; most reliable piece of equipment
- **Deep freezers** for OPV (−15° to −25°C)
- **Cold boxes** for transport between stores
- **Vaccine carriers** for outreach sessions

**Freeze-sensitive vaccines** — must NEVER freeze: DPT, Penta, Hep B, TT, PCV, RVV. Freezing destroys them and makes them look normal. Use the **shake test** to detect frozen-and-thawed DPT/Penta/HepB (frozen vials clump and do not re-disperse). Use **Freeze Watch indicators** in cold boxes.

**Vaccine Vial Monitors (VVM)**: a heat-sensitive label on each vial. As cumulative heat exposure rises, the inner square darkens. Do not use the vaccine if the inner square is as dark as or darker than the outer circle.

**Nurse's role in cold chain**: check temperatures twice daily, record in a register, never put food or drinks in a vaccine refrigerator, place vaccines according to their cold-chain requirement (OPV at the top/freezer, freeze-sensitive at the bottom/away from freezing coils), do not put vaccines in the door (temperature fluctuates most there).

## 2.8 Adverse events following immunisation (AEFI)

An **AEFI** is any untoward medical event that follows immunisation. It may or may not be caused by the vaccine.

### Classification

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vaccine-induced** | Caused by the vaccine's inherent properties | BCG abscess, OPV-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (rare) |
| **Programme error** | Caused by errors in preparation/handling/administration | Abscess from non-sterile technique, wrong dose, wrong vaccine |
| **Injection-related** | Caused by the injection act, not the vaccine | Fainting, local pain, nerve injury |
| **Coincidental** | Would have happened anyway; not caused by vaccine | Fever from another infection on the same day |
| **Unknown** | Cannot be classified | — |

### Common AEFIs

- **Local**: redness, swelling, tenderness at the site — most common; manage with cold compress.
- **BCG**: small ulcer at the injection site (normal reaction); BCG lymphadenitis (enlarged, sometimes suppurating axillary lymph node) — refer to district for management.
- **DPT/Penta**: fever, local swelling, irritability — reassure parents; paracetamol for fever.
- **OPV**: very rarely vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP) — 1 in ~2.5 million doses.
- **MMR/Measles**: fever 7–10 days after vaccination, mild rash — normal.
- **Febrile seizures**: can follow fever from any vaccine, most commonly DTwP — reassure parents that most are self-limiting.
- **Anaphylaxis** (very rare): within minutes; bronchospasm, urticaria, collapse — keep adrenaline 1:1000 available; give 0.01 mL/kg IM immediately, call for help.

### Nurse's role

1. Record all AEFIs in the immunisation register and on the AEFI form.
2. Manage local reactions (cold compress, paracetamol for fever).
3. Manage anaphylaxis immediately.
4. Report serious AEFIs (hospitalisation, death, cluster) to the district immunisation officer within 24 hours.
5. Counsel parents that mild reactions (pain, fever) are normal and expected.

### Contraindications

- **True contraindications to live vaccines** (OPV, measles/MR, BCG, JE): severe immunodeficiency (chemotherapy, congenital immune defects), **except** HIV-positive children who are asymptomatic or on ART with adequate immune status (BCG and measles may be given).
- **High fever** on the day of vaccination — defer and revisit; mild illness (cough, cold) is NOT a contraindication.
- **Previous anaphylaxis** to a specific vaccine component — do not repeat that vaccine.
- Malnutrition is NOT a contraindication; malnourished children need vaccines more.

### Catch-up immunisation

If a child missed one or more doses, do not restart from zero. Continue from where it was left off, leaving the minimum interval between doses (usually 4 weeks for primary series). The nurse's role: identify missed children from registers, do outreach sessions to reach them.

## Remember

- Primary prevention (immunisation, breastfeeding) is cheaper and more effective than treatment.
- IYCF: exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months; complementary feeding from 6 months; breastfeeding until 2 years.
- MUAC < 11.5 cm = SAM; 11.5–12.5 cm = MAM.
- National Immunisation Schedule: BCG, OPV, Penta, PCV, RVV at 6–10–14 weeks; MR at 9 months; boosters at 16–24 months; HPV for girls Class 6–9.
- Cold chain: 2–8°C; ILR is the most reliable piece; freeze-sensitive vaccines (DPT, Penta, HepB, TT) must never freeze.
- VVM: inner square darker than outer circle = discard vaccine.
- AEFI: record, manage, report within 24 hours if serious; keep adrenaline ready for anaphylaxis.
- Malnutrition is NOT a contraindication to immunisation.
- RBSK screens for 4 Ds; ICDS provides 6 services; POSHAN Abhiyaan targets stunting, undernutrition, anaemia.
