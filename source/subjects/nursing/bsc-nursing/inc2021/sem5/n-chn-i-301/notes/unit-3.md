# Unit 3: The hospitalised child and child health nursing procedures

This unit covers INC Unit III: the effects of hospitalisation on children and families, family-centred care, therapeutic play, and the key nursing procedures performed in paediatric settings. Understanding the developmental impact of hospitalisation helps the nurse plan care that reduces distress.

---

## 3.1 Effects of hospitalisation on children

Children experience hospitalisation differently depending on their developmental stage.

| Age group | Main fears | Behavioural responses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Infant (0–12 months)** | Separation from parent, pain, strange environment | Crying, feeding difficulties, regression in development |
| **Toddler (1–3 years)** | Most severely affected; separation anxiety, loss of control | Protest (crying loudly, reaching for parent), Despair (withdrawn, quiet crying), Detachment (appears adjusted but has emotionally withdrawn) |
| **Preschool (3–6 years)** | Fear of body mutilation, guilt (thinks illness is punishment), magical thinking | Regression (bedwetting, thumb-sucking), aggression, withdrawal |
| **School-age (6–12 years)** | Loss of control, boredom, fear of death, peer separation | Cooperation with procedures but worries about missing school and friends; asks detailed questions |
| **Adolescent (12–18 years)** | Threat to identity and body image, separation from peers, loss of independence | Anger, non-compliance, withdrawal; concerned about appearance |

**Robertson's three phases** of separation response in toddlers:
1. **Protest** — loud crying, calling for the parent, cannot be comforted.
2. **Despair** — quiet, withdrawn, still grieving but appearing calmer; nurses may mistake this for acceptance.
3. **Detachment** — appears settled, interacts, but has emotionally distanced from the parent; most harmful phase.

### Effects on the family

- **Parents**: anxiety, guilt, fear, disrupted work and finances; they may feel helpless and blame themselves.
- **Siblings**: jealousy, rivalry, feeling ignored; may show behavioural or school problems.
- **Financial stress**: parents in India often face substantial out-of-pocket expenses even in public hospitals; job loss from hospital stays is common.

## 3.2 Minimising the stress of hospitalisation

### Family-centred care

The philosophy that the family is the constant in the child's life and that the healthcare system must support and partner with families. Key elements:
- Parents are welcome 24 hours a day (**rooming-in**); a parent's presence is the single most effective way to reduce separation anxiety.
- Parents participate in direct care (feeding, bathing, comforting) — teach them rather than taking over.
- Respect cultural and family values in care decisions.
- Treat parents as partners, give them honest, clear information.

### Preparation for procedures

- Use **age-appropriate language** — say "it will feel like a pinch" not "this won't hurt."
- Give **honest, brief explanations** just before the procedure, not hours ahead (which creates anxiety in young children).
- Allow the child to handle equipment if safe.
- Use **therapeutic play** — let the child role-play procedures on a doll.
- Allow the **parent to be present** during most procedures; evidence shows it reduces distress for both child and parent.

### Maintaining routine

Keeping mealtimes, nap times, and familiar objects (a favourite toy or blanket — a "transitional object") reduces disorientation.

### Comfort positions

- **Side-lying with knees drawn up**: for lumbar puncture.
- **Upright on parent's lap with arms wrapped**: for IV insertion in young children (less frightening than being held down on the bed).
- **Semi-prone**: for children with breathing difficulty.
- Never physically restrain a child unless essential and only for the minimum time needed.

## 3.3 Play in hospital

**Play is the work of childhood** — it promotes development even during illness.

| Type | Description | Hospital use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Developmental play** | Age-appropriate activities for normal growth | Art, puzzles, books, games brought from home |
| **Therapeutic play** | Planned play to help child cope with hospitalisation | Medical play (syringes without needles, bandaging a doll), puppet play to express fears |
| **Distraction play** | Used during procedures | Blowing bubbles, music, holding a favourite toy |

A **play specialist/child life specialist** may be available in larger hospitals; the nurse initiates basic therapeutic play when this specialist is not available.

## 3.4 Medication administration in children

### Principles

- Always use **weight-based dosing** (mg/kg); calculate carefully.
- Double-check calculations for all IV medications.
- Explain the procedure to the child and parent.

### Routes

**Oral**:
- Use an oral syringe (not a household spoon) for accuracy.
- Mix crushed tablets or liquid in a small amount of food or juice — but not in the full feeding (the child may not finish it).
- Avoid masking taste with essential foods; children may then refuse that food.

**Intramuscular (IM)**:
- **Vastus lateralis** (outer middle third of the thigh) is the preferred site in infants and toddlers (largest muscle mass; no major nerves or vessels).
- **Deltoid** may be used in older children (≥3 years) for small volumes.
- Use the smallest-gauge needle adequate; use EMLA cream or cold spray beforehand when available.

**Intravenous (IV)**:
- Calculate the volume and rate carefully: use a volume-control burette (Buretrol/Metriset) to prevent accidental fluid overload.
- Rate: prescribed as mL/hour; for microdrip sets (60 drops/mL), drip rate = mL/h.
- **Smart pumps** (syringe drivers and infusion pumps) are preferred for accuracy and safety.
- Monitor hourly for extravasation (swelling, blanching, pain around the site) — especially important in neonates and infants.
- In infants when no peripheral vein is accessible: **scalp veins** (superficial temporal, frontal, occipital) may be used; explain to parents that shaving is temporary.

## 3.5 Intravenous access

| Access type | Use | Nurse's role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Peripheral IV cannula** | Short-term IV therapy | Insert at peripheral site (back of hand, antecubital fossa, foot, scalp vein in infants); check every hour for infiltration, phlebitis |
| **Umbilical venous catheter (UVC)** | Neonates in first few days | Inserted by doctor; nurse monitors position, secures, prevents disconnection |
| **PICC (peripherally inserted central catheter)** | Long-term antibiotics, TPN | Inserted under ultrasound guidance; nurse maintains dressing, monitors for infection |
| **Central venous line** | Critical illness, chemotherapy | Doctor inserts; nurse monitors all connections, keeps sterile dressing |

## 3.6 Fluid and electrolyte management

### Holliday-Segar formula for daily maintenance fluids

| Weight | Daily requirement |
| --- | --- |
| First 10 kg | 100 mL/kg/day |
| Next 10 kg (10–20 kg) | 50 mL/kg/day |
| Each kg above 20 kg | 20 mL/kg/day |

**Example**: a 25 kg child needs (10 × 100) + (10 × 50) + (5 × 20) = 1000 + 500 + 100 = **1600 mL/day** = 67 mL/hour.

### Dehydration assessment

| Feature | Mild (<5%) | Moderate (5–10%) | Severe (>10%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alertness | Normal | Irritable | Lethargic/unconscious |
| Eyes | Normal | Sunken | Very sunken |
| Skin turgor | Normal | Goes back slowly (>2 s) | Goes back very slowly (>3 s) |
| Mouth/tongue | Moist | Dry | Very dry, parched |
| Tears | Present | Decreased | Absent |
| Capillary refill | <2 s | 2–3 s | >3 s |
| Urine output | Normal | Reduced | Very reduced or absent |

**ORS (Oral Rehydration Solution)**: WHO/UNICEF low-osmolarity ORS (Na 75 mEq/L, glucose 75 mmol/L). Zinc supplement 20 mg/day for 10 days with every episode of diarrhoea reduces duration and severity.

**IMNCI fluid plans**:
- **Plan A** (no dehydration): give ORS at home, 50–100 mL after each loose stool; continue breastfeeding.
- **Plan B** (some dehydration): give 75 mL/kg of ORS over 4 hours in the facility; reassess after 4 hours.
- **Plan C** (severe dehydration): IV Ringer's lactate 100 mL/kg (30 mL/kg fast in 30 min for infants, then 70 mL/kg over 2.5 h; for older children 30 mL/kg fast then 70 mL/kg over 2.5 h).

## 3.7 Nasogastric tube feeding

**Indications**: unable to feed orally, prematurity, respiratory distress, post-operative feeding.

**Tube size**: 5 Fr neonates; 6–8 Fr infants; 10–12 Fr older children.

**Placement check**:
- Measure from tip of nose to ear to xiphoid process to determine insertion length; mark and insert.
- Confirm placement: aspirate gastric contents (pH should be <5.5); X-ray is gold standard if in doubt; never rely only on auscultation.
- Secure with hypoallergenic tape; document the external length.

**Feeding**: bolus (over 20–30 min by gravity) is more physiological; continuous pump feeding for premature infants or those with high aspiration risk.

**Checking residuals**: aspirate before each bolus; if residual > half the last feed volume, hold feeding and re-assess.

**Parental education for home NG**: teach placement check, gravity feeding, flushing with clean water, signs of displacement (coughing, choking, change in colour).

## 3.8 Paediatric blood transfusion

- Volume: **10–15 mL/kg** of packed red cells raises Hb by approximately 2–3 g/dL.
- Pre-transfusion: check patient identity, blood group and cross-match certificate, blood bag label for expiry and appearance; take baseline vital signs.
- Infuse slowly for first 15 minutes; monitor closely; complete within 4 hours.
- Monitor for transfusion reactions: fever, chills, urticaria, back pain, haemoglobinuria — stop immediately, keep IV line open with saline, inform doctor.

## 3.9 Restraints

Restraints are used only when essential to protect the child during a procedure, never as routine.

- **Mummy (papoose) restraint**: wrap the child snugly in a sheet to restrain arms and legs for procedures on the head/neck.
- **Elbow restraint**: keeps the elbow straight (prevents the child from bending and touching a scalp IV or face wound).
- **Documentation**: type, time applied, time removed, skin condition checks.
- Release at the earliest opportunity; comfort the child immediately after.

## 3.10 Paediatric pain assessment

| Scale | Age group | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FLACC** (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability) | Pre-verbal, 0–3 years | Observer scores 0–2 each item; total 0–10 |
| **Wong-Baker FACES** | 3–8 years | Child points to the face that matches their pain; score 0–10 |
| **NRS** (Numeric Rating Scale) | ≥8 years | Child rates pain 0–10 verbally |

### Pain management

**Non-pharmacological**:
- **Sucrose (oral)** 0.5–2 mL of 24% sucrose 2 minutes before a procedure in neonates — reduces cry duration and FLACC score.
- Distraction (music, bubbles, story, holding a toy).
- **EMLA cream** (eutectic mixture of local anaesthetics) — apply 45–60 minutes before venepuncture under an occlusive dressing; reduces needle-insertion pain.
- Breastfeeding during the procedure in infants.

**Pharmacological**:
- **Paracetamol** 15 mg/kg orally every 4–6 hours for mild-moderate pain.
- **Ibuprofen** 5–10 mg/kg every 6–8 hours for mild-moderate pain (avoid < 3 months).
- **Morphine infusion** 20–40 mcg/kg/hour (titrated) for severe pain — requires careful monitoring of respiratory rate.

## 3.11 Informed consent and assent in paediatrics

- **Informed consent** must be obtained from a parent or legal guardian for any procedure or treatment.
- **Assent** — explaining the procedure in age-appropriate terms and asking for the child's agreement — should be sought from children old enough to understand (generally ≥7 years); it respects the child's developing autonomy even though it cannot replace parental consent.
- Document both consent and assent in the case record.

## Remember

- Toddlers suffer most from hospitalisation; the three phases of separation are Protest, Despair, Detachment.
- Rooming-in and parental presence reduce separation anxiety most effectively.
- Maintenance fluids: 100/50/20 rule (Holliday-Segar).
- IM injection in infants/toddlers: vastus lateralis.
- Confirm NG placement by aspiration (pH <5.5); X-ray if in doubt.
- Blood transfusion: 10–15 mL/kg packed red cells; complete within 4 hours.
- FLACC for pre-verbal, Faces for 3–8 years, NRS for ≥8 years.
- Sucrose 0.5–2 mL reduces procedural pain in neonates; EMLA cream 45 min before venepuncture.
- Informed consent from parent; assent from child ≥7 years.
