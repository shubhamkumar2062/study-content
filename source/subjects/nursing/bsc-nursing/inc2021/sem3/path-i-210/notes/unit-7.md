# Unit 7: Special pathology — skeletal and endocrine systems

INC Unit II (third part): bone healing, osteoporosis, osteomyelitis and bone tumours; rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis; diabetes mellitus, goitre and carcinoma of the thyroid.

## Part A: Skeletal system

## 7.1 Bone healing (fracture healing)

A **fracture** is a break in the continuity of bone. Bone is one of the few tissues that heals by **regeneration** — new bone, not a scar — if the ends are kept in contact and still.

### Stages of fracture healing

```text
 1. HAEMATOMA (day 0–5)
    Torn vessels bleed → clot between the bone ends; bone cells at the ends die
    (no blood supply) → acute inflammation
          ▼
 2. GRANULATION TISSUE / PROCALLUS (days 3–14)
    Capillaries, fibroblasts and osteoprogenitor cells grow into the clot;
    macrophages remove dead tissue
          ▼
 3. SOFT (FIBROCARTILAGINOUS) CALLUS (2–3 weeks)
    Fibroblasts and chondroblasts lay down collagen and cartilage;
    the fragments are loosely joined
          ▼
 4. HARD (BONY) CALLUS (3 weeks to about 3 months)
    Osteoblasts lay down woven bone; cartilage is replaced by bone;
    the fracture is clinically united; visible on X-ray
          ▼
 5. REMODELLING (months to years)
    Osteoclasts remove excess callus; woven bone → mature lamellar bone;
    the medullary cavity is restored; shape follows stress lines (Wolff's law)
```

Primary healing (no visible callus) occurs when the ends are fixed rigidly and tightly together with surgical plates.

### Factors that delay healing

- **Local**: movement at the fracture site (poor immobilisation), poor blood supply (e.g., scaphoid, neck of femur), infection, soft tissue between the ends, wide gap, comminuted fracture, pathological fracture (bone tumour), radiation.
- **General**: old age, malnutrition (protein, vitamin C, vitamin D, calcium), diabetes, steroids, smoking, anaemia.

### Complications

Delayed union, **non-union** (pseudarthrosis — a false joint), **malunion** (healing in a wrong position), infection (osteomyelitis in open fractures), fat embolism, avascular necrosis, compartment syndrome, deep vein thrombosis, joint stiffness.

## 7.2 Osteoporosis

- **Definition**: a disease in which **bone mass is reduced** and the bone micro-architecture deteriorates, while the remaining bone is **normally mineralised** — bones become porous and fragile, and fracture easily.
- Compare **osteomalacia** (rickets in children): bone matrix is present but **poorly mineralised** because of vitamin D deficiency.
- **Mechanism**: bone resorption by osteoclasts exceeds formation by osteoblasts. Peak bone mass is reached around age 30; after that, loss is gradual.

| Primary | Secondary |
| --- | --- |
| **Postmenopausal** (type I) — oestrogen falls → more osteoclast activity → mainly spongy (trabecular) bone; vertebral and wrist fractures | Steroid therapy (commonest drug cause), hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing's syndrome |
| **Senile / age-related** (type II) — after 70 in both sexes; cortical and trabecular bone; hip fractures | Immobilisation, malnutrition, low calcium and vitamin D, alcohol, smoking, chronic kidney and liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, some anticonvulsants, heparin |

- **Risk factors**: female sex, early menopause, thin body build, family history, lack of exercise, low calcium intake, smoking, alcohol.
- **Features**: "silent" until a fracture — fractures of the **vertebrae** (compression fractures → loss of height, back pain, kyphosis — "dowager's hump"), **hip** (neck of femur) and **wrist** (Colles' fracture) after minor falls.
- **Lab and investigations**: **bone mineral density by DEXA scan** (T-score of −2.5 or lower = osteoporosis); serum calcium, phosphate and alkaline phosphatase are usually **normal** (helps to distinguish it from osteomalacia, where alkaline phosphatase is high and calcium/phosphate may be low); vitamin D level; tests for secondary causes (thyroid, parathyroid).

## 7.3 Osteomyelitis

**Osteomyelitis** is inflammation of bone and its marrow, usually due to infection.

### Acute pyogenic (haematogenous) osteomyelitis

- **Cause**: ***Staphylococcus aureus*** (commonest at all ages); *Salmonella* in **sickle cell disease**; *Haemophilus* and streptococci in children; *E. coli* and group B strep in newborns; *Pseudomonas* after puncture wounds and in IV drug users.
- **Routes**: blood spread from a distant focus (boil, throat infection) — common in children; direct entry through open fractures, surgery or prosthetic joints; spread from a nearby infection (diabetic foot ulcer).
- **Site in children**: the **metaphysis** of long bones (lower femur, upper tibia) — here blood flows slowly in looped vessels, so bacteria settle.

```text
 Bacteria settle in metaphysis → acute inflammation, pus under pressure in rigid bone
   → pus spreads through Haversian canals to the surface → lifts the periosteum
   → blood supply cut → a piece of dead bone forms = SEQUESTRUM
   → lifted periosteum lays down new bone around it = INVOLUCRUM
   → openings in the involucrum through which pus drains = CLOACAE
   → sinus tracts to the skin
```

- **Features**: high fever, severe pain, refusal to move the limb, local tenderness, warmth and swelling.
- **Lab**: neutrophil leucocytosis, **raised ESR and CRP** (useful for follow-up), **blood culture** (positive in many), pus or bone culture; X-ray changes appear only after about **10–14 days**; MRI and bone scan detect it early.
- **Complications**: chronic osteomyelitis, septicaemia, septic arthritis, pathological fracture, growth disturbance (damage to the growth plate), secondary amyloidosis, squamous cell carcinoma in a long-standing sinus.

### Chronic osteomyelitis

Follows inadequately treated acute infection; dead bone (sequestrum) harbours bacteria, so it persists with discharging sinuses and flare-ups. Treatment needs removal of the sequestrum.

### Tuberculous osteomyelitis

Spread from the lungs through blood; commonest in the **spine** (**Pott's disease** — destroys vertebral bodies and discs, causing a gibbus/kyphosis, cold abscess such as a psoas abscess, and paraplegia), then hip and knee.

## 7.4 Bone tumours

**Metastatic (secondary) tumours are the commonest malignant tumours of bone** in adults — from breast, prostate, lung, kidney and thyroid.

| Tumour | Nature | Usual age | Usual site | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Osteochondroma (exostosis) | Benign, commonest benign bone tumour | Adolescents | Metaphysis near the knee | Bony outgrowth capped with cartilage |
| Osteoma | Benign | Adults | Skull, facial bones | Slow-growing bony lump |
| Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma) | Benign but locally aggressive; may recur | 20–40 years | **Epiphysis** of long bones (around the knee, lower radius) | "Soap-bubble" appearance on X-ray; many osteoclast-like giant cells |
| Osteosarcoma (osteogenic sarcoma) | **Most common primary malignant bone tumour** (excluding myeloma) | 10–25 years (second peak in the elderly with Paget's disease) | **Metaphysis** around the knee (lower femur, upper tibia), upper humerus | Tumour cells form bone (osteoid); periosteal reaction — **Codman's triangle**, "**sunburst**" appearance; early spread by blood to the **lungs** |
| Ewing's sarcoma | Malignant, small round blue cells | 5–20 years | **Diaphysis** of long bones, pelvis | Fever and raised ESR may mimic infection; "**onion-peel**" layers of periosteal new bone |
| Chondrosarcoma | Malignant cartilage tumour | Middle age, older | Pelvis, shoulder, ribs | Slow-growing |
| Multiple myeloma | Malignant plasma cell tumour of marrow | Above 50 | Vertebrae, skull, ribs, pelvis | "Punched-out" lytic lesions; bone pain, anaemia, high calcium, kidney failure; **M-band on serum protein electrophoresis**; **Bence Jones protein** in urine; raised ESR |

Features of malignant bone tumours: pain (often worse at night), swelling, pathological fracture, weight loss. Lab: raised **alkaline phosphatase** (osteosarcoma, bone-forming metastases), raised calcium (lytic metastases, myeloma); diagnosis by X-ray, MRI and **biopsy**.

## 7.5 Arthritis

### Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)

- **Definition**: a chronic **autoimmune** systemic inflammatory disease that mainly attacks the **synovium** of small joints, symmetrically.
- **Who**: women about 3 times more than men; usually 30–50 years; genetic link (HLA-DR4).
- **Mechanism**: an unknown trigger in a susceptible person → T cells and B cells attack joint tissue → cytokines (TNF, IL-1, IL-6) → inflamed, thickened synovium spreads over the cartilage as **pannus** (granulation tissue) → pannus erodes cartilage and bone → fibrous and then bony fusion of the joint (ankylosis) → deformity.
- **Joints**: small joints of the hands (**proximal interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal** joints; distal interphalangeal joints are spared), wrists, feet, knees — **symmetrical**.
- **Features**: pain, swelling, warmth, **morning stiffness lasting more than an hour**, tiredness, low fever; deformities — ulnar deviation, **swan-neck** and **boutonnière** deformities.
- **Extra-articular**: rheumatoid nodules (elbows), anaemia, lung fibrosis, pleurisy, pericarditis, vasculitis, eye inflammation, Sjögren's syndrome, secondary amyloidosis.
- **Lab**: **rheumatoid factor** (positive in many; not specific), **anti-CCP antibodies** (more specific), raised ESR and CRP (activity), anaemia of chronic disease; synovial fluid is inflammatory (cloudy, many neutrophils); X-ray shows narrowing of joint space, erosions near the joint margins and thinning of bone around joints.

### Osteoarthritis (OA)

- **Definition**: a **degenerative** ("wear-and-tear") disease of **articular cartilage** with reactive new bone formation; not primarily inflammatory.
- **Types**: **primary** — ageing, the commonest joint disease; **secondary** — previous injury, obesity, deformity, occupation (heavy lifting, kneeling), previous RA or infection, diabetes.
- **Mechanism**: chondrocytes fail to maintain cartilage → cartilage softens and splits (**fibrillation**) and is worn away → underlying bone becomes thick and polished like ivory (**eburnation**) → bone cysts beneath → bony outgrowths at the joint edges (**osteophytes**) → bits of cartilage and bone break off (joint mice).
- **Joints**: weight-bearing joints — **knees** (very common in Indian women, aggravated by squatting and cross-legged sitting), hips, lumbar and cervical spine; in the hands, **distal interphalangeal** (Heberden's nodes) and proximal interphalangeal (Bouchard's nodes) joints. Often asymmetrical.
- **Features**: pain worse on use and at the end of the day, relieved by rest; short morning stiffness (under 30 minutes); creaking (crepitus); reduced movement; no systemic illness.
- **Lab**: blood tests are **normal** (ESR normal, RA factor negative); synovial fluid non-inflammatory; X-ray — narrowed joint space (often uneven), osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cysts.

| Feature | Rheumatoid arthritis | Osteoarthritis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Autoimmune, inflammatory, systemic | Degenerative, local |
| Age, sex | 30–50, women | Elderly, both |
| Joints | Small joints of hands (PIP, MCP), wrists; symmetrical | Weight-bearing (knee, hip), DIP; asymmetrical |
| Primary tissue | Synovium (pannus) | Cartilage |
| Morning stiffness | More than 1 hour | Less than 30 minutes |
| Systemic features | Fever, anaemia, nodules | None |
| Lab | RA factor, anti-CCP, raised ESR/CRP | Normal |
| X-ray | Erosions, juxta-articular osteopenia | Osteophytes, sclerosis, cysts |

## Part B: Endocrine system

## 7.6 Diabetes mellitus

**Diabetes mellitus** is a group of metabolic disorders marked by **chronic hyperglycaemia** due to a defect in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both, with disturbed carbohydrate, fat and protein metabolism.

### Types

| Feature | Type 1 | Type 2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Autoimmune destruction of **beta cells** of the islets → absolute insulin deficiency | **Insulin resistance** + relative (progressive) insulin deficiency |
| Proportion | Small minority | Most cases (India has a very large number) |
| Age | Usually children and young people | Usually adults (now younger too) |
| Body build | Thin | Often obese (abdominal obesity) |
| Onset | Sudden | Gradual, often found on routine tests |
| Genetics | HLA-linked | Strong family history |
| Antibodies | Islet antibodies present (anti-GAD) | Absent |
| Ketoacidosis | Common | Uncommon |
| Pancreas | Islets inflamed (insulitis), beta cells lost | Islets initially normal; later amyloid deposits, fewer beta cells |
| Treatment | Insulin essential | Lifestyle, oral drugs, insulin later |

Other types: **gestational diabetes** (first found in pregnancy), and secondary diabetes (pancreatitis, pancreatic surgery or cancer, Cushing's syndrome, steroid therapy, acromegaly).

### Pathophysiology of the symptoms

```text
 Lack of insulin effect
   │
   ├─► Glucose cannot enter muscle and fat; liver keeps releasing glucose → HYPERGLYCAEMIA
   │      └─► glucose in urine (glycosuria) → osmotic diuresis → POLYURIA → dehydration → POLYDIPSIA
   ├─► Cells starve → breakdown of fat and protein → WEIGHT LOSS, POLYPHAGIA, tiredness
   └─► Excess fat breakdown → ketone bodies → KETOACIDOSIS (mainly type 1)
```

### Complications

- **Acute**: diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA: high sugar, ketones, acidosis, deep sighing Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath, dehydration); hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (type 2, very high sugar, severe dehydration, confusion); **hypoglycaemia** (from treatment — sweating, tremor, confusion, fits); infections.
- **Chronic** (due to non-enzymatic glycosylation of proteins, sorbitol pathway and vessel damage):
  - **Macrovascular** — accelerated atherosclerosis: myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral vascular disease, gangrene.
  - **Microvascular** — thickened capillary basement membranes:
    - **Nephropathy** — nodular glomerulosclerosis (**Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules**), protein in urine, chronic kidney disease.
    - **Retinopathy** — microaneurysms, haemorrhages, new vessels; a leading cause of blindness.
    - **Neuropathy** — loss of sensation in the feet ("glove and stocking"), autonomic neuropathy.
  - **Diabetic foot** — neuropathy + ischaemia + infection → ulcers, gangrene, amputation.
  - Infections (TB, urinary tract, skin, fungal), cataract, delayed wound healing.

### Laboratory diagnosis

| Test | Diabetes (standard WHO/ADA cut-offs) |
| --- | --- |
| Fasting plasma glucose (no calories for at least 8 h) | 126 mg/dL or more |
| 2-hour plasma glucose after 75 g oral glucose (OGTT) | 200 mg/dL or more |
| HbA1c | 6.5% or more |
| Random plasma glucose with classic symptoms | 200 mg/dL or more |

- Without symptoms, an abnormal result should be confirmed by repeating the test.
- **HbA1c** reflects average glucose control over the past **2–3 months** (lifespan of red cells).
- Urine tests: glucose, **ketones** (DKA), **microalbumin** (early kidney damage).
- Others: lipid profile, kidney function, fundus examination, ECG.

## 7.7 Goitre

**Goitre** = enlargement of the thyroid gland. It may occur with normal function (euthyroid), overactivity (hyperthyroid) or underactivity (hypothyroid).

### Simple (non-toxic) goitre

- **Endemic goitre**: due to **iodine deficiency** in hilly and sub-Himalayan regions and flood-prone areas where soil iodine is low; prevented by **iodised salt** (India's National Iodine Deficiency Disorders Control Programme).
- **Sporadic goitre**: increased demand (puberty, pregnancy), goitrogens (cabbage, cassava, some drugs), inherited defects of hormone synthesis.
- **Mechanism**: low thyroid hormone → pituitary releases more **TSH** → thyroid cells undergo hyperplasia and hypertrophy → diffuse enlargement → repeated cycles over years → **multinodular goitre** (nodules, haemorrhage, cystic change, calcification).
- **Features**: neck swelling that moves up on swallowing; large goitres cause pressure — difficulty breathing (trachea), swallowing (oesophagus), hoarseness; retrosternal extension.
- Severe iodine deficiency in pregnancy causes **cretinism** (stunted growth and intellectual disability) in the child.

### Toxic goitre

- **Graves' disease**: autoimmune; antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor (TSI) → diffuse goitre with **hyperthyroidism**, exophthalmos (bulging eyes) and pretibial myxoedema.
- **Toxic multinodular goitre** (Plummer's disease): one or more nodules become overactive.
- Features of hyperthyroidism: weight loss with good appetite, heat intolerance, sweating, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, diarrhoea.

### Other

- **Hashimoto's thyroiditis**: autoimmune; goitre with lymphocytic infiltration; leads to **hypothyroidism** (weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, slow pulse, dry skin, myxoedema).

**Lab (thyroid function tests)**: **TSH** is the best screening test; free T4 and T3; thyroid antibodies (anti-TPO in Hashimoto's, TSH receptor antibodies in Graves'); ultrasound of the neck; radioiodine uptake scan (hot or cold nodule); **FNAC** of a nodule.

## 7.8 Carcinoma of the thyroid

- **Risk factors**: radiation to the neck in childhood, family history, iodine deficiency (follicular type), MEN 2 syndromes (medullary type).
- A **solitary thyroid nodule**, especially a "cold" nodule on scan, hard, fixed, in a man, a child or the elderly, with hoarseness or enlarged neck nodes, should be checked by FNAC.

| Type | Frequency | Features | Spread | Prognosis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Papillary carcinoma | **Commonest** | Young adults, women; papillae; nuclei with "**Orphan Annie eye**" (clear) appearance, grooves; **psammoma bodies** (calcified spheres) | **Lymphatic** to neck nodes | Excellent |
| Follicular carcinoma | Second | Middle age; iodine-deficient areas; invades capsule and vessels | **Blood** — to bone and lungs | Good |
| Medullary carcinoma | Uncommon | From **parafollicular C cells**; secretes **calcitonin** (marker); amyloid in stroma; may be familial (MEN 2) | Nodes and blood | Moderate |
| Anaplastic carcinoma | Rare | Elderly; rapidly growing hard mass with pressure symptoms | Local invasion | Very poor |

Lab: FNAC (diagnoses papillary, medullary, anaplastic; follicular cancer needs histology to prove capsular or vascular invasion), thyroid function usually normal, serum calcitonin (medullary), **thyroglobulin** for follow-up after thyroidectomy (papillary and follicular).

## 7.9 Application in nursing

- **Fracture care**: immobilise properly; check the circulation, sensation and movement distal to casts (pain on passive stretch, pallor, numbness → compartment syndrome — report at once); diet rich in protein, calcium and vitamin D; watch for fat embolism and DVT.
- **Osteoporosis prevention**: weight-bearing exercise, calcium- and vitamin D-rich diet, sunlight, stop smoking and alcohol; **fall prevention** for the elderly — good lighting, non-slip floors, grab bars, safe footwear.
- **Osteomyelitis**: collect blood cultures before antibiotics; immobilise and elevate the limb; aseptic sinus dressing; monitor temperature and ESR/CRP trends; teach completion of the long treatment course.
- **Arthritis**: joint protection, rest during flares, warm or cold applications, range-of-motion exercises, weight reduction in knee OA, assistive devices.
- **Diabetes**: capillary blood glucose monitoring technique and recording; recognise hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia; **foot care education** (daily inspection, wash and dry between toes, proper footwear, never walk barefoot, nail care, report wounds); diet and exercise; teach the meaning of HbA1c.
- **Goitre**: promote use of iodised salt; observe for breathing difficulty in large goitres; after thyroidectomy, watch for bleeding, airway obstruction, hoarseness and low calcium (tingling, spasms).

## Remember

- Fracture healing: haematoma → granulation tissue → soft callus → hard callus → remodelling.
- Non-union, malunion and delayed union; movement and infection delay healing.
- Osteoporosis: less bone, normally mineralised; DEXA T-score ≤ −2.5; serum calcium, phosphate and ALP normal.
- Osteomyelitis: *S. aureus*, metaphysis in children; sequestrum (dead bone), involucrum (new bone), cloaca; *Salmonella* in sickle cell disease; Pott's spine.
- Commonest malignant bone tumours: metastases; commonest primary: osteosarcoma (metaphysis, Codman's triangle, sunburst, lungs).
- Ewing's: diaphysis, onion-peel; giant cell tumour: epiphysis, soap-bubble.
- Myeloma: M-band, Bence Jones protein, punched-out lesions.
- RA: autoimmune, pannus, symmetrical small joints, morning stiffness > 1 h, RA factor, anti-CCP.
- OA: degenerative, cartilage loss, osteophytes, eburnation, Heberden's nodes (DIP), normal blood tests.
- Diabetes: fasting ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-h OGTT ≥ 200, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%; type 1 autoimmune beta-cell loss, type 2 insulin resistance.
- Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules = diabetic nephropathy.
- Endemic goitre: iodine deficiency → ↑ TSH → hyperplasia; iodised salt.
- Thyroid cancer: papillary commonest (Orphan Annie nuclei, psammoma bodies, lymph nodes); follicular → blood; medullary → calcitonin; anaplastic worst.
