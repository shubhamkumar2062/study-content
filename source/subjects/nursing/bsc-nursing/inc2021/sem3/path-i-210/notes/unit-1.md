# Unit 1: Introduction, cell injury and cellular adaptations

## 1.1 What pathology is and why a nurse studies it

**Pathology** (Greek *pathos* = suffering, *logos* = study) is the study of disease: what causes it, how it develops, what it does to cells, tissues and organs, and how these changes produce the signs and symptoms we see at the bedside.

Pathology is the bridge between the basic sciences (anatomy, physiology, biochemistry, microbiology) and clinical nursing. When you know *why* a patient has oedema, *why* a wound is not healing or *why* the haemoglobin is low, your observations become purposeful and your care plan becomes logical.

Why it matters to the nurse:

- **Understanding the disease** — the nurse can explain the condition to the patient and family in simple words.
- **Observation** — knowing the expected course helps you spot complications early (for example, a pale, cold limb after a fracture may mean blocked blood flow).
- **Laboratory work** — nurses collect blood, urine, stool, sputum and body fluids. The right container, timing and labelling decide whether the result is useful. A haemolysed or mislabelled sample can harm a patient.
- **Interpreting reports** — a nurse who understands a raised ESR, a low platelet count or a long prothrombin time knows which precautions to take.
- **Prevention and health education** — knowing risk factors (smoking, alcohol, obesity) lets the nurse teach prevention.

### Branches of pathology

| Branch | What it studies | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General pathology | Basic reactions of cells and tissues common to all diseases | Cell injury, inflammation, healing, neoplasia |
| Systemic (special) pathology | Diseases of each organ system | Pneumonia, peptic ulcer, cirrhosis |
| Histopathology | Tissue changes seen under the microscope | Biopsy of a breast lump |
| Cytopathology | Individual cells shed or aspirated | Pap smear, fine-needle aspiration (FNAC) |
| Haematology | Blood and blood-forming organs | Anaemia, leukaemia, bleeding disorders |
| Clinical pathology | Laboratory tests on body fluids | Urine, stool, CSF examination |
| Chemical pathology (clinical biochemistry) | Chemicals in blood and fluids | Blood sugar, urea, bilirubin |
| Immunopathology | Immune system in disease | Allergy, autoimmunity |
| Forensic pathology | Disease and injury in legal cases | Medicolegal autopsy |

## 1.3 Cell injury: causes (aetiology)

Cells constantly adjust to demands. When a stress is too strong or lasts too long, the cell is **injured**. If the stress stops early, the injury is **reversible**; if it continues, the cell dies (**irreversible injury**).

```text
 Normal cell ──stress──► Adaptation (atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia)
      │
      └── severe stress ──► Reversible injury ──► recovery if cause removed
                                     │
                                     └── continued ──► Irreversible injury ──► cell death
                                                                   (necrosis or apoptosis)
```

Main causes of cell injury:

1. **Hypoxia** — the most common cause. Due to ischaemia (blocked artery), poor oxygenation of blood (respiratory failure), reduced oxygen-carrying capacity (anaemia, carbon monoxide poisoning).
2. **Physical agents** — trauma, extreme heat (burns) or cold (frostbite), electric shock, radiation, sudden changes in atmospheric pressure.
3. **Chemical agents and drugs** — strong acids and alkalis, poisons, alcohol, tobacco smoke, some drugs in excess, even too much glucose or salt.
4. **Infectious agents** — viruses, bacteria (and their toxins), fungi, parasites.
5. **Immunological reactions** — allergy (anaphylaxis) and autoimmune diseases, where the body attacks its own cells.
6. **Genetic defects** — sickle cell anaemia, inborn errors of metabolism.
7. **Nutritional imbalance** — protein–energy malnutrition, vitamin deficiency, and also excess (obesity, high-fat diet leading to atherosclerosis).
8. **Ageing** — reduced capacity of cells to repair themselves.
9. **Psychological factors** — prolonged stress contributes to some diseases.

## 1.4 Pathogenesis: how cells get injured

Different causes act through a few common mechanisms:

| Mechanism | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ATP depletion | Lack of oxygen stops aerobic respiration; the sodium pump fails, so sodium and water enter the cell and it swells; anaerobic glycolysis produces lactic acid and lowers pH | Ischaemia of heart muscle |
| Mitochondrial damage | Less ATP; leakage of proteins that trigger apoptosis | Hypoxia, toxins |
| Calcium influx | Excess calcium inside the cell activates enzymes that digest membranes, proteins and DNA | Ischaemia, toxins |
| Free radicals (reactive oxygen species) | Unstable oxygen molecules damage lipids of membranes, proteins and DNA | Radiation, reperfusion after ischaemia, chemicals, ageing |
| Membrane damage | Loss of selective permeability; contents leak out | Bacterial toxins, complement |
| DNA and protein damage | Cell triggers apoptosis if repair fails | Radiation, drugs |

### Reversible cell injury

The cell is damaged but recovers if the cause is removed.

- **Cellular swelling (cloudy swelling, hydropic change)** — the earliest change. Pump failure lets water in; the organ looks pale, swollen and heavy.
- **Fatty change (steatosis)** — fat droplets collect in the cytoplasm, mostly in the liver (alcohol, malnutrition, diabetes, obesity). The liver is enlarged, yellow and greasy.
- Other features: loss of microvilli, blebs on the cell surface, swelling of mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, clumping of nuclear chromatin.

### Irreversible cell injury

Two features mark the "point of no return":

1. Inability to restore mitochondrial function (no ATP even after the oxygen returns).
2. Severe damage to cell membranes, including lysosomal membranes, so enzymes leak and digest the cell.

Clinically, injured cells leak their enzymes into blood. This is the basis of useful tests: cardiac troponins and CK-MB after myocardial infarction, ALT and AST after liver cell injury, amylase and lipase after pancreatic injury.

## 1.5 Necrosis

**Necrosis** is the death of a group of cells or tissue in a living body, followed by breakdown of the dead cells by their own enzymes and by enzymes of white cells. It is always pathological and usually causes inflammation around it.

Nuclear changes in necrosis (seen under the microscope):

- **Pyknosis** — the nucleus shrinks and becomes dark.
- **Karyorrhexis** — the nucleus breaks into fragments.
- **Karyolysis** — the nucleus fades away.

The cytoplasm becomes pink (eosinophilic) because of denatured proteins.

### Types of necrosis

| Type | Mechanism and appearance | Typical site / example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coagulative | Proteins are denatured; outline of dead cells is kept for some days; tissue is firm and pale | Infarcts of heart, kidney, spleen (any solid organ except brain) |
| Liquefactive | Enzymes digest the tissue into a liquid mass | Brain infarct; abscess with pus |
| Caseous | Cheese-like, white, soft, crumbly; a mix of coagulative and liquefactive; surrounded by a granuloma | Tuberculosis |
| Fat necrosis | Fat broken down by lipase; fatty acids combine with calcium to form chalky white spots (saponification) | Acute pancreatitis; injury to the breast |
| Fibrinoid | Protein-like (fibrin) material deposited in damaged vessel walls | Malignant hypertension, vasculitis |
| Gangrenous | Necrosis with putrefaction (see 1.6) | Limbs, bowel |

## 1.6 Gangrene

**Gangrene** is necrosis of a large mass of tissue, usually with added bacterial putrefaction (decay). It commonly affects the limbs, the bowel, the appendix and the gall bladder.

| Feature | Dry gangrene | Wet gangrene | Gas gangrene |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Slow arterial blockage, venous drainage normal | Arterial and venous blockage, or infection of moist tissue | Wound infection with *Clostridium perfringens* and related organisms |
| Common site | Toes, feet (limbs) | Bowel (strangulated hernia, volvulus), diabetic foot, lung | Deep muscle wounds, crushed tissue |
| Appearance | Dry, shrunken, black, mummified | Swollen, soft, moist, foul-smelling | Swollen, crepitus (crackling gas under skin) |
| Line of demarcation | Clear | Poor or absent | Absent |
| Spread | Slow | Rapid | Very rapid |
| Systemic toxicity | Little | Severe (septicaemia) | Severe toxaemia, shock |
| Prognosis | Better | Poor | Poor; an emergency |
| Examples | Atherosclerosis, Buerger's disease, frostbite, ergot | Diabetic foot, strangulated bowel | Road traffic and war wounds |

The black colour comes from iron sulphide formed from haemoglobin breakdown.

## 1.7 Apoptosis

**Apoptosis** ("falling off", like leaves from a tree) is programmed cell death: a controlled, energy-dependent suicide of single cells that removes unwanted cells without inflammation.

Examples:

- **Physiological** — shedding of the endometrium during menstruation, removal of extra cells during embryo development (separation of fingers), death of old neutrophils, removal of self-reactive lymphocytes in the thymus, shrinkage of the breast after weaning.
- **Pathological** — cells with damaged DNA (radiation, anticancer drugs), cells killed by cytotoxic T cells in viral hepatitis, atrophy of an organ after duct obstruction.

Steps: the cell shrinks → chromatin condenses → the cell breaks into membrane-bound pieces (apoptotic bodies) → phagocytes eat them quickly. Enzymes called **caspases** carry out the process.

| Feature | Apoptosis | Necrosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of cells | Single cells | Groups of cells, tissue |
| Cause | Physiological or pathological signals | Always pathological |
| Energy | Needs ATP (active) | Passive |
| Cell size | Shrinks | Swells |
| Cell membrane | Intact | Broken |
| Contents | Packed in apoptotic bodies | Leak out |
| Inflammation | None | Present |
| Nuclear change | Chromatin condensation and fragmentation | Pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis |

Too little apoptosis allows abnormal cells to survive (cancer, autoimmune disease); too much destroys useful cells (neurodegenerative diseases).

## 1.8 Cellular adaptations

Adaptations are reversible changes in the **size, number or type** of cells in response to a changed demand or a stress. They let the cell survive.

```text
 Increased demand ──► Hypertrophy (bigger cells) and/or Hyperplasia (more cells)
 Decreased demand ──► Atrophy (smaller cells)
 Chronic irritation ──► Metaplasia (one adult cell type replaced by another)
 Continued irritation ──► Dysplasia (disordered growth) ──► may become cancer
```

| Adaptation | Definition | Physiological example | Pathological example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Atrophy | Decrease in size of cells and so of the organ | Uterus after delivery; thymus after puberty; ovaries after menopause | Disuse (limb in a plaster cast), denervation (polio), loss of blood supply (brain in old age), malnutrition, pressure (bed sores, tumour pressing on tissue) |
| Hypertrophy | Increase in size of cells and so of the organ, without new cells | Skeletal muscle of athletes; uterus in pregnancy (with hyperplasia) | Left ventricular hypertrophy in hypertension and aortic stenosis |
| Hyperplasia | Increase in the number of cells | Breast at puberty and in pregnancy; liver regeneration after partial removal | Endometrial hyperplasia (excess oestrogen), benign prostatic hyperplasia, warts (viral) |
| Metaplasia | One mature (adult) cell type replaced by another better able to bear the stress | — | Bronchial ciliated columnar → squamous in smokers; squamous → columnar in lower oesophagus with acid reflux (Barrett's oesophagus); cervix (squamous metaplasia) |
| Dysplasia | Disordered growth: cells vary in size and shape, nuclei large and dark, loss of normal layering | — | Cervix (CIN), bronchus of smokers, oral leukoplakia |

Points to note:

- Hypertrophy happens in cells that cannot divide (heart and skeletal muscle); hyperplasia in cells that can divide. Many organs (uterus in pregnancy) show both.
- Metaplasia is protective but loses some function (squamous epithelium in the bronchus has no cilia and no mucus, so clearing of secretions is poorer). If the irritant continues, metaplasia can progress to dysplasia and cancer.
- **Dysplasia is not a true adaptation**; it is a **premalignant** change. Mild dysplasia can revert when the cause is removed; severe dysplasia involving the full thickness of the epithelium is called **carcinoma in situ** (see unit 3).
- **Aplasia** is failure of an organ to develop; **agenesis** is complete absence; **hypoplasia** is incomplete development. These are developmental, not adaptive.

## 1.9 Intracellular accumulations and calcification (short note)

- **Fatty change** (see 1.4) — liver, heart.
- **Pigments** — melanin, haemosiderin (iron, from old bleeding), bilirubin (jaundice), lipofuscin ("wear-and-tear" pigment in old age).
- **Pathological calcification**:
  - **Dystrophic** — calcium deposited in dead or damaged tissue with normal blood calcium (caseous TB, atheroma, damaged heart valves).
  - **Metastatic** — calcium deposited in normal tissues because blood calcium is high (hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D excess, bone destruction by tumour).

## 1.10 Application in nursing

- **Prevent hypoxic injury**: keep the airway clear, give prescribed oxygen, position the breathless patient upright, and watch colour, SpO2 and level of consciousness.
- **Pressure injury (bed sore)**: prolonged pressure causes ischaemia and necrosis. Turn bedridden patients regularly, keep skin clean and dry, use pressure-relieving mattresses and check bony prominences daily.
- **Disuse atrophy**: encourage early mobilisation and active or passive range-of-motion exercises for patients on bed rest or in casts.
- **Diabetic foot care**: daily inspection, proper footwear, nail care and early reporting of colour change prevent gangrene. Teach the patient to recognise dry and wet gangrene.
- **Gas gangrene**: deep, dirty wounds need thorough cleaning; report swelling, crackling under the skin and a foul smell at once.
- **Cancer screening**: metaplasia and dysplasia are warning signs. Encourage women for cervical screening and smokers to quit.
- **Enzyme tests**: understand that troponin, ALT/AST and amylase rise because injured cells leak them; collect samples at the right times.
- **Burns, frostbite, radiation**: these physical agents injure cells directly; protect skin and keep the area clean.

## Remember

- Aetiology = cause; pathogenesis = mechanism; morphology = structural change; prognosis = outcome.
- Hypoxia is the commonest cause of cell injury; ATP depletion → Na+ pump failure → cell swelling.
- Earliest reversible change: cellular swelling. Fatty change is reversible, commonly in the liver.
- Point of no return: mitochondrial failure + membrane damage.
- Nuclear changes of necrosis: pyknosis → karyorrhexis → karyolysis.
- Coagulative = infarcts of heart/kidney; liquefactive = brain and abscess; caseous = TB; fat = pancreatitis.
- Dry gangrene: clear line of demarcation, slow, better prognosis; wet gangrene: no line, fast, toxaemia.
- Gas gangrene: *Clostridium perfringens*, crepitus.
- Apoptosis: single cells, energy-dependent, no inflammation, apoptotic bodies, caspases.
- Atrophy ↓ size; hypertrophy ↑ size; hyperplasia ↑ number; metaplasia = change of cell type; dysplasia = premalignant disorder.
- Smoker's bronchus: columnar → squamous metaplasia; Barrett's oesophagus: squamous → columnar.
