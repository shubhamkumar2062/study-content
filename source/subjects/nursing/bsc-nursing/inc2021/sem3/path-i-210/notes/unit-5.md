# Unit 5: Special pathology — respiratory and cardiovascular systems

INC Unit II (first part): pulmonary infections (pneumonia, lung abscess, pulmonary tuberculosis), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (chronic bronchitis, emphysema, bronchial asthma, bronchiectasis), tumours of the lung; atherosclerosis, ischaemia and infarction, rheumatic heart disease and infective endocarditis. For each disease learn: cause → mechanism → gross and microscopic changes → clinical features → laboratory findings → nursing points.

## Part A: Respiratory system

## 5.1 Pneumonia

**Pneumonia** is acute inflammation of the lung tissue (alveoli and bronchioles) with filling of the alveoli by exudate, called **consolidation** (the lung becomes solid).

Causes: bacteria (*Streptococcus pneumoniae* — the commonest cause of community-acquired pneumonia; *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Pseudomonas* in hospital), viruses (influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2), atypical organisms (*Mycoplasma*), fungi and *Pneumocystis* in immunocompromised people, and aspiration of stomach contents.

Risk factors: extremes of age, smoking, alcoholism, diabetes, heart failure, COPD, immobility, unconsciousness, poor swallowing (aspiration), HIV, after anaesthesia and surgery.

### Anatomical types

| Feature | Lobar pneumonia | Bronchopneumonia |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution | Whole lobe, evenly | Patchy, around bronchioles, often both lower lobes |
| Usual organism | *S. pneumoniae* | Staphylococci, *Haemophilus*, streptococci, many others |
| Usual patient | Healthy young adults | Infants, elderly, debilitated, after surgery |
| Pleura | Usually involved (fibrinous pleurisy) | Less often |
| Outcome | Usually resolves completely | Less complete; may cause abscess or fibrosis |

**Interstitial (atypical) pneumonia** — viral or *Mycoplasma*; inflammation in the alveolar walls rather than the spaces.

### Stages of lobar pneumonia

```text
 1. Congestion (day 1–2): lobe heavy, red, boggy; vessels engorged; fluid with bacteria in alveoli
 2. Red hepatisation (day 2–4): lobe red, firm, liver-like; alveoli filled with
    red cells, neutrophils and fibrin
 3. Grey hepatisation (day 4–8): lobe grey-brown, dry; red cells broken down,
    fibrin and neutrophils remain
 4. Resolution (from about day 8): enzymes digest the exudate; coughed up or
    absorbed; lung returns to normal
```

### Clinical and laboratory features

- Sudden fever with chills, cough with rusty sputum (lobar pneumonia), pleuritic chest pain, fast breathing, crackles, bronchial breath sounds over consolidation.
- Lab: neutrophil leucocytosis, raised ESR and CRP; sputum Gram stain and culture; blood culture; chest X-ray shows consolidation (lobar opacity with air bronchogram) or patchy shadows; arterial blood gases in severe cases.
- Complications: pleural effusion, **empyema** (pus in the pleural cavity), lung abscess, septicaemia, meningitis, respiratory failure.

## 5.2 Lung abscess

A localised area of **suppurative necrosis** of the lung forming a cavity filled with pus.

- **Causes**: aspiration of infected material (unconsciousness, alcohol, anaesthesia, seizures, dental sepsis) — the commonest; complication of bacterial pneumonia (*Staphylococcus*, *Klebsiella*); obstruction of a bronchus by a tumour or foreign body; septic emboli; spread from nearby infection.
- **Site**: aspiration abscesses are more common on the right side (the right main bronchus is more vertical); in the posterior segment of the upper lobe and the apical segment of the lower lobe when lying down.
- **Morphology**: cavity with necrotic debris and pus, surrounded by inflamed and later fibrous wall; an air-fluid level on X-ray once it drains into a bronchus.
- **Features**: high fever, cough with large amounts of foul-smelling, purulent sputum, sometimes blood; clubbing in chronic cases.
- **Lab**: leucocytosis; sputum culture (often mixed anaerobic bacteria); chest X-ray or CT showing a cavity with air-fluid level; bronchoscopy to exclude a tumour.
- **Complications**: empyema, bleeding, brain abscess, secondary amyloidosis.

## 5.3 Pulmonary tuberculosis

A chronic granulomatous infection caused by ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** (an acid-fast bacillus), spread by **droplet nuclei** from coughing patients with open (sputum-positive) disease. India has the largest number of TB cases in the world.

### Pathogenesis

Protection depends on **cell-mediated immunity** (T cells and macrophages). The tissue damage is largely due to this immune reaction (type IV hypersensitivity), which makes caseous necrosis.

```text
 Inhaled bacilli → alveolar macrophages ingest them but cannot kill them at first
   → bacilli multiply; after about 3 weeks T cells activate macrophages
   → granulomas with caseous necrosis form (tubercle)
   → Mantoux test becomes positive
```

### Primary tuberculosis (first infection, often in children)

- **Ghon focus** — a small (about 1–1.5 cm) grey-white caseous lesion just under the pleura, usually in the lower part of the upper lobe or the upper part of the lower lobe.
- **Ghon complex** = Ghon focus + enlarged caseous hilar lymph nodes (+ connecting lymphangitis).
- Usually heals by fibrosis and calcification. In infants, malnourished or immunocompromised people it may progress: **progressive primary TB**, spread through blood to many organs (**miliary TB** — tiny millet-seed lesions in lungs, liver, spleen, meninges).

### Secondary (post-primary) tuberculosis

- Reactivation of old dormant bacilli (or re-infection) in a previously sensitised adult, often when immunity drops (HIV, diabetes, malnutrition, steroids, old age).
- Typically in the **apex of the lung** (high oxygen).
- Caseous lesions enlarge and break into a bronchus → **cavities**, which spread bacilli in the sputum (infectious) and through the bronchial tree.
- Erosion of a vessel in a cavity causes **haemoptysis**.
- Complications: tuberculous pleural effusion, empyema, laryngeal and intestinal TB (swallowed sputum), miliary spread, TB meningitis, bronchiectasis, fibrosis, secondary amyloidosis, aspergilloma in an old cavity.

### Microscopy

**Tubercle** — granuloma with central caseous necrosis, epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells, lymphocytes and peripheral fibrosis (see unit 2).

### Clinical and laboratory features

- Cough for two weeks or more, sputum, blood in sputum, evening rise of temperature, night sweats, weight loss, loss of appetite, chest pain.
- **Sputum microscopy** for acid-fast bacilli (Ziehl–Neelsen stain — bacilli appear red against blue) and **rapid molecular tests** (cartridge-based nucleic acid amplification, which also detects rifampicin resistance); sputum culture.
- Chest X-ray: upper-zone shadows, cavities, hilar nodes, effusion.
- Raised ESR, mild anaemia, lymphocytosis.
- **Tuberculin (Mantoux) test** shows past exposure or BCG, not necessarily active disease.
- Biopsy (lymph node, pleura) shows caseating granulomas.

Check the current National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) guidelines for the diagnostic tests used in your state.

## 5.4 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

A group of diseases with **long-term obstruction of airflow**, mainly on breathing out. The syllabus groups four conditions here; chronic bronchitis and emphysema are COPD proper, while asthma (reversible) and bronchiectasis are related obstructive diseases.

**Main cause: tobacco smoking.** Others: indoor air pollution from biomass fuel (chulha smoke, very important in rural Indian women), outdoor pollution, occupational dust, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (inherited, early emphysema).

### Chronic bronchitis

- **Clinical definition**: productive cough for at least **3 months in each of 2 consecutive years**, other causes excluded.
- **Mechanism**: irritants → **hypertrophy and hyperplasia of mucous glands** (Reid index raised — gland thickness compared to the bronchial wall) and goblet cells → excess mucus; inflammation; squamous metaplasia; narrowing of small airways.
- **Features**: "**blue bloater**" — cough with sputum, recurrent infections, cyanosis, carbon dioxide retention, and later right heart failure (**cor pulmonale**) with oedema.

### Emphysema

- **Definition**: permanent enlargement of the air spaces beyond the terminal bronchiole with **destruction of their walls**, without obvious fibrosis.
- **Mechanism (protease–antiprotease imbalance)**: smoke attracts neutrophils and macrophages, which release elastase; smoke also inactivates alpha-1 antitrypsin (the protective antiprotease) → elastic tissue destroyed → alveoli merge into large spaces, small airways collapse on expiration (air trapping).
- **Types**: **centriacinar** (centrilobular) — smokers, upper lobes; **panacinar** — alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, lower lobes; paraseptal; irregular. Large air spaces (bullae) may rupture → **pneumothorax**.
- **Features**: "**pink puffer**" — breathlessness, pursed-lip breathing, barrel-shaped chest, thin, use of accessory muscles; oxygen levels maintained until late.

### Bronchial asthma

- **Definition**: chronic inflammatory disease of the airways with **episodic, reversible** bronchoconstriction due to hyper-responsive airways.
- **Types**: **extrinsic (atopic, allergic)** — children, family history of allergy, triggered by dust mites, pollen, animal dander, food; raised IgE; **intrinsic (non-atopic)** — adults, triggered by infections, cold air, exercise, stress, aspirin, pollutants.
- **Mechanism**: allergen binds IgE on mast cells → release of histamine and leukotrienes (immediate phase: bronchospasm, mucus, oedema) → eosinophils and other cells arrive hours later (late phase: more inflammation).
- **Morphology**: thick mucus plugs with spirals (**Curschmann spirals**), eosinophils and their crystals (**Charcot–Leyden crystals**) in sputum; thickened basement membrane; hypertrophy of smooth muscle and mucous glands; overinflated lungs.
- **Features**: wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, often at night or early morning. **Status asthmaticus** — a severe attack not relieved by usual treatment; life-threatening ("silent chest" is an ominous sign).
- **Lab**: eosinophilia in blood and sputum, raised IgE, spirometry showing reversible obstruction (FEV1 improves after bronchodilator), peak expiratory flow monitoring.

### Bronchiectasis

- **Definition**: permanent abnormal **dilatation of bronchi** due to destruction of muscle and elastic tissue, from chronic necrotising infection.
- **Causes**: severe childhood infections (measles, whooping cough), TB, bronchial obstruction (tumour, foreign body), cystic fibrosis, immune deficiency, Kartagener syndrome (immotile cilia).
- **Mechanism**: obstruction + infection → damage to bronchial wall → dilatation → secretions stagnate → more infection (vicious cycle).
- **Features**: chronic cough with **copious foul-smelling purulent sputum**, especially on changing posture (morning), haemoptysis, clubbing, recurrent pneumonia; usually affects the lower lobes. Complications: lung abscess, brain abscess, amyloidosis, cor pulmonale.
- **Lab**: sputum culture; high-resolution CT shows dilated bronchi.

### Comparison table

| Feature | Chronic bronchitis | Emphysema | Asthma | Bronchiectasis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main site | Bronchi (mucous glands) | Acinus (alveolar walls) | Bronchi and bronchioles | Bronchi (dilated) |
| Key change | Mucous gland hyperplasia | Wall destruction, air trapping | Reversible spasm, inflammation | Permanent dilatation with pus |
| Main cause | Smoking | Smoking, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency | Allergy, triggers | Necrotising infection, obstruction |
| Sputum | Mucoid, excess | Scanty | Thick mucus, eosinophils | Copious, purulent, foul |
| Reversible | No | No | Yes | No |

## 5.5 Tumours of the lung

**Bronchogenic carcinoma** is the commonest cancer of the lung. Secondary (metastatic) tumours from other organs are even more frequent in the lung than primaries.

- **Causes**: **cigarette and bidi smoking** (the main cause; risk rises with the number and years of smoking), passive smoking, radon, asbestos (with smoking the risk multiplies), air pollution, industrial exposure (arsenic, chromium, nickel).
- **Pathogenesis**: smoke carcinogens → squamous metaplasia → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive cancer.

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Squamous cell carcinoma | Strongly linked with smoking; central (hilar) near large bronchi; may cause high calcium |
| Adenocarcinoma | Commonest type now, in women and non-smokers too; peripheral |
| Small cell carcinoma | Central; very aggressive, early spread; strongly linked with smoking; produces hormones (ADH, ACTH) |
| Large cell carcinoma | Undifferentiated, peripheral |

(Clinically: small cell lung cancer versus non-small cell lung cancer.) Benign tumours are rare (hamartoma, bronchial carcinoid is a low-grade malignant tumour).

- **Features**: persistent cough, blood-streaked sputum, chest pain, breathlessness, weight loss, hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve), recurrent pneumonia in the same lobe (obstruction), pleural effusion; **superior vena cava syndrome** (swollen face and arms); **Pancoast tumour** at the apex causes Horner's syndrome (drooping eyelid, small pupil, no sweating on one side) and arm pain; clubbing; paraneoplastic syndromes.
- **Spread**: hilar and mediastinal nodes, then liver, brain, bones and adrenal glands.
- **Lab and diagnosis**: chest X-ray and CT, sputum cytology, bronchoscopy with biopsy and washings, CT-guided needle biopsy, pleural fluid cytology.

## Part B: Cardiovascular system

## 5.6 Atherosclerosis

**Atherosclerosis** is a disease of large and medium-sized arteries in which the intima is thickened by **plaques (atheromas)** — lipid-rich cores covered by a fibrous cap. It is the main cause of heart attacks, strokes and gangrene of the legs. (**Arteriosclerosis** means "hardening of arteries" in general; atherosclerosis is its most important form.)

Common sites: abdominal aorta, coronary arteries, popliteal arteries, internal carotids, circle of Willis.

### Risk factors

| Non-modifiable | Modifiable |
| --- | --- |
| Increasing age | High blood cholesterol (high LDL, low HDL) |
| Male sex (women catch up after menopause) | Hypertension |
| Family history and genetics | Cigarette smoking |
| | Diabetes mellitus |
| | Obesity, lack of exercise, stress, high homocysteine, raised CRP |

### Pathogenesis (response-to-injury idea)

```text
 1. Endothelial injury (hypertension, smoking, high LDL, diabetes)
 2. LDL enters the intima and is oxidised
 3. Monocytes stick, enter, become macrophages and swallow lipid → FOAM CELLS
    (yellow FATTY STREAK — seen even in children)
 4. Cytokines and growth factors → smooth muscle cells move into the intima,
    multiply and make collagen
 5. FIBROFATTY PLAQUE: lipid core (cholesterol, foam cells, debris) under a fibrous cap
 6. COMPLICATED PLAQUE: calcification, ulceration, rupture, haemorrhage,
    THROMBOSIS on top, embolism, aneurysm
```

### Complications

- **Narrowing** of the lumen → ischaemia: angina, intermittent claudication (leg pain on walking).
- **Plaque rupture + thrombosis** → sudden occlusion: myocardial infarction, stroke, gangrene.
- **Embolism** of plaque material or thrombus.
- **Weakening of the wall** → aneurysm (abdominal aortic aneurysm), which may rupture.

Lab: lipid profile (total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides — fasting), blood sugar and HbA1c, renal function.

## 5.7 Ischaemia and myocardial infarction

**Ischaemic heart disease** results from an imbalance between the supply and demand of oxygen to heart muscle, almost always due to coronary atherosclerosis.

| Condition | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Stable angina | Fixed narrowing; chest pain on exertion, relieved by rest; no necrosis |
| Unstable angina | Plaque disruption with a partial non-occlusive thrombus; pain at rest or increasing |
| Myocardial infarction (MI) | Death of heart muscle due to prolonged ischaemia, usually a thrombus on a ruptured plaque |
| Sudden cardiac death | Fatal arrhythmia, often within an hour of symptoms |

### Myocardial infarction

- Most common in the territory of the **left anterior descending artery** (anterior wall of left ventricle and front of septum), then right coronary (inferior wall), then left circumflex (lateral wall).
- **Transmural** (full thickness) or **subendocardial** (inner third) infarct.
- Irreversible damage begins after about **20–40 minutes** of severe ischaemia.

Sequence of changes (coagulative necrosis):

| Time | Changes |
| --- | --- |
| 0–12 hours | Usually no visible change; electron microscopy and special stains show damage |
| 12–24 hours | Dark mottling; coagulative necrosis begins, wavy fibres |
| 1–3 days | Pale area with hyperaemic border; dense neutrophil infiltration |
| 3–7 days | Soft yellow centre; macrophages remove dead tissue — **weakest stage, risk of rupture** |
| 1–2 weeks | Granulation tissue at the edges |
| 2–8 weeks | Grey-white fibrous scar |

**Clinical features**: severe crushing central chest pain lasting more than 30 minutes, radiating to the left arm, neck or jaw, not relieved by rest; sweating, nausea, breathlessness, anxiety. Diabetics and the elderly may have "silent" MI.

**Lab**: ECG (ST elevation, Q waves, T inversion); **cardiac troponin I or T** (most sensitive and specific; rises in 3–4 hours, stays raised for 7–10 days); **CK-MB** (rises in 3–8 hours, back to normal in 2–3 days — useful to detect a re-infarction); neutrophil leucocytosis, raised ESR; echocardiography.

**Complications**: arrhythmias (the commonest cause of early death), left ventricular failure and cardiogenic shock, rupture of the wall, septum or papillary muscle (days 3–7), mural thrombus and embolism, pericarditis, ventricular aneurysm.

## 5.8 Rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease

**Rheumatic fever** is an acute **immune-mediated** inflammatory disease that follows a throat infection with **group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus** (*Streptococcus pyogenes*) after 2–3 weeks, mostly in children aged 5–15 years. It is common where there is overcrowding and poverty and remains an important cause of heart disease in India.

- **Mechanism**: antibodies against streptococcal antigens (M protein) cross-react with the patient's heart, joints and other tissues (**molecular mimicry**).
- **Major manifestations (Jones criteria)**: carditis, migratory polyarthritis of large joints, Sydenham's chorea, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum.
- **Minor**: fever, joint pain, raised ESR/CRP, prolonged PR interval on ECG.
- Diagnosis needs evidence of recent streptococcal infection (raised **ASO titre**, positive throat culture) plus the criteria.

**Pathology**: the characteristic lesion is the **Aschoff body** — a focus of fibrinoid change surrounded by lymphocytes and large macrophages (**Anitschkow cells** with "caterpillar" nuclei; Aschoff giant cells). **Pancarditis** — all three layers:

- Pericarditis: fibrinous ("bread and butter").
- Myocarditis: Aschoff bodies; may cause heart failure.
- Endocarditis: small warty vegetations (1–2 mm) along the lines of valve closure, mainly **mitral**, then aortic.

**Rheumatic heart disease (chronic)**: repeated attacks scar the valves — thickened, fused commissures, shortened chordae. **Mitral stenosis** ("fish-mouth" or "button-hole" valve) is the most typical, often with mitral regurgitation; aortic valve next. Results: left atrial enlargement, atrial fibrillation, thrombi and embolism, pulmonary congestion, heart failure, risk of infective endocarditis.

## 5.9 Infective endocarditis

Infection of the heart valves or endocardium by microorganisms, forming **bulky, friable vegetations** containing organisms, fibrin and inflammatory cells.

| Feature | Acute | Subacute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organism | *Staphylococcus aureus* (virulent) | *Streptococcus viridans* (low virulence) |
| Valves | Often previously normal; right-sided in IV drug users | Previously damaged (rheumatic, congenital, prosthetic) |
| Course | Days to weeks, destructive | Weeks to months |

- **Predisposing factors**: damaged or artificial valves, congenital heart disease, IV drug use, central lines and cannulas, dental procedures with bleeding, poor oral hygiene, immunosuppression.
- **Features**: fever, new or changing heart murmur, anaemia, weakness, splenomegaly, clubbing, small haemorrhages (splinter haemorrhages under nails, petechiae), Osler's nodes (painful finger nodules), Janeway lesions (painless palm spots), Roth spots in the retina, haematuria.
- **Complications**: emboli (stroke, kidney and spleen infarcts, septic abscesses), valve destruction and heart failure, glomerulonephritis.
- **Lab**: **repeated blood cultures** (three sets from separate sites before antibiotics), anaemia, leucocytosis, raised ESR/CRP, microscopic haematuria, **echocardiography** to see vegetations.

## 5.10 Application in nursing

- **Sputum collection**: early morning, deep cough (not saliva), into a sterile labelled container; for TB, follow the current NTEP instructions for the number and timing of samples, and send promptly; teach cough etiquette and safe disposal.
- **TB control**: airborne precautions (well-ventilated room, mask for coughing patients, N95 for staff in high-risk areas); support adherence to the full treatment course; screen contacts; nutritional support.
- **Pneumonia and abscess care**: positioning, deep breathing, chest physiotherapy and postural drainage (bronchiectasis, abscess), fluids, oxygen as prescribed, monitor SpO2 and temperature.
- **COPD and asthma**: teach correct inhaler technique, avoidance of triggers and smoke (including chulha smoke), pursed-lip breathing; controlled oxygen in chronic CO2 retainers as ordered; recognise signs of a severe attack.
- **Smoking cessation** counselling at every contact.
- **Chest pain**: record ECG promptly, collect troponin samples at the ordered times, keep the patient at rest, monitor for arrhythmia.
- **Rheumatic fever prevention**: early treatment of sore throat in children; teach the importance of regular secondary prophylaxis as prescribed; school health screening.
- **Endocarditis**: aseptic care of IV lines; blood cultures collected aseptically with correct volumes before antibiotics; good oral hygiene for patients with valve disease.
- **Atherosclerosis**: lifestyle education — healthy diet, exercise, weight control, BP and sugar control.

## Remember

- Lobar pneumonia stages: congestion → red hepatisation → grey hepatisation → resolution; *S. pneumoniae*.
- Lung abscess: aspiration, right side more, foul sputum, air-fluid level.
- Ghon focus + hilar node = Ghon complex (primary TB); secondary TB at the apex with cavities; ZN stain, CBNAAT.
- Chronic bronchitis: cough with sputum 3 months × 2 years; mucous gland hyperplasia, "blue bloater".
- Emphysema: alveolar wall destruction; protease–antiprotease; "pink puffer"; alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency → panacinar.
- Asthma: reversible; Curschmann spirals, Charcot–Leyden crystals, eosinophils.
- Bronchiectasis: permanent dilatation, copious foul sputum, clubbing.
- Lung cancer: smoking; squamous and small cell central, adenocarcinoma peripheral.
- Atherosclerosis: fatty streak → fibrofatty plaque → complicated plaque; foam cells.
- MI: LAD commonest; troponin most specific; rupture risk days 3–7.
- Rheumatic fever: group A strep, 2–3 weeks later, Aschoff bodies, mitral stenosis, ASO titre.
- Infective endocarditis: acute = *S. aureus*; subacute = *S. viridans*; blood cultures + echo.
