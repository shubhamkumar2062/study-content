# Unit 2: Inflammation and wound healing

INC Unit I (second part): acute inflammation (vascular and cellular events, systemic effects), chronic inflammation (granulomatous inflammation, systemic effects) and wound healing.

## 2.1 Definition and purpose

**Inflammation** is the protective response of living vascularised tissue to injury. It brings fluid, proteins and white blood cells from the blood to the site of injury to destroy or wall off the cause, remove dead tissue and start repair.

- It is a **defence**, not a disease in itself. Without it, infections would spread and wounds would not heal.
- It can also **harm**: swelling of the larynx can block the airway; inflammation of the brain or its coverings is dangerous; long-lasting inflammation scars organs (rheumatoid arthritis, cirrhosis).
- The suffix **"-itis"** means inflammation: appendicitis, hepatitis, gastritis.

Causes: infection (the most common), trauma, burns, chemicals, dead tissue (infarct), foreign bodies (sutures, splinters) and immune reactions (allergy, autoimmune disease).

**Inflammation is not the same as infection.** Infection means microbes are present and multiplying; inflammation is the tissue's response, which may or may not be due to microbes (a sprained ankle is inflamed but not infected).

## 2.2 Cardinal signs

| Sign | Latin | Cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Redness | Rubor | Dilatation of small vessels, more blood in the area |
| Heat | Calor | Increased blood flow |
| Swelling | Tumor | Fluid and cells leaving the vessels (exudate) |
| Pain | Dolor | Mediators (bradykinin, prostaglandins) and stretching of tissue |
| Loss of function | Functio laesa | Pain and swelling limit movement (added by Virchow) |

The first four were described by Celsus.

## 2.3 Acute inflammation

Acute inflammation is rapid in onset (seconds to minutes), lasts a few hours to a few days, and is marked by **fluid exudation** and **neutrophils**.

### A. Vascular events

```text
 Injury
   │
   ▼
 1. Transient vasoconstriction of arterioles (seconds)
   │
   ▼
 2. Vasodilatation (histamine, nitric oxide) → more blood flow → redness, heat
   │
   ▼
 3. Increased vascular permeability (gaps between endothelial cells)
    → protein-rich fluid leaks into tissue → swelling (exudate)
   │
   ▼
 4. Slowing of blood flow (stasis); blood becomes thicker as fluid leaves
   │
   ▼
 5. White cells move to the edge of the vessel → cellular events begin
```

Mechanisms of increased permeability:

- Contraction of endothelial cells creating gaps (histamine, bradykinin) — fast and short-lived; mainly in venules.
- Direct injury to endothelium (burns, toxins) — immediate and lasting.
- Delayed leakage hours later (sunburn, mild heat).
- Leakage through new blood vessels during repair.

### B. Cellular events (leucocyte events)

Neutrophils arrive first (within hours); monocytes/macrophages follow after 24–48 hours.

```text
 Margination ──► Rolling ──► Adhesion ──► Emigration (diapedesis)
      ──► Chemotaxis ──► Phagocytosis ──► Killing and degradation
```

1. **Margination** — as flow slows, white cells move from the centre of the stream to the vessel wall.
2. **Rolling** — they tumble along the endothelium, loosely held by adhesion molecules called **selectins**.
3. **Adhesion (pavementing)** — they stick firmly through **integrins** on the leucocytes binding to molecules on the endothelium.
4. **Emigration (transmigration, diapedesis)** — they squeeze between endothelial cells and cross the basement membrane into the tissue. Red cells may escape passively through the damaged wall (diapedesis of red cells).
5. **Chemotaxis** — they move towards the site of injury, attracted by chemicals (bacterial products, complement C5a, leukotriene B4, chemokines such as IL-8).
6. **Phagocytosis** — recognition and attachment (helped by **opsonins** — antibodies and complement C3b coating the microbe), engulfment into a phagosome, which fuses with a lysosome.
7. **Killing** — by oxygen-dependent mechanisms (reactive oxygen species, the "respiratory burst"; myeloperoxidase forming hypochlorite) and oxygen-independent mechanisms (lysozyme, lactoferrin, defensins).

### C. Chemical mediators (in brief)

| Mediator | Source | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Histamine | Mast cells, basophils, platelets | Vasodilatation, increased permeability (first mediator released) |
| Serotonin | Platelets | Permeability (like histamine) |
| Prostaglandins | Cell membranes (cyclo-oxygenase pathway) | Vasodilatation, pain, fever |
| Leukotrienes | Lipoxygenase pathway | Permeability, bronchospasm, chemotaxis (LTB4) |
| Bradykinin | Plasma (kinin system) | Pain, permeability, vasodilatation |
| Complement (C3a, C5a, C3b) | Plasma | Chemotaxis, opsonisation, mast cell release; C5b–C9 lyses microbes |
| Cytokines (IL-1, TNF, IL-6) | Macrophages, lymphocytes | Fever, acute-phase response, endothelial activation |
| Nitric oxide | Endothelium, macrophages | Vasodilatation, killing of microbes |

Anti-inflammatory drugs act on some of these (for example, aspirin and similar drugs block prostaglandin formation; antihistamines block histamine).

### D. Exudate and its types

The fluid that collects in inflamed tissue is an **exudate** — rich in protein and cells (compare transudate in unit 4).

| Type | Content | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Serous | Thin, watery, little protein | Blister of a burn, early pleural effusion |
| Fibrinous | Rich in fibrin | Fibrinous pericarditis ("bread-and-butter" appearance), lobar pneumonia |
| Suppurative (purulent) | Pus: dead neutrophils, bacteria, debris | Abscess, boil, empyema |
| Haemorrhagic | Many red cells | Severe injury of vessels |
| Catarrhal | Mucus on a mucous membrane | Common cold |
| Pseudomembranous | Membrane of fibrin and dead cells | Diphtheria, antibiotic-associated colitis |

- **Abscess** — a localised collection of pus walled off by a pyogenic membrane (boil, liver abscess).
- **Cellulitis** — diffuse spreading inflammation in loose tissue, usually streptococcal.
- **Ulcer** — loss of surface epithelium with inflammation of the base (peptic ulcer, pressure ulcer).

### E. Systemic effects of acute inflammation (acute-phase response)

Caused mainly by cytokines IL-1, TNF and IL-6.

- **Fever** — cytokines raise prostaglandin production in the hypothalamus and reset the body's thermostat.
- **Leucocytosis** — raised white cell count. Neutrophilia in bacterial infection; lymphocytosis in viral infection; eosinophilia in allergy and worm infestation. **Shift to the left** = more young (band) neutrophils. Some infections (typhoid, some viral infections) cause leucopenia.
- **Acute-phase proteins** made by the liver: C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, serum amyloid A. Raised fibrinogen makes red cells clump and settle faster — hence a **raised ESR**.
- Other effects: raised pulse, rigors (shivering), loss of appetite, malaise, sleepiness.
- In severe infection: **sepsis and septic shock** — fall in blood pressure, poor organ perfusion (see unit 4).

### F. Outcomes of acute inflammation

```text
                      ┌─► Resolution (complete return to normal) — mild injury, little damage
 Acute inflammation ──┼─► Abscess / suppuration — pyogenic organisms
                      ├─► Healing by fibrosis (scar) — much tissue destruction
                      └─► Chronic inflammation — cause persists
                          (also: spread to lymph nodes and blood → septicaemia)
```

## 2.4 Chronic inflammation

Chronic inflammation lasts weeks to months or years. Inflammation, tissue destruction and attempts at repair go on **at the same time**.

Causes:

- Persistent infection by organisms that resist killing (tuberculosis, leprosy, syphilis, fungi).
- Prolonged exposure to toxic agents (silica, asbestos, tobacco smoke, cholesterol in atherosclerosis).
- Autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease).
- Repeated attacks of acute inflammation (recurrent cholecystitis).

Features:

- Infiltration by **mononuclear cells** — macrophages, lymphocytes and plasma cells.
- **Tissue destruction** caused by the persisting agent and by the inflammatory cells.
- **Repair** — new small blood vessels (angiogenesis) and **fibrosis** (scar).

### Acute versus chronic inflammation

| Feature | Acute | Chronic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Sudden | Slow, or follows acute |
| Duration | Hours to days | Weeks to years |
| Main cells | Neutrophils | Macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells |
| Vascular change | Marked (exudate, oedema) | Less; new vessel formation |
| Tissue damage | Usually mild, self-limited | Progressive |
| Fibrosis | Little | Marked |
| Signs | Prominent | Less obvious |

### Granulomatous inflammation

A special type of chronic inflammation in which activated macrophages collect to form small nodules called **granulomas**.

```text
            Lymphocytes (outer cuff)
        ┌──────────────────────────────┐
        │   Epithelioid cells          │
        │   (activated macrophages)    │
        │      ┌──────────────┐        │
        │      │  Caseous     │  Giant │
        │      │  necrosis    │  cell  │
        │      │  (centre)    │(Langhans)
        │      └──────────────┘        │
        └──────────────────────────────┘
              Fibroblasts and fibrosis (outermost, later)
     Tubercle: a caseating granuloma of tuberculosis
```

Parts of a granuloma: **epithelioid cells** (modified macrophages), **multinucleated giant cells** (Langhans type in TB — nuclei arranged like a horseshoe at the edge; foreign-body type — nuclei scattered), a collar of **lymphocytes**, and **fibrosis** around it. In TB the centre shows **caseous necrosis**.

Causes of granulomas:

- **Infections**: tuberculosis (caseating), leprosy, syphilis (gumma), fungal infections, cat-scratch disease.
- **Foreign bodies**: sutures, talc, silica.
- **Unknown cause**: sarcoidosis (non-caseating), Crohn's disease.

### Systemic effects of chronic inflammation

- Low-grade fever, weight loss, tiredness, loss of appetite.
- **Anaemia of chronic disease** (iron cannot be used properly).
- Raised ESR; raised immunoglobulins (plasma cells).
- **Secondary (reactive) amyloidosis** — abnormal protein deposited in kidney, liver, spleen after long-standing disease (TB, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic osteomyelitis, bronchiectasis).
- Organ scarring and loss of function (cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis).

## 2.5 Wound healing (repair)

**Repair** is the restoration of injured tissue. It happens in two ways:

1. **Regeneration** — dead cells are replaced by the same type of cell, restoring normal structure. Possible only in tissues whose cells can divide.
2. **Healing by fibrosis (scar formation)** — dead tissue is replaced by connective tissue when regeneration is not possible or damage is extensive.

### Cells by their ability to divide

| Type | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Labile (continuously dividing) | Divide throughout life | Skin epidermis, lining of gut, bone marrow cells |
| Stable (quiescent) | Normally resting but divide when injured | Liver, kidney tubules, pancreas, fibroblasts, smooth muscle |
| Permanent (non-dividing) | Cannot divide after birth | Neurons, cardiac muscle, (most) skeletal muscle |

That is why a heart infarct heals by a scar, while the liver can regenerate.

### Granulation tissue

The key tissue of repair. Pink, soft, granular and bleeds easily. Made of:

- New capillaries (angiogenesis) that give the granular appearance.
- Fibroblasts that make collagen.
- Inflammatory cells (macrophages).
- Oedema fluid.

With time, collagen increases, vessels decrease, and granulation tissue matures into a pale **scar**.

### Phases of wound healing

```text
 Haemostasis (minutes) → Inflammation (days 1–3) → Proliferation (day 3 to ~3 weeks)
                                          → Maturation / remodelling (3 weeks to 1–2 years)
```

1. **Haemostasis** — vessel constriction, platelet plug and clot; the clot forms a scab.
2. **Inflammation** — neutrophils clean bacteria, then macrophages remove debris and release growth factors.
3. **Proliferation** — granulation tissue fills the defect; epithelium grows in from the edges; **wound contraction** by myofibroblasts reduces the size of the wound.
4. **Maturation (remodelling)** — collagen is rearranged and cross-linked; the scar becomes paler and stronger. A scar never regains full strength — it reaches about 70–80% of normal skin strength.

### Healing by first and second intention

| Feature | First (primary) intention | Second (secondary) intention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wound | Clean, edges brought together (surgical incision with sutures) | Large, gaping, edges apart, often infected, tissue lost (ulcer, abscess cavity, burns) |
| Clot and inflammation | Small | Large |
| Granulation tissue | Little | Abundant, fills from the base upward |
| Wound contraction | Minimal | Marked |
| Healing time | Short | Long |
| Scar | Thin, neat | Large, may be deforming |
| Infection risk | Low | Higher |

**Delayed primary closure (third intention)**: a contaminated wound is left open for a few days, cleaned, and then sutured.

Timeline of a clean sutured incision: the gap fills with clot on day 1; neutrophils appear; epithelium bridges the surface by about 24–48 hours; granulation tissue by day 3; collagen bridges the incision by day 5; by the end of the second week inflammation settles and blanching begins; strength increases over months.

### Factors affecting wound healing

| Local factors | Systemic factors |
| --- | --- |
| Infection (the single most important cause of delay) | Age (slower in the elderly) |
| Poor blood supply (atherosclerosis, varicose veins) | Malnutrition — protein, vitamin C (collagen formation), zinc |
| Foreign bodies (dirt, sutures) | Diabetes mellitus |
| Movement and mechanical stress on the wound | Steroid therapy (reduces inflammation and collagen formation) |
| Size, location and type of wound | Anaemia and poor oxygenation |
| Haematoma and dead tissue | Obesity, smoking, jaundice, uraemia, cancer, radiation |
| Pressure (bed sores) | Immunosuppression |

### Complications of wound healing

- **Wound infection** and dehiscence (wound bursting open, often from infection, poor nutrition or raised abdominal pressure).
- **Hypertrophic scar** — raised, red scar that stays within the wound edges.
- **Keloid** — excess collagen that grows **beyond** the wound edges; more common in dark-skinned people; often recurs after removal.
- **Contracture** — scar contraction limits movement, especially across joints after burns.
- **Incisional hernia**, painful scar (neuroma), implantation cyst, rarely malignant change in a long-standing scar or ulcer (Marjolin's ulcer).

### Healing in specific tissues (in brief)

- **Bone** — callus formation (see unit 7).
- **Nerve** — peripheral nerve can regenerate along its sheath slowly; brain and spinal cord form a glial scar.
- **Heart** — infarct replaced by a fibrous scar.
- **Liver** — regeneration if the framework is intact; scarring in cirrhosis.

## 2.6 Application in nursing

- **Assess inflammation**: look for the five cardinal signs; record temperature, pulse and the size of swelling; mark the edge of cellulitis with a pen to see whether it spreads.
- **Fever care**: tepid sponging, fluids, light clothing, rest, monitoring, and prescribed antipyretics; record the fever chart pattern.
- **Laboratory support**: collect blood for total and differential count, ESR and CRP; send pus or swabs for culture **before** the first antibiotic dose when possible.
- **Abscess**: prepare for incision and drainage; follow aseptic dressing technique; observe drainage amount and colour.
- **Wound care**: aseptic technique and hand hygiene; keep the wound clean, moist and protected; observe for redness, warmth, discharge, smell, separation of edges; remove sutures as ordered.
- **Promote healing**: protein- and vitamin C-rich diet, good glycaemic control in diabetes, stop smoking, mobilise early but splint the wound, prevent pressure.
- **Burns**: watch for contractures; position and exercise joints; pressure garments as advised.
- **Health education**: complete prescribed treatment for TB; explain that steroids can mask signs of infection and slow healing.

## Remember

- Cardinal signs: rubor, calor, tumor, dolor, functio laesa.
- Vascular events: brief vasoconstriction → vasodilatation → increased permeability → stasis.
- Cellular events: margination → rolling (selectins) → adhesion (integrins) → emigration → chemotaxis → phagocytosis → killing.
- Histamine is the first mediator; C5a and LTB4 are chemotactic; C3b is an opsonin; IL-1/TNF cause fever.
- Acute = neutrophils; chronic = macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells.
- Granuloma = epithelioid cells + giant cells + lymphocytes ± caseation (TB) — Langhans giant cell.
- Systemic acute: fever, leucocytosis, raised CRP and ESR. Chronic: anaemia, amyloidosis, weight loss.
- Labile, stable, permanent cells — heart and neurons cannot regenerate.
- Granulation tissue = new capillaries + fibroblasts + inflammatory cells.
- First intention: clean sutured wound, thin scar; second intention: open wound, much granulation and contraction.
- Infection is the commonest local cause of delayed healing; vitamin C and protein are needed for collagen.
- Keloid grows beyond the wound margin; hypertrophic scar does not.
