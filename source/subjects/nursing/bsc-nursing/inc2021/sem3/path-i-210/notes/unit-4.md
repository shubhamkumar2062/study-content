# Unit 4: Circulatory disturbances, oedema and body fluids

INC Unit I (fourth part): thrombosis, embolism and shock; disturbance of body fluids and electrolytes — oedema, transudates and exudates. Ischaemia and infarction are explained here too, because thrombosis and embolism lead to them and unit 5 builds on them.

## 4.1 Normal haemostasis (background)

Blood stays fluid inside vessels and clots quickly when a vessel is cut. This balance depends on three things:

1. **Vessel wall (endothelium)** — normally smooth and anti-clotting; when injured it exposes collagen and tissue factor.
2. **Platelets** — stick to exposed collagen (adhesion, helped by von Willebrand factor), release ADP and thromboxane A2, clump together (aggregation) and form a **platelet plug**.
3. **Coagulation cascade** — clotting factors in plasma act in a chain to convert **fibrinogen → fibrin**, which strengthens the plug.

```text
 Extrinsic pathway (tissue factor, factor VII) ──┐
                                                 ├─► Factor X → Xa ─► Prothrombin → THROMBIN
 Intrinsic pathway (XII, XI, IX, VIII) ──────────┘                       │
                                                         Fibrinogen ─────┴─► FIBRIN (clot)
 PT tests the extrinsic + common pathway; APTT tests the intrinsic + common pathway.
 Fibrinolysis: plasminogen → plasmin dissolves fibrin.
```

Calcium (factor IV) and vitamin K (needed by the liver to make factors II, VII, IX and X) are essential.

## 4.2 Thrombosis

**Thrombosis** is the formation of a solid mass (**thrombus**) from the constituents of blood **inside a blood vessel or the heart during life**.

A thrombus is different from a **blood clot**, which forms outside the body or after death:

| Feature | Thrombus | Post-mortem clot / clot in a tube |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formed | In flowing blood during life | In still blood |
| Attachment | Attached to the vessel wall | Not attached |
| Consistency | Firm, dry, friable | Soft, rubbery, moist |
| Surface | Rough, with lines of Zahn | Smooth, shiny |
| Colour | Grey-red, layered | "Chicken fat" yellow top and dark red "currant jelly" below |

### Virchow's triad (causes)

```text
                 1. Endothelial injury
                       /\
                      /  \
                     / THROMBOSIS
                    /      \
 2. Abnormal blood flow ──── 3. Hypercoagulability
  (stasis, turbulence)        (blood clots more easily)
```

1. **Endothelial injury** — the most important factor in the heart and arteries: atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction (damaged endocardium), inflamed valves, vasculitis, hypertension, smoking, trauma, cannulas.
2. **Abnormal blood flow**:
   - **Stasis** (slow flow) — the main factor in veins: prolonged bed rest, long surgery or flights, heart failure, varicose veins, immobilised limbs, pregnancy (pressure on pelvic veins), atrial fibrillation (stasis in the atrium), aneurysms.
   - **Turbulence** — at branches, over atheromatous plaques, around damaged valves.
3. **Hypercoagulability** — inherited (factor V Leiden, protein C or S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency) or acquired (pregnancy and after delivery, oral contraceptives with oestrogen, cancer, major surgery, trauma and burns, dehydration, obesity, smoking, nephrotic syndrome, antiphospholipid antibodies).

### Morphology

- **Lines of Zahn** — alternating pale layers of platelets and fibrin and dark layers of red cells, seen in thrombi formed in flowing blood.
- **Arterial thrombi** are pale (white), rich in platelets, usually block the vessel (occlusive), and form over atheroma (coronary, cerebral, femoral arteries).
- **Venous thrombi** (phlebothrombosis) are red, rich in red cells, long and cast-like, mostly in the deep veins of the legs (**deep vein thrombosis, DVT**).
- **Cardiac (mural) thrombi** form on the wall of a chamber after MI or in a fibrillating atrium; **vegetations** form on valves in infective endocarditis.

### Fate of a thrombus

```text
             ┌─► Propagation — grows longer, may block more of the vessel
 Thrombus ───┼─► Embolisation — pieces break off and travel (most dangerous)
             ├─► Dissolution (lysis) — fibrinolysis removes a fresh thrombus
             └─► Organisation and recanalisation — fibroblasts and new capillaries
                 grow in; new channels may restore some flow
```

### Effects

- Arterial thrombosis → ischaemia and **infarction** (heart attack, stroke, gangrene of a limb).
- Venous thrombosis → congestion and oedema of the drained part (swollen painful calf in DVT) and **pulmonary embolism**.

## 4.3 Embolism

An **embolus** is a detached solid, liquid or gaseous mass carried by the blood to a site away from its origin, where it blocks a vessel. **Embolism** is the process. About 99% of emboli are pieces of thrombus (**thromboembolism**).

| Type | Source | Result / example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pulmonary thromboembolism | DVT of leg or pelvic veins → right heart → pulmonary artery | Small: breathlessness, chest pain, cough with blood; large ("saddle" at the bifurcation): sudden collapse and death |
| Systemic (arterial) thromboembolism | Heart (mural thrombus after MI, left atrium in atrial fibrillation, vegetations), atheromatous aorta | Stroke, gangrene of a leg, infarcts of kidney, spleen, bowel |
| Fat embolism | Fracture of long bones (femur), crush injury, extensive burns | 1–3 days after injury: breathlessness, confusion, pin-point skin haemorrhages (petechiae) on chest and axilla |
| Air (gas) embolism | Air enters veins — neck or chest injury, IV lines, during delivery or abortion; decompression sickness in divers (nitrogen bubbles) | Frothy blockage of the right heart or pulmonary vessels; a large amount can cause death |
| Amniotic fluid embolism | Amniotic fluid enters the mother's circulation during labour or delivery | Sudden breathlessness, shock, bleeding (DIC); high death rate |
| Tumour embolism | Cancer cells in vessels | Metastasis |
| Septic embolism | Infected vegetations or thrombi | Abscesses in organs |
| Foreign bodies | Catheter fragments, talc in drug users | Granulomas |

**Paradoxical embolism** — a venous embolus crosses to the arterial side through a hole in the heart (patent foramen ovale) and causes a stroke.

## 4.4 Ischaemia and infarction

- **Ischaemia** — inadequate blood supply to a tissue. Causes damage through lack of oxygen and nutrients and build-up of wastes.
- **Infarction** — death (necrosis) of tissue due to loss of blood supply; the dead area is an **infarct**. Most are caused by thrombosis or embolism of an artery.

| Feature | Pale (anaemic, white) infarct | Red (haemorrhagic) infarct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Solid organs with end-arteries: heart, kidney, spleen | Loose tissue, organs with double blood supply or venous blockage: lung, intestine, ovarian/testicular torsion |
| Shape | Wedge-shaped, apex towards the blocked vessel | Wedge-shaped (lung) or segmental (bowel) |
| Necrosis | Coagulative (liquefactive in the brain) | Coagulative with haemorrhage |

Factors deciding whether infarction occurs: type of blood supply (end-artery or collateral), speed of blockage (slow allows collaterals), sensitivity of tissue to hypoxia (brain neurons die in 3–5 minutes; heart muscle in 20–40 minutes), and oxygen content of blood (anaemia worsens damage).

## 4.5 Shock

**Shock** is a state of **circulatory failure** leading to **inadequate perfusion of tissues**, cellular hypoxia and, if uncorrected, organ damage and death. Low blood pressure is common but not essential early on.

### Types and causes

| Type | Mechanism | Causes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypovolaemic | Loss of blood or fluid volume | Haemorrhage (trauma, GI bleed, postpartum), burns (plasma loss), severe vomiting and diarrhoea (cholera), excess urine |
| Cardiogenic | Pump failure | Myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, severe heart failure, cardiac tamponade, massive pulmonary embolism (obstructive) |
| Septic | Microbial products → widespread vasodilatation, leaky vessels, poor oxygen use by cells | Gram-negative and gram-positive bacterial infection, fungal infection |
| Anaphylactic | Severe allergic (IgE) reaction → histamine → vasodilatation, leaky vessels, bronchospasm | Drugs (penicillin), insect stings, foods, blood transfusion |
| Neurogenic | Loss of sympathetic tone → vasodilatation | Spinal cord injury, spinal anaesthesia, severe pain or fright (vasovagal fainting) |

Some books group cardiogenic and obstructive separately, and septic, anaphylactic and neurogenic together as **distributive** shock.

### Pathogenesis and stages

```text
 Fall in cardiac output or effective blood volume
        │
        ▼
 Stage 1 — NON-PROGRESSIVE (compensated)
   Baroreceptors → sympathetic activity, catecholamines, renin–angiotensin, ADH
   → fast pulse, vasoconstriction (cold pale skin), less urine; vital organs protected
        │  (if not corrected)
        ▼
 Stage 2 — PROGRESSIVE (decompensated)
   Tissue hypoxia → anaerobic metabolism → LACTIC ACIDOSIS → arterioles dilate,
   blood pools; fall in BP, confusion, low urine output, organ damage begins
        │
        ▼
 Stage 3 — IRREVERSIBLE
   Severe cell injury, lysosomal enzymes, DIC, multiple organ failure
   (kidney — acute tubular necrosis; lung — ARDS; brain; heart; gut; liver) → death
```

Septic shock is an exception at first: the skin is **warm and flushed** (vasodilatation), with fever, before it becomes cold later.

### Clinical and laboratory features

- Rapid weak (thready) pulse, low blood pressure, rapid shallow breathing.
- Cold, clammy, pale skin (except early septic shock); delayed capillary refill.
- Restlessness, anxiety, confusion, later unconsciousness.
- Oliguria (reduced urine output).
- Lab: raised lactate, metabolic acidosis on blood gases, raised urea and creatinine, falling haemoglobin in haemorrhage (after a few hours), raised white cell count in sepsis, positive blood cultures, abnormal clotting tests (DIC).

### Morphological changes in organs

Kidney — acute tubular necrosis; lungs — "shock lung" (diffuse alveolar damage, ARDS); heart — small areas of necrosis; brain — ischaemic damage; gut — haemorrhagic patches; adrenal — haemorrhage (especially in meningococcal septicaemia); liver — central fatty change and necrosis.

## 4.6 Oedema

**Oedema (edema)** is the abnormal accumulation of fluid in the **interstitial tissue spaces** or body cavities.

- Fluid in body cavities has special names: **ascites** (peritoneal cavity), **hydrothorax / pleural effusion** (pleural cavity), **hydropericardium** (pericardial cavity).
- **Anasarca** — severe generalised oedema of the whole body.
- **Pitting oedema** — pressing with a finger leaves a dent (common in the legs); **non-pitting** in lymphoedema and myxoedema.

### Normal fluid exchange (Starling forces)

```text
 Arterial end of capillary                       Venous end of capillary
 Hydrostatic pressure HIGH  ─► fluid OUT         Hydrostatic pressure LOW
 Oncotic pressure (albumin) ─► pulls fluid IN    Oncotic pressure ─► fluid IN
 A little extra fluid left in the tissue is drained by LYMPHATICS
```

### Mechanisms and causes

| Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure | Congestive heart failure (generalised, legs and back), deep vein thrombosis (local), cirrhosis (portal hypertension → ascites), pregnancy, fluid overload |
| 2. Reduced plasma oncotic pressure (low albumin) | Nephrotic syndrome (protein lost in urine — puffy face in the morning), cirrhosis (less albumin made), malnutrition (kwashiorkor), protein-losing gut disease |
| 3. Lymphatic obstruction (lymphoedema) | Filariasis (elephantiasis), cancer spread to nodes, removal of axillary nodes after breast surgery, radiation |
| 4. Increased capillary permeability | Inflammation, burns, allergy (angio-oedema), sepsis |
| 5. Sodium and water retention | Kidney failure, acute glomerulonephritis, excess steroids; also part of heart failure (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone activation) |

Special types:

- **Cardiac oedema** — dependent (ankles when standing, sacrum when lying), pitting, worse in the evening.
- **Renal oedema** — around the eyes and face, noticed in the morning; generalised in nephrotic syndrome.
- **Pulmonary oedema** — fluid in alveoli (left heart failure, fluid overload); breathlessness, frothy pink sputum, crackles in the lungs. An emergency.
- **Cerebral oedema** — after head injury, infection, tumour; raises intracranial pressure.

## 4.7 Transudate and exudate

| Feature | Transudate | Exudate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Imbalance of hydrostatic/oncotic pressure; vessels intact | Inflammation; vessels damaged and leaky |
| Example | Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome | Pneumonia, TB, cancer, peritonitis, abscess |
| Appearance | Clear, pale yellow, watery | Cloudy, turbid, may be purulent or blood-stained |
| Protein | Low (below about 3 g/dL) | High (above about 3 g/dL) |
| Specific gravity | Low (below 1.012–1.015) | High (above 1.018–1.020) |
| Cells | Few, mainly mesothelial cells | Many — neutrophils, lymphocytes, red cells, organisms |
| Clotting on standing | Does not clot | May clot (contains fibrinogen) |
| LDH | Low | High |
| Glucose | Same as blood | May be low (infection) |

For pleural fluid, **Light's criteria** are used: exudate if fluid/serum protein ratio is above 0.5, fluid/serum LDH ratio above 0.6, or fluid LDH above two-thirds of the upper normal serum limit.

## 4.8 Disturbances of water and electrolytes (in brief)

| Disturbance | Common causes | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dehydration (volume depletion) | Vomiting, diarrhoea, poor intake, burns, fever, diabetes | Thirst, dry tongue, sunken eyes, poor skin turgor, low urine, fast pulse, low BP |
| Overhydration (water excess) | Excess IV fluids, kidney failure, heart failure | Oedema, raised BP and JVP, breathlessness, low sodium (confusion) |
| Hyponatraemia (low Na+) | Diarrhoea, diuretics, excess water, SIADH | Confusion, fits, weakness |
| Hypernatraemia (high Na+) | Water loss, poor intake (elderly, infants) | Thirst, irritability, confusion |
| Hypokalaemia (low K+) | Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics | Muscle weakness, cramps, ileus, arrhythmias |
| Hyperkalaemia (high K+) | Kidney failure, tissue breakdown, haemolysed sample (false) | Arrhythmias, cardiac arrest |

## 4.9 Application in nursing

- **Prevent DVT**: early ambulation after surgery and delivery, leg and ankle exercises, adequate hydration, avoiding pillows under the knees, compression stockings and prescribed anticoagulants. Assess calves daily for swelling, warmth, tenderness and difference in size.
- **Recognise pulmonary embolism**: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, rapid pulse, falling SpO2 — call for help, sit the patient up, give oxygen as ordered.
- **Fat embolism**: handle long-bone fractures gently, immobilise early; watch for breathlessness, confusion and petechiae in the first three days.
- **Air embolism**: prime all IV tubing, close stopcocks, use the head-down left-side position if air embolism is suspected while removing central lines.
- **Shock**: monitor pulse, BP, respiratory rate, SpO2, skin colour and temperature, level of consciousness and hourly urine output; lie the patient flat with legs raised (unless head or chest injury or breathlessness); keep warm; secure IV access; collect blood for grouping and cross-matching, lactate, culture.
- **Oedema**: daily weight at the same time with the same scale, intake–output chart, measure abdominal girth in ascites, elevate oedematous limbs, protect the stretched skin from pressure, follow sodium and fluid restriction as advised.
- **Body fluid samples**: label pleural or ascitic fluid clearly; send for protein, glucose, LDH, cell count, Gram stain and culture, and cytology as ordered.

## Remember

- Virchow's triad: endothelial injury + abnormal flow + hypercoagulability.
- Arterial thrombi pale, platelet-rich, over atheroma; venous thrombi red, in leg veins (stasis).
- Lines of Zahn = thrombus formed in flowing blood.
- Fate of thrombus: propagation, embolisation, lysis, organisation/recanalisation.
- Most emboli are thromboemboli; DVT → pulmonary embolism. Fat embolism after femur fracture (petechiae, confusion, breathlessness).
- Pale infarcts: heart, kidney, spleen; red infarcts: lung, bowel.
- Shock types: hypovolaemic, cardiogenic, septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic; stages: compensated → progressive → irreversible.
- Lactic acidosis marks progressive shock; ATN and ARDS are typical organ damage.
- Oedema causes: ↑ hydrostatic, ↓ oncotic (low albumin), lymphatic block, ↑ permeability, Na+ retention.
- Transudate: low protein, low SG, few cells; exudate: high protein, high SG, many cells, clots.
- Cardiac oedema dependent; renal oedema periorbital in the morning.
