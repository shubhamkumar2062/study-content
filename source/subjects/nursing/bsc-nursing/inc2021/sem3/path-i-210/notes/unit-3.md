# Unit 3: Neoplasia

INC Unit I (third part): nomenclature of tumours, normal versus cancer cells, benign and malignant tumours, carcinoma in situ, and tumour metastasis — general mechanism, routes of spread with examples of each. Causes, grading, staging and the nurse's role are added because they are needed to answer questions on this topic.

## 3.1 Definitions

- **Neoplasia** means "new growth". A **neoplasm (tumour)** is an abnormal mass of tissue whose growth is excessive, uncoordinated with the normal tissue, and continues even after the stimulus that started it has stopped.
- **Tumour** originally meant any swelling; today it is used for a neoplasm.
- **Cancer** is the common term for all **malignant** tumours (Latin *cancer* = crab, because the tumour sends claw-like extensions into tissue).
- **Oncology** is the study of tumours.

How a neoplasm differs from hyperplasia: hyperplasia is a controlled response that stops when the stimulus stops; a neoplasm is **autonomous** — it keeps growing on its own.

Every tumour has two parts:

1. **Parenchyma** — the tumour cells themselves; they decide the behaviour and the name.
2. **Stroma** — supporting connective tissue and blood vessels. A tumour with much fibrous stroma is hard (scirrhous), as in many breast cancers.

## 3.2 Nomenclature (naming tumours)

The name depends on the **tissue of origin** and whether the tumour is **benign or malignant**.

| Tissue of origin | Benign (suffix "-oma") | Malignant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface epithelium (squamous) | Squamous cell papilloma | Squamous cell carcinoma |
| Glandular epithelium | Adenoma | Adenocarcinoma |
| Fibrous tissue | Fibroma | Fibrosarcoma |
| Fat | Lipoma | Liposarcoma |
| Cartilage | Chondroma | Chondrosarcoma |
| Bone | Osteoma | Osteosarcoma |
| Smooth muscle | Leiomyoma (fibroid of uterus) | Leiomyosarcoma |
| Skeletal muscle | Rhabdomyoma | Rhabdomyosarcoma |
| Blood vessels | Haemangioma | Angiosarcoma |
| Melanocytes | Naevus (mole) | Malignant melanoma |
| Blood-forming cells | — | Leukaemia, lymphoma |
| Germ cells | Mature teratoma (dermoid cyst) | Seminoma, immature teratoma |

Rules to remember:

- **Carcinoma** = malignant tumour of **epithelial** origin (skin, linings, glands). This is the most common cancer in adults.
- **Sarcoma** = malignant tumour of **mesenchymal** (connective tissue) origin — bone, cartilage, fat, muscle, vessels.
- **Exceptions** — some names end in "-oma" but are malignant: **lymphoma, melanoma, seminoma, hepatoma (hepatocellular carcinoma), mesothelioma, glioma**.
- Some "-omas" are not tumours at all: **haematoma** (collection of blood), **granuloma** (inflammation), **hamartoma** (disorganised mass of normal tissue native to the site, such as some lung nodules).
- **Choristoma** — normal tissue in an abnormal place (pancreatic tissue in the stomach wall).
- **Teratoma** — a tumour containing tissues from all three germ layers (skin, hair, teeth, cartilage), usually in the ovary or testis.
- **Blastoma** — tumours of embryonic tissue, mostly in children: retinoblastoma, nephroblastoma (Wilms' tumour), neuroblastoma.

## 3.3 Normal cell versus cancer cell

| Feature | Normal cell | Cancer cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth control | Divides only when needed; stops on contact with neighbours (contact inhibition) | Divides without control; no contact inhibition |
| Differentiation | Mature, specialised, does its normal job | Poorly differentiated or undifferentiated; loses normal function |
| Size and shape | Uniform | Varies (pleomorphism) |
| Nucleus | Normal size; nuclear : cytoplasmic ratio about 1 : 4–1 : 6 | Large, dark (hyperchromatic), N : C ratio towards 1 : 1, prominent nucleoli |
| Mitosis | Few, normal | Many, some abnormal (tripolar, bizarre) |
| Lifespan | Limited; undergoes apoptosis | Escapes apoptosis; effectively immortal (telomerase active) |
| Adhesion | Cells stick together | Less adhesion; cells break away |
| Invasion | Stays within its basement membrane | Breaks through basement membrane, invades, spreads |
| Chromosomes | Normal number | Often abnormal (aneuploidy) |
| Blood supply | Normal | Stimulates new vessels (angiogenesis) |
| Immune recognition | Recognised as self | May show new antigens but evades the immune system |

**Anaplasia** means lack of differentiation — a hallmark of malignancy. The less a tumour cell resembles its parent cell, the more aggressive it usually is.

## 3.4 Benign versus malignant tumours

| Feature | Benign | Malignant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Differentiation | Well differentiated, resembles parent tissue | Variable; may be anaplastic |
| Rate of growth | Slow; may stop | Usually rapid |
| Mode of growth | Expansion, pushes tissue aside | Infiltration and invasion of surrounding tissue |
| Capsule | Usually encapsulated | Not encapsulated, irregular edges |
| Mitoses | Few, normal | Many, abnormal |
| Necrosis and haemorrhage | Rare | Common (growth outruns blood supply) |
| Metastasis | **Never** | **Common** — the most reliable sign of malignancy |
| Recurrence after removal | Rare | Common |
| Effect on the patient | Usually local pressure; rarely fatal unless in a vital site (brain) or producing hormones | Cachexia, organ failure, often fatal if untreated |
| Example | Lipoma, fibroadenoma breast, leiomyoma uterus | Carcinoma breast, osteosarcoma, leukaemia |

## 3.5 Dysplasia and carcinoma in situ

```text
 Normal epithelium → Metaplasia → Mild dysplasia → Moderate → Severe dysplasia
                                    (lower 1/3)     (2/3)        (full thickness)
                                                                   │
                                                  Carcinoma in situ (CIS)
                                                  basement membrane INTACT
                                                                   │
                                                  Invasive carcinoma
                                                  basement membrane BREACHED → can spread
```

- **Dysplasia** — disordered growth of epithelium with abnormal cells (variation in size and shape, large dark nuclei, more mitoses, loss of layering). Can regress if the cause is removed.
- **Carcinoma in situ (CIS)** — malignant-looking cells involve the **whole thickness** of the epithelium but have **not broken through the basement membrane**. Because there are no blood vessels or lymphatics in the epithelium, CIS **cannot metastasise** and is curable by complete removal.
- Common sites: cervix (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, CIN III), skin, oral cavity, breast ducts (ductal CIS), bronchus.
- Once the cells invade through the basement membrane, it is **invasive (frank) carcinoma**.

This is the scientific basis of **screening**: detecting dysplasia or CIS (for example by a Pap smear or visual inspection with acetic acid for the cervix) allows cure before invasion.

## 3.6 Causes (carcinogenesis) — in brief

Cancer results from accumulated **mutations** in genes that control cell growth:

- **Oncogenes** (activated growth-promoting genes) — act like a stuck accelerator.
- **Tumour suppressor genes** lost or inactivated (p53, RB) — act like failed brakes.
- Genes of DNA repair and apoptosis.

Carcinogens (agents that cause cancer):

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Chemical | Tobacco smoke and chewing tobacco (lung, mouth, bladder), aflatoxin from mouldy grains (liver), asbestos (mesothelioma), benzene (leukaemia), aniline dyes (bladder), alcohol |
| Physical | Ultraviolet light (skin), ionising radiation (leukaemia, thyroid) |
| Viral | Human papillomavirus (cervix, oral), hepatitis B and C (liver), Epstein–Barr virus (Burkitt's lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), HIV (Kaposi sarcoma, lymphomas), HTLV-1 |
| Bacterial | *Helicobacter pylori* (stomach cancer, gastric lymphoma) |
| Hereditary | BRCA1/BRCA2 (breast, ovary), familial polyposis (colon), retinoblastoma |
| Hormonal | Unopposed oestrogen (endometrium) |

Carcinogenesis occurs in steps: **initiation** (irreversible DNA damage) → **promotion** (repeated stimulation of the damaged cells to divide) → **progression** (more mutations, invasion and spread).

## 3.7 Tumour metastasis

**Metastasis** is the spread of tumour cells from the primary site to distant tissues, where they form secondary tumours not connected to the original one. It is the single most reliable feature of malignancy and the main cause of cancer death.

### General mechanism (invasion–metastasis cascade)

```text
 1. Detachment: cancer cells lose adhesion molecules (E-cadherin) and separate
 2. Invasion: enzymes (collagenases, metalloproteinases) digest the basement membrane
    and the matrix; cells move into the stroma
 3. Intravasation: cells enter lymphatics or blood vessels
 4. Survival in circulation: cells travel alone or as clumps with platelets
 5. Arrest and extravasation: cells stick to the vessel wall of a distant organ and leave the vessel
 6. Colonisation: cells multiply and stimulate new blood vessels (angiogenesis)
    → secondary tumour (metastasis)
```

Most cancer cells that enter the blood die; only a few succeed. Some organs are common targets because of blood flow pattern (the lungs and liver filter blood) and "soil" suitability.

### Routes of spread

| Route | How | Typical examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Direct (local) invasion | Grows into neighbouring tissues | Cervical carcinoma into bladder and rectum; oral cancer into the jaw |
| 2. Lymphatic spread | Cells enter lymphatics and reach regional lymph nodes, which become enlarged, hard and matted | **Carcinomas** usually: breast cancer → axillary nodes; stomach cancer → left supraclavicular node (Virchow's node, Troisier's sign); lung cancer → hilar nodes |
| 3. Haematogenous (blood) spread | Cells enter veins (thin walls) | **Sarcomas** usually; also some carcinomas. Via **portal vein → liver** (colorectal, stomach, pancreas cancers); via **systemic veins → lungs** (sarcomas, kidney cancer); via vertebral veins → spine (prostate cancer); renal cell carcinoma grows into the renal vein; hepatocellular carcinoma into hepatic veins |
| 4. Transcoelomic spread (across body cavities) | Cells fall into the peritoneal, pleural or pericardial cavity and seed its surface | Ovarian cancer spreads throughout the peritoneum (malignant ascites); gastric cancer to both ovaries (**Krukenberg tumour**); lung cancer → pleural effusion |
| 5. Implantation | Cells carried mechanically | Along a needle biopsy tract, surgical wound, instruments; along the CSF (medulloblastoma) |

Common sites of metastasis: **lymph nodes, liver, lungs, bone, brain**. Cancers that commonly spread to bone: breast, prostate, lung, kidney, thyroid. Prostate cancer deposits in bone are typically bone-forming (osteoblastic); most others destroy bone (osteolytic) and raise blood calcium.

## 3.8 Grading and staging

- **Grading** is based on the microscopic appearance — how differentiated the cells are and how many mitoses. Grade I (well differentiated) to Grade III or IV (poorly differentiated/anaplastic). A higher grade usually means more aggressive behaviour.
- **Staging** describes how far the cancer has spread; it predicts outcome better than grade. The common system is **TNM**:
  - **T** — size and local extent of the primary tumour (T0–T4; Tis = carcinoma in situ).
  - **N** — regional lymph node involvement (N0–N3).
  - **M** — distant metastasis (M0 absent, M1 present).

## 3.9 Effects of tumours on the patient

- **Local effects**: pressure (brain tumour raises intracranial pressure), obstruction (bowel, bile duct causing jaundice, bronchus causing collapse), ulceration and bleeding, pain, pathological fracture.
- **Cancer cachexia**: loss of appetite, weight and muscle, weakness — caused by cytokines (TNF) and tumour products.
- **Anaemia**: chronic blood loss, marrow replacement, poor nutrition.
- **Infections**: reduced immunity, especially in leukaemia and during chemotherapy.
- **Hormone production**: functional tumours (insulinoma causing low blood sugar).
- **Paraneoplastic syndromes**: effects not explained by local or metastatic spread — high calcium, Cushing's syndrome from lung cancer, increased clotting (migratory thrombophlebitis).

## 3.10 Laboratory diagnosis of tumours (overview)

- **Histopathology** — biopsy (incisional, excisional, needle core); the definitive diagnosis. Fixed in 10% formalin.
- **Frozen section** — rapid report during surgery.
- **Cytology** — exfoliative (Pap smear, sputum, fluids) and fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC).
- **Immunohistochemistry** — identifies cell type using antibodies.
- **Tumour markers** in blood (help monitoring, rarely diagnose alone): alpha-fetoprotein (liver cancer, germ-cell tumours), CEA (colorectal), PSA (prostate), CA-125 (ovary), beta-hCG (choriocarcinoma, testicular tumours).
- Imaging (X-ray, ultrasound, CT, MRI) and endoscopy locate the tumour.

## 3.11 Application in nursing

- **Early warning signs of cancer** to teach the public (commonly summarised as "CAUTION"): Change in bowel or bladder habits; A sore that does not heal; Unusual bleeding or discharge; Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere; Indigestion or difficulty swallowing; Obvious change in a wart or mole; Nagging cough or hoarseness.
- **Prevention**: stop tobacco in all forms and limit alcohol; healthy diet and weight; HPV and hepatitis B vaccination as per national schedules; protection from occupational carcinogens.
- **Screening**: oral visual examination, breast self- and clinical examination, cervical screening; India's national programme for non-communicable diseases includes screening for oral, breast and cervical cancer — check the current guidelines of the Ministry of Health for age groups.
- **Specimen handling**: biopsy into adequate formalin (about ten times the tissue volume), labelled with name, site and date; send with a request form and relevant history.
- **Patient care**: emotional support at diagnosis; nutrition for cachexia; infection prevention in low white cell counts; pain assessment; care of pathological fracture risk in bone metastasis.
- **Observation for complications**: signs of raised intracranial pressure, obstruction, bleeding, and confusion or constipation from high calcium.

## Remember

- Neoplasm = autonomous, excessive growth that continues after the stimulus stops.
- Benign "-oma"; malignant epithelial = carcinoma; malignant connective tissue = sarcoma.
- Malignant despite "-oma": lymphoma, melanoma, seminoma, hepatoma, mesothelioma, glioma.
- Hamartoma = disorganised native tissue; choristoma = normal tissue in the wrong place.
- Anaplasia = loss of differentiation; pleomorphism, hyperchromasia, high N : C ratio, abnormal mitoses.
- Metastasis is the most reliable sign of malignancy; benign tumours never metastasise.
- CIS: full-thickness atypia, basement membrane intact, cannot metastasise.
- Carcinomas → lymphatics; sarcomas → blood.
- Gastrointestinal cancers → liver (portal vein); sarcomas → lungs.
- Krukenberg tumour = ovarian metastasis from stomach; Virchow's node = left supraclavicular.
- Grade = microscopic differentiation; stage = spread (TNM). Stage matters more for prognosis.
