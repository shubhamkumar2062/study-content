## 4.1 Immunity: definition and classification

**Immunity** is the resistance of the body to infection and to foreign substances. The **immune system** recognises "self" from "non-self" and removes what is foreign.

```text
                         IMMUNITY
            ┌───────────────┴────────────────┐
     INNATE (natural, non-specific)    ACQUIRED (adaptive, specific)
     – present from birth              ┌──────────┴──────────┐
     – species, racial, individual   ACTIVE               PASSIVE
                                     (own antibodies)     (ready-made antibodies)
                                   ┌──────┴─────┐       ┌──────┴───────┐
                                Natural    Artificial  Natural     Artificial
                                (infection) (vaccine)  (mother →   (immunoglobulin,
                                                        baby)       antiserum)
```

### Innate (non-specific) immunity

Present from birth; acts quickly; the same against every organism; no memory.

| Line of defence | Components |
| --- | --- |
| First line — barriers | Intact skin; mucous membranes with mucus; cilia of respiratory tract; cough and sneeze reflexes; tears and saliva (lysozyme); acid of the stomach; acid pH of vagina; flushing by urine; normal flora |
| Second line — internal non-specific defences | **Phagocytes** (neutrophils, macrophages); **natural killer (NK) cells**; **complement** proteins; **interferons** (block virus spread); **inflammation** (redness, heat, swelling, pain, loss of function); **fever** |

### Acquired (adaptive, specific) immunity

Develops after exposure to an antigen; specific to that antigen; has **memory**, so the second response is faster and stronger.

| Feature | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antibodies made by | The person's own body | Another person or animal; given ready-made |
| Onset | Slow (days to weeks; a lag period) | Immediate |
| Duration | Long (months to years, often life-long) | Short (weeks to a few months) |
| Memory | Yes; booster responses | No |
| Natural examples | Recovery from measles or chickenpox | Maternal IgG across the placenta; IgA in colostrum and breast milk |
| Artificial examples | Vaccines (BCG, measles, tetanus toxoid) | Immunoglobulins and antisera: rabies immunoglobulin, hepatitis B immunoglobulin, anti-snake venom, anti-tetanus immunoglobulin |
| Use | Prevention | Immediate protection after exposure, or when there is no time to build immunity |

**Humoral versus cell-mediated immunity**

| | Humoral | Cell-mediated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cells | B lymphocytes → plasma cells | T lymphocytes (helper CD4, cytotoxic CD8) |
| Product | Antibodies in blood and secretions | Activated cells; cytokines |
| Main targets | Bacteria and toxins in body fluids, viruses before they enter cells | Viruses inside cells, TB, fungi, cancer cells, grafts |
| Example | Antitoxin neutralises tetanus toxin | Mantoux reaction; graft rejection |

**Primary and secondary response**: on first exposure antibodies appear after a lag of about one to two weeks, mainly **IgM**, in low amounts. On second exposure (or a booster) the response is quicker, larger and mainly **IgG** — the basis of booster doses.

## 4.2 Antigen and antibody

- **Antigen**: any substance that the body recognises as foreign and that provokes a specific immune response (proteins, polysaccharides; parts of microbes, toxins, pollen, transplanted tissue). The small part that binds to an antibody is the **epitope** (antigenic determinant). A **hapten** is a small molecule that becomes antigenic only when joined to a protein carrier (e.g. some drugs such as penicillin).
- **Antibody (immunoglobulin, Ig)**: a protein (gamma globulin) made by plasma cells in response to an antigen, which combines specifically with it.

**Antigen–antibody reaction**: the antibody binds the antigen like a lock and key; the binding is specific, reversible and needs the right proportion of both. Results: neutralisation of toxins and viruses, clumping (agglutination), precipitation, opsonisation (coating that helps phagocytosis), and activation of complement that bursts the cell.

## 4.3 Immunoglobulins: structure, types and properties

```text
        antigen-binding sites (variable regions)
           \  /             \  /
            \/               \/
            ||  light chain  ||
            ||   (L)         ||
             \\             //
              \\  hinge    //   ← disulphide bonds join chains
               ||         ||
               || heavy   ||
               || chains  ||
               ||   (H)   ||
              Fc (constant) region — binds cells and complement
```

- Basic unit: **four polypeptide chains** — two identical **heavy** and two identical **light** chains, held by disulphide bonds, in a Y shape.
- **Fab** (fragment antigen-binding) — the two arms; the tips (variable regions) bind antigen.
- **Fc** (fragment crystallisable) — the stem; decides what the antibody does (crossing the placenta, binding phagocytes, mast cells, complement).
- Five classes, named by the heavy chain: IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE.

| Class | Structure | Approximate share of serum Ig | Key properties |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **IgG** | Monomer | Largest (about 75–80%) | Main antibody of the secondary response; **only one that crosses the placenta** (protects the newborn); neutralises toxins; longest half-life (about 3 weeks) |
| **IgA** | Monomer in serum; **dimer** in secretions (secretory IgA) | About 10–15% | Protects mucous surfaces (gut, respiratory, urinary); present in **colostrum**, breast milk, saliva, tears |
| **IgM** | **Pentamer** — the largest | About 5–10% | **First antibody** made in an infection; its presence means **recent infection** (e.g. IgM anti-HAV, dengue IgM); good at agglutination and complement activation; does not cross placenta, so IgM in a newborn means infection of the baby |
| **IgD** | Monomer | Less than 1% | On the surface of B cells as an antigen receptor |
| **IgE** | Monomer | Smallest amount | Binds mast cells and basophils; causes **allergy and anaphylaxis** (type I hypersensitivity); defence against worms |

Memory aid: **IgM = first ("M" for "make first"), IgG = goes across the placenta, IgA = in secretions, IgE = allergy, IgD = B-cell receptor.**

## 4.4 Hypersensitivity reactions

**Hypersensitivity** is an exaggerated or inappropriate immune response that damages the body. Gell and Coombs classification:

| Type | Mechanism | Onset | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I — Immediate (anaphylactic)** | Allergen binds IgE on mast cells → release of histamine and other mediators | Minutes | **Anaphylaxis** (to drugs, insect stings, foods), asthma, hay fever, urticaria |
| **II — Cytotoxic** | IgG or IgM against antigens on cell surfaces → cell destruction with complement | Minutes to hours | **Mismatched blood transfusion reaction**, haemolytic disease of the newborn (Rh incompatibility), autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, drug-induced cytopenias |
| **III — Immune complex** | Antigen–antibody complexes deposit in vessel walls, kidneys, joints → inflammation | Hours to days | Serum sickness, Arthus reaction, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, systemic lupus erythematosus |
| **IV — Delayed (cell-mediated)** | Sensitised T cells release cytokines | 24–72 hours | **Mantoux (tuberculin) test**, contact dermatitis (nickel, latex, plants), graft rejection |

**Nursing in hypersensitivity**

- Take an allergy history before giving any drug, blood or vaccine; record allergies on the chart and wristband.
- Observe for 15–30 minutes after an injection or vaccine as per protocol; keep an **anaphylaxis kit** ready.
- Early signs of anaphylaxis: itching, flushing, urticaria, swelling of lips and face, wheeze, hoarseness, low blood pressure. Stop the drug or infusion, call for help, keep the airway open, lay the patient flat with legs raised (sitting up if breathless), give oxygen and emergency drugs as prescribed by protocol, and monitor.
- In a transfusion reaction: stop the transfusion at once, keep the line open with saline, inform the doctor and blood bank, return the bag and set, collect blood and urine samples.
- Rh-negative mothers receive anti-D immunoglobulin as prescribed to prevent Rh sensitisation.
- Latex allergy: use latex-free gloves and equipment.

## 4.5 Serological tests

**Serology** is the study of antigen–antibody reactions in serum, used to diagnose infection (by finding antibody or antigen), group blood and test for immunity.

| Reaction | Principle | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Agglutination** | Antibody clumps particulate antigens (cells, bacteria, latex beads) | **Widal test** (typhoid), blood grouping and cross-matching, latex agglutination (CRP, RA factor, ASO), Weil–Felix test (rickettsiae) |
| **Precipitation / flocculation** | Antibody combines with soluble antigen to form a visible precipitate | **VDRL** and RPR (syphilis), immunodiffusion |
| Complement fixation | Antigen–antibody complex uses up complement | Older tests for some viral infections |
| Neutralisation | Antibody blocks a toxin or virus | Tests of toxins and viral antibodies |
| **ELISA** (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay) | Enzyme-labelled antibody gives a colour change | HIV antibody, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), dengue NS1 antigen and IgM |
| Immunofluorescence | Fluorescent-labelled antibody seen under UV microscope | Some viral and autoimmune tests |
| **Rapid card tests (immunochromatography)** | Antigen or antibody moves along a strip and forms a coloured line | Malaria antigen RDT, HIV rapid test, pregnancy test, COVID-19 antigen test |
| Western blot | Antibodies to separated proteins | Confirmatory test in some infections |
| Chemiluminescence (CLIA) | Light-producing label; automated | Hormone and infection markers in large labs |

Interpreting serology: a **rising titre** (fourfold rise between two samples 10–14 days apart) or **IgM** shows current infection; IgG alone may mean past infection or vaccination. The **window period** is the time after infection before tests turn positive (important in HIV testing and blood donation).

Nursing: collect blood in a plain (red-top) tube for serum, avoid haemolysis, label correctly, note vaccination history and date of onset of illness.

## 4.6 Vaccines: types and classification

A **vaccine** is a preparation of a killed or weakened organism, its toxin made harmless, or a part of it, that produces active immunity without causing the disease.

| Type | What it contains | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Live attenuated** | Living organisms weakened so they cannot cause disease | **BCG**, **OPV** (oral polio), **measles–rubella (MR)**, rotavirus, varicella, yellow fever, live JE vaccine | Usually one or two doses give long immunity; generally avoided in pregnancy and in people with weak immunity; most are heat-sensitive |
| **Killed (inactivated)** | Whole organisms killed by heat or chemicals | **IPV** (injectable polio), rabies cell-culture vaccine, hepatitis A, whole-cell pertussis (in DPT/pentavalent) | Several doses and boosters needed; safe in low immunity |
| **Toxoid** | Bacterial exotoxin made harmless (formaldehyde) but still antigenic | **Tetanus toxoid**, diphtheria toxoid (in DPT, Td) | Protects against the toxin |
| **Subunit / recombinant** | Purified part of the organism, often made by genetic engineering | **Hepatitis B** vaccine (HBsAg), HPV vaccine, acellular pertussis | Very safe |
| **Conjugate** | Polysaccharide joined to a protein carrier so infants respond well | **Hib** (in pentavalent), **pneumococcal conjugate (PCV)** | Infants respond poorly to plain polysaccharide |
| Newer platforms | mRNA, viral vector, protein subunit | COVID-19 vaccines | — |
| Combination | Several antigens in one injection | **Pentavalent** (DPT + hepatitis B + Hib), MR | Fewer injections |

**Adjuvants** (such as aluminium salts) in some vaccines boost the immune response; these "adsorbed" vaccines are damaged by freezing.

## 4.7 Storage, handling and the cold chain

The **cold chain** is the system of people, equipment and procedures that keeps vaccines at the recommended temperature from the manufacturer to the point of use. Heat and (for some) freezing destroy potency, and damage cannot be seen or reversed.

```text
 Manufacturer → Government Medical Store Depot → State store → Regional/District store
   → PHC / CHC (ice-lined refrigerator) → Sub-centre / session site (vaccine carrier)
   → Child
```

| Equipment | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Walk-in coolers and freezers | Large state and regional stores |
| **Ice-lined refrigerator (ILR)** | Main storage at district and PHC levels; keeps +2 °C to +8 °C even with some power cuts |
| **Deep freezer** | Freezing ice packs (and, at higher levels, storing OPV frozen) |
| Cold box | Transporting and storing for a few days during campaigns or power failure |
| **Vaccine carrier with 4 conditioned ice packs** | Taking vaccines to the session site for the day |
| Thermometers, temperature logbooks, data loggers; eVIN (electronic vaccine stock and temperature monitoring) | Monitoring |

**Rules of storage**

- Most vaccines at **+2 °C to +8 °C**. Keep a thermometer in the ILR and record the temperature twice daily.
- **Freeze-sensitive vaccines** (never freeze): hepatitis B, pentavalent, DPT, Td, IPV, PCV, and others with adjuvants. Keep them away from the ILR floor and the freezing surfaces. If freezing is suspected, the **shake test** shows whether the vaccine was frozen (frozen vaccine settles faster with flakes).
- **Heat-sensitive vaccines**: OPV, measles–rubella, BCG, rotavirus — keep in the coldest permitted place, protect from heat and sunlight.
- **Vaccine vial monitor (VVM)**: a heat-sensitive square inside a circle on the label. If the inner square is lighter than the outer circle the vaccine can be used (if not expired). If the square matches or is darker than the circle (discard point), do not use.
- Diluents are kept cold before reconstitution and only the diluent supplied with that vaccine is used.
- Reconstituted vaccines (BCG, measles–rubella, JE) are used within 4 hours or by the end of the session, whichever is earlier, then discarded. Under the **open vial policy**, opened multi-dose vials of some liquid vaccines (such as OPV, DPT, Td, hepatitis B, pentavalent and IPV) may be used in later sessions for up to 4 weeks if conditions are met (not expired, VVM usable, cold chain maintained, no contamination, date of opening written). Check the current national guidelines for the exact list.
- Conditioned ice packs: frozen packs are kept out until the ice starts to melt (water sloshes) before putting them in carriers, so freeze-sensitive vaccines are not frozen.
- Use the "**Earliest Expiry First Out**" principle; never keep food or drinks in the vaccine refrigerator.

**Adverse events following immunization (AEFI)**: minor (pain, swelling, fever) and serious (anaphylaxis, seizures, death, hospitalisation). Observe for 30 minutes after vaccination, report serious AEFI through the national surveillance system, and keep an AEFI kit at every session.

## 4.8 Immunization schedule

The **National Immunization Schedule (NIS)** of the Universal Immunization Programme (UIP) is set by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. The table below follows the NIS as published on the National Health Mission website. The schedule is updated from time to time, so check the current version on the MoHFW / NHM site before an exam or a clinical posting.

| Age | Vaccines |
| --- | --- |
| **Pregnant woman** | Td-1 early in pregnancy, Td-2 four weeks after Td-1; a single Td booster if she received two doses in a pregnancy within the last 3 years |
| **Birth** | BCG, OPV-0 (zero dose), hepatitis B birth dose |
| **6 weeks** | OPV-1, Pentavalent-1, Rotavirus (RVV)-1, fractional IPV (fIPV)-1, PCV-1 |
| **10 weeks** | OPV-2, Pentavalent-2, RVV-2 |
| **14 weeks** | OPV-3, Pentavalent-3, fIPV-2, RVV-3, PCV-2 |
| **9–12 months** | Measles–Rubella (MR)-1, JE-1 (in endemic districts), PCV booster |
| **16–24 months** | MR-2, JE-2 (endemic districts), DPT booster-1, OPV booster |
| **5–6 years** | DPT booster-2 |
| **10 years and 16 years** | Td |

Points to check in the current schedule: the NHM table showed PCV only in selected states when it was published, and PCV was later extended to the whole country; a third fIPV dose at 9 months has since been added. Vitamin A is given with some of these visits (it is a supplement, not a vaccine).

**Routes (learn with the schedule)**: BCG — intradermal, left upper arm; OPV — oral drops; RVV — oral; hepatitis B, pentavalent, PCV, DPT — intramuscular, anterolateral thigh in infants; fIPV — intradermal, right upper arm; MR — subcutaneous, right upper arm; Td in older children and pregnant women — intramuscular, upper arm. Follow the exact volume on the vaccine label and national guidelines.

**Vaccination for other diseases and groups**

- Rabies: post-exposure vaccination with or without rabies immunoglobulin depending on the category of bite.
- Tetanus: Td after injury as per immunization status.
- Hepatitis B for health care workers (see Unit 8).
- Typhoid, hepatitis A, influenza, varicella, HPV and COVID-19 vaccines are available. HPV vaccine is given to girls before they become sexually active to prevent cervical cancer; check whether it is part of the current national schedule in your state.
- Pulse polio campaigns give additional OPV to children under 5 years.

## 4.9 Role of the nurse in immunization

1. Plan sessions, list due beneficiaries, mobilise families.
2. Maintain the cold chain; check temperature, VVM and expiry before use.
3. Check contraindications and history of previous reactions; counsel the mother on what vaccine is given, expected minor reactions and next visit.
4. Use one new auto-disable syringe for every injection; follow injection safety and do not recap; put used syringes in a puncture-proof container (hub cutter).
5. Give the vaccine by the correct route and site; record in the MCP card and register / digital system.
6. Observe for 30 minutes; manage and report AEFI.
7. Track drop-outs; educate the community.

## Remember

- Innate = present at birth, non-specific, no memory. Acquired = specific, memory.
- Active natural = infection; active artificial = vaccine; passive natural = maternal antibodies; passive artificial = immunoglobulin/antiserum.
- IgG crosses the placenta and is the most abundant; IgM is first and pentameric; IgA in secretions and colostrum; IgE in allergy.
- Hypersensitivity: I anaphylaxis (IgE), II cytotoxic (transfusion reaction, Rh), III immune complex (serum sickness), IV delayed (Mantoux, contact dermatitis).
- Widal = agglutination; VDRL = flocculation; ELISA for HIV, HBsAg.
- Live vaccines: BCG, OPV, MR, rotavirus. Toxoid: tetanus, diphtheria. Recombinant: hepatitis B.
- Cold chain +2 °C to +8 °C; never freeze hepatitis B, pentavalent, DPT, Td, IPV; shake test detects freezing; VVM inner square lighter than circle = usable.
- Reconstituted BCG, MR, JE: use within 4 hours.
- Birth: BCG, OPV-0, Hep B. 6, 10, 14 weeks: OPV, Pentavalent, RVV (+ fIPV, PCV). 9 months: MR-1. 16–24 months: MR-2, DPT-B1, OPV booster. 5–6 years: DPT-B2. 10 and 16 years: Td.
