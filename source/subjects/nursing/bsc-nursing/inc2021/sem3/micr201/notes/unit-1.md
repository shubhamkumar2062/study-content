# Unit 1: Introduction to microbiology and general characteristics of microbes

## 1.1 What microbiology is and why a nurse needs it

**Microbiology** is the study of living things too small to be seen with the naked eye (micro-organisms or microbes): bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, and the microscopic stages of worms (helminths). **Medical microbiology** deals with those that live on or in humans and cause disease.

Microbes are everywhere — in soil, water, air, on skin, in the gut. Most are harmless or useful (gut flora that make vitamin K, bacteria used to make curd and vaccines). Only a small number are **pathogens**.

**Importance and relevance to nursing**

| Area of nursing work | How microbiology helps |
| --- | --- |
| Infection prevention | Knowing how microbes spread lets the nurse break the chain of infection: hand hygiene, asepsis, isolation, sterilization |
| Specimen collection | Correct sample, correct time, correct container and quick transport give a correct report |
| Patient care | Recognising signs of infection (fever, pus, raised pulse), wound care, catheter and line care |
| Medicines | Giving antibiotics on time, after cultures are sent, and supporting antibiotic stewardship |
| Immunization | Storing vaccines in the cold chain, giving them correctly, recognising adverse events |
| Community health | Safe water, food hygiene, vector control, outbreak reporting |
| Own safety | Needle-stick prevention, staff vaccination, safe handling of specimens and waste |

## 1.2 Historical perspective

| Scientist | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (17th century, Netherlands) | Made simple microscopes and first described microbes ("animalcules"); called the father of microbiology (and of bacteriology) |
| Edward Jenner (1796) | Protected people against smallpox using cowpox material — the beginning of vaccination |
| Ignaz Semmelweis (1840s, Vienna) | Showed that hand washing by doctors in chlorinated lime solution cut deaths from puerperal (childbed) fever |
| Florence Nightingale (1850s) | Cut deaths in military hospitals through cleanliness, ventilation, nutrition and records; founder of modern nursing |
| Louis Pasteur (France) | Disproved spontaneous generation; germ theory of disease; pasteurization; vaccines against anthrax, chicken cholera and rabies; father of modern microbiology |
| Joseph Lister (1867) | Antiseptic surgery using carbolic acid (phenol); father of antiseptic surgery |
| Robert Koch (Germany) | Isolated the causes of anthrax, tuberculosis and cholera; solid media and pure culture methods; Koch's postulates |
| Hans Christian Gram (1884) | Gram staining |
| Dmitri Ivanovsky, Martinus Beijerinck (1890s) | Discovered filterable agents — the first viruses (tobacco mosaic virus) |
| Paul Ehrlich | Idea of the "magic bullet" — a drug that kills microbes but spares the host |
| Alexander Fleming (1928) | Discovered penicillin |

**Koch's postulates** (to prove an organism causes a disease):

1. The organism is found in every case of the disease.
2. It can be isolated and grown in pure culture.
3. The pure culture causes the same disease when put into a susceptible animal.
4. The same organism can be recovered again from that animal.

Exceptions exist: some organisms cannot be grown in culture (*Mycobacterium leprae*, *Treponema pallidum*), and carriers may have the organism without disease.

## 1.3 Concepts and terminology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Pathogen | An organism that can cause disease |
| Opportunistic pathogen | Normally harmless, causes disease when defences are low (e.g. *Candida* in diabetes, *Pneumocystis* in AIDS) |
| Commensal / normal flora | Organisms that live on the body without harm and often protect it by competing with pathogens |
| Pathogenicity | Ability to cause disease |
| Virulence | Degree of pathogenicity — how severe the disease is |
| Infection | Entry and multiplication of a pathogen in the body |
| Contamination | Presence of microbes on a surface or object |
| Colonisation | Organisms grow on a body site without causing illness |
| Carrier | A person who harbours a pathogen without symptoms and can pass it on (e.g. typhoid carrier) |
| Reservoir | Where the organism normally lives and multiplies (human, animal, soil, water) |
| Incubation period | Time from entry of the organism to the first symptom |
| Endogenous infection | From the patient's own flora |
| Exogenous infection | From outside — other people, equipment, environment |
| Nosocomial / healthcare-associated infection | Acquired in a healthcare setting; not present or incubating at admission |
| Zoonosis | Disease spread from animals to humans (rabies, plague, leptospirosis) |
| Toxin | Poisonous product: **exotoxin** (protein released by living bacteria, e.g. tetanus, diphtheria toxin) or **endotoxin** (lipopolysaccharide of the Gram-negative cell wall, released when cells die; causes fever and shock) |
| Asepsis | Absence of pathogens; aseptic technique keeps them away |
| Sterilization | Killing or removing all microbes, including spores |
| Disinfection | Killing most harmful microbes on objects, usually not spores |
| Antiseptic | A chemical that can be safely used on living tissue to kill or inhibit microbes |

## 1.4 Principles of microbiology

1. Microbes are everywhere; most are harmless or useful.
2. Each disease has a specific cause (germ theory).
3. Microbes need food, water, the right temperature, pH and oxygen conditions to grow. Remove these and they stop growing.
4. Microbes spread by definite routes — the **chain of infection** — and each link can be broken.
5. They can be killed or removed by physical and chemical agents (sterilization, disinfection).
6. The body defends itself through natural and acquired immunity; vaccines build acquired immunity.
7. Microbes change: they can become resistant to antibiotics.

**Chain of infection**

```text
 Infectious agent → Reservoir → Portal of exit → Mode of transmission
        ↑                                                   ↓
 Susceptible host ←──────────── Portal of entry ←───────────┘
```

| Link | Examples | How the nurse breaks it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Agent | Bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites | Early diagnosis, treatment, disinfection |
| Reservoir | Patients, carriers, staff, water, equipment | Cleaning, isolation, staff health checks |
| Portal of exit | Respiratory secretions, blood, urine, faeces, wound pus | Cover coughs, dressings, safe disposal of body fluids |
| Mode of transmission | Contact (direct, indirect), droplet, airborne, vehicle (food, water, blood), vector | Hand hygiene, PPE, sterilization, isolation, vector control |
| Portal of entry | Broken skin, mucosa, urinary catheter, IV line, ventilator tube | Aseptic technique, catheter and line care |
| Susceptible host | Newborns, elderly, diabetics, cancer patients, ICU patients | Immunization, nutrition, skin care |

## 1.5 Classification of microbes

All living things are grouped into cells with a true nucleus (**eukaryotes**) and cells without (**prokaryotes**). Viruses are not cells at all.

| Feature | Prokaryote (bacteria) | Eukaryote (fungi, protozoa, human cells) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nucleus | No nuclear membrane; single circular chromosome in the nucleoid | True nucleus with membrane; several chromosomes |
| Organelles | No mitochondria, no endoplasmic reticulum | Mitochondria, ER, Golgi body |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (except mycoplasma) | Fungi: chitin; protozoa and animal cells: none |
| Division | Binary fission | Mitosis |
| Size | Usually 1–10 µm | Usually 10–100 µm |

**Groups of medically important microbes**

| Group | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bacteria | Single-celled prokaryotes; cell wall; divide by binary fission | *Staphylococcus*, *E. coli*, *Mycobacterium* |
| Viruses | Not cells; DNA or RNA in a protein coat; grow only inside living cells | HIV, hepatitis B, influenza, dengue |
| Fungi | Eukaryotes; yeasts (single cells) and moulds (threads called hyphae) | *Candida*, *Aspergillus*, dermatophytes |
| Protozoa | Single-celled animal-like eukaryotes | *Plasmodium*, *Entamoeba*, *Giardia* |
| Helminths | Multicellular worms; eggs and larvae are microscopic | Roundworm, hookworm, tapeworm |
| Special bacteria | Rickettsiae and chlamydiae (live inside cells), mycoplasma (no cell wall), spirochaetes (spiral, flexible) | Scrub typhus agent, *Chlamydia trachomatis*, *Treponema pallidum* |

Bacteria are also classified by **shape**, **Gram staining**, **oxygen need**, and **spore formation**.

## 1.6 Structure of a bacterial cell

```text
          flagellum
             ~~~~~
   ┌──────────────────────────┐ ← capsule (slime layer outside)
   │ ┌──────────────────────┐ │ ← cell wall (peptidoglycan)
   │ │ ┌──────────────────┐ │ │ ← cytoplasmic membrane
   │ │ │  nucleoid (DNA)  │ │ │
   │ │ │  ribosomes  ○ ○  │ │ │
   │ │ │  plasmid ◯       │ │ │
   │ │ └──────────────────┘ │ │
   │ └──────────────────────┘ │
   └──────────────────────────┘
       | | | |  pili / fimbriae
```

| Part | Structure | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capsule | Layer of polysaccharide (sometimes polypeptide) outside the wall | Resists phagocytosis → more virulent (pneumococcus, *Klebsiella*); seen by negative staining; used in vaccines (pneumococcal, Hib) |
| Cell wall | Rigid peptidoglycan layer that gives shape | Basis of Gram staining; target of penicillins; Gram-negative wall carries endotoxin |
| Cytoplasmic membrane | Lipid bilayer | Controls entry and exit; site of energy production |
| Cytoplasm | Contains ribosomes (70S), granules (e.g. volutin granules of diphtheria bacilli) | Ribosomes are the target of several antibiotics |
| Nucleoid | Single circular DNA, no nuclear membrane | Carries genes |
| Plasmids | Small extra pieces of circular DNA | Often carry antibiotic-resistance genes; pass between bacteria |
| Flagella | Long whip-like threads | Movement (motility) |
| Pili (fimbriae) | Short hair-like threads | Attachment to host cells (e.g. *E. coli* to the bladder lining); sex pili transfer DNA |
| Spores (endospores) | Resting form with a thick coat, formed inside the cell in bad conditions | Resist heat, drying and disinfectants; *Bacillus* and *Clostridium*; killed only by sterilization |

**Gram-positive versus Gram-negative cell wall**

| Feature | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peptidoglycan | Thick, many layers | Thin, one or two layers |
| Teichoic acid | Present | Absent |
| Outer membrane with lipopolysaccharide | Absent | Present (endotoxin) |
| Colour in Gram stain | Purple / violet | Pink / red |
| Examples | *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Clostridium* | *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Pseudomonas*, *Neisseria* |

Acid-fast bacteria (*Mycobacterium*) have a waxy wall rich in mycolic acid, so they need the Ziehl–Neelsen stain.

## 1.7 Morphological types, size and form

Bacteria are measured in micrometres (µm; 1 µm = 1/1000 mm). Most cocci are about 1 µm across; most bacilli are 1–5 µm long. They are seen with the light microscope (oil immersion lens, ×100 objective). Viruses are much smaller (about 20–300 nm) and need the electron microscope.

```text
 COCCI (round)                 BACILLI (rods)        OTHERS
 ●●           diplococci       ▬                    ⌒  vibrio (comma)
 ●●●●●●       streptococci     ▬▬  diplobacilli     ∿∿ spirilla (rigid spiral)
 ●●●          staphylococci    ▬▬▬ streptobacilli   ∿∿∿∿ spirochaetes (flexible)
 ●●●            (grape-like    ▭  coccobacilli
  ●●             clusters)     branching threads — actinomycetes
```

| --- | --- | --- |
| Cocci | Pairs (diplococci) | Pneumococcus (lancet-shaped), *Neisseria* (kidney-shaped) |
| | Chains | *Streptococcus* |
| | Grape-like clusters | *Staphylococcus* |
| Bacilli | Single rods | *E. coli*, *Salmonella* |

| Comma-shaped | — | *Vibrio cholerae* |

## 1.8 Motility

Most motile bacteria move by flagella.

| Arrangement of flagella | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Monotrichous — one flagellum at one end | *Vibrio cholerae* (darting motility) |
| Lophotrichous — a tuft at one end | *Spirillum* |
| Amphitrichous — at both ends | some spirilla |
| Peritrichous — all around the cell | *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Proteus* (swarming) |
| Atrichous — no flagella (non-motile) | *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Klebsiella*, *Shigella* |

Spirochaetes move by internal axial filaments. Motility is tested by the **hanging drop** method or in semi-solid agar.

## 1.9 Colonisation

Colonisation means organisms settling and growing on a body surface. Steps in infection: **attachment** (by pili, capsule) → **colonisation** (multiplying on the surface) → **invasion** (entering tissues using enzymes such as hyaluronidase, coagulase) → **damage** (by toxins or the body's own inflammatory reaction).

Normal flora colonise the skin (*Staphylococcus epidermidis*), mouth and throat (viridans streptococci), large intestine (*E. coli*, *Bacteroides*) and vagina (lactobacilli, which keep the pH acid). They protect by using up nutrients and space (colonisation resistance). Antibiotics that kill normal flora allow overgrowth of *Candida* or *Clostridioides difficile*.

In hospitals, patients' skin, nose and gut quickly become colonised with resistant hospital organisms (MRSA, resistant Gram-negative bacilli). Colonised patients are a reservoir even when they are not ill — the reason for hand hygiene between every patient.

## 1.10 Growth and nutrition of microbes

Bacteria multiply by **binary fission** — one cell splits into two. The time taken to double is the **generation time** (about 20 minutes for *E. coli* in good conditions; about 14–15 hours for *M. tuberculosis*, which is why TB cultures take weeks).

**Bacterial growth curve** (in a closed liquid culture)

```text
 log number
 of cells
   │            ┌────────────┐ stationary
   │           /              \
   │          /  log           \ decline
   │         /  (exponential)   \ (death)
   │  ______/                    \
   │  lag                         \
   └──────────────────────────────────→ time
```

1. **Lag phase** — cells adjust, make enzymes; no increase in number.
2. **Log (exponential) phase** — fastest division; cells most sensitive to antibiotics.
3. **Stationary phase** — food runs out, waste builds up; births = deaths; spores and exotoxins often formed.
4. **Decline (death) phase** — cells die faster than they divide.

**Nutrition**: bacteria need water, a carbon source, nitrogen, minerals and sometimes growth factors (vitamins, blood). Most pathogens are **heterotrophs** (use organic carbon from other living things). Fastidious organisms need enriched media (blood, serum).

**Factors that affect growth**

| Factor | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | **Psychrophiles** grow best in the cold (below 20 °C); **mesophiles** at 25–40 °C — all human pathogens, optimum about 37 °C; **thermophiles** at 55–80 °C. Refrigeration slows growth; heat kills |
| Moisture | Water is essential; drying stops growth (dried foods keep). Spores and some bacteria (*M. tuberculosis*, *Staphylococcus*) survive drying in dust |
| Oxygen | **Obligate aerobes** need O2 (*Pseudomonas*, *M. tuberculosis*); **facultative anaerobes** grow with or without (*E. coli*, *Staphylococcus*); **microaerophiles** need a little (*Campylobacter*, *Helicobacter*); **obligate anaerobes** are killed by O2 (*Clostridium*, *Bacteroides*) |
| Carbon dioxide | Some (*Neisseria*, *Brucella*) grow better in 5–10% CO2 (candle jar) |
| pH | Most pathogens prefer pH 7.2–7.6; *Vibrio cholerae* grows in alkaline pH; lactobacilli and fungi tolerate acid |
| Light | Ultraviolet rays and direct sunlight kill many bacteria |
| Osmotic pressure | High salt or sugar draws water out of cells (pickles, jam); *Staphylococcus aureus* tolerates salt |
| Blood and body fluids | Blood, serum, urine, pus and tissue fluid are rich in nutrients and keep organisms alive and growing — the reason spills of blood and body fluids are treated as infectious, and why stagnant urine in a catheter bag lets bacteria multiply |

## 1.11 Application and implication in nursing

- **Temperature**: specimens for culture are sent promptly; urine may be refrigerated (2–8 °C) if delayed so bacteria do not multiply and give a falsely high count. CSF and blood cultures are never refrigerated, because the organisms that cause meningitis die in the cold.
- **Moisture**: wet dressings and damp linen favour growth — keep wounds and skin folds dry; dry equipment fully after cleaning.
- **Oxygen**: deep, dirty wounds with dead tissue are anaerobic — risk of tetanus and gas gangrene; good wound debridement and immunization protect.
- **Spores** survive boiling and most disinfectants, so surgical instruments must be sterilized, not merely disinfected.
- **Normal flora**: unnecessary antibiotics upset the flora — watch for oral thrush and diarrhoea.
- **Body fluids**: treat all blood and body fluids as potentially infectious (standard precautions).
- Understand that the log phase is when infection spreads fastest — give antibiotics on time and do not miss doses.
