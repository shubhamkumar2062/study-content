# Unit 8: Safety protocols and employee safety

Section B (Infection Control and Safety), INC Units X and XI joined: safety protocols (5S, radiation, laser, fire, hazardous materials, environmental safety, emergency codes, the nurse in disaster) and employee safety indicators (vaccination, needle-stick injuries, falls, radiation, health checks, healthcare worker immunization and management of occupational exposure).

## 8.1 5S — workplace organisation

5S is a Japanese method for keeping the workplace orderly, clean and safe. It reduces time wasted searching, prevents errors (wrong item picked) and keeps infection risks down.

| Step | Japanese | Meaning | Ward example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sort** | Seiri | Remove what is not needed | Remove expired drugs, broken equipment, unused forms from the nursing station |
| **Set in order** | Seiton | A place for everything, everything in its place, labelled | Labelled shelves; emergency trolley arranged in a standard order; frequently used items within reach |
| **Shine** | Seiso | Clean the workplace and inspect while cleaning | Daily cleaning of trolleys and counters; report damage found |
| **Standardize** | Seiketsu | Make the first three steps routine with standards | Checklists, colour codes, photographs of how a shelf should look |
| **Sustain** | Shitsuke | Make it a habit; audit and train | Regular 5S audits, rewards, leadership involvement |

## 8.2 Radiation safety

Ionising radiation (X-rays, CT, fluoroscopy, radioactive isotopes in nuclear medicine and radiotherapy) can damage cells and cause cancer, cataract and harm to the fetus. In India, radiation use is regulated by the **Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB)**.

**Principles**

- **ALARA** — keep exposure **As Low As Reasonably Achievable**.
- **Time** — spend as little time near the source as possible.
- **Distance** — stay as far away as possible (exposure falls sharply with distance — inverse square law); about 2 metres from a portable X-ray machine is the usual rule.
- **Shielding** — lead aprons, thyroid shields, lead glass, mobile lead screens, lead-lined walls.

**Practical measures**

1. Only trained staff in the room during exposure; others step out or behind a shield.
2. Wear a **lead apron** (and thyroid collar) if you must stay; never hold the patient yourself if a holder or relative (with apron) can do it; pregnant staff should not hold patients.
3. Wear a **personal dosimeter** (thermoluminescent dosimeter, TLD badge) if working in radiation areas; badges are read regularly and records kept.
4. Check **pregnancy status** of women of child-bearing age before X-rays; shield the gonads where possible.
5. Warning signs (radiation trefoil symbol) and red warning lights outside X-ray rooms.
6. Patients with radioactive implants or isotopes: follow the time and distance rules, use the room assigned, handle body fluids and linen as instructed, and limit visitors.
7. Store and hang lead aprons properly (folding cracks the lead) and check them periodically.

## 8.3 Laser safety

Lasers produce an intense, focused beam of light used in surgery, ophthalmology and dermatology. Hazards: **eye injury** (retinal or corneal burns, even from reflected beams), **skin burns**, **fire** (drapes, gauze, endotracheal tubes in an oxygen-rich atmosphere), **surgical smoke (plume)** containing toxic chemicals and possibly viable organisms, and electrical hazards.

Lasers are classed by hazard (Class 1, 1M, 2, 2M, 3R, 3B, 4); most surgical lasers are **Class 4** — the most hazardous.

**Safety measures**

1. A trained **laser safety officer** and written laser policy.
2. **Protective eyewear** of the correct wavelength and optical density for everyone in the room, including the patient (eye shields or wet gauze for the patient's eyes).
3. **Warning signs** on doors; restrict entry; cover windows.
4. Keep the laser in **standby** when not firing; only the operator activates the footswitch.
5. **Fire prevention**: wet drapes and swabs near the site, avoid alcohol-based skin preparations that are still wet, laser-safe endotracheal tubes, keep oxygen as low as possible near the airway; water and extinguisher ready.
6. **Smoke evacuator** and high-filtration masks for the plume.
7. Matt, non-reflective instruments.
8. Equipment check and maintenance log.

## 8.4 Fire safety

**Fire triangle**: a fire needs **heat**, **fuel** and **oxygen**; removing any one puts it out (a chemical chain reaction is the fourth side of the "fire tetrahedron"). Hospitals have a high fire risk — oxygen supply, electrical equipment, flammable liquids (alcohol, spirit), linen — and many patients who cannot escape on their own.

### Types and classification of fire

| Class | Burning material | Suitable extinguisher |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Ordinary solid combustibles — paper, wood, cloth, linen, plastics | Water, foam, multi-purpose (ABC) dry powder |
| **B** | Flammable liquids — petrol, oil, alcohol, spirit, paints | Foam, CO2, dry powder; **not water** (spreads the fire) |
| **C** | Flammable gases — LPG, piped gases | Dry powder; shut off the gas supply first |
| **D** | Combustible metals — sodium, magnesium | Special dry powder |
| **Electrical fires** | Live electrical equipment | **CO2** or dry powder; **switch off the power**; never water or foam |
| **F / K** | Cooking oils and fats (kitchens) | Wet chemical extinguisher, fire blanket |

Note: in Indian and European usage, Class C means gases and electrical fires are treated separately; in the US system Class C means electrical fires. Follow the labels on your hospital's extinguishers.

### Fire alarms and fire-fighting equipment

- **Detection and alarm**: smoke and heat detectors, **manual call points** ("break glass" or push switches), alarm bells/sirens, public address system, fire control panel.
- **Fire-fighting equipment**: portable **fire extinguishers** (water, foam, CO2, dry chemical powder, clean agent), **fire blankets**, **hose reels** and **wet risers / hydrants**, automatic **sprinklers**, sand buckets.
- **Passive protection**: fire doors (keep closed, never wedged open), fire exits and illuminated exit signs, evacuation maps, compartmentation of wards.

### What the nurse does in a fire — RACE and PASS

```text
 RACE                                   PASS (using an extinguisher)
 R – Rescue patients in immediate        P – Pull the safety pin
     danger                              A – Aim the nozzle at the BASE of the fire
 A – Alarm: raise the alarm (call point,  S – Squeeze the handle
     announce the fire code)              S – Sweep from side to side
 C – Contain: close doors and windows,
     switch off oxygen to the area (as
     directed) and electrical equipment
 E – Extinguish (if small and safe) or
     Evacuate
```

**Evacuation**: horizontal first (to the next fire compartment on the same floor, behind fire doors), then vertical (down the stairs) if needed; **never use lifts**; ambulatory patients first, then wheelchair patients, then bed-ridden patients (unless instructed otherwise by the fire plan); take patient records and count patients at the assembly point; stay low in smoke and cover the nose with a wet cloth.

**Prevention**: no smoking; safe storage of flammables; regular electrical checks; do not overload sockets; keep exits clear; extinguishers checked and in date; staff trained; **regular fire drills (mock drills)**.

## 8.5 HAZMAT (hazardous materials) safety

**Hazardous materials** in hospitals: disinfectants and cleaning chemicals (hypochlorite, glutaraldehyde, formalin, phenol), **cytotoxic drugs**, **mercury** (old thermometers, BP apparatus), laboratory reagents, compressed gases (oxygen, nitrous oxide), anaesthetic gases, ethylene oxide, radioactive materials, and blood and body fluids (biological hazards).

### Types of spill

- **Chemical spill** — disinfectants, laboratory reagents, formalin.
- **Cytotoxic (chemotherapy) spill**.
- **Mercury spill** — broken thermometer or sphygmomanometer.
- **Biological spill** — blood, body fluids, cultures.
- **Radioactive spill** — nuclear medicine areas.
- Spills are also graded as **small** (can be cleaned by trained ward staff with a spill kit) or **large** (evacuate the area and call the trained HAZMAT/emergency team).

### Spillage management (general steps)

1. **Assess**: identify the substance (check the label and the safety data sheet), the amount and the risk; keep people away; ventilate if safe.
2. **Protect yourself**: PPE — gloves (double for cytotoxics), gown, mask/respirator, goggles, shoe covers.
3. **Contain**: use the appropriate **spill kit** — absorbent pads or granules, working from the outside towards the centre to stop spread.
4. **Clean up**: collect the material and absorbents, place in the correct labelled waste container (yellow for cytotoxic and chemical waste), clean the area as recommended (detergent and water; 1% hypochlorite for blood spills).
5. **Dispose** of waste as per BMW and hazardous waste rules.
6. **Decontaminate yourself**: remove PPE, wash hands; if skin or eye contact, wash with plenty of water for at least 15 minutes and seek medical help.
7. **Report and document** the spill as an incident; restock the spill kit.

**Mercury spill**: do not touch with bare hands, **do not use a vacuum cleaner or broom** (spreads vapour); remove jewellery; use the mercury spill kit — push droplets together with stiff paper, collect with a dropper or tape, place in a sealed container with water, label and hand over as hazardous waste; ventilate the room. Hospitals are encouraged to replace mercury devices with digital ones.

### MSDS (Material Safety Data Sheet)

An **MSDS** (now often called a **Safety Data Sheet, SDS**, in the internationally harmonised 16-section format) is a document from the manufacturer for each hazardous chemical. It gives: identification of the product and supplier; hazards; composition; **first-aid measures**; fire-fighting measures; **accidental release (spill) measures**; handling and storage; exposure controls and **PPE**; physical and chemical properties; stability and reactivity; toxicology; ecological information; **disposal**; transport; regulations; and other information. MSDS must be **available where the chemical is used and stored**, and staff should know where to find them and how to read them.

## 8.6 Environmental safety

### Risk assessment

A **risk assessment** identifies hazards in the work area, estimates how likely and how serious harm would be, and decides controls.

```text
 1 Identify hazards → 2 Decide who may be harmed and how
 → 3 Evaluate risk (likelihood × severity) and choose controls
 → 4 Record findings and act → 5 Review regularly and after any incident
```

**Hierarchy of controls** (most to least effective): **elimination** → **substitution** (e.g. digital instead of mercury thermometers) → **engineering controls** (ventilation, safety-engineered needles, lead shielding) → **administrative controls** (policies, training, signage, rotas) → **PPE**.

### Aspect–impact analysis

An **environmental aspect** is any activity, product or service of the hospital that interacts with the environment (e.g. generating biomedical waste, using water, energy, chemicals, emitting incinerator smoke, discharging effluent). The **impact** is the change it causes to the environment (soil and water contamination, air pollution, depletion of resources). The analysis lists every aspect of each department, rates the significance of its impact, and sets controls (segregation, effluent treatment, energy saving, spill control). It is part of environmental management systems such as ISO 14001.

### Maintenance of temperature and humidity (department-wise)

Correct temperature, humidity, air changes and pressure reduce infection, protect patients and keep drugs and equipment working. Typical design ranges (check your hospital's engineering and accreditation standards):

| Area | Typical requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Operating theatres | Temperature about 20–24 °C (some standards allow 18–25 °C); relative humidity about 30–60%; **positive pressure**; filtered (HEPA) air with a high number of air changes per hour |
| ICUs, wards | Comfortable temperature (about 22–26 °C), humidity about 40–60%, good air changes |
| Airborne isolation rooms | **Negative pressure**, at least 6–12 air changes per hour, air exhausted outside or through HEPA |
| Protective isolation (transplant, burns) | **Positive pressure**, HEPA-filtered air |
| CSSD sterile store | Cool and dry, controlled humidity to protect sterile packs |
| Pharmacy / drug stores | Room temperature drugs below the labelled maximum; refrigerated items at 2–8 °C |
| Vaccine and blood storage | Vaccines 2–8 °C (cold chain); blood bank refrigerators 2–6 °C for whole blood and red cells |

Temperatures and humidity are **recorded on charts** (often twice daily), out-of-range readings are reported, and equipment is maintained.

### Audits

Safety and infection control **audits** compare actual practice with standards and feed back results: hand hygiene audits, bundle compliance audits, BMW segregation audits, fire safety audits (extinguishers, exits), environmental cleaning audits (checklists, surface swabs where indicated), radiation and laser safety audits, documentation audits. The **audit cycle**: set standard → measure practice → compare → change practice → re-audit.

## 8.7 Emergency codes

Hospitals use **colour codes** announced over the public address system so staff respond quickly without causing panic. Codes vary between hospitals; learn your hospital's list. Commonly used codes in Indian hospitals:

| Code | Usual meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Code Red** | Fire |
| **Code Blue** | Cardiac or respiratory arrest (adult; some hospitals have a separate paediatric code) |
| **Code Pink** | Infant or child abduction |
| **Code Black** | Bomb threat (in some hospitals, a violent person) |
| **Code Yellow** | External disaster / mass casualty (varies) |
| **Code Orange** | Hazardous material spill (in some hospitals, a disaster) |
| **Code Grey / Violet** | Violent or aggressive person / security threat (varies) |
| **Code Brown** | Missing patient or severe weather (varies) |

When a code is announced, staff follow their assigned role in the hospital's emergency plan; the code is declared "all clear" when over.

## 8.8 Role of the nurse in times of disaster

A **disaster** is a sudden event that overwhelms the local capacity to cope — natural (earthquake, flood, cyclone) or man-made (fire, building collapse, chemical leak, epidemic, mass accident).

| Phase | Role of the nurse |
| --- | --- |
| **Preparedness** | Know the hospital disaster plan and own role; take part in **mock drills**; keep emergency supplies and triage tags ready; maintain a staff call-in list; community education |
| **Response** | Report as per the plan; **triage** using colour tags — **red** (immediate, life-threatening but salvageable), **yellow** (delayed/urgent), **green** (minor, walking wounded), **black** (dead or unsalvageable); first aid and life support; infection control in crowded settings; records and identification of victims; communication with families; psychological first aid; discharge stable patients to free beds |
| **Recovery / rehabilitation** | Continuing care, rehabilitation, mental health support (post-traumatic stress), prevention of epidemics (safe water, sanitation, vaccination, vector control), debriefing and evaluation of the response |

## 8.9 Employee safety indicators

**Employee safety indicators** measure how well the hospital protects its staff. Examples:

| Indicator | Measure |
| --- | --- |
| **Vaccination** | Percentage of healthcare workers fully vaccinated against hepatitis B (with antibody checked), and other recommended vaccines |
| **Needle-stick injuries (NSI)** | Number of sharps injuries per month (or per 100 staff); percentage reported and given post-exposure care in time |
| **Falls** | Staff slips, trips and falls |
| **Radiation safety** | Dosimeter readings within limits; percentage of radiation workers monitored |
| **Annual health check** | Percentage of staff completing the annual (pre-employment and periodic) health check |
| Others | Back injuries from lifting, workplace violence, occupational TB, latex allergy, burnout |

**Prevention of employee injuries**

- **Needle-stick prevention**: no recapping (one-handed scoop technique only if unavoidable), sharps containers at the point of use, filled no more than three-quarters, safety-engineered devices and auto-disable syringes, never pass sharps hand to hand (use a tray), training, adequate staffing and lighting.
- **Fall prevention** for staff: dry floors with warning signs, good lighting, non-slip footwear, clear corridors.
- **Radiation safety**: time, distance, shielding, TLD badges, lead aprons.
- **Safe patient handling**: lifting aids, team lifts, body mechanics.
- **Annual health check**: physical examination, vaccination status, screening as per job (e.g. chest X-ray for TB exposure where indicated, eye check for laser staff), occupational illness record.

## 8.10 Healthcare worker immunization programme and occupational exposure

**Occupational health ordinance**: Indian law protects workers through labour laws such as the Employees' Compensation Act, 1923, the Employees' State Insurance Act, 1948 and the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020, together with the BMW Rules (which require immunization of waste handlers) and accreditation standards (NABH) that require an occupational health programme for staff. Each hospital has its own occupational health policy based on these.

**Vaccination programme for healthcare staff**

| Vaccine | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Hepatitis B** | Most important for all staff exposed to blood: three doses (0, 1 and 6 months); check anti-HBs antibody 1–2 months after the course — a level of 10 mIU/mL or more means protection; non-responders get a repeat series as per guidelines |
| Tetanus–diphtheria (Td) | As per schedule / after injury |
| Influenza | Every year |
| Measles–mumps–rubella (MMR) and varicella | For staff without evidence of immunity |
| COVID-19 | As per national guidance |
| Others as needed | Hepatitis A (food handlers, some lab staff), typhoid (lab, kitchen), meningococcal (some lab staff) |

Records of each staff member's immunization are kept; new staff are screened and vaccinated at joining.

### Needle-stick injuries: prevention and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)

**Risk of infection** after a needle-stick from an infected source (approximate, commonly quoted): hepatitis B up to about 30% (in a non-immune worker, higher if the source is HBeAg positive), hepatitis C about 2–3%, HIV about 0.3%. Hollow-bore needles with blood and deep injuries carry the highest risk.

**Immediate first aid**

1. **Do not panic.** Remove gloves.
2. **Wash the wound with soap and running water** — **do not squeeze, suck or scrub** the wound, and do not use bleach or strong antiseptics.
3. Splash to eyes or mouth: rinse with plenty of clean water or saline; do not rub the eyes. Splash on intact skin: wash with soap and water.
4. **Report immediately** to the in-charge and the occupational health / infection control team or the designated PEP centre (ICTC/ART centre as per NACO guidelines); fill the exposure report.

**Assessment**: type of exposure (percutaneous, mucous membrane, non-intact skin), the amount of blood or fluid, the source patient's status (test for HIV, HBsAg, HCV with consent and counselling), and the exposed worker's hepatitis B vaccination and antibody status. Baseline blood tests of the worker.

**Post-exposure prophylaxis**

- **HIV**: if indicated, antiretroviral PEP should start **as early as possible, ideally within 2 hours** and **not later than 72 hours**, and is continued for **28 days**, using the regimen in the current **NACO** guidelines. Follow-up HIV testing as per guidelines (commonly at 6 weeks, 3 months and 6 months); counselling; safe sex and no blood donation during follow-up.
- **Hepatitis B**: depends on the worker's immunity — a vaccinated worker with protective antibody needs no treatment; an unvaccinated or non-immune worker exposed to an HBsAg-positive or unknown source receives **hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG)** and **starts / completes hepatitis B vaccination** as soon as possible (ideally within 24 hours).
- **Hepatitis C**: no vaccine or standard PEP; follow-up testing for early detection and treatment.
- Document, investigate the cause (RCA) and act to prevent recurrence; confidentiality is maintained.

## Remember

- 5S: Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardize, Sustain.
- Radiation: ALARA; time, distance, shielding; TLD badge; AERB regulates in India.
- Laser: Class 4 surgical lasers; correct goggles, warning signs, standby mode, fire and smoke precautions.
- Fire triangle: heat, fuel, oxygen. RACE (Rescue, Alarm, Contain, Extinguish/Evacuate) and PASS (Pull, Aim at the base, Squeeze, Sweep). Never water on electrical or oil fires; never use lifts.
- Spill: assess, protect, contain, clean, dispose, decontaminate, report. Mercury: no vacuum or broom. MSDS/SDS at the point of use.
- Hierarchy of controls: elimination → substitution → engineering → administrative → PPE.
- Operating theatre positive pressure; airborne isolation negative pressure.
- Common codes: Red fire, Blue cardiac arrest, Pink child abduction (others vary).
- Disaster triage tags: red immediate, yellow delayed, green minor, black dead.
- Hepatitis B vaccine 0, 1, 6 months; anti-HBs 10 mIU/mL or more = protected.
- Needle-stick: wash with soap and water, do not squeeze or scrub, report at once; HIV PEP within 2 hours ideally, not after 72 hours, for 28 days (NACO regimen).
