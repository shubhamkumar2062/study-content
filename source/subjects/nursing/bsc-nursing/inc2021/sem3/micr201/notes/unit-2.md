# Unit 2: Laboratory identification: staining, culture media and culture methods

Section A (Applied Microbiology), second half of INC Unit II: laboratory methods for identifying micro-organisms — staining, culture media, pure culture techniques and anaerobic culture. The nurse does not usually run these tests, but must understand them to collect the right specimen and to read a report.

## 2.1 How a microbiology laboratory identifies an organism

```text
Specimen received → Direct examination (wet mount, stained smear)
                  → Culture on suitable media (18–48 hours; weeks for TB)
                  → Identification (colony appearance, Gram stain of colony,
                    biochemical tests, automated systems)
                  → Antibiotic sensitivity testing
                  → Serology / antigen tests / molecular tests (PCR) where needed
                  → Report to the ward
```

**Direct microscopy** gives a quick first answer (Gram-positive cocci in clusters, acid-fast bacilli seen) within an hour; culture confirms the species and its antibiotic sensitivity.

**Wet preparations** (unstained):

- **Saline wet mount** — motile protozoa (trophozoites of *Entamoeba*, *Trichomonas*), pus cells.
- **Iodine mount** — cysts of protozoa in stool.
- **Hanging drop** — motility of bacteria (darting motility of *Vibrio cholerae*).
- **KOH mount** — fungi in skin, hair and nail (see 2.4).

## 2.2 Staining: principles

Bacteria are almost transparent, so they are stained to be seen. A smear is made on a clean glass slide, **air-dried** and **heat-fixed** (passed quickly through a flame two or three times) so the cells stick to the slide and are killed.

| Type | Principle | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple stain | One basic dye stains all cells the same colour; shows shape and arrangement | Methylene blue, crystal violet, dilute carbol fuchsin |
| Differential stain | Two dyes separate bacteria into groups | Gram stain, Ziehl–Neelsen (acid-fast) stain |
| Special stain | Shows a particular structure | Capsule (negative staining), spore stain, Albert's stain for metachromatic granules |
| Fungal preparations | Show fungal elements | Lactophenol cotton blue (LPCB), KOH mount |

## 2.3 Differential stains

### Gram stain

| Step | Reagent | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Primary stain | Crystal violet, about 1 minute | All cells stain purple |
| 2. Mordant | Gram's iodine, about 1 minute | Forms a crystal violet–iodine complex that is held firmly in thick walls |
| 3. Decolouriser | Acetone or alcohol, a few seconds, then wash | Removes the dye from Gram-negative cells (thin wall, outer membrane dissolved) |
| 4. Counterstain | Safranin (or dilute carbol fuchsin), about 30 seconds to 1 minute | Stains decolourised cells pink |

Result: **Gram-positive** = purple/violet; **Gram-negative** = pink/red.

```text
 Gram +ve:  thick peptidoglycan traps dye–iodine complex → stays PURPLE
 Gram −ve:  thin wall + outer membrane, decolourised → takes safranin → PINK
```

Uses: first clue to the cause of infection (e.g. Gram-negative diplococci inside pus cells in urethral pus suggest gonorrhoea; Gram-positive cocci in clusters suggest staphylococci), and to guide early antibiotic choice.

### Ziehl–Neelsen (acid-fast bacilli, AFB) stain

Mycobacteria have waxy mycolic acid in the wall; they resist ordinary stains but, once stained with hot carbol fuchsin, they resist decolourisation by acid — hence "acid-fast".

| Step | Reagent | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Primary stain | Strong carbol fuchsin, heated until steam rises (do not boil) for about 5 minutes | Heat drives the dye through the waxy wall |
| 2. Decolouriser | 20% sulphuric acid (5% for *M. leprae*), then acid–alcohol in some methods | Removes dye from non-acid-fast cells |
| 3. Counterstain | Methylene blue (or malachite green) | Background and other cells stain blue |

Result: **acid-fast bacilli = bright red/pink rods** on a blue background. Used for sputum in tuberculosis (national programme sputum microscopy) and slit-skin smears in leprosy. Fluorescent staining (auramine) is a faster, more sensitive alternative used in larger laboratories.

## 2.4 Special stains and fungal preparations

| Stain | How it works | Result / use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Capsule — negative staining** (India ink or nigrosin) | The dark background stains, but the capsule does not take the dye | Clear halo around the organism against a black background; used for *Cryptococcus neoformans* in CSF; capsules of pneumococcus, *Klebsiella* |
| **Spore stain** (e.g. malachite green with heat, counterstain safranin) | Heat drives green dye into the tough spore coat | Green spores inside pink cells; *Bacillus*, *Clostridium* |
| **Albert's stain** | Toluidine blue and malachite green | Bluish-black metachromatic (volutin) granules in green bacilli — *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* |
| **LPCB (lactophenol cotton blue)** | Phenol kills fungi, lactic acid preserves structure, cotton blue stains chitin | Blue fungal hyphae and spores; used to identify moulds grown in culture |
| **KOH mount** (10–20% potassium hydroxide) | KOH dissolves keratin of skin, hair and nail, leaving fungi intact | Branching hyphae or spores seen directly; used for ringworm (dermatophytes), candidiasis of skin and nails |

Nursing points for KOH samples: scrape the active edge of a skin lesion with a sterile blade onto black paper or into a clean envelope; clip nails or pluck hair; do not apply creams before sampling.

## 2.5 Culture media

A **culture medium** is a preparation of nutrients on or in which microbes are grown in the laboratory. Bacteria grow as visible **colonies** on solid media and as turbidity (cloudiness) in liquid media.

**Ingredients**: water; peptone (protein digest — nitrogen); meat extract or yeast extract (vitamins, minerals); sodium chloride; **agar** (a seaweed polysaccharide used to solidify — it melts at about 100 °C and sets at about 40 °C and is not digested by most bacteria); and special additives (blood, serum, sugars, indicators, inhibitors).

**Media preparation (outline)**: weigh ingredients → dissolve in distilled water → adjust pH (about 7.2–7.4) → add agar for solid media → dispense into tubes or flasks → sterilize by autoclaving (heat-sensitive additives such as blood or serum are added after cooling to about 50 °C) → pour into Petri dishes or set as slopes → check sterility by incubating a sample → label and store in the refrigerator.

### Classification by physical state

| Type | Agar | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | About 1.5–2% | Colonies, pure culture, colony counts (plates, slopes) |
| Semi-solid | About 0.2–0.5% | Motility testing, transport |
| Liquid (broth) | None | Growing large numbers, blood culture bottles, enrichment |

### Classification by composition

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synthetic (chemically defined) | Every ingredient and its amount known | Used in research and nutrition studies |
| Semi-synthetic | Mostly defined ingredients plus a small amount of a natural substance of unknown composition | A defined salts-and-sugar medium with added yeast extract |
| Complex / non-synthetic | Contains natural extracts (peptone, meat extract, blood) of variable composition | Nutrient agar, blood agar (most routine media) |

### Classification by purpose

| Type | Principle | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basal / simple | Supports organisms that are not fastidious | Nutrient broth, nutrient agar, peptone water |
| **Enriched** | Basal medium plus blood, serum or egg to grow fastidious organisms | Blood agar (5–10% sheep blood), chocolate agar (heated blood — *Haemophilus*, *Neisseria*), Loeffler's serum slope (diphtheria) |
| **Enrichment** (liquid) | Liquid that favours the wanted organism and holds back others, so a few pathogens in a mixed specimen can multiply before plating | Selenite F broth and tetrathionate broth (*Salmonella*, *Shigella* in stool), alkaline peptone water (*Vibrio cholerae*) |
| **Selective** (solid) | Contains substances that allow only the wanted organism to grow | Lowenstein–Jensen medium (*M. tuberculosis*; malachite green inhibits others), TCBS agar (*Vibrio*), deoxycholate citrate agar and XLD (*Salmonella*, *Shigella*), Sabouraud's agar with antibiotics (fungi) |
| **Differential** (indicator) | Colonies of different organisms look different | **MacConkey agar** — lactose fermenters (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*) give pink colonies, non-fermenters (*Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Pseudomonas*) pale; **blood agar** shows haemolysis (alpha — green, beta — clear zone, gamma — none) |
| Transport | Keeps organisms alive without letting them multiply, during transport | Stuart's and Amies media (swabs), Cary–Blair (stool), viral transport medium (VTM) |
| Anaerobic | Contain reducing agents that remove oxygen | Robertson's cooked meat broth, thioglycollate broth |

Many media are both selective and differential (MacConkey agar contains bile salts that stop most Gram-positive bacteria and lactose with an indicator to show fermentation).

## 2.6 Pure culture techniques

A **pure culture** contains only one kind of organism, grown from a single colony. Clinical specimens are mixed, so the laboratory separates organisms first.

| Method | How it is done | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Streak plate** | A loop of specimen is spread over one area of an agar plate, then streaked in successive areas, flaming the loop between them, so organisms are thinned out and single colonies form in the last areas | Routine method for isolating pathogens |
| **Pour plate** | A measured amount of diluted specimen is mixed with melted, cooled (about 45 °C) agar and poured into a Petri dish; colonies grow throughout and on the surface | Counting viable bacteria (water, milk) |
| **Spread plate** | A small measured volume of diluted specimen is spread evenly over the surface with a sterile bent glass rod (L-shaped spreader) | Counting colonies; isolation |
| **Tube (serial) dilution** | The specimen is diluted step by step (1 in 10, 1 in 100, …) in tubes of sterile broth or saline; dilutions are then plated | Estimating numbers of organisms; also used to find the lowest concentration of an antibiotic that stops growth (MIC) |

```text
 Streak plate (four-quadrant method)
   ┌───────────────┐
   │ 1 ≡≡≡≡        │   1 = heavy growth (inoculum)
   │      2 ////   │   2, 3 = thinner
   │  4 ··  3 \\\\ │   4 = separate (isolated) colonies
   └───────────────┘
```

Plates are incubated upside down (lid down) at 37 °C so condensation does not drip on the colonies.

**Colony count in urine**: a calibrated loop places a known volume on the plate; counts of 10^5 (100,000) colony-forming units per mL or more in a properly collected midstream specimen usually mean significant urinary infection.

## 2.7 Anaerobic cultivation of bacteria

Obligate anaerobes (*Clostridium*, *Bacteroides*) die in oxygen, so they need special methods:

1. **Reducing media** — Robertson's cooked meat broth (meat particles contain reducing substances), thioglycollate broth.
2. **McIntosh–Fildes anaerobic jar** — plates are placed in a sealed metal or plastic jar; oxygen is removed by a catalyst that combines it with hydrogen to form water. Modern jars use a **gas-generating sachet (GasPak)**, which releases hydrogen and carbon dioxide when water is added. An indicator (methylene blue strip) turns colourless when the jar is anaerobic.
3. **Anaerobic chambers / workstations** — sealed cabinets filled with an oxygen-free gas mixture.
4. Older methods: boiling media to drive off oxygen, sealing with paraffin, candle jar (this only raises CO2 and is for capnophiles, not true anaerobes).

Specimens for anaerobic culture: pus aspirated in a syringe (better than a swab), tissue pieces; sent quickly in anaerobic transport medium; never refrigerated.

## 2.8 Other identification and sensitivity tests (overview)

| Test | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Biochemical tests (catalase, coagulase, oxidase, sugar fermentation, indole, urease) | Identify species (e.g. coagulase-positive = *S. aureus*) |
| Antibiotic sensitivity testing (Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion; MIC methods) | Reports organisms as sensitive, intermediate or resistant to each antibiotic |
| Automated systems (blood culture machines, identification and sensitivity analysers) | Faster results |
| Serology (ELISA, agglutination) | Detects antibodies or antigens (see Unit 4) |
| Molecular tests (PCR, cartridge-based nucleic acid tests for TB) | Detect DNA/RNA; fast and sensitive; also detect drug resistance genes |
| Animal inoculation and tissue culture | Mostly for research and virus isolation |

## 2.9 Application and implication in nursing

- Send specimens **before starting antibiotics**; antibiotics already given may stop organisms growing and give a falsely negative culture.
- Use the **correct container and transport medium** (VTM for viral swabs, Cary–Blair for stool if delayed, blood culture bottles for blood).
- Label specimens with patient details, specimen type, site, date and time, and any antibiotics given.
- A **Gram stain report** comes first and may change treatment within hours; tell the doctor promptly. The **culture and sensitivity** report comes in 2–3 days (weeks for TB culture).
- In TB, collect good-quality sputum (deep cough, not saliva) for AFB smear and molecular testing as directed by the national TB programme.
- Know that "no growth" from a poorly collected or delayed specimen does not rule out infection.
- Handle all specimens and cultures as infectious; laboratory staff are protected only if containers are leak-proof and correctly packed.

## Remember

- Gram stain order: crystal violet → iodine (mordant) → acetone/alcohol (decolouriser) → safranin (counterstain). Positive purple, negative pink.
- ZN stain: hot carbol fuchsin → 20% H2SO4 → methylene blue; AFB stain red.
- Negative staining (India ink) shows capsule of *Cryptococcus*; Albert's stain shows granules of *C. diphtheriae*; LPCB for fungal culture; KOH for skin, hair, nail.
- Enriched = blood/chocolate agar; enrichment = liquid (selenite F, alkaline peptone water); selective = LJ, TCBS; differential = MacConkey (pink lactose fermenters), blood agar (haemolysis).
- Pure culture: streak (routine), pour and spread (counts), tube dilution (counts, MIC).
- Anaerobes: Robertson's cooked meat medium, McIntosh–Fildes jar with GasPak.
- Specimens before antibiotics; correct container; quick transport.
