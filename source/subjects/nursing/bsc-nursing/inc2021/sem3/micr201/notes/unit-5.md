# Unit 5: Hospital acquired infections, isolation precautions, PPE and hand hygiene

Section B (Infection Control and Safety), INC Units I, II and III joined: hospital acquired infection and the bundle approach, surveillance, isolation precautions and personal protective equipment, and hand hygiene. These three units are taught together because they are practised together at every bedside.

## 5.1 Hospital acquired infection (healthcare-associated infection, HAI)

**Definition**: an infection that a patient develops while receiving care in a hospital or other healthcare facility, which was **not present or incubating at the time of admission**. For surveillance, infections that appear **48 hours or more after admission** (in the US NHSN system, on or after the third calendar day of admission), or within a set period after discharge or surgery, are counted as healthcare-associated. Infections in staff acquired at work are also HAIs.

**Why it matters**: HAIs prolong hospital stay, raise costs, increase antibiotic use and resistance, and cause avoidable deaths. A large share is preventable with simple, consistent practices.

**Common HAIs**

| HAI | Main risk factor | Common organisms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Catheter-associated urinary tract infection (**CAUTI**) | Indwelling urinary catheter | *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterococcus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Candida* |
| Surgical site infection (**SSI**) | Surgery, especially long or contaminated procedures | *S. aureus* (incl. MRSA), Gram-negative bacilli |
| Ventilator-associated pneumonia / events (**VAP/VAE**) | Mechanical ventilation | *Pseudomonas*, *Acinetobacter*, *Klebsiella*, *S. aureus* |
| Central line-associated bloodstream infection (**CLABSI**) | Central venous catheter | Coagulase-negative staphylococci, *S. aureus*, *Candida*, Gram-negative bacilli |
| Others | Antibiotics, poor hygiene | *C. difficile* diarrhoea, phlebitis from peripheral lines, skin and soft-tissue infection |

**Sources**: the patient's own flora (endogenous), other patients, staff hands, visitors, contaminated equipment, water, air, food, and the hospital environment (exogenous).

**Factors**: host (extremes of age, diabetes, cancer, immunosuppression, malnutrition, burns), devices and procedures (catheters, lines, ventilators, surgery), environment (overcrowding, poor ventilation, inadequate cleaning), and practices (missed hand hygiene, poor asepsis, overuse of antibiotics).

## 5.2 The bundle approach

A **care bundle** is a small set (usually 3–5) of evidence-based practices for one procedure or device that, when **all are performed together every time**, give a better result than any one alone. Compliance is measured as "all or none" — if one element is missed for a patient, the bundle is counted as not done. Each bundle has an **insertion** part and a **maintenance** part.

### Prevention of urinary tract infection (CAUTI bundle)

**Insertion**

1. Insert a catheter only when there is a valid indication (urinary retention, accurate output measurement in the critically ill, certain surgeries, comfort at end of life); consider alternatives (condom catheter, intermittent catheterisation, bladder scanner).
2. Hand hygiene; insert using **aseptic technique** and sterile equipment by trained staff.
3. Use the smallest suitable catheter; secure it to the thigh to prevent movement.

**Maintenance**

1. Keep a **closed drainage system**; do not disconnect the catheter from the tubing.
2. Keep the bag **below the level of the bladder** and off the floor; keep the tubing free of kinks for unobstructed flow.
3. Empty the bag regularly into a clean, separate container for each patient, without the outlet touching the container.
4. Daily meatal care with soap and water (no routine antiseptics).
5. Collect urine samples from the **sampling port** with aseptic technique, never from the bag.
6. **Review the need every day** and remove the catheter as soon as possible.
7. Hand hygiene and gloves for every handling.

### Prevention of surgical site infection (SSI bundle)

**Before surgery**: bath or shower before the operation; treat existing infections; control blood sugar; stop smoking where possible; **do not shave** — if hair must be removed, use **clippers** just before surgery; give prophylactic antibiotic as prescribed **within 60 minutes before the incision** (timing set by hospital protocol).

**During surgery**: surgical hand antisepsis; skin preparation with an alcohol-based antiseptic (e.g. chlorhexidine–alcohol), allowed to dry; sterile gowns, gloves, drapes and instruments; keep the patient warm (normothermia); maintain blood glucose control; limit traffic and door opening in the theatre.

**After surgery**: keep the sterile dressing on for the time set by protocol (usually 24–48 hours); hand hygiene and aseptic technique for dressing changes; observe the wound for redness, swelling, discharge, fever; educate the patient on wound care at home. SSIs are watched for 30 days after surgery, or up to 90 days when an implant is placed.

### Prevention of ventilator-associated events (VAP/VAE bundle)

1. **Elevate the head of the bed 30–45°** (unless contraindicated) to reduce aspiration.
2. **Daily sedation interruption** ("sedation vacation") and daily assessment of readiness to wean / spontaneous breathing trial, so the patient comes off the ventilator sooner.
3. **Oral care** regularly with tooth brushing and an approved oral antiseptic as per protocol.
4. **Subglottic secretion drainage** with special endotracheal tubes where available; keep the cuff pressure in the recommended range.
5. Prevent stress ulcers and deep vein thrombosis as prescribed (part of many ventilator bundles).
6. Avoid unnecessary disconnection of the circuit; drain condensate away from the patient; change circuits only when visibly soiled or malfunctioning.
7. Aseptic suctioning; hand hygiene before and after.
8. Prefer non-invasive ventilation where suitable; early mobility.

### Prevention of central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI bundle)

**Insertion**

1. Hand hygiene.
2. **Maximal sterile barrier precautions**: cap, mask, sterile gown and gloves for the operator; large sterile drape covering the patient.
3. **Chlorhexidine–alcohol skin antisepsis**, allowed to dry.
4. **Optimal site**: avoid the femoral vein in adults where possible (subclavian is preferred for lowest infection risk, considering other risks).
5. Use an insertion checklist; an observer can stop the procedure if a step is missed.

**Maintenance**

1. **Review the need daily** and remove unnecessary lines promptly.
2. "**Scrub the hub**" with an antiseptic (e.g. alcohol or chlorhexidine–alcohol) for several seconds before every access.
3. Keep a clean, dry, intact sterile transparent dressing; change as per protocol or when soiled or loose.
4. Change administration sets at set intervals (sooner for blood and lipids).
5. Hand hygiene and gloves; minimise line access; use dedicated lumens for parenteral nutrition.

## 5.3 Surveillance of HAI

**Surveillance** is the continuous, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of data on HAIs, and sharing it with those who need to act.

**Steps**: define the event (standard definitions, e.g. CDC/NHSN, adapted in India by the ICMR / national HAI surveillance network) → find cases (daily ward rounds, laboratory reports, device lists) → collect data on cases and on the population at risk → calculate rates → analyse trends and compare → report to the wards and administration → act and check the effect.

**Rates commonly used**

```text
 CAUTI rate  = Number of CAUTIs ÷ Number of urinary catheter-days × 1000
 CLABSI rate = Number of CLABSIs ÷ Number of central line-days × 1000
 VAP rate    = Number of VAPs ÷ Number of ventilator-days × 1000
 SSI rate    = Number of SSIs ÷ Number of surgeries of that type × 100
 Device utilisation ratio = Device-days ÷ Patient-days
```

A **device-day** is one patient with the device for one day (counted at the same time every day).

**Types**: active (trained staff look for cases) or passive (reports from wards); prospective or retrospective; hospital-wide or targeted (ICU, high-risk surgery). **Outbreak**: more cases than expected in a given place and time — investigated and controlled urgently.

### Infection control team and committee

| | Hospital Infection Control Committee (HICC) | Infection Control Team (ICT) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Role | Policy-making body: approves infection control policies, reviews surveillance data and outbreaks, plans resources and training | Day-to-day working group that carries out the programme |
| Members | Chairperson (medical superintendent / head of hospital), infection control officer (usually microbiologist), infection control nurse(s), heads of clinical departments, nursing superintendent, pharmacist, engineering, housekeeping, CSSD, laundry, dietary and biomedical waste representatives | Infection control officer, **infection control nurse(s)**, sometimes an epidemiologist and data assistant |
| Meets | Regularly (often monthly) and in outbreaks | Daily work |

**Infection control nurse (ICN)**: surveillance and data, ward rounds and audits (hand hygiene, bundles, BMW, cleaning), training of staff, outbreak investigation, staff exposure management, advising on isolation, antibiotic stewardship support, record keeping. A common recommendation is one ICN for about every 100 beds — check your hospital or accrediting standard.

## 5.4 Isolation precautions

**Isolation** separates infected or colonised patients (source isolation, **barrier nursing**) or protects highly susceptible patients (protective or **reverse barrier** isolation) to prevent spread.

**Types of isolation systems (historical and current)**

- **Category-specific isolation** (older): strict, contact, respiratory, TB (AFB), enteric, drainage/secretion, blood and body fluid precautions.
- **Disease-specific isolation** (older): precautions chosen for each disease.
- **Universal precautions** (1980s, after HIV): treat all blood and certain body fluids of every patient as infectious.
- **Body substance isolation**: gloves for all moist body substances.
- **Current CDC two-tier system (1996, updated 2007)**: **standard precautions** for all patients, plus **transmission-based precautions** (contact, droplet, airborne) for patients known or suspected to have particular infections.
- **Protective (reverse barrier) isolation**: for neutropenic patients, transplant recipients and patients with severe burns — single room with positive pressure where available, strict hand hygiene, no sick visitors or staff, careful food hygiene, no fresh flowers or plants.

### Standard precautions (for every patient, every time)

1. **Hand hygiene** (5 moments).
2. **PPE** chosen by risk assessment: gloves for contact with blood, body fluids, mucosa, broken skin; gown/apron, mask and eye protection when splashes are likely.
3. **Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette**: cover the mouth and nose, tissues in the bin, hand hygiene, masks for coughing patients, spatial separation (at least 1 metre) in waiting areas.
4. **Safe injection practices**: one needle, one syringe, one time; single-dose vials where possible; aseptic preparation.
5. **Sharps safety**: no recapping, immediate disposal in a puncture-proof container, safety-engineered devices.
6. **Patient placement**: single room for patients who may contaminate the environment.
7. **Patient-care equipment**: clean and reprocess between patients; single-use items not reused.
8. **Environmental cleaning** of high-touch surfaces.
9. **Linen**: handle with minimum agitation.
10. **Waste**: segregation as per biomedical waste rules.
11. **Safe lumbar puncture practices**: face mask for spinal procedures.

### Transmission-based precautions

| | **Contact** | **Droplet** | **Airborne** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How it spreads | Direct touch (patient to hands) or indirect via contaminated objects (fomites) | Large respiratory droplets (more than 5 µm) that travel a short distance (about 1–2 metres) when coughing, sneezing, talking | Small droplet nuclei (5 µm or less) that stay suspended in air and travel long distances |
| Examples | MRSA and other MDROs, *C. difficile*, wound infections, scabies, lice, herpes zoster (localised), acute diarrhoea in incontinent patients, rotavirus | Influenza, diphtheria (pharyngeal), pertussis, meningococcal infection, mumps, rubella, streptococcal pharyngitis, mycoplasma | **Pulmonary TB, measles, chickenpox** (varicella, and disseminated zoster) |
| Room | Single room or cohort with same organism | Single room or cohort; spacing over 1 metre with curtain between beds | **Airborne infection isolation room** — negative pressure, at least 6–12 air changes per hour, door kept closed; if not available, single room with good natural ventilation and exhaust |
| PPE | Gloves and gown on entering the room | **Surgical mask** within about 1 metre (on entering the room) | **N95 (or higher) respirator**, fit-checked, before entering |
| Patient transport | Limit; cover affected areas | Surgical mask on patient | Surgical mask on patient |
| Equipment | Dedicated (stethoscope, BP cuff, thermometer) or disinfected after use | — | — |

Some diseases need more than one kind (e.g. chickenpox: airborne + contact; COVID-19: droplet and contact, with airborne precautions for aerosol-generating procedures — follow current national guidance).

### Epidemiology and infection prevention — CDC guidelines

The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines on isolation precautions (2007) and hand hygiene (2002) are widely used; India's national infection prevention and control guidelines (MoHFW/NCDC) follow similar principles. Key epidemiological ideas: know the **mode of transmission** of each organism, **break the chain of infection** at its weakest link, use **risk assessment** to choose precautions, and measure results by **surveillance**.

## 5.5 Personal protective equipment (PPE)

**PPE** is specialised clothing or equipment worn by staff to protect against infectious material. It is the **last line of protection**, after engineering controls (ventilation, single rooms) and administrative controls (policies, training).

| Item | Protects | When used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gloves (clean non-sterile; sterile) | Hands | Contact with blood, body fluids, mucosa, non-intact skin, contaminated items; sterile for aseptic procedures |
| Gown / plastic apron | Clothes and skin | Splashes, contact precautions; fluid-resistant gown when much splashing is expected |
| Surgical (medical) mask | Mouth and nose from droplets and splashes | Droplet precautions; procedures; sterile procedures (to protect the patient) |
| N95 respirator | Breathing in small airborne particles (filters at least 95% of particles) | Airborne precautions; aerosol-generating procedures |
| Goggles or face shield | Eyes (and face) | Splash risk: suctioning, intubation, irrigation |
| Cap, shoe covers | Hair, footwear | Operating theatre, high-risk procedures |

**Effective use of PPE — principles**

1. Choose PPE according to the expected exposure (risk assessment), not "everything for everyone".
2. Perform hand hygiene before putting on and after removing PPE.
3. Put on PPE before contact with the patient, usually before entering the room.
4. Do not touch the face or adjust PPE while caring for the patient; work from clean to dirty areas.
5. Change gloves between patients and between dirty and clean tasks on the same patient; **never wash or reuse disposable gloves**; gloves are not a substitute for hand hygiene.
6. Remove PPE carefully to avoid contaminating yourself, at the doorway or anteroom; discard into the correct biomedical waste bin.
7. Do a **seal check** each time an N95 is worn and a **fit test** as per hospital policy.

**Sequence (CDC)**

```text
 DONNING (putting on)              DOFFING (taking off)
 Hand hygiene                      1. Gloves
 1. Gown                           2. Goggles / face shield
 2. Mask or respirator             3. Gown
 3. Goggles / face shield          4. Mask or respirator (after leaving room
 4. Gloves (over gown cuffs)          for N95)
                                   Hand hygiene (also between steps if
                                   hands get contaminated)
```

Rule for removal: the **front** of mask, goggles and gown is contaminated — handle them by the ties, ear loops or headband; peel gloves inside-out (glove-to-glove, then skin-to-skin). Some hospitals remove gown and gloves together; follow local protocol.

## 5.6 Hand hygiene

Hand hygiene is the **single most important measure** to prevent HAIs, because staff hands carry organisms from patient to patient.

**Types of hand hygiene**

| Type | What | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Routine (social) hand washing** | Plain soap and running water, removes dirt and transient flora | Visibly dirty hands, after toilet, before eating |
| **Hand rub (antiseptic hand rub)** | Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR), 20–30 seconds; kills most transient organisms quickly; preferred for routine clinical care when hands are not visibly soiled | At the 5 moments |
| **Antiseptic hand washing** | Soap containing an antiseptic (e.g. chlorhexidine) and water, 40–60 seconds | Before some procedures; per policy |
| **Surgical hand antisepsis (surgical scrub)** | Antiseptic soap scrub or surgical alcohol hand rub of hands and forearms, longer duration as per protocol (often 2–6 minutes for scrub) | Before surgery and invasive procedures with sterile gloves |

**Hand washing with soap and water (40–60 seconds)** — use when hands are visibly dirty or soiled with blood/body fluids, after using the toilet, and when exposure to spore-forming organisms (*C. difficile*) or norovirus is likely, because alcohol does not kill spores.

**Steps of hand rubbing / hand washing (WHO technique)**

```text
 0. Wet hands (for washing) / take a palmful of rub to cover all surfaces
 1. Rub palm to palm
 2. Right palm over left back of hand with fingers interlaced, and vice versa
 3. Palm to palm with fingers interlaced
 4. Backs of fingers to opposing palm with fingers interlocked
 5. Rotational rubbing of left thumb clasped in right palm, and vice versa
 6. Rotational rubbing, backwards and forwards, with clasped fingers of right hand
    in left palm (fingertips), and vice versa
 7. (Washing) Rinse, dry thoroughly with a single-use towel, use the towel to
    turn off the tap.  (Rub) Continue until hands are dry.
```

Many Indian teaching hospitals add rubbing of the **wrists** as a separate step and teach "7 steps" — learn the order your institution uses.

**Before hand hygiene**: nails short, no nail polish or artificial nails, remove rings, watches and bracelets ("bare below the elbows"), cover cuts with waterproof dressing.

### The WHO "My 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene"

```text
            ┌──────── PATIENT ZONE ────────┐
  1. BEFORE touching a patient             3. AFTER body fluid exposure risk
  2. BEFORE a clean/aseptic procedure      4. AFTER touching a patient
                                           5. AFTER touching patient surroundings
```

| Moment | Why | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Before touching a patient | Protect the patient from germs on your hands | Shaking hands, helping to move, taking pulse, BP |
| 2. Before a clean/aseptic procedure | Protect the patient from germs entering the body, including their own | Oral care, giving eye drops, inserting a catheter, dressing a wound, preparing medicines, IV access |
| 3. After body fluid exposure risk | Protect yourself and the environment | After drawing blood, emptying a urine bag, handling dressings, removing gloves |
| 4. After touching a patient | Protect yourself and the environment | After examination, bathing, feeding |
| 5. After touching patient surroundings | Protect yourself and the environment, even if the patient was not touched | After changing bed linen, adjusting the drip rate, touching the bed rail or bedside table |

### WHO hand hygiene promotion

WHO's **multimodal improvement strategy** has five components:

1. **System change** — alcohol hand rub at the point of care; sinks, soap, single-use towels.
2. **Training and education** for all staff.
3. **Evaluation and feedback** — observing compliance (direct observation audits using the 5 moments), monitoring rub consumption, giving results back to staff.
4. **Reminders in the workplace** — posters, stickers.
5. **Institutional safety climate** — leadership commitment, patient participation, role models.

WHO marks **World Hand Hygiene Day on 5 May** each year ("SAVE LIVES: Clean Your Hands"); **Global Handwashing Day** (community) is 15 October.

**Hand hygiene compliance** = (number of hand hygiene actions performed ÷ number of opportunities observed) × 100.

## 5.7 Application and implication in nursing

- Do hand hygiene at all 5 moments; carry a pocket rub if dispensers are not at every bed.
- Review every device daily ("Is this catheter/line still needed?") and prompt the doctor to remove it.
- Follow the bundles every time and document them on the bundle checklist.
- Put up the correct isolation signboard at the door and teach visitors.
- Choose PPE by risk; never wear the same gloves or gown between patients.
- Report fever, wound discharge, cloudy urine or new chest signs early; send cultures before antibiotics.
- Take part in surveillance and audits and act on feedback.

## Remember

- HAI = not present or incubating at admission; usually appears 48 hours or more after admission.
- The big four device/procedure HAIs: CAUTI, SSI, VAP/VAE, CLABSI. Rate per 1000 device-days (SSI per 100 surgeries).
- Bundle = 3–5 practices, all done every time; "all or none" compliance.
- CAUTI: indication, aseptic insertion, closed system, bag below bladder, daily review.
- SSI: no razor (clippers), antibiotic within 60 minutes before incision, chlorhexidine–alcohol, normothermia, glucose control.
- VAP: head up 30–45°, daily sedation break and weaning trial, oral care.
- CLABSI: hand hygiene, maximal barrier, chlorhexidine skin prep, avoid femoral, scrub the hub, daily review.
- Standard precautions for all patients; transmission-based for contact, droplet (>5 µm, surgical mask) and airborne (≤5 µm, N95, negative pressure room).
- Donning: gown → mask → goggles → gloves. Doffing: gloves → goggles → gown → mask.
- Hand rub 20–30 s; hand wash 40–60 s; soap and water for *C. difficile* and visible soiling.
- 5 Moments: before patient, before aseptic task, after body fluid risk, after patient, after surroundings. World Hand Hygiene Day 5 May.
