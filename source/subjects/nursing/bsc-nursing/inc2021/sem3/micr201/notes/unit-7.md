# Unit 7: Patient safety indicators, incidents and International Patient Safety Goals

Section B (Infection Control and Safety), INC Units VIII and IX joined: patient safety indicators, capturing and analysing incidents and adverse events (RCA, CAPA, report writing), and the International Patient Safety Goals (IPSG).

## 7.1 Patient safety: basic ideas

**Patient safety** is the prevention of errors and avoidable harm to patients during healthcare. WHO describes it as reducing the risk of unnecessary harm to an acceptable minimum. "First, do no harm."

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Iatrogenic injury** | Harm caused by healthcare itself — a procedure, drug, device or error (not the disease) |
| **Error** | Failure to carry out a planned action as intended, or using the wrong plan |
| **Incident** | Any event that caused, or could have caused, unnecessary harm to a patient |
| **Near miss** | An incident that did not reach the patient (caught in time) — e.g. a wrong drug noticed before giving |
| **No-harm incident** | Reached the patient but caused no harm |
| **Adverse event** | An incident that caused harm |
| **Sentinel event** | An unexpected event causing death or serious physical or psychological harm (e.g. surgery on the wrong side, infant abduction, transfusion of the wrong blood group) — needs immediate investigation |
| **Patient safety indicator** | A measurable sign of how safe care is, tracked over time (e.g. falls per 1000 patient-days, medication errors, HAI rates) |

**Why errors happen — the "Swiss cheese" model**: every system has several layers of defence (policies, checks, staff, equipment), each with holes. Harm happens when holes in several layers line up. Fixing the system (adding checks, removing hazards) works better than blaming an individual. A **just culture** encourages staff to report errors without fear, while still holding people accountable for reckless behaviour.

**Patient safety indicators** used by hospitals in India (for example under NABH accreditation) include: medication errors and adverse drug reactions, patient falls, pressure injuries, HAI rates (CAUTI, CLABSI, VAP, SSI), transfusion reactions, needle-stick injuries, unplanned return to the operating theatre, unplanned reintubation or return to ICU, incomplete surgical safety checklists, patient identification errors and incomplete documentation. Each is expressed as a rate and compared month by month.

## 7.2 Care of vulnerable patients

**Vulnerable patients** are at greater risk of harm: newborns and children, the elderly, unconscious or sedated patients, patients with mental illness, intellectual or physical disability, those with communication barriers (language, hearing loss), immunocompromised patients, pregnant women, victims of abuse, and patients on restraints.

Care: identify them on admission (risk screening), place them near the nursing station, side rails and fall precautions, frequent monitoring, involve family and interpreters, protect from abuse and neglect, special consent arrangements, child safety (abduction prevention, identification bands for mother and baby), pressure-area care and nutrition.

## 7.3 Prevention of iatrogenic injury

- Follow protocols and checklists; double-check high-risk medicines and procedures.
- Correct patient identification before every intervention.
- Aseptic technique and hand hygiene to prevent infections.
- Safe positioning and pressure-area care during surgery and immobility (prevent pressure injuries — risk assessment with the Braden scale, two-hourly turning, pressure-relieving mattresses, nutrition, skin care).
- Prevent burns from hot packs and diathermy, and injury from equipment; check equipment before use.
- Monitor for adverse drug reactions and report them.

## 7.4 Care of lines, drains and tubings

1. **Label** every line and tube (name of infusion, date and time of insertion) and trace each line from the patient to its source before connecting or giving anything ("trace the line") — this prevents wrong-route errors such as giving a feed into an IV line.
2. Secure lines to prevent pulling out; check position (marking at the nostril for nasogastric tubes; confirm NG tube position before every feed as per protocol).
3. Keep drainage bags below the level of the body part; prevent kinks; measure and record output; observe the colour and amount of drainage.
4. Aseptic technique for any opening of a closed system; scrub the hub.
5. Inspect insertion sites every shift for redness, swelling, leakage; document.
6. Review daily whether each line or tube is still needed.
7. Use different connectors for enteral and IV systems where available.

## 7.5 Restraint policy and care — physical and chemical

**Restraint** is any method that restricts a patient's freedom of movement or normal access to their body.

- **Physical restraint**: limb holders, vest/jacket restraints, mittens, bed rails used to stop a patient getting out, tight sheets.
- **Chemical restraint**: a drug given mainly to control behaviour or movement, rather than as standard treatment for a condition.

**Policy principles**

1. Use only as a **last resort** after less restrictive alternatives fail (reorienting, a family member at the bedside, diversion, treating pain, hunger or a full bladder, a bed in a low position).
2. Only for the **safety** of the patient or others, never for staff convenience or punishment.
3. Needs a **doctor's written order** with the reason, type and time limit; renewed within the time set by hospital policy; emergency application is followed by an order quickly.
4. Informed **consent** from the patient or family where possible; explain the reason.
5. Use the **least restrictive** method for the **shortest time**.

**Nursing care of a restrained patient**

- Apply correctly: padding, two fingers between restraint and skin, quick-release knot tied to the bed frame (not the side rail).
- Check frequently as per policy (commonly every 15 minutes): circulation, skin colour, temperature, sensation and movement of the restrained part.
- Release at regular intervals (for example every 2 hours) for range-of-motion exercise, skin care, toileting, fluids and food, repositioning.
- Monitor breathing and the mental state; never restrain in the prone position unsupervised.
- For chemical restraint: monitor level of consciousness, breathing, blood pressure and falls risk.
- **Document** the reason, alternatives tried, time applied and removed, checks done, and the patient's response; remove as soon as possible.

## 7.6 Blood and blood transfusion policy

1. **Written consent** for transfusion.
2. Correctly labelled sample for grouping and cross-matching, collected from a correctly identified patient (one patient at a time, labelled at the bedside).
3. Collect blood from the blood bank only when ready to start; transfusion is started promptly after issue (commonly within 30 minutes) and each unit completed within the time set by policy (usually within 4 hours).
4. **Bedside check by two staff members**: patient identity (two identifiers, wristband), blood group and unit number on the bag and the compatibility label/form, expiry date, appearance of the bag (leaks, clots, discolouration).
5. Use a blood administration set with a filter; do not add any drug to blood; only normal saline can share the line.
6. Record baseline **vital signs**; **stay with the patient for the first 15 minutes** and start slowly, because severe reactions appear early; then monitor at intervals set by policy and at the end.
7. **Transfusion reaction**: stop the transfusion, keep the IV line open with normal saline, check vitals, inform the doctor and blood bank, re-check the identity and bag, send the bag, set and blood/urine samples, and complete a transfusion reaction form.
8. Document the unit number, times, vital signs, volume and any reaction.
9. Haemovigilance: reactions are reported through the national haemovigilance programme.

## 7.7 Prevention of IV complications

| Complication | Signs | Prevention / nursing action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phlebitis** (inflamed vein) | Pain, redness, warmth, swelling along the vein, a hard cord | Aseptic insertion, secure the cannula, appropriate size; assess the site every shift with a phlebitis score (e.g. VIP score); remove the cannula at the first signs |
| **Infiltration** (non-irritant fluid into tissues) | Swelling, coolness, pallor, slow flow | Check the site regularly; stop and remove; elevate the limb |
| **Extravasation** (irritant/vesicant drug leaks) | Pain, burning, blistering, tissue damage | Stop at once, leave the cannula in until the doctor decides, follow the extravasation protocol |
| Infection (local and bloodstream) | Pus, fever | Hand hygiene, aseptic technique, scrub the hub, dressing care |
| **Air embolism** | Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, low BP | Prime lines fully, close clamps, secure connections; if suspected, place on the left side with head down and give oxygen, call for help |
| **Circulatory overload** | Breathlessness, crackles, raised BP, distended neck veins | Correct rate (infusion pump), monitor intake and output, especially in elderly, children and heart patients |
| Occlusion, dislodgement | No flow | Secure fixation; flush as per protocol |

Remove peripheral cannulas when no longer needed, and re-site according to hospital policy (often when clinically indicated rather than routinely). Label each cannula with the date of insertion.

## 7.8 Prevention of falls

**Risk factors**: age over 65, history of falls, confusion, dizziness, weakness, sedatives and blood-pressure drugs, frequent toileting, poor eyesight, unfamiliar surroundings, wet floors, poor lighting.

**Risk assessment** on admission, after a fall, after transfer and when the condition changes — for example with the **Morse Fall Scale** (history of falls, secondary diagnosis, ambulatory aid, IV line, gait, mental status) or the Humpty Dumpty scale in children.

**Prevention**: identify high-risk patients (wristband / bed sign); bed in the lowest position with brakes locked; side rails as per policy; call bell and personal items within reach; regular toileting rounds; non-slip footwear; good lighting, especially at night; dry, clutter-free floors; assist with walking; review medicines with the doctor; educate patient and family. After a fall: assess for injury, inform the doctor, monitor, report as an incident, and review the plan.

## 7.9 Prevention of deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

DVT is a clot in a deep vein, usually of the leg; a piece can break off and block the lung arteries (pulmonary embolism). Risks (Virchow's triad): slow blood flow (immobility, surgery), injury to the vessel wall, and increased clotting (cancer, pregnancy, dehydration).

**Prevention**: risk assessment on admission (tools such as the Caprini or Padua score); **early ambulation**; leg exercises (ankle pumps, deep breathing); adequate hydration; **graduated compression stockings** and **intermittent pneumatic compression** devices as prescribed; prescribed anticoagulant prophylaxis; avoid pillows under the knees and crossing legs. Watch for calf pain, swelling, warmth and redness, and for sudden breathlessness or chest pain (pulmonary embolism).

## 7.10 Shifting and transporting of patients

- Assess the patient's condition and stability; decide the mode (wheelchair, stretcher, bed) and escort (nurse, doctor) needed.
- **Identify** the patient; take the case file and reports; give a structured **handover** at the destination.
- Carry what is needed: oxygen, portable monitor, suction, emergency drugs for critically ill patients; secure lines, drains and catheters.
- Use **safe transfer techniques**: enough staff, slide sheets/boards, brakes on, side rails up, belts on stretchers; good body mechanics for staff.
- Keep the patient covered, warm and with privacy; communicate with the patient.
- Infectious patients: inform the receiving area; patient wears a mask if needed; clean the trolley after use.

## 7.11 Surgical safety

The **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist** has three parts:

```text
 SIGN IN (before induction of anaesthesia)
  – Identity, site, procedure and consent confirmed
  – Site marked; anaesthesia safety check; pulse oximeter working
  – Known allergy? Difficult airway / aspiration risk? Risk of major blood loss?
 TIME OUT (before skin incision)
  – Team members introduce themselves by name and role
  – Patient, site and procedure confirmed aloud
  – Antibiotic prophylaxis given in the last 60 minutes?
  – Anticipated critical events (surgeon, anaesthetist, nurse); imaging displayed?
 SIGN OUT (before the patient leaves the operating room)
  – Name of procedure recorded; instrument, sponge and needle counts correct
  – Specimens labelled (with patient name); equipment problems noted
  – Key concerns for recovery and management
```

Other surgical safety measures: pre-operative checklist on the ward, correct consent, site marking by the surgeon with the patient awake, fasting status, removal of jewellery and dentures, counting of swabs and instruments by the scrub and circulating nurses, safe use of diathermy, prevention of positioning injuries, and correct labelling of specimens.

## 7.12 Medication reconciliation and administration

**Medication reconciliation** is the process of making an accurate list of all the medicines a patient is taking (prescribed, over-the-counter, herbal) and comparing it with the doctor's orders at **admission, transfer and discharge**, to catch omissions, duplications, wrong doses and interactions. It prevents errors at transitions of care — a common care-coordination event.

**Safe administration — the "rights"**: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, right documentation, plus right reason, right response and the patient's right to refuse. Other measures: check allergies; three checks of the label; do not give medicines prepared by someone else; avoid interruptions during drug rounds; **independent double check** for high-alert medicines; report medication errors and near misses.

## 7.13 Prevention of communication errors

- **Structured handover** at every change of shift and transfer — **SBAR**: **S**ituation, **B**ackground, **A**ssessment, **R**ecommendation (or ISBAR with Identify first).
- **Verbal and telephone orders** only in emergencies: write down, **read back** to confirm, and get them signed within the set time. Critical laboratory results are also read back.
- Avoid **unsafe abbreviations** (a "do-not-use" list: e.g. "U" for units, "OD", trailing zeros "1.0 mg"); write legibly.
- Use the patient's language or an interpreter; teach back to check understanding.
- Closed-loop communication in emergencies.

## 7.14 Prevention of HAI

Covered in Unit 5: hand hygiene, standard and transmission-based precautions, device and surgical bundles, cleaning, safe injection, surveillance — HAI rates are themselves patient safety indicators.

## 7.15 Documentation

Good documentation is a safety measure and a legal record. It should be **accurate, complete, concise, timely** (written at the time of care), **legible**, dated and timed, **signed** with name and designation, factual (what was seen, done and said), in ink or the electronic record; errors are corrected by a single line, initialled and dated — never erased or overwritten. Record assessments, care given, response, incidents, communications with the doctor and patient education. "Not documented means not done."

## 7.16 Incidents and adverse events

### Capturing of incidents

1. Attend to the patient first — ensure safety, give care, inform the doctor.
2. Inform the nurse in charge.
3. **Report** the incident through the hospital system (paper form or electronic) as soon as possible, ideally within the shift / 24 hours; sentinel events immediately.
4. Report all incidents, including **near misses** — they show weak spots before harm happens.
5. The quality / patient safety department classifies incidents by type and severity and decides on investigation.
6. **Disclosure**: the patient and family are told honestly about harmful errors (as per hospital policy).

### Root cause analysis (RCA)

A **systematic investigation** to find the underlying causes (not just the person) of an adverse event, so it does not recur.

**Steps of RCA**

1. Form a team (people not directly involved, plus experts).
2. Collect facts: records, interviews, the scene, equipment.
3. Draw a **timeline / flowchart** of what happened.
4. Identify what went wrong and **contributing factors** — people, task, equipment, environment, communication, policies, organisation.
5. Find the **root causes** using tools such as the "**5 Whys**" (ask "why?" repeatedly) and the **fishbone (Ishikawa, cause-and-effect) diagram**.
6. Develop an action plan (CAPA), share learning, and monitor.

```text
 Fishbone (cause-and-effect) diagram
   People        Methods/policies      Equipment
       \               |                 /
        \______________|________________/____→  PROBLEM (e.g. patient fall)
        /              |                \
       /               |                 \
   Environment   Communication     Patient factors
```

**Example (fall)**: Why did the patient fall? — Got up alone at night. Why? — Call bell out of reach and needed the toilet. Why? — No toileting round and bell left behind the bed. Why? — No fall-risk plan because the risk assessment was not done on admission. Root cause → admission assessment not part of the checklist.

### CAPA (Corrective and Preventive Action)

- **Corrective action** fixes the problem that has happened and its cause (e.g. re-train the staff, repair the equipment, reassess all patients in the ward).
- **Preventive action** stops a similar problem happening elsewhere or in future (e.g. add fall-risk assessment to the admission checklist across the hospital, audit compliance monthly).
- Each action has an **owner, a deadline and a measure of success**; effectiveness is checked by follow-up audits.

### Report writing

An incident report is **factual, objective and complete**:

- Date, time and exact place of the incident.
- Persons involved (patient, staff, witnesses) — identified as per policy.
- **What happened**, in clear sequence, in your own words, describing only what you saw and did (no guesses, no blame, no opinions).
- Patient's condition, injuries and care given, who was informed and when (doctor, family).
- Immediate actions taken.
- Name, designation and signature of the reporter.
- The incident report is a confidential quality document; do not copy it into the patient's notes, but record the facts of the event and care given in the notes.

## 7.17 International Patient Safety Goals (IPSG)

The IPSGs are set by **Joint Commission International (JCI)** and used in accreditation; NABH in India has similar standards. They focus on the areas where errors most often cause harm.

| Goal | What it means | Key nursing actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Identify patients correctly** | Use at least **two identifiers** (e.g. full name and date of birth or hospital number) — **never the room or bed number** | Check the wristband and ask the patient to state their name and date of birth before medicines, blood, specimens, procedures; label specimens at the bedside |
| **2. Improve effective communication** | Verbal and telephone orders and critical results are written down and **read back**; structured handover | SBAR handover; read-back; avoid abbreviations |
| **3. Improve the safety of high-alert medications** | Medicines that cause serious harm if given wrongly: **concentrated electrolytes** (potassium chloride, hypertonic saline), insulin, heparin and other anticoagulants, opioids, chemotherapy, sedatives; **look-alike, sound-alike (LASA)** drugs | Store concentrated electrolytes away from ward stock or labelled; independent double check; "tall man" lettering and separate storage for LASA drugs; high-alert stickers |
| **4. Ensure safe surgery** (correct site, correct procedure, correct patient) | Pre-operative verification, site marking, time-out | WHO Surgical Safety Checklist; counts |
| **5. Reduce the risk of healthcare-associated infections** | Evidence-based hand hygiene programme | 5 Moments; bundles |
| **6. Reduce the risk of patient harm resulting from falls** | Assess and reassess fall risk; take action | Morse scale; fall precautions |

The INC syllabus adds **reducing harm associated with clinical alarm systems** (an alarm safety goal used in some accreditation programmes, e.g. by the US Joint Commission): **alarm fatigue** happens when staff hear so many alarms that they ignore or silence them. Prevention: set alarm limits for each patient, never switch alarms off, ensure alarms are audible at the nursing station, respond promptly, replace sensors and electrodes correctly to reduce false alarms, and check alarms at handover.

## Remember

- Near miss = did not reach the patient; adverse event = harm; sentinel event = death or serious harm.
- Report every incident including near misses; just culture, not blame.
- RCA tools: 5 Whys, fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, timeline. CAPA = corrective (fix now) + preventive (stop recurrence).
- Restraint: last resort, doctor's order, least restrictive, shortest time, check frequently, release regularly, document.
- Blood: two-person bedside check, stay for the first 15 minutes, complete within the set time (usually 4 hours), stop at any reaction.
- Falls: Morse Fall Scale; bed low, brakes on, call bell in reach.
- DVT: early ambulation, stockings, compression devices, prescribed prophylaxis.
- WHO checklist: Sign in, Time out, Sign out.
- Medication reconciliation at admission, transfer and discharge.
- SBAR handover; read back verbal orders.
- IPSG: identify correctly (2 identifiers), communicate effectively, high-alert medicines, safe surgery, reduce HAI, reduce falls (+ clinical alarms in the INC list).
