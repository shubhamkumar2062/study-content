# Unit 6: Disinfection and sterilization, specimen collection, biomedical waste, laundry and antibiotic stewardship

Section B (Infection Control and Safety), INC Units IV, V, VI and VII joined. Each of these units is only one or two hours, and they share one idea: handling instruments, samples, waste, linen and antibiotics so that infection does not spread and organisms do not become resistant.

## 6.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Sterilization** | Process that destroys or removes **all** forms of microbial life, **including spores** |
| **Disinfection** | Process that destroys most pathogenic micro-organisms on inanimate objects, but usually **not spores** |
| **Antisepsis** | Destruction or inhibition of micro-organisms on **living tissue** (skin, mucosa); the chemical is an antiseptic |
| **Cleaning** | Removal of visible dirt, blood and organic matter with water and detergent; it must always come **before** disinfection or sterilization, because organic matter protects microbes and inactivates chemicals |
| **Decontamination** | Process that makes an item safe to handle (cleaning, disinfection or sterilization) |
| Bactericidal / bacteriostatic | Kills bacteria / stops their growth |
| Sporicidal, virucidal, fungicidal | Kills spores / viruses / fungi |
| Sanitisation | Reduces microbes to a level judged safe by public health standards (e.g. kitchen utensils) |

**Levels of disinfection**

| Level | Kills | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High-level disinfection (HLD)** | All micro-organisms except large numbers of bacterial spores | Glutaraldehyde 2%, ortho-phthalaldehyde, hydrogen peroxide, peracetic acid; boiling (in the field) |
| **Intermediate-level** | Bacteria including *M. tuberculosis*, most viruses and fungi; not spores | Alcohols (60–90%), chlorine compounds, phenolics, iodophors |
| **Low-level** | Most vegetative bacteria, some viruses and fungi; not TB or spores | Quaternary ammonium compounds, some phenolics |

## 6.2 Spaulding's principle (classification of patient-care items)

Spaulding classified items by the **risk of infection** from their use, which decides the level of processing.

| Category | Contact with | Processing | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Critical** | Enter sterile tissue or the vascular system | **Sterilization** | Surgical instruments, needles, implants, urinary catheters, IV cannulas, biopsy forceps |
| **Semi-critical** | Mucous membranes or non-intact skin | **High-level disinfection** (sterilization preferred if possible) | Endoscopes, laryngoscope blades, endotracheal tubes, respiratory therapy and anaesthesia equipment, vaginal specula |
| **Non-critical** | Intact skin only | **Low- or intermediate-level disinfection** or cleaning | BP cuffs, stethoscopes, bedpans, bed rails, crutches, thermometers (non-mucosal), furniture |

## 6.3 Types of sterilization

### Physical methods

| Method | Conditions | Used for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Moist heat — autoclave (steam under pressure)** | **121 °C at 15 lb/in² (about 1 atmosphere above normal) for 15–20 minutes**; or 134 °C for about 3–4 minutes in high-vacuum autoclaves | Surgical instruments, linen, dressings, gowns, culture media, glass, rubber that tolerates heat | Most widely used and reliable; kills spores; steam must reach every surface — pack loosely |
| Boiling | 100 °C for 10–30 minutes | Field use | Not sterilization — spores and some viruses survive; only high-level disinfection |
| Pasteurization | 63 °C for 30 minutes or 72 °C for 15–20 seconds | Milk; some respiratory equipment | Kills most vegetative bacteria, not spores |
| **Dry heat — hot air oven** | **160 °C for 2 hours** (or 170 °C for 1 hour, 180 °C for 30 minutes) | Glassware, metal instruments, powders, oils, petroleum jelly, sharp instruments | Cannot be used for rubber, plastics, linen |
| Flaming, red heat | Direct flame | Inoculating loops, needles in the laboratory | — |
| **Incineration** | High temperature burning | Infectious waste, anatomical waste | Reduces waste to ash |
| **Radiation** — ionising (gamma rays) | Cold sterilization | Disposable syringes, catheters, sutures, gloves (industrial) | Used by manufacturers |
| Radiation — non-ionising (ultraviolet) | UV light | Surfaces, air in safety cabinets and operating rooms | Poor penetration |
| **Filtration** | Membrane filters (about 0.22 µm) and HEPA filters | Heat-sensitive liquids (sera, antibiotic solutions); air in operating theatres and isolation rooms | Removes bacteria; may not remove small viruses |
| **Plasma sterilization** (hydrogen peroxide gas plasma) | Low temperature | Heat-sensitive instruments, electronics | Short cycle, no toxic residue |

### Chemical methods

| Agent | Use | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ethylene oxide (ETO) gas** | Heat- and moisture-sensitive items: plastics, catheters, endoscopes, heart–lung machine parts | Sterilizes; toxic, flammable and carcinogenic — needs long aeration before use |
| **Glutaraldehyde 2%** (activated) | Endoscopes, anaesthetic equipment | HLD in about 20 minutes; sterilization after much longer contact (about 10 hours); irritant — use in ventilated area, rinse items well with sterile water |
| Formaldehyde (formalin; fumigation) | Fumigation of rooms in some settings, preserving specimens (10% formalin) | Irritant; use declining |
| **Chlorine compounds** — sodium hypochlorite (bleach) | Blood and body-fluid spills (**1% available chlorine** for spills; 0.5% often for surfaces and in some protocols), water disinfection, floors | Corrosive to metals; inactivated by organic matter; make fresh solution daily |
| **Alcohols** (ethyl or isopropyl, 60–90%, best about 70%) | Skin antisepsis, hand rub, small surfaces, thermometers | Do not kill spores; evaporate quickly; flammable |
| Phenolics (phenol, cresol, chloroxylenol) | Floors, environmental surfaces; some antiseptics | — |
| Iodine and iodophors (povidone-iodine) | Skin antisepsis before surgery and injections | Broad spectrum |
| Chlorhexidine (with alcohol) | Surgical skin preparation, hand antiseptic, central line care, oral care | Long-lasting action on skin |
| Hydrogen peroxide | Wound cleaning (3%), surface disinfection, plasma sterilization | — |
| Quaternary ammonium compounds | Floors, walls, furniture (low level) | Inactivated by soap |

### Monitoring sterilization

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Physical (mechanical) | Temperature, pressure and time gauges and printouts of each cycle |
| Chemical | Autoclave indicator tape (stripes change colour when exposed — shows exposure, not sterility); internal chemical indicators in each pack; Bowie–Dick test daily for air removal in vacuum autoclaves |
| **Biological** (the most reliable) | Spores of ***Geobacillus stearothermophilus*** for the autoclave (and hydrogen peroxide plasma); spores of ***Bacillus atrophaeus*** (formerly *B. subtilis* var. *niger*) for dry heat and ethylene oxide |

**CSSD** (Central Sterile Services Department) receives used items, cleans, inspects, packs, sterilizes, stores and issues them — with one-way flow from dirty to clean to sterile areas. Sterile packs are stored in a clean, dry, closed place; check the indicator, expiry (or event-related shelf life) and that the pack is intact and dry before use.

## 6.4 Environment and equipment cleaning

**Environment cleaning principles**

1. Clean from **clean areas to dirty areas** and from **top to bottom**; clean before disinfecting.
2. Use **damp/wet mopping** — never dry sweeping, which raises dust; use the **three-bucket** (or two-bucket) system: detergent solution, plain rinse water, and wringing.
3. Separate colour-coded mops and cloths for different areas (e.g. ward, toilet, kitchen).
4. **High-touch surfaces** (bed rails, call bells, switches, door handles, monitors, bedside tables) need cleaning more often, at least daily and more often in ICUs.
5. **Terminal cleaning** after discharge, transfer or death, and after isolation, includes all surfaces, mattress and furniture.
6. **Blood and body-fluid spill management**: wear gloves (and apron, eye protection if needed) → cover the spill with absorbent material → pour 1% sodium hypochlorite (or use a spill kit with granules) → leave for the contact time (about 10–20 minutes as per policy) → wipe up from the edge towards the centre with paper towels → discard into the yellow bag → clean the area with detergent and water → hand hygiene. Broken glass is picked up with forceps or a scoop, not fingers.
7. Housekeeping staff are trained, vaccinated and supervised; cleaning checklists are signed.

**Equipment cleaning**: clean between patients according to Spaulding category; dedicated equipment for isolation patients; follow the manufacturer's instructions; reusable equipment goes to CSSD; single-use items are never reprocessed.

**Guides on use of disinfectants**

- Use the **right agent at the right concentration** for the right contact time, as per the hospital disinfectant policy.
- **Clean first**; organic matter inactivates disinfectants.
- **Dilute correctly**; label containers with name, strength, date and time of preparation; prepare fresh daily (especially hypochlorite).
- Do not **mix chemicals** (bleach with acids or ammonia releases toxic gases).
- Do not top up old solution; keep containers closed; check expiry.
- Wear PPE (gloves, goggles) when preparing; store safely; keep the safety data sheet available.
- Antiseptics are for skin; disinfectants for objects — do not interchange.

## 6.5 Specimen collection

**Principles of specimen collection**

1. Collect the **right specimen** from the **right site** at the **right time** — at the active site of infection, during the acute phase, and **before antibiotics** are started.
2. Identify the patient with **two identifiers**; check the request form.
3. Use **aseptic technique** to avoid contamination with normal flora (e.g. midstream urine, skin antisepsis before blood culture).
4. Collect an **adequate quantity**.
5. Use the **correct sterile, leak-proof container** and transport medium.
6. **Label** the container (not the lid): name, ID number, age/sex, ward, specimen type and site, date and time of collection, collector's initials. Fill the request form: clinical details, provisional diagnosis, antibiotics given.
7. **Transport promptly** — ideally within 1–2 hours; if delayed, store as directed (some refrigerated, some at room temperature).
8. Follow **standard precautions** throughout.

**Types of specimens, techniques and special considerations**

| Specimen | Technique and special points | Container / storage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Blood culture** | Before antibiotics, ideally at the onset of fever. Hand hygiene, gloves; clean the skin with chlorhexidine–alcohol (or alcohol then povidone-iodine) and let it dry; do not touch the site again; disinfect bottle tops. Collect **2 sets from 2 different sites** (not from an existing line unless line infection is suspected — then one set from the line and one from a vein), with the volume stated on the bottle | Aerobic and anaerobic blood culture bottles; send at once; **do not refrigerate** |
| **Urine — midstream clean catch** | Clean the genital area with soap and water; pass the first part into the toilet, collect the middle part, then finish | Sterile universal container; send within 1–2 hours or refrigerate at 2–8 °C (for a limited time per lab policy) |
| Urine — catheterised patient | Clamp the tubing below the port briefly if needed, clean the **sampling port** with alcohol, aspirate with a sterile syringe; never from the bag | Sterile container |
| 24-hour urine | Discard the first morning sample, collect all urine for the next 24 hours including the next morning's first sample | Large container, with preservative if required |
| **Sputum** | Early morning; rinse the mouth with plain water; deep cough after a few deep breaths to bring up sputum from the lungs (not saliva); for TB, collect as directed by the national programme (usually two samples, one early morning) | Sterile wide-mouthed screw-capped container; send quickly |
| **Stool** | Collect a fresh sample in a clean container (not mixed with urine or water), choosing parts with blood or mucus; for amoebic trophozoites examine within 30 minutes; rectal swab in infants | Clean screw-capped container; Cary–Blair medium if delay |
| **Throat swab** | Good light, tongue depressor; rub the tonsils and posterior pharyngeal wall, avoid touching the tongue and cheeks | Sterile swab in transport medium |
| Nasopharyngeal swab (viruses) | Tilt the head; pass a flexible swab along the floor of the nose to the nasopharynx; rotate | Viral transport medium; keep cold as per lab |
| **Wound swab / pus** | Clean the surface with sterile saline first; aspirate pus with a syringe if possible (best); otherwise swab the wound base with rotation (avoid surface slough) | Swab in transport medium; pus in syringe (cap, no needle) or sterile container |
| **CSF** | Collected by a doctor by lumbar puncture under strict asepsis; nurse positions the patient and labels tubes in order | Sterile tubes; **send immediately**, keep at room temperature, never refrigerate |
| Eye, ear, genital swabs | Before topical antibiotics | Swabs in transport media |
| Skin scrapings, hair, nail | For fungi (see Unit 2) | Clean black paper / envelope |
| Blood for serology | Plain tube, avoid haemolysis | Clot, separate serum |

**Transportation of the sample**

- Close caps tightly; put the specimen in a **sealed plastic bag** with the request form in a separate pocket.
- **Triple packaging** for transport outside the building: (1) leak-proof primary container, (2) leak-proof secondary container with enough absorbent material, (3) rigid outer packaging with the biohazard label and sender/receiver details.
- Carry upright in a covered, cleanable box or carrier; never in pockets or hands.
- Keep the right temperature (room temperature, refrigerated, or on ice as the test needs).
- Use pneumatic tube systems only for permitted specimens.

**Staff precautions in handling specimens**

1. Treat all specimens as infectious; follow standard precautions.
2. Wear gloves (and apron, mask, eye protection if splashes are likely).
3. Never recap needles; dispose of sharps at once in a puncture-proof container.
4. Avoid contaminating the outside of containers; wipe spills with disinfectant.
5. Do not eat, drink, or apply cosmetics in specimen areas.
6. Hand hygiene after removing gloves.
7. Label high-risk specimens (e.g. suspected TB, HIV, hepatitis) as per policy.
8. Report needle-stick injuries and spills immediately.

## 6.6 Biomedical waste (BMW) management

**Biomedical waste** is any waste generated during diagnosis, treatment or immunization of humans or animals, or in research or the production and testing of biologicals, and in health camps.

**Laws and guidelines in India**: the **Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016** under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, amended in 2018 and 2019 (the "country ordinance"), with **national guidelines** issued by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and MoHFW on how to apply them (the INC syllabus refers to the national BMW guidelines of 2017). They apply to every healthcare facility, and the **State Pollution Control Board** gives authorisation and monitors compliance.

**Waste management process**

```text
 Generation → SEGREGATION at source (colour-coded) → Collection → Storage
 → Packaging and labelling → Transportation → Treatment and disposal
 (at a Common Bio-medical Waste Treatment Facility, CBWTF)
```

**Segregation — the colour coding (BMW Rules 2016, as amended)**

| Colour | Type of container | Waste | Treatment and disposal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Yellow** | Non-chlorinated plastic bags (separate collection system for chemical liquid waste) | (a) Human anatomical waste (tissues, organs, body parts, placenta); (b) animal anatomical waste; (c) **soiled waste** — items contaminated with blood and body fluids: dressings, cotton swabs, plaster casts, bags with residual blood; (d) expired or discarded medicines, including cytotoxic drugs; (e) chemical waste; (f) chemical liquid waste; (g) discarded linen, mattresses and beddings contaminated with blood or body fluids; (h) microbiology, biotechnology and clinical laboratory waste (cultures, stocks, specimens) — pre-treated on site by autoclaving or a similar method before being sent | **Incineration** or plasma pyrolysis; deep burial only where allowed (rural or remote areas without a CBWTF); chemical liquid waste is pre-treated before discharge |
| **Red** | Non-chlorinated plastic bags or containers | **Contaminated recyclable waste**: tubing, bottles, IV tubes and sets, catheters, urine bags, syringes **without needles** and fixed-needle syringes **with needles cut**, vacutainers, and gloves | Autoclaving / microwaving / hydroclaving, then **shredding** and sent to authorised **recyclers**; must not be incinerated or buried |
| **White (translucent)** | **Puncture-proof, leak-proof, tamper-proof** container | **Waste sharps including metals**: needles, syringes with fixed needles, needles from needle-tip cutters or burners, scalpels, blades, other contaminated sharp objects | Autoclaving or dry-heat sterilization, then shredding or mutilation or encapsulation; sent to an iron foundry or sanitary landfill / sharps pit |
| **Blue** | Puncture-proof, leak-proof cardboard boxes with blue marking (or blue containers) | **Glassware**: broken or discarded and contaminated glass, including medicine vials and ampoules (except those contaminated with cytotoxic waste); metallic body implants also go in blue | Disinfection (by soaking in hypochlorite) or autoclaving / microwaving / hydroclaving, then **recycling** |

Other important points of the rules:

- Segregate **at the point of generation** — the nurse at the bedside is where segregation succeeds or fails.
- Bags and containers carry the **biohazard symbol** (cytotoxic waste carries the cytotoxic hazard symbol) and a **label** with the generator's details, date, category and a **bar code** for tracking.
- Bags are filled to **no more than three-quarters**, then tied and replaced.
- **Untreated waste must not be stored beyond 48 hours**; the CBWTF collects it in covered vehicles.
- **Chlorinated plastic bags, gloves and blood bags** were to be phased out under the rules.
- Laboratory and highly infectious waste is **pre-treated on site** (autoclave) before handing over.
- Liquid waste (blood, body fluids) is disinfected before discharge into drains.
- Records are kept, an **annual report** is sent to the State Pollution Control Board, and accidents (spills, needle-stick injuries) are recorded.
- All workers handling waste are **trained** at least once a year and **immunized** against hepatitis B and tetanus.
- General (non-infectious) waste — paper, food, packaging — goes in separate bins as per municipal solid waste rules (commonly green/black), and must not be mixed with BMW.

**Waste collection and storage**: collect from wards at fixed times in covered trolleys, along routes away from patient and food areas; store in a **separate, locked, ventilated central storage area** with a biohazard sign, away from public access, until collected by the CBWTF.

**Staff precautions in waste handling**

1. Wear heavy-duty gloves, apron, mask, boots (and goggles where needed).
2. Never put hands into bags or bins; do not compress bags; carry bags by the neck away from the body.
3. Do not recap or bend needles; use needle cutters/destroyers; drop sharps into the white container at the point of use.
4. Report injuries at once; hepatitis B vaccination; hand hygiene.
5. Do not open or re-sort segregated bags; do not let waste be sold or reused.

## 6.7 Laundry management

Used linen can carry pathogens; poor handling spreads them to staff and patients.

**Categories**: **used (soiled) linen**; **infected / contaminated linen** (from isolation patients, or soiled with blood or body fluids); and **clean linen**. Hospitals use separate bags (often colour-coded by local policy) for each — this laundry colour code is separate from the BMW colours.

**Laundry management process**

1. **Handle with minimum agitation** — do not shake linen; roll it inward with the soiled side inside.
2. **Bag at the point of use**; do not sort or rinse in patient areas; do not put linen on the floor.
3. Infected linen goes into a separate, clearly marked, leak-proof bag (water-soluble alginate bags in some hospitals so the bag goes straight into the washer).
4. Remove sharps or other items before bagging.
5. Transport in **closed, covered trolleys**; separate trolleys for dirty and clean linen; clean the trolleys regularly.
6. In the laundry, a **one-way flow**: dirty receiving and sorting area → washing → drying → ironing → clean storage, with physical separation.
7. **Washing**: hot water (for example at least 71 °C for 25 minutes, as in CDC guidance) with detergent, or low-temperature wash with an appropriate chemical disinfectant; then drying and ironing.
8. Store clean linen in a clean, dry, closed area; transport covered.
9. Linen so soiled with blood or body fluids that it cannot be reused is discarded as BMW (yellow category).
10. Laundry staff wear PPE (gloves, apron, mask), are vaccinated and trained, and wash hands.

## 6.8 Antibiotic stewardship and antimicrobial resistance

**Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)** is the ability of micro-organisms (bacteria, fungi, viruses, parasites) to survive and multiply in the presence of medicines that once killed them. Infections then become harder, longer and more expensive to treat, and some become untreatable.

**Causes of AMR**

- Overuse and misuse of antibiotics: for viral colds and diarrhoea, without prescription (over-the-counter), wrong choice, incomplete courses.
- Antibiotics in animal farming and agriculture.
- Poor infection control in hospitals — resistant organisms spread on hands and equipment.
- Poor sanitation and water supply in the community.
- Few new antibiotics being developed.

**Important resistant organisms (multidrug-resistant organisms, MDROs)**

| Organism | Short name |
| --- | --- |
| Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* | **MRSA** |
| Vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus* | VRE |
| Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Gram-negative bacteria (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*) | ESBL |
| Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales / *Acinetobacter* / *Pseudomonas* | CRE, CRAB, CRPA |
| Multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant TB | MDR-TB, XDR-TB |
| *Candida auris* (resistant fungus) | — |

The "**ESKAPE**" group of hospital organisms: *Enterococcus faecium*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Enterobacter* species.

**Antibiotic (antimicrobial) stewardship** is a coordinated programme that promotes the appropriate use of antimicrobials — the **right drug, right dose, right route, right time and right duration** — to improve patient outcomes, reduce resistance and avoid side effects such as *C. difficile* infection.

**Importance**: saves lives, preserves antibiotics for the future, reduces HAIs by resistant organisms, side effects and hospital costs.

**Core elements of a hospital stewardship programme** (CDC core elements; ICMR has issued antimicrobial stewardship guidelines for Indian hospitals)

1. **Leadership commitment** — resources and time.
2. **Accountability** — a physician leader.
3. **Pharmacy expertise** — a pharmacist co-leader.
4. **Action** — prescribing policies, prior approval for restricted antibiotics, **prospective audit and feedback**, **de-escalation** after culture reports, IV-to-oral switch, stop dates.
5. **Tracking** — antibiotic use and resistance patterns (hospital **antibiogram**).
6. **Reporting** — feedback to prescribers and staff.
7. **Education** of staff, patients and public.

**National steps in India**: the **National Action Plan on AMR (2017)** with a "One Health" approach (humans, animals, environment); ICMR's antimicrobial resistance surveillance network; restriction of over-the-counter sale of certain antibiotics (Schedule H1); the "**Red Line**" campaign — medicines with a red vertical line on the pack must be sold only on a doctor's prescription. WHO classifies antibiotics into **Access, Watch and Reserve (AWaRe)** groups to guide use. **World AMR Awareness Week** is observed in November.

**Role of the nurse in antibiotic stewardship**

1. Collect cultures **before the first dose** of antibiotic, correctly.
2. Give antibiotics **on time** — the first dose promptly in sepsis; keep correct intervals; check allergies first.
3. Prompt the doctor for **review at 48–72 hours** (an "antibiotic time-out") and for de-escalation, IV-to-oral switch and stop dates when culture reports arrive.
4. Monitor response and side effects; report diarrhoea (possible *C. difficile*).
5. Avoid giving antibiotics without a written order; do not keep leftover antibiotics on the ward.
6. Educate patients to complete the prescribed course, not to self-medicate, not to share or keep leftovers, and to dispose of unused medicines properly.
7. Practise infection prevention — every infection prevented is an antibiotic course not needed.
8. Record and audit antibiotic administration.

**Prevention of MRSA and MDRO in the healthcare setting**

1. **Hand hygiene** at all five moments — the most important measure.
2. **Contact precautions** for colonised or infected patients: single room or cohort, gloves and gown on entry, dedicated equipment.
3. **Screening** (active surveillance cultures) of high-risk patients on admission to ICUs, transplant and surgical units as per policy (nose, groin, rectal swabs).
4. **Decolonisation** of MRSA carriers as prescribed (nasal antibacterial ointment and chlorhexidine body wash).
5. **Environmental cleaning** and disinfection of high-touch surfaces; terminal cleaning.
6. **Antibiotic stewardship** to reduce selection pressure.
7. **Device bundles** (CAUTI, CLABSI, VAP, SSI).
8. **Flagging** the patient's record so precautions continue on readmission and transfer; inform the receiving unit.
9. Education of staff, patients and visitors; surveillance and feedback.

## Remember

- Sterilization kills everything including spores; disinfection does not kill spores; antiseptics are for living tissue; always clean first.
- Spaulding: critical → sterilize; semi-critical → high-level disinfection; non-critical → low-level disinfection.
- Autoclave 121 °C, 15 lb/in², 15–20 min (indicator: *Geobacillus stearothermophilus*). Hot air oven 160 °C for 2 h (indicator: *Bacillus atrophaeus*). ETO for heat-sensitive items. Glutaraldehyde 2% for endoscopes.
- Blood spill: 1% hypochlorite, contact time, wipe from edge to centre, yellow bag.
- Specimens: right specimen, site, time, before antibiotics, labelled, quick transport; CSF and blood culture never refrigerated; urine from catheter port, not bag; triple packaging.
- BMW Rules 2016 (amended 2018, 2019): **yellow** — anatomical, soiled, medicines, chemical, lab waste → incineration; **red** — contaminated recyclable plastics → autoclave, shred, recycle; **white translucent** — sharps; **blue** — glass and metal implants. No storage beyond 48 hours. Bags ¾ full; bar code labels.
- Linen: no shaking, bag at point of use, separate infected linen, closed trolleys, hot wash.
- Stewardship: right drug, dose, route, time, duration; culture first; review at 48–72 hours; de-escalate. NAP-AMR 2017; Red Line campaign; AWaRe.
- MRSA/MDRO: hand hygiene, contact precautions, screening, decolonisation, cleaning, stewardship.
