# Unit 3: Drugs acting on the respiratory system and the skin

INC Unit IV (2 hours) — antiasthmatics and bronchodilators (salbutamol inhalers), decongestants, expectorants, antitussives and mucolytics, bronchoconstrictors and antihistamines — joined here with INC Unit VII (1 hour) — drugs for the skin: antihistamines and antipruritics, benzyl benzoate, gamma BHC, clotrimazole, miconazole and silver sulfadiazine for burns. The two units are taught together because antihistamines belong to both. Doses are not given; follow the prescriber's order.

## 3.1 Asthma and COPD in brief

In **bronchial asthma**, allergens, infection, cold air, exercise, smoke or drugs (aspirin, β-blockers) trigger an inflammatory response in sensitive airways. Mast cells release histamine and leukotrienes → bronchial smooth muscle contracts, the mucosa swells and thick mucus is produced. The patient has wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, often worse at night. In **COPD** (chronic bronchitis and emphysema, usually from smoking) the airflow limitation is largely fixed.

Two kinds of drug are needed:

```text
 ASTHMA DRUGS
 ├── Relievers (bronchodilators) — open the airway quickly
 │     β₂ agonists, anticholinergics, methylxanthines
 └── Controllers (preventers) — reduce inflammation over time
       inhaled corticosteroids, leukotriene antagonists,
       mast cell stabilisers, anti-IgE antibody
```

## 3.2 Bronchodilators

**β₂-adrenergic agonists** — stimulate β₂ receptors on bronchial muscle → cAMP rises → relaxation.

| Type | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short-acting (SABA) | Salbutamol, terbutaline | Quick relief of an attack; before exercise |
| Long-acting (LABA) | Salmeterol, formoterol | Long-term control, only together with an inhaled steroid |

- Routes: inhaler (metered-dose or dry-powder), nebuliser, oral, injection. Inhalation is preferred: rapid action with fewer side effects.
- Side effects: tremor of the hands, palpitations, tachycardia, nervousness, headache, low potassium (with frequent use), muscle cramps.
- Terbutaline and salbutamol relax the uterus too, so they have been used to delay preterm labour.
- Caution in heart disease, hyperthyroidism and diabetes.
- Interactions: non-selective β-blockers (propranolol) block their effect and may provoke bronchospasm.

**Anticholinergics** — ipratropium (short-acting), tiotropium (long-acting). Block muscarinic receptors in the airways → bronchodilation and less mucus. Preferred in COPD; ipratropium added to salbutamol in acute severe asthma. Side effects: dry mouth, bitter taste; caution in glaucoma (keep the nebuliser mist away from the eyes) and enlarged prostate.

**Methylxanthines** — theophylline, aminophylline (IV form), doxofylline. Relax bronchial muscle (phosphodiesterase inhibition and adenosine blockade) and stimulate breathing.
- Narrow therapeutic index; blood levels are monitored.
- Side effects: nausea, vomiting, restlessness, insomnia, tachycardia, arrhythmias and convulsions at high levels.
- Interactions: levels rise with erythromycin, ciprofloxacin and cimetidine (enzyme inhibitors) and fall in smokers and with rifampicin.
- Aminophylline is given by slow IV injection or infusion only.

## 3.3 Anti-inflammatory (controller) drugs

**Corticosteroids** — the most effective controllers.
- Inhaled: beclomethasone, budesonide, fluticasone. Reduce airway inflammation, mucus and hyper-reactivity; regular daily use prevents attacks; they do not relieve an acute attack.
- Side effects of inhaled steroids: oral thrush (candidiasis) and hoarseness — **rinse mouth and gargle after each use**; a spacer reduces deposition in the mouth.
- Systemic: oral prednisolone, IV hydrocortisone in acute severe asthma (side effects in Unit 6).

**Leukotriene receptor antagonist** — montelukast: blocks leukotriene action; oral; used for mild asthma, exercise-induced asthma and allergic rhinitis. Report mood changes or sleep disturbance.

**Mast cell stabilisers** — sodium cromoglycate, ketotifen: prevent release of histamine from mast cells. Prophylaxis only; useless in an acute attack.

**Anti-IgE monoclonal antibody** — omalizumab (SC injection) for severe allergic asthma.

## 3.4 Acute severe asthma — what the nurse does

1. Sit the patient upright; stay calm and reassure.
2. Give oxygen as ordered and monitor SpO₂.
3. Give nebulised salbutamol (with ipratropium if ordered); systemic steroid as ordered.
4. Monitor respiratory rate, pulse, speech (can the patient finish a sentence?), use of accessory muscles, peak flow and SpO₂.
5. Danger signs to report at once: silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion, confusion, falling pulse rate.
6. Avoid sedatives — they depress breathing.

## 3.5 Inhaler technique — teaching the patient

**Metered-dose inhaler (MDI)**

1. Remove the cap and shake the inhaler.
2. Breathe out fully, away from the inhaler.
3. Seal the lips around the mouthpiece (or use a spacer).
4. Start a slow deep breath and press the canister once at the same time.
5. Continue breathing in slowly and deeply.
6. Hold the breath for about 10 seconds, then breathe out slowly.
7. Wait about a minute before a second puff if ordered.
8. After a steroid inhaler, rinse the mouth and spit.

A **spacer** with a mask is used for children and anyone who cannot coordinate pressing and breathing. **Dry-powder inhalers** need a fast, deep breath and must not be shaken or breathed into. A **nebuliser** turns a solution into a mist through a mask — useful in severe attacks and small children. Teach the patient to carry the reliever at all times, to know the triggers, and to check the dose counter.

## 3.6 Decongestants

Nasal decongestants are α-adrenergic agonists that constrict the swollen blood vessels of the nasal mucosa.

- Topical: xylometazoline, oxymetazoline, phenylephrine nasal drops/sprays. Quick relief in colds and sinusitis, and before nasal procedures. Using them for more than a few days causes **rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa)**.
- Oral: pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine — often in "cold" combinations. They can raise blood pressure and heart rate, cause insomnia and urinary retention.
- Avoid or use with care in hypertension, heart disease, hyperthyroidism, glaucoma, enlarged prostate, and with MAO inhibitors. Saline drops are safest for infants.

## 3.7 Expectorants, mucolytics and antitussives

Cough is a protective reflex. A **productive** (wet) cough should not be suppressed; a **dry, irritating** cough that disturbs sleep may be suppressed.

| Group | Examples | How they act | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Expectorants | Guaifenesin, ammonium chloride, potassium iodide; steam inhalation | Increase the volume and looseness of respiratory secretions so they are coughed out | Plenty of oral fluids helps most |
| Mucolytics | Bromhexine, ambroxol, carbocisteine, acetylcysteine | Break mucus fibres and lower viscosity | Acetylcysteine nebulisation can cause bronchospasm (give with a bronchodilator) |
| Antitussives – opioid | Codeine, pholcodine | Depress the cough centre | Drowsiness, constipation, dependence; not for children under 12 years |
| Antitussives – non-opioid | Dextromethorphan, noscapine | Depress the cough centre | Little sedation; no analgesia |
| Antihistamines | Diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine | Sedation and drying of secretions | In many cough syrups |
| Demulcents | Lozenges, honey, glycerin syrups | Soothe the throat | Safe |

**Nursing**: assess the cough (dry or productive, sputum colour, amount, blood); encourage fluids, steam inhalation, deep breathing and chest physiotherapy; do not give suppressants in productive cough or COPD with thick sputum; warn about drowsiness with codeine and antihistamine syrups; keep syrups away from children.

## 3.8 Bronchoconstrictors

Drugs that narrow the airways are mainly important because they can **worsen asthma**:

- **Histamine** and **methacholine** — used only in a controlled bronchial challenge test to diagnose airway hyper-reactivity.
- **Non-selective β-blockers** (propranolol) and even β₁-selective ones at high levels — contraindicated in asthma.
- **Cholinergic drugs** (neostigmine, pilocarpine) — increase bronchial secretions and constriction.
- **Aspirin and other NSAIDs** — in aspirin-sensitive asthma.
- Adenosine, some contrast media.

Always ask a patient with asthma about these drugs and check orders before giving them.

## 3.9 Antihistamines (H₁-receptor antagonists)

Histamine is stored in mast cells and basophils. Through **H₁ receptors** it causes bronchoconstriction, vasodilatation, increased capillary permeability (swelling, wheals), itching and sneezing; through **H₂ receptors** it increases gastric acid.

| Generation | Examples | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | Chlorpheniramine, diphenhydramine, promethazine, hydroxyzine, cyproheptadine, dimenhydrinate | Enter the brain → drowsiness; anticholinergic effects; antiemetic and anti-motion-sickness effects |
| Second | Cetirizine, levocetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine | Little or no sedation; once-daily |

**Uses**: allergic rhinitis, urticaria and itching, allergic conjunctivitis, insect bites, drug reactions, motion sickness and vomiting (first generation), sleep (promethazine), and as an add-on in anaphylaxis — **adrenaline is the first drug in anaphylaxis, not an antihistamine**.

**Side effects**: sedation, poor concentration, dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention; paradoxical excitement in children; confusion and falls in the elderly.

**Contraindications/cautions**: glaucoma, enlarged prostate, driving or operating machines, with alcohol or other sedatives, very young infants.

**Nursing**: give sedating antihistamines at bedtime; warn not to drive; keep the bed rails up for elderly patients; mouth care for dryness; ask about other sedatives and alcohol.

## 3.10 Antipruritics

Itching (pruritus) is treated by removing the cause and by:
- **Oral antihistamines** (hydroxyzine, cetirizine, chlorpheniramine).
- **Calamine lotion** (zinc oxide and ferric oxide): soothing and cooling; shake before use.
- **Menthol and camphor** in creams: cooling.
- **Crotamiton**: antipruritic that also acts against scabies mites.
- **Topical corticosteroids** for inflammatory itchy rashes (eczema): apply a thin layer, not on broken or infected skin, avoid long use on the face (thinning of skin).
- Emollients for dry skin.

Nursing: keep nails short, cool cotton clothes, avoid hot baths and strong soaps, pat skin dry.

## 3.11 Drugs for scabies and lice

**Scabies** is caused by the mite *Sarcoptes scabiei*; it spreads by close contact.

**Benzyl benzoate**
- Acts by killing mites and their eggs; applied as an emulsion.
- Method: after a bath, dry the skin and apply over the whole body from the neck down (in infants including scalp and face, avoiding eyes), especially between fingers, wrists, axillae, genitals and under nails; leave on and repeat as prescribed.
- Side effects: burning and irritation (worse on broken skin), contact dermatitis; avoid the eyes and mucous membranes. It is diluted for children as directed.

**Gamma benzene hexachloride (gamma BHC, lindane)**
- An organochlorine that kills mites and lice by acting on their nervous system.
- It can be absorbed through skin and cause nerve toxicity, including convulsions, especially in infants, young children, pregnant and breastfeeding women, the elderly and people with broken skin or seizures. For this reason it is restricted or withdrawn in many countries and is not the first choice now.
- Applied only once to cool, dry skin (not after a hot bath, which increases absorption), and washed off after the prescribed time.

**Preferred now**: permethrin cream (topical, first choice) or oral ivermectin. Crotamiton is an alternative.

**Nursing for scabies**: treat all close contacts at the same time; wash clothes, towels and bed linen in hot water and dry in the sun; items that cannot be washed are sealed in a bag for a few days; trim nails; explain that itching may continue for 2–4 weeks after successful treatment and does not mean failure.

## 3.12 Topical antifungals: clotrimazole and miconazole

Both are **imidazole** antifungals. They inhibit the fungal enzyme that makes ergosterol (an essential part of the fungal cell membrane) → the membrane leaks and the fungus dies.

- **Uses**: ringworm (tinea corporis, cruris, pedis), cutaneous and vaginal candidiasis, oral thrush (clotrimazole lozenges, miconazole oral gel), pityriasis versicolor, fungal ear infections.
- **Forms**: cream, powder, lotion, vaginal pessary or cream, oral gel or troche.
- **Side effects**: local burning, itching, redness; rarely contact allergy.
- **Interaction**: miconazole oral gel is absorbed enough to increase the effect of warfarin (bleeding) — report bruising.
- **Nursing/teaching**: clean and dry the area; apply a thin layer beyond the edge of the lesion; continue for 2 weeks after the skin looks clear to prevent relapse; keep skin folds dry; use separate towels; vaginal preparations at bedtime, and treat the partner if advised.

## 3.13 Silver sulfadiazine in burns

- Combines silver (toxic to bacteria) with a sulfonamide; broad spectrum, including *Pseudomonas* and *Staphylococcus*, and some *Candida*.
- **Use**: prevention and treatment of infection in second- and third-degree burns; also some leg ulcers.
- Applied as a cream with a sterile gloved hand or spatula to the cleaned burn, covered or left exposed as per unit policy; old cream is gently removed at each dressing change.
- **Side effects**: burning or itching, skin discoloration, transient fall in white cell count, allergy in people sensitive to sulfonamides.
- **Avoid**: sulfonamide allergy, late pregnancy and newborns in the first months of life (risk of kernicterus), G6PD deficiency (caution).
- **Nursing**: strict asepsis; give analgesia before dressing; observe the wound for infection (smell, colour of discharge, fever); monitor white cell count when large areas are treated; maintain fluids and nutrition.

## 3.14 General rules for applying topical drugs

1. Check the order, the site and the patient; wear gloves.
2. Clean and dry the skin; remove old applications gently.
3. Apply thinly in the direction of hair growth; do not rub too hard.
4. Do not apply on broken skin unless the drug is meant for wounds.
5. Watch for local irritation and allergy; record and report.

## Remember

- Salbutamol (SABA) = reliever; inhaled steroid = controller; LABA always with a steroid.
- β₂ agonists: tremor, tachycardia, low potassium. Theophylline: narrow index; levels rise with erythromycin and ciprofloxacin.
- Rinse the mouth after inhaled steroids (thrush, hoarseness).
- MDI: shake, breathe out, press and breathe in slowly, hold about 10 seconds.
- Nasal decongestants for a few days only (rebound congestion).
- Do not suppress a productive cough; codeine not in children under 12.
- Non-selective β-blockers and aspirin can provoke bronchospasm in asthma.
- First-generation antihistamines sedate; adrenaline, not an antihistamine, is first in anaphylaxis.
- Scabies: treat all contacts; permethrin is first choice now; lindane (gamma BHC) is neurotoxic.
- Clotrimazole and miconazole block ergosterol synthesis; continue 2 weeks after clearing.
- Silver sulfadiazine for burns; avoid in sulfa allergy, late pregnancy and newborns.
