# Unit 6: Drugs used in endocrine disorders

INC Unit VI (2 hours): insulin and oral hypoglycaemics, thyroid and antithyroid drugs, corticosteroids and anabolic steroids, and calcitonin, parathormone, vitamin D₃ and calcium salts in calcium metabolism. Doses are not given; follow the prescriber's order and the product leaflet.

## 6.1 Diabetes mellitus and insulin in brief

Insulin is made by the **β-cells of the islets of Langerhans** in the pancreas. It lowers blood glucose by moving glucose into muscle and fat cells, storing glucose as glycogen in the liver and muscles, and stopping glucose production by the liver; it also promotes fat and protein storage and moves potassium into cells.

- **Type 1 diabetes**: β-cells destroyed → no insulin → insulin is essential for life; risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).
- **Type 2 diabetes**: insulin resistance and gradual β-cell failure → lifestyle, oral or injectable drugs, and insulin later if needed.
- **Gestational diabetes**: diet, metformin or insulin.

## 6.2 Insulin

**Sources**: older insulins came from pork and beef pancreas; today **human insulin** is made by recombinant DNA technology, and **insulin analogues** have small changes in structure to alter the speed and length of action.

**Types of insulin** (onset and duration are approximate and vary with the product, site and person):

| Type | Examples | Appearance | Onset | Peak | Duration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rapid-acting analogue | Lispro, aspart, glulisine | Clear | About 10–15 minutes | About 1–2 hours | About 3–5 hours |
| Short-acting | Regular (soluble) human insulin | Clear | About 30 minutes | About 2–4 hours | About 6–8 hours |
| Intermediate-acting | NPH (isophane) insulin | Cloudy | About 1–2 hours | About 4–10 hours | About 12–18 hours |
| Long-acting analogue | Glargine, detemir, degludec | Clear | 1–2 hours | Little or no peak | About 24 hours or more |
| Premixed | e.g. 30% regular + 70% NPH; analogue mixes | Cloudy | — | Two peaks | — |

**Regular insulin is the only type given IV** (DKA, hyperkalaemia, surgery, critically ill patients). All types can be given SC.

**Uses**: type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes not controlled by other drugs, DKA and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state, diabetes in pregnancy, diabetes during surgery, infection or stress, and (with glucose) to lower dangerously high potassium.

**Adverse effects**
- **Hypoglycaemia** — the most important. Causes: too much insulin, missed or late meals, extra exercise, alcohol, kidney failure. Signs: sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, anxiety, headache, confusion, abnormal behaviour, slurred speech, convulsions, unconsciousness. β-blockers can hide the early signs.
- **Lipodystrophy** (lumps or hollows of fat) when the same site is used repeatedly — injections there are absorbed unevenly.
- Weight gain, low potassium, local or general allergy, temporary blurring of vision when control improves.

**Managing hypoglycaemia (nursing)**
1. If conscious and able to swallow: give fast-acting sugar by mouth (glucose tablets, sugar or glucose in water, fruit juice) as per the hospital protocol; recheck blood glucose after about 15 minutes and repeat if still low; then give a snack or the next meal containing complex carbohydrate.
2. If unconscious or unable to swallow: nothing by mouth; place in the recovery position; give IV dextrose or IM/SC glucagon as ordered or as per protocol; call for help.
3. Find the cause and record the episode.

**Insulin administration — nursing points**
1. Check the type, the strength (units per mL) and the matching syringe or pen; insulin is a **high-alert drug** — double-check with another nurse.
2. Check blood glucose before giving and relate the timing to meals (rapid-acting just before eating, regular about 30 minutes before).
3. Roll cloudy insulin gently between the palms to mix — do not shake.
4. When mixing in one syringe: **draw up clear (regular) before cloudy (NPH)**; never mix glargine with other insulins.
5. Sites: abdomen (fastest absorption, avoid the area around the umbilicus), outer upper arms, front and outer thighs, buttocks. **Rotate** within a region.
6. Pinch a skin fold if needed and inject at the angle suited to the needle length; do not massage afterwards.
7. Storage: unopened vials and pens in the refrigerator (not the freezer, not in the door shelf next to the freezer); the vial or pen in use can be kept at room temperature away from heat and sunlight for the period stated on the pack; discard if frozen, clumped or discoloured; write the opening date.
8. Teach the patient and family: self-injection, home glucose monitoring, signs and treatment of hypoglycaemia, carrying sugar and an identity card, sick-day rules (never stop insulin when ill; check glucose more often and contact the doctor), foot care, diet and exercise.

## 6.3 Oral and other non-insulin antidiabetic drugs

| Group | Examples | How they act | Important side effects / points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Biguanide | Metformin | Reduces glucose output from the liver; improves insulin sensitivity | First choice in type 2; GI upset (take with food), metallic taste, vitamin B₁₂ deficiency with long use; rare lactic acidosis — withheld in kidney failure, severe liver disease and as ordered before iodinated contrast and major surgery; does not cause hypoglycaemia alone; weight-neutral |
| Sulfonylureas | Glimepiride, gliclazide, glibenclamide, glipizide | Stimulate insulin release from β-cells | **Hypoglycaemia** (especially in the elderly and with kidney disease or missed meals), weight gain; take before or with breakfast |
| Meglitinides | Repaglinide, nateglinide | Short-acting insulin release | Taken just before meals; skip if the meal is skipped |
| DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins) | Sitagliptin, vildagliptin, teneligliptin | Prolong action of the gut hormones (incretins) → more insulin after meals | Low hypoglycaemia risk; rarely pancreatitis |
| SGLT2 inhibitors (gliflozins) | Dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin | Make the kidney pass glucose in urine | Genital fungal and urinary infections, dehydration, rarely ketoacidosis; also protect heart and kidneys |
| Thiazolidinediones (glitazones) | Pioglitazone | Increase insulin sensitivity | Fluid retention, weight gain, heart failure worsening, fractures |
| α-Glucosidase inhibitors | Acarbose, voglibose | Delay carbohydrate digestion in the gut | Flatulence, diarrhoea; treat their hypoglycaemia (with other drugs) with **glucose**, not table sugar |
| GLP-1 receptor agonists | Liraglutide, dulaglutide, semaglutide (injection; semaglutide also oral) | Increase insulin after meals, slow stomach emptying, reduce appetite | Nausea, vomiting; weight loss |

**Interactions**: β-blockers mask hypoglycaemia; alcohol increases the risk of hypoglycaemia; corticosteroids, thiazides and some antipsychotics raise blood glucose.

**Nursing**: monitor blood glucose and HbA1c; give drugs in relation to meals as ordered; teach diet, exercise and foot care; warn about hypoglycaemia with sulfonylureas; encourage regular check-ups for eyes, kidneys and feet.

## 6.4 Thyroid and antithyroid drugs

The thyroid gland makes **thyroxine (T₄)** and **tri-iodothyronine (T₃)** from iodine, under the control of TSH from the pituitary. They increase metabolic rate, heat production, heart rate and are essential for growth and brain development.

### Thyroid hormones (for hypothyroidism)

- **Levothyroxine** (T₄): drug of choice in hypothyroidism (including congenital hypothyroidism — early treatment prevents intellectual disability) and after thyroid removal. Liothyronine (T₃) acts faster and is used in special situations such as myxoedema coma.
- **Administration**: once daily on an empty stomach, usually in the morning about 30–60 minutes before breakfast, with water; keep calcium, iron, antacids and PPIs a few hours apart; usually lifelong.
- **Signs of too much** (report): palpitations, chest pain, tremor, sweating, heat intolerance, weight loss, insomnia, diarrhoea.
- **Caution**: elderly and heart patients are started slowly (may provoke angina).
- **Monitoring**: TSH levels; pulse.

### Antithyroid drugs (for hyperthyroidism)

| Drug | Action | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbimazole, methimazole (thioamides) | Block thyroid hormone synthesis | Take weeks to work; rash, joint pain; **agranulocytosis** (rare, serious) — teach the patient to report **sore throat, fever or mouth ulcers** at once (blood count is checked); avoided in the first trimester |
| Propylthiouracil (PTU) | Blocks synthesis and conversion of T₄ to T₃ | Used in the first trimester of pregnancy and in thyroid storm; can cause liver damage |
| Iodine (Lugol's iodine, potassium iodide) | Rapidly reduces hormone release and the gland's blood supply | Before thyroid surgery for a short period; dilute in milk or juice and give through a straw (taste, teeth staining); also protects the thyroid in radiation emergencies |
| Radioactive iodine (¹³¹I) | Taken up by the gland and destroys thyroid tissue | Oral; contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding; follow radiation safety advice (distance, separate toilet use, avoid close contact with children for the period advised); often leads to hypothyroidism later |
| β-blockers (propranolol) | Relieve tremor, palpitations, anxiety | Do not treat the disease itself; used in thyroid storm and before surgery |

**Nursing**: record pulse, weight and sleep; cool environment and high-calorie diet in hyperthyroidism; eye protection in exophthalmos; observe for signs of the opposite state (hypothyroidism) during treatment; teach the importance of regular blood tests.

## 6.5 Corticosteroids

The adrenal cortex makes **glucocorticoids** (cortisol) and **mineralocorticoids** (aldosterone). Synthetic steroids differ in strength and in how much salt-retaining effect they have.

| Drug | Glucocorticoid (anti-inflammatory) action | Salt-retaining action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrocortisone | Moderate | Some |
| Prednisolone | Stronger | Less |
| Methylprednisolone | Stronger | Little |
| Dexamethasone, betamethasone | Very strong, long-acting | Almost none |
| Fludrocortisone | Weak | Strong (mineralocorticoid replacement) |

**Actions**: reduce inflammation and immune responses; raise blood glucose (gluconeogenesis); break down protein; redistribute fat; retain sodium and water and lose potassium (mainly hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone); suppress the pituitary–adrenal axis.

**Uses**
- **Replacement**: Addison's disease and adrenal insufficiency (hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone), congenital adrenal hyperplasia.
- **Anti-inflammatory / immunosuppressive**: asthma and COPD attacks, severe allergic reactions and anaphylaxis (after adrenaline), rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, inflammatory bowel disease, nephrotic syndrome, skin diseases (topical), eye inflammation, organ transplantation, autoimmune blood disorders.
- **Others**: cerebral oedema around tumours (dexamethasone), prevention of chemotherapy vomiting, some cancers (lymphomas, leukaemias), **antenatal steroids in threatened preterm birth** to mature fetal lungs (betamethasone or dexamethasone), septic shock not responding to vasopressors.

**Adverse effects** (mainly with long-term systemic use)

```text
 C  Cushingoid appearance (moon face, buffalo hump, central obesity, striae)
 U  Ulcers (peptic) — more with NSAIDs
 S  Suppression of the adrenal axis; skin thinning, bruising, acne
 H  Hyperglycaemia, hypertension, hirsutism
 I  Infections (more frequent, masked), impaired wound healing
 N  Necrosis of bone (avascular, femoral head)
 G  Growth retardation in children
 O  Osteoporosis (fractures), oedema
 I  Increased intra-ocular pressure (glaucoma), cataract
 D  Depression, mood swings, psychosis; muscle wasting (myopathy)
```

**Do not stop suddenly** after more than a short course: the adrenal glands have been suppressed, and sudden withdrawal can cause an **adrenal crisis** (weakness, vomiting, low BP, collapse). The doctor reduces the dose gradually (tapering). Extra steroid cover is needed during surgery, injury or severe illness in patients on long-term steroids.

**Nursing responsibilities**
1. Give oral steroids in the morning with food (mimics the natural rhythm and protects the stomach); a PPI may be ordered.
2. Monitor BP, weight, blood glucose, fluid balance, potassium and signs of infection (fever may be masked).
3. Protect from infection: hand hygiene, avoid contact with people who have infections; no live vaccines on high doses.
4. Encourage a diet high in protein, calcium and potassium and low in salt and simple sugars; weight-bearing exercise.
5. Teach: never stop on your own, carry a steroid card, report black stools, mood changes, blurred vision or wounds that do not heal.
6. Inhaled steroids: rinse the mouth (Unit 3). Topical steroids: thin layer, not on infected skin, limited use on the face.

## 6.6 Anabolic steroids

Anabolic steroids are synthetic relatives of testosterone with more protein-building (anabolic) and less masculinising (androgenic) action — for example nandrolone, oxandrolone and stanozolol.

- **Action**: increase protein synthesis and muscle mass, retain nitrogen, stimulate red cell production.
- **Uses** (limited): wasting in chronic illness and severe burns, some anaemias (aplastic, of kidney disease — largely replaced by erythropoietin), occasionally osteoporosis; testosterone itself is used in male hypogonadism.
- **Side effects**: masculinisation in women (deep voice, hirsutism, menstrual irregularity, enlarged clitoris), acne, salt and water retention, raised LDL and lowered HDL cholesterol, liver damage and jaundice with some oral forms, suppression of sperm production and testicular shrinkage, early closure of growth plates in children, mood changes and aggression.
- **Misuse**: by athletes and body-builders to increase muscle mass; they are banned in sport. Nurses should recognise misuse and educate young people.
- **Contraindications**: pregnancy, prostate and breast cancer, liver disease.

## 6.7 Calcium metabolism: parathormone, calcitonin, vitamin D₃ and calcium salts

Blood calcium is kept in a narrow range because it is needed for nerve and muscle function, heart rhythm, clotting and bone.

| Hormone | Source | Effect on blood calcium | How |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parathormone (PTH) | Parathyroid glands | ↑ | Releases calcium from bone, reduces calcium loss in urine, activates vitamin D |
| Calcitriol (active vitamin D₃) | Skin (sunlight) → liver → kidney | ↑ | Increases calcium and phosphate absorption from the gut |
| Calcitonin | C-cells of the thyroid | ↓ | Reduces bone resorption |

**Signs of low calcium (hypocalcaemia)**: tingling around the mouth and in fingers, muscle cramps, **tetany**, Chvostek's sign (twitch of the face on tapping the facial nerve), Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with a BP cuff inflated), laryngospasm, convulsions. Seen after thyroid or parathyroid surgery, in vitamin D deficiency, kidney failure and acute pancreatitis.

**Signs of high calcium (hypercalcaemia)**: thirst, polyuria, constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, confusion, kidney stones ("bones, stones, groans and moans").

### Drugs

**Calcium salts**
- Oral: calcium carbonate (best taken with food), calcium citrate, calcium lactate, calcium gluconate — for prevention and treatment of osteoporosis, rickets and osteomalacia (with vitamin D), pregnancy and lactation, mild hypocalcaemia.
- IV: **calcium gluconate** for acute symptomatic hypocalcaemia (tetany) and severe high potassium (protects the heart) — given only as ordered, **slowly**, with cardiac monitoring; calcium chloride is more irritating to veins.
- Cautions: leakage into tissues causes damage; calcium increases the effect of digoxin (arrhythmias); do not mix with sodium bicarbonate or phosphate in the same line (precipitate); oral calcium reduces absorption of iron, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones and levothyroxine (separate them); constipation and kidney stones with long use.

**Vitamin D preparations**
- Cholecalciferol (D₃), ergocalciferol (D₂): rickets, osteomalacia, osteoporosis, vitamin D deficiency.
- Calcitriol and alfacalcidol (already activated or needing only liver activation): used in **chronic kidney disease** and hypoparathyroidism, because the diseased kidney cannot activate vitamin D.
- Excess causes hypercalcaemia — serum calcium is monitored during treatment; teach the patient not to take high-strength vitamin D on their own.

**Calcitonin** (salmon calcitonin, injection or nasal spray)
- Uses: hypercalcaemia, Paget's disease of bone, pain of acute osteoporotic fractures.
- Side effects: nausea, flushing, local irritation (nasal spray: rhinitis — alternate nostrils).

**Parathormone analogue** — **teriparatide** (daily SC injection): stimulates new bone formation; used in severe osteoporosis for a limited period.

**Other drugs for osteoporosis (for completeness)**: bisphosphonates such as alendronate — taken on an empty stomach in the morning with a full glass of plain water, staying upright for about 30 minutes before food to prevent oesophageal irritation.

**Nursing in calcium disorders**: after thyroid or parathyroid surgery observe for tingling and tetany and keep calcium gluconate available as per protocol; monitor serum calcium; encourage calcium-rich diet (milk, curd, ragi, green leafy vegetables, sesame), sunlight exposure and weight-bearing exercise; fall prevention in osteoporosis.

## Remember

- Regular insulin is the only insulin given IV; NPH is cloudy; draw clear before cloudy; never mix glargine.
- Hypoglycaemia is the main danger of insulin and sulfonylureas: sugar by mouth if conscious; IV dextrose or glucagon if not.
- Rotate injection sites to prevent lipodystrophy; store insulin in the fridge, never frozen.
- Metformin: first choice in type 2; no hypoglycaemia alone; withheld before contrast and in kidney failure; B₁₂ deficiency.
- Acarbose users with hypoglycaemia need glucose, not sucrose. SGLT2 inhibitors: genital infections.
- Levothyroxine: empty stomach, morning, away from calcium and iron; lifelong.
- Carbimazole: report sore throat or fever (agranulocytosis). PTU in first trimester and thyroid storm. Lugol's iodine before thyroidectomy. Radioactive iodine not in pregnancy.
- Steroids: morning with food; hyperglycaemia, infection, osteoporosis, Cushingoid features; **never stop suddenly**; steroid card.
- Anabolic steroids: masculinisation, liver damage, misuse in sport.
- PTH and vitamin D raise calcium; calcitonin lowers it. IV calcium gluconate slowly with monitoring; calcium increases digoxin effect.
