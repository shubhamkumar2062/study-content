# Unit 1: Introduction to pharmacology, pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics

INC Unit I (3 hours): definitions and branches, nature and sources of drugs, dosage forms and routes, terminology, classification, abbreviations, prescription, drug calculation, weights and measures, pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics, principles of drug administration, the Indian Pharmacopoeia and drug laws, rational use of drugs and principles of therapeutics. This unit is the base for every drug you meet later, so learn its terms well.

## 1.1 Definitions and branches

- **Drug** (WHO): any substance or product that is used or intended to be used to modify or explore physiological systems or pathological states for the benefit of the recipient. Drugs are used to prevent, diagnose or treat disease.
- **Pharmacology**: the science of drugs and their interaction with living systems — what a drug does to the body and what the body does to the drug.
- **Medicine**: a drug in a form ready to give to a patient (tablet, injection, syrup).

| Branch | What it studies |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacodynamics | What the drug does to the body: actions and mechanism of action |
| Pharmacokinetics | What the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion (ADME) |
| Pharmacotherapeutics | Use of drugs to prevent and treat disease |
| Chemotherapy | Drugs that kill or stop the growth of microbes, parasites and cancer cells |
| Toxicology | Harmful effects of chemicals and drugs, their detection and treatment |
| Clinical pharmacology | Study of drugs in human beings, healthy and sick |
| Pharmacy | Preparing, compounding and dispensing medicines |
| Pharmacognosy | Drugs obtained from natural sources, mainly plants |
| Pharmacogenetics | How inherited differences change drug response |
| Pharmacoepidemiology | Use and effects of drugs in large populations |

## 1.2 Nature and sources of drugs

Drugs may be solids, liquids or gases; most are weak acids or weak bases, which affects how they are absorbed and excreted.

| Source | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Plants | Morphine (opium poppy), atropine (belladonna), digoxin (foxglove), quinine (cinchona bark) |
| Animals | Heparin, older insulins (pork and beef pancreas), thyroid extract |
| Minerals | Iron salts, magnesium sulfate, liquid paraffin, kaolin |
| Microorganisms | Penicillin (Penicillium mould), streptomycin (Streptomyces) |
| Synthetic | Paracetamol, sulfonamides, most modern drugs |
| Semi-synthetic | Ampicillin, amoxicillin (built on a natural nucleus) |
| Biotechnology (recombinant DNA) | Human insulin, hepatitis B vaccine, erythropoietin |

## 1.3 Dosage forms

| Group | Dosage forms |
| --- | --- |
| Solid | Tablets (plain, film- or enteric-coated, sustained-release, dispersible, sublingual), capsules, powders, granules, lozenges |
| Liquid (oral) | Syrups, elixirs, suspensions (shake well), emulsions, solutions, drops |
| Semi-solid (topical) | Ointments, creams, gels, pastes, lotions |
| Parenteral | Injections (ampoules, vials, prefilled syringes), infusions |
| Others | Suppositories (rectal), pessaries (vaginal), inhalers and nebuliser solutions, transdermal patches, eye and ear drops, implants |

Nursing points: never crush enteric-coated or sustained-release tablets (the coating protects the stomach or controls release); shake suspensions; store as the label says (insulin and vaccines in the refrigerator, not frozen).

## 1.4 Routes of drug administration

```text
                ROUTES
   ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
 Local         Systemic: enteral   Systemic: parenteral
 (topical,     (oral, sublingual,  (IV, IM, SC, intradermal,
 eye, ear,     buccal, rectal)     intrathecal, intra-articular,
 inhaled for                       inhalation for general
 lungs)                            effect, transdermal)
```

| Route | Advantages | Disadvantages / nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral | Safe, cheap, painless, self-administered | Slow; first-pass metabolism; not for vomiting or unconscious patients; irritant drugs cause nausea |
| Sublingual / buccal | Quick; avoids first-pass metabolism | Only small, lipid-soluble drugs (glyceryl trinitrate); patient must not swallow |
| Rectal | Useful in vomiting or unconscious patients, children | Irregular absorption; may irritate |
| Intravenous (IV) | Fastest, 100% bioavailability, exact control, large volumes | Cannot be recalled; risk of infection, thrombophlebitis, air embolism, extravasation, speed shock |
| Intramuscular (IM) | Depot and oily preparations; faster than SC | Painful; nerve injury if wrong site; avoid in bleeding disorders |
| Subcutaneous (SC) | Self-injection (insulin, heparin) | Small volumes; slow in shock |
| Intradermal | Skin tests, BCG vaccine | Very small volume |
| Inhalation | Direct action on airways, fewer systemic effects | Needs correct technique |
| Transdermal | Steady levels over a long time | Skin irritation; rotate sites |
| Topical | Local action | Some may be absorbed through broken skin |

## 1.5 Terms used in pharmacology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Dose | The amount of drug given at one time |
| Therapeutic effect | The intended beneficial effect |
| Side effect | Unwanted but expected effect at a normal dose (dry mouth with atropine) |
| Adverse effect | Any harmful, unintended effect |
| Toxic effect | Harmful effect from excess drug or accumulation |
| Idiosyncrasy | Unusual, genetically based reaction (haemolysis with primaquine in G6PD deficiency) |
| Allergy (hypersensitivity) | Immune reaction to a drug, not related to dose (penicillin anaphylaxis) |
| Tolerance | Larger amount needed for the same effect after repeated use (nitrates, opioids) |
| Tachyphylaxis | Rapid tolerance after a few closely spaced doses (ephedrine) |
| Cumulation | Build-up of a slowly eliminated drug (digoxin) |
| Dependence | Need to keep taking the drug; withdrawal symptoms on stopping |
| Teratogenicity | Ability to cause birth defects (thalidomide, warfarin) |
| Iatrogenic disease | Disease caused by treatment |
| Placebo | Inert substance that can still produce a response through expectation |
| Half-life (t½) | Time taken for the plasma concentration to fall by half |
| Therapeutic index | Ratio of the toxic to the effective dose; a narrow index means a small margin of safety (digoxin, lithium, warfarin) |

## 1.6 Classification of drugs

Drugs can be grouped in several ways; each is useful for a different purpose.

- **By the system acted on**: drugs acting on the CNS, cardiovascular system, GI tract.
- **By therapeutic use**: antihypertensives, analgesics, antimicrobials.
- **By mechanism**: β-blockers, proton pump inhibitors, ACE inhibitors.
- **By chemical structure**: penicillins, sulfonamides, benzodiazepines.
- **By legal status**: prescription (Schedule H, H1, X) and non-prescription (over-the-counter) drugs.

A drug has three names: chemical name (exact structure), **generic (non-proprietary) name** (paracetamol) and **brand (trade) name** given by a company. Nurses should know generic names; one generic drug can be sold under many brands, which is a common cause of double dosing.

## 1.7 Abbreviations used in prescriptions

| Abbreviation | Meaning | Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rx | Take (recipe) | stat | Immediately, once |
| OD | Once a day | SOS | If needed, once |
| BD / BID | Twice a day | PRN | When required |
| TDS / TID | Three times a day | ac | Before food |
| QID | Four times a day | pc | After food |
| HS | At bedtime | PO | By mouth |
| IM / IV / SC | Intramuscular / intravenous / subcutaneous | SL | Sublingual |
| NPO / NBM | Nothing by mouth | gtt | Drops |

Safety: write "units" in full (a handwritten "U" can be read as 0), avoid trailing zeros ("1.0" may be read as 10) and always put a zero before a decimal point (0.5). Unclear orders must be clarified with the prescriber before giving.

## 1.8 Prescription

A prescription is a written order from a registered medical practitioner for a medicine for a named patient.

```text
 Name, qualification, registration number, address of prescriber
 Date
 Patient: name, age, sex, weight, address
 Superscription:  Rx
 Inscription:     drug name, strength, dosage form
 Subscription:    instructions to the pharmacist (how much to dispense)
 Signa (transcription): instructions to the patient — dose, route, frequency, duration
 Signature of the prescriber
```

In hospital the nurse works from the doctor's medication order sheet. A **standing order** is to be carried out until cancelled; a **stat order** once immediately; a **PRN order** when needed within limits; a **verbal or telephone order** only in emergencies, read back and signed by the doctor as soon as possible.

## 1.9 Weights, measures and drug calculation

**Metric system** (used in India):

| Unit | Equals |
| --- | --- |
| 1 kilogram (kg) | 1000 grams (g) |
| 1 gram (g) | 1000 milligrams (mg) |
| 1 milligram (mg) | 1000 micrograms (µg or mcg) |
| 1 litre (L) | 1000 millilitres (mL) |
| 1 mL | about 1 cubic centimetre (cc) |

Some drugs are measured in **units** (insulin, heparin) or **international units** (vitamin D). Household spoons are not accurate; use a marked measuring cup or oral syringe.

**Basic formula for tablets and liquids**

```text
 Amount to give = (Dose ordered ÷ Strength available) × Quantity (volume) it comes in
                 = (D ÷ H) × Q
```

Practice example with an imaginary "Drug X" (figures for arithmetic practice only): ordered 250 mg; stock 500 mg in 5 mL → (250 ÷ 500) × 5 = 2.5 mL.

**IV infusion rate**

```text
 Drops per minute = Total volume (mL) × Drop factor (drops/mL) ÷ Time (minutes)
 mL per hour      = Total volume (mL) ÷ Time (hours)
```

Drop factor: standard (macro) sets about 15 or 20 drops/mL (check the pack); microdrip (paediatric) sets 60 drops/mL. Example: 1000 mL over 8 hours with a 20 drops/mL set → 1000 × 20 ÷ 480 ≈ 42 drops per minute.

**Children's dose formulas** (rarely used now; dosing is by kg or body surface area from a reference):

- Young's rule: child dose = (age in years ÷ (age + 12)) × adult dose.
- Clark's rule: child dose = (weight in kg ÷ 70) × adult dose.
- Body surface area: child dose = (BSA in m² ÷ 1.73) × adult dose.

Always have a second nurse check calculations for high-alert drugs (insulin, heparin, potassium chloride infusion, chemotherapy, paediatric doses).

## 1.10 Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics is the study of drug actions and how they are produced.

**Types of action**: stimulation (adrenaline on the heart), depression (diazepam on the brain), irritation (counter-irritants), replacement (insulin in diabetes, iron in anaemia), and killing of microbes or cancer cells (cytotoxic action).

**Mechanisms of action**

1. **On receptors** — most drugs. A receptor is a protein on or in a cell to which a drug binds to start a response.
   - Agonist: binds and activates (salbutamol on β2 receptors).
   - Antagonist: binds but does not activate; blocks the agonist (atropine blocks muscarinic receptors).
   - Partial agonist: produces less than the maximum response (buprenorphine).
2. **On enzymes** — inhibit an enzyme (aspirin on cyclo-oxygenase, neostigmine on cholinesterase, ACE inhibitors).
3. **On ion channels** — calcium channel blockers (amlodipine), local anaesthetics block sodium channels.
4. **On transporters/pumps** — omeprazole blocks the proton pump, digoxin blocks Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, furosemide blocks a co-transporter in the kidney.
5. **Physical or chemical action** — antacids neutralise acid; osmotic laxatives hold water; activated charcoal adsorbs.

**Combined effects of drugs**

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Additive | Effect equals the sum (1 + 1 = 2) | Two antacids |
| Synergism | Effect greater than the sum (1 + 1 > 2) | Sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim; alcohol + sedatives |
| Potentiation | One drug with no effect of its own increases another's | Carbidopa increases levodopa effect |
| Antagonism | One drug reduces another's effect | Atropine vs acetylcholine; naloxone vs morphine; protamine vs heparin (chemical) |

Types of antagonism: physical (charcoal adsorbs), chemical (antacid neutralises acid; protamine binds heparin), physiological (adrenaline reverses histamine's bronchospasm through different receptors), receptor (atropine vs acetylcholine — competitive).

**Tolerance** may be natural or acquired; **cross-tolerance** occurs between related drugs (alcohol and sedatives).

**Therapeutic, adverse and toxic effects.** Every drug has a range between the dose that helps and the dose that harms. Drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (digoxin, warfarin, lithium, aminoglycosides, theophylline) need close monitoring of signs and sometimes blood levels.

**Pharmacovigilance** (WHO): the science and activities relating to detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. In India the **Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)** is coordinated by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad, with ADR monitoring centres in hospitals. Nurses can and should report suspected adverse drug reactions on the ADR reporting form.

## 1.11 Pharmacokinetics

```text
 Drug given → ABSORPTION → blood → DISTRIBUTION → tissues / site of action
                                ↓
                      METABOLISM (mainly liver)
                                ↓
                      EXCRETION (mainly kidney; also bile, lungs, milk, sweat)
```

**Absorption** — movement of a drug from the site of administration into the blood. Faster with lipid-soluble, un-ionised, small molecules, a large absorbing area and good blood flow. Food can slow or reduce absorption (give tetracyclines away from milk and antacids). Shock reduces absorption from IM and SC sites.

**Bioavailability** — the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. IV = 100%. Oral may be less because of incomplete absorption and **first-pass metabolism** (drug absorbed from the gut passes through the liver first and part is destroyed — glyceryl trinitrate is therefore given sublingually).

**Distribution** — spread from blood to tissues. Factors: plasma protein binding (bound drug is inactive and stays in the blood; displacement by another drug can cause toxicity, e.g. warfarin displaced by aspirin), lipid solubility, blood flow, and barriers:
- Blood–brain barrier: only lipid-soluble drugs enter easily.
- Placental barrier: most drugs cross — care in pregnancy.
- Drugs also pass into breast milk.

**Metabolism (biotransformation)** — chemical change of a drug, mainly in the liver, usually making it more water-soluble and easier to excrete.
- Phase I: oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis (often by cytochrome P450 enzymes).
- Phase II: conjugation (glucuronide, sulfate, acetyl) — products are usually inactive.
- A **prodrug** is inactive until metabolised (enalapril → enalaprilat; levodopa → dopamine).
- **Enzyme inducers** (rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone, alcohol, smoking) speed metabolism of other drugs → reduced effect (oral contraceptive failure with rifampicin).
- **Enzyme inhibitors** (cimetidine, erythromycin, ketoconazole, ciprofloxacin) slow metabolism → toxicity of the other drug.
- Newborns and elderly people and those with liver disease metabolise drugs slowly.

**Excretion** — removal from the body. Kidney (most drugs: glomerular filtration and tubular secretion; reabsorption depends on urine pH), bile and faeces, lungs (anaesthetic gases), breast milk, saliva and sweat. In kidney disease many drugs accumulate and doses are reduced by the doctor.

**Drug interactions** — change in the effect of one drug by another drug, food or alcohol.
- Pharmacokinetic: absorption (antacids or iron bind tetracycline), distribution (protein-binding displacement), metabolism (induction or inhibition), excretion (probenecid prolongs penicillin).
- Pharmacodynamic: additive or opposing effects (sedatives with alcohol; β-blockers block salbutamol).
- Pharmaceutical: incompatibility outside the body — do not mix drugs in one syringe or infusion unless known to be compatible.

**Key kinetic terms**: plasma half-life decides dosing frequency; about 4–5 half-lives are needed to reach a steady state with regular dosing (and to clear a drug after stopping); a **loading dose** is used when an effect is needed quickly; a **maintenance dose** keeps the level steady.

## 1.12 Principles of drug administration and individualising treatment

**Rights of medication administration** — the classic five rights are right patient, right drug, right dose, right route and right time; many hospitals add right documentation, right reason, right response, right to refuse and right education.

Nursing principles:
1. Check the order; clarify anything unclear.
2. Check the drug label three times (when taking it from storage, when preparing, before returning or discarding the container).
3. Identify the patient with two identifiers (name and hospital number), not the bed number.
4. Ask about allergies before the first dose.
5. Prepare and give medicines yourself; never give a drug prepared by someone else.
6. Check expiry date, colour and clarity.
7. Stay until oral medicines are swallowed.
8. Record immediately after giving — never before.
9. Observe for therapeutic and adverse effects and report.
10. Report and record any medication error at once.

**Factors that affect dose and response** (why treatment must be individualised):

| Factor | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Age | Newborns and the elderly have less liver and kidney capacity; the elderly take many drugs |
| Body weight / surface area | Children's doses are calculated by weight |
| Sex and pregnancy | Some drugs harm the fetus; drugs in labour affect the newborn |
| Route | Magnesium sulfate is a laxative by mouth but acts on the nervous system when injected |
| Disease | Liver disease slows metabolism; kidney disease slows excretion |
| Genetics | Slow acetylators get isoniazid neuropathy; G6PD deficiency |
| Tolerance and dependence | More drug needed for the same effect |
| Psychological factors | Placebo response; trust in the nurse |
| Other drugs and food | Interactions |
| Time of administration | Hypnotics at bedtime; some drugs before or after food |

## 1.13 Indian Pharmacopoeia, drug laws and schedule drugs

**Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)** — the official book of standards for drugs in India: identity, purity and strength of drugs and how to test them. It is published by the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad**, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, and it is legally recognised under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act. New editions appear every few years (IP 2022 is a recent edition; check the current one).

**Main Indian drug laws**

| Law | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and Rules, 1945 | Control of import, manufacture, sale and distribution of drugs and cosmetics; standards of quality; schedules |
| Pharmacy Act, 1948 | Regulates the profession of pharmacy; registration of pharmacists |
| Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act, 1985 | Control of narcotic and psychotropic drugs (morphine, pethidine); strict storage and records |
| Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 | Stops misleading advertisements for cures |
| New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019 | Approval of new drugs and conduct of clinical trials |

**Schedules under the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules** (most asked in nursing exams):

| Schedule | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| G | Drugs to be taken only under medical supervision; label carries a caution |
| H | Prescription-only drugs; label carries "Rx" and the warning that they are to be sold on the prescription of a registered medical practitioner only |
| H1 | Selected antibiotics, anti-TB and habit-forming drugs; sold only on prescription with a boxed warning in red, and the pharmacist keeps a separate record of each sale |
| X | Narcotic and psychotropic drugs with the strictest control; labelled "XRx"; the pharmacist keeps a copy of the prescription |
| K | Drugs and situations exempted from certain provisions of the Act (for example some household remedies, and drugs supplied in certain government health programmes) — check the exact items in the current Rules |
| C and C1 | Biological products (vaccines, sera, insulin) needing special storage |
| M | Good manufacturing practices |

Nursing responsibilities: keep narcotic and controlled drugs in a **double-locked cupboard**, count and record them at every shift handover, sign the register for each dose with a witness, and return unused or wasted drug as per hospital policy.

## 1.14 Rational use of drugs and principles of therapeutics

**Rational use of medicines** (WHO): patients receive medicines appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their own individual requirements, for an adequate period of time, and at the lowest cost to them and their community.

Irrational practices: too many drugs (polypharmacy), unnecessary antibiotics and injections, self-medication, wrong dose or duration, fixed-dose combinations without reason, not following treatment.

**Essential medicines** are those that satisfy the priority health care needs of the population. India's **National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)** guides hospital supply and pricing; the latest revision is NLEM 2022.

**Principles of therapeutics**

1. Make a correct diagnosis and know the goal of treatment.
2. Choose the drug by effectiveness, safety, suitability for the patient and cost.
3. Prefer one drug where possible; use generic names.
4. Choose the right dose, route, frequency and duration for the patient.
5. Explain the treatment and warn about common side effects.
6. Monitor the response and adverse effects, and stop drugs no longer needed.

**Role of the nurse in drug therapy**: safe storage and administration, observation of effects and adverse reactions, patient teaching (purpose, how and when to take, what to avoid, side effects to report, completing the course), maintaining records, reporting errors and ADRs, and supporting adherence.

## Remember

- Pharmacodynamics = what the drug does to the body; pharmacokinetics = what the body does to the drug (ADME).
- IV route gives 100% bioavailability; sublingual avoids first-pass metabolism.
- Agonist activates, antagonist blocks, partial agonist gives a submaximal response.
- Enzyme inducers (rifampicin) reduce other drugs' effects; enzyme inhibitors (cimetidine, erythromycin) increase them.
- Narrow therapeutic index: digoxin, warfarin, lithium, theophylline, aminoglycosides.
- D ÷ H × Q for dose; volume × drop factor ÷ minutes for drops per minute.
- Schedule H = prescription only; H1 = stricter with a sales record; X = strictest (narcotics and psychotropics); G = medical supervision; K = exemptions.
- IP is published by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad; PvPI collects ADR reports.
- Rights of administration: patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation (and more).
