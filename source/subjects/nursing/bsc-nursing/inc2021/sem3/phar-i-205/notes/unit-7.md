# Unit 7: Drugs used in infections and infestations

INC Unit VIII (5 hours) — the largest unit: general principles for the use of antimicrobials; the main antibiotic groups (penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, macrolides and other broad-spectrum antibiotics, sulfonamides, quinolones and miscellaneous antimicrobials); drugs for anaerobic infections; antitubercular, antileprosy, antimalarial, antiretroviral and antiviral drugs; and antihelminthics, antiscabies and antifungal agents. Doses are not given here; follow the prescriber's order, the national programme guidelines and your hospital protocol. Learn each group by its spectrum, main uses, important adverse effects and the nurse's role.

## 7.1 General principles for the use of antimicrobials

An **antimicrobial** is a drug that kills or stops the growth of microorganisms. **Antibiotics** are antimicrobials originally from living organisms; the word is now used broadly for antibacterials.

**Ways of classifying antibacterials**

- **Bactericidal** (kill bacteria): penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin, metronidazole, rifampicin, isoniazid.
- **Bacteriostatic** (stop multiplication; the body's defences then clear them): sulfonamides, tetracyclines, macrolides (mostly), chloramphenicol, clindamycin.
- **Narrow spectrum** (few organisms) vs **broad spectrum** (many organisms, Gram-positive and Gram-negative).
- **By mechanism of action**:

| Mechanism | Groups |
| --- | --- |
| Inhibit cell-wall synthesis | Penicillins, cephalosporins, vancomycin |
| Inhibit protein synthesis | Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, chloramphenicol, clindamycin, linezolid |
| Inhibit nucleic-acid synthesis / function | Fluoroquinolones (DNA gyrase), rifampicin (RNA polymerase), metronidazole |
| Block folic-acid synthesis (antimetabolites) | Sulfonamides, trimethoprim |
| Damage the cell membrane | Polymyxins |

**Principles the nurse should understand**

1. Give antibiotics only for a proven or strongly suspected **bacterial** infection — not for ordinary colds and most sore throats, which are viral.
2. Take **specimens for culture and sensitivity** (blood, urine, pus, sputum) **before the first dose** where possible.
3. Start **empirical** treatment (a best guess) in serious infection, then switch to a **definitive** narrow-spectrum drug once the report is back ("de-escalation").
4. Choose the drug by the likely organism, the site of infection (can the drug reach it?), the patient (allergy, age, pregnancy, kidney and liver function) and cost.
5. Give the correct dose, route and **full duration**; stopping early encourages resistance and relapse.
6. **Prophylaxis** (prevention) is used in defined situations — before certain surgery, rheumatic fever prevention, after exposure to meningococcus — not routinely.
7. Combinations are used to widen cover, prevent resistance (TB, HIV) or get a synergistic effect; unnecessary combinations are avoided.

**Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)** is a major problem: bacteria survive drugs that once killed them (MRSA, multidrug-resistant TB, ESBL-producing Gram-negatives). Causes: overuse and misuse, wrong dose or duration, incomplete courses, antibiotics in animals, poor infection control. **Antimicrobial stewardship** — the right drug, dose, route and duration, reviewed regularly — and good infection control slow resistance. India has a **National Programme on AMR Containment**; many broad-spectrum and reserve antibiotics are in **Schedule H1** (a separate sales record is kept).

**Superinfection**: broad-spectrum antibiotics kill the normal flora and let resistant organisms or fungi overgrow — oral or vaginal thrush, and antibiotic-associated diarrhoea including *Clostridioides difficile* colitis.

**Nurse's general role with any antibiotic**: ask about allergy before the first dose; take cultures before starting; give on time to keep steady blood levels; complete the course; watch for the therapeutic response (fever settling, counts improving) and for adverse effects, superinfection and allergy; teach the patient to finish the course and not to share or save antibiotics.

## 7.2 Penicillins (beta-lactam antibiotics)

**Action**: block the building of the bacterial **cell wall** → the wall weakens and the organism bursts (bactericidal). They share a **beta-lactam ring**, which bacterial enzymes called **beta-lactamases (penicillinases)** can break, causing resistance.

| Group | Examples | Spectrum / uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural | Benzylpenicillin (penicillin G, injectable), phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V, oral), benzathine penicillin (long-acting) | Streptococcal sore throat, rheumatic fever prevention (benzathine), syphilis, many Gram-positive infections |
| Penicillinase-resistant | Cloxacillin, flucloxacillin | *Staphylococcus* that makes penicillinase (skin, bone, wound) |
| Aminopenicillins (broad) | Ampicillin, amoxicillin | Respiratory, urinary and ear infections, typhoid, *H. pylori* |
| With a beta-lactamase inhibitor | Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid; piperacillin + tazobactam | Resistant organisms; the inhibitor protects the penicillin |
| Antipseudomonal | Piperacillin | Serious Gram-negative infection including *Pseudomonas* |

**Adverse effects**: **allergy** is the most important — rash, itching, and occasionally life-threatening **anaphylaxis** (minutes after an injection); diarrhoea (more with amoxicillin–clavulanate); pain at the injection site; superinfection; high IV doses may cause convulsions. **People allergic to one penicillin are allergic to all of them**, and some react to cephalosporins too.

**Nursing responsibilities**: **always ask about penicillin allergy and check the record before giving**; after an injection keep the patient under observation for a while and **keep adrenaline and resuscitation equipment ready**; recognise anaphylaxis (wheeze, swelling, fall in BP, rash) and act at once; benzathine penicillin is a thick suspension given deep IM, never IV; give oral penicillins as directed around food (amoxicillin is unaffected by food, ampicillin and penicillin V are better on an empty stomach); complete the course even when the patient feels well (especially streptococcal throat, to prevent rheumatic fever).

## 7.3 Cephalosporins

**Action**: like penicillins, they block cell-wall synthesis (bactericidal) and have a beta-lactam ring. They are grouped into "generations"; broadly, later generations cover more Gram-negative organisms.

| Generation | Examples | General cover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | Cefazolin, cephalexin | Mainly Gram-positive; surgical prophylaxis, skin, urinary |
| Second | Cefuroxime | Gram-positive and more Gram-negative; respiratory |
| Third | Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, cefixime, ceftazidime | Broad; serious infections, meningitis (cross into CSF), typhoid, gonorrhoea; ceftazidime covers *Pseudomonas* |
| Fourth | Cefepime | Very broad, including *Pseudomonas* |
| Fifth | Ceftaroline | Active against MRSA |

**Adverse effects**: allergy (about 10% of penicillin-allergic people also react — avoid in severe penicillin allergy), diarrhoea and *C. difficile* colitis, pain on injection, raised liver enzymes; **a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol** with a few agents; **ceftriaxone must not be mixed with or given close to calcium-containing IV fluids in newborns** (dangerous precipitate).

**Nursing**: ask about penicillin and cephalosporin allergy; reconstitute and give as directed; watch for allergy and diarrhoea; do not mix ceftriaxone with calcium solutions in neonates; teach the patient to avoid alcohol with the agents that interact.

## 7.4 Aminoglycosides

**Examples**: gentamicin, amikacin, tobramycin, streptomycin, neomycin (topical/oral).

**Action**: inhibit bacterial protein synthesis (bactericidal); good against aerobic Gram-negative bacilli; often combined with a penicillin or cephalosporin for serious infection (synergy). Not absorbed from the gut, so given by injection for systemic infection.

**Important adverse effects** (the two that examiners always ask):
- **Ototoxicity** — damage to hearing and balance (ringing in the ears, deafness, dizziness, unsteadiness), which can be permanent.
- **Nephrotoxicity** — kidney damage, usually reversible if caught early.
- Also neuromuscular block (weakness, especially with muscle relaxants or in myasthenia gravis).

The risk is higher with large or prolonged dosing, in kidney failure, in the elderly and with other oto- or nephrotoxic drugs (loop diuretics, vancomycin). They have a **narrow therapeutic index**, so blood levels and kidney function are monitored.

**Nursing**: check kidney function before and during treatment; monitor urine output and blood levels as ordered; ask about and watch for hearing changes, ringing in the ears, dizziness and unsteadiness, and report them at once; ensure good hydration; avoid combining with other oto-/nephrotoxic drugs where possible; streptomycin is used in TB (below).

## 7.5 Macrolides and other broad-spectrum antibiotics

### Macrolides
**Examples**: erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin. **Action**: inhibit protein synthesis (bacteriostatic). **Uses**: a good alternative in penicillin allergy; respiratory infections, atypical pneumonia, whooping cough, diphtheria carriers, *H. pylori* (clarithromycin), some sexually transmitted infections (azithromycin). **Adverse effects**: nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps (erythromycin), QT prolongation; erythromycin and clarithromycin are **enzyme inhibitors** — they raise levels of theophylline, warfarin, carbamazepine and statins.

### Tetracyclines
**Examples**: doxycycline, tetracycline, minocycline. **Action**: inhibit protein synthesis (bacteriostatic, broad spectrum). **Uses**: acne, cholera, brucellosis, rickettsial and chlamydial infections, atypical pneumonia, as part of some combinations; doxycycline for malaria prophylaxis and leptospirosis. **Adverse effects**: **staining of developing teeth and effect on growing bone — avoided in pregnancy, breastfeeding and children under about 8 years**; photosensitivity (avoid strong sun); GI upset; do not use outdated tetracycline (kidney damage). **Absorption is reduced by milk, antacids, iron and calcium** — give apart from them.

### Chloramphenicol
Broad spectrum but kept in reserve because it can cause **aplastic anaemia** (rare, can be fatal) and, in newborns, **grey baby syndrome**. Used in some serious infections and as eye drops.

### Glycopeptides and others for resistant Gram-positives
- **Vancomycin**: IV for serious Gram-positive infection including **MRSA**; oral for *C. difficile* colitis. Nephro- and ototoxic; a rapid IV infusion causes flushing of the upper body ("red man syndrome") — **infuse slowly**.
- **Linezolid**, **teicoplanin**, **daptomycin**: other reserve drugs for resistant Gram-positives.

### Clindamycin
Good for anaerobes, bone infection and some skin infections; an important cause of *C. difficile* colitis.

## 7.6 Sulfonamides and quinolones

### Sulfonamides and cotrimoxazole
**Action**: block the bacterial synthesis of folic acid (bacteriostatic). **Cotrimoxazole** = sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim, a synergistic combination. **Uses**: urinary infections, some chest infections, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia (and its prevention in HIV), toxoplasmosis; silver sulfadiazine for burns (Unit 3). **Adverse effects**: allergy and rashes, including severe skin reactions (Stevens–Johnson syndrome); **crystals in the urine and kidney damage — advise plenty of fluids**; blood disorders and folate deficiency with long use; **haemolysis in G6PD deficiency**; **kernicterus in newborns — avoid in late pregnancy, breastfeeding mothers and newborns**.

### Quinolones / fluoroquinolones
**Examples**: ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, norfloxacin. **Action**: inhibit the bacterial enzyme DNA gyrase (bactericidal, broad spectrum); well absorbed by mouth. **Uses**: urinary, gut and respiratory infections, typhoid, gonorrhoea, some bone and soft-tissue infections. **Adverse effects**: nausea, headache, dizziness; **tendon pain and rupture** (especially the Achilles tendon, more in the elderly and with steroids); QT prolongation; photosensitivity; lower the seizure threshold; **best avoided in children and pregnancy** (effect on growing cartilage). **Absorption is reduced by antacids, iron, calcium and milk** — give apart from them.

## 7.7 Drugs for anaerobic infections — metronidazole

**Metronidazole** (and tinidazole) act against **anaerobic bacteria and protozoa**. **Uses**: anaerobic wound, pelvic and abdominal infections, *C. difficile* colitis, amoebiasis, giardiasis, trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis, *H. pylori* (in combination), dental and gum infections. **Adverse effects**: metallic taste, nausea, dark urine, headache; **a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol** (flushing, vomiting, headache) — **the patient must avoid alcohol during treatment and for a short period after**; numbness of hands and feet with long courses.

## 7.8 Antitubercular drugs

Tuberculosis is treated under the **National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP)** with several drugs together for several months, in two phases (an intensive phase and a continuation phase), to cure the patient and prevent resistance. Fixed-dose combination tablets and daily regimens are used; always follow the current programme guideline.

**First-line drugs** (remember **RIPE** + S):

| Drug | Key adverse effects / points |
| --- | --- |
| **Isoniazid (H)** | **Peripheral neuropathy** (numbness, tingling) — prevented by giving **pyridoxine (vitamin B₆)**; liver injury; more neuropathy in slow acetylators |
| **Rifampicin (R)** | **Orange-red colour of urine, tears, sweat and saliva — warn the patient (harmless)**; liver injury; a strong **enzyme inducer** (reduces the effect of oral contraceptives — advise another method, and of warfarin, some antiretrovirals and others) |
| **Pyrazinamide (Z)** | Liver injury; raised uric acid (joint pain, gout); GI upset |
| **Ethambutol (E)** | **Optic neuritis** — loss of visual acuity and red–green colour vision; ask about vision and report changes |
| **Streptomycin (S)** | Ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity (an aminoglycoside, given IM) |

**Multidrug-resistant and drug-resistant TB** need longer treatment with other drugs (for example levofloxacin, bedaquiline, linezolid) under the programme.

**Nursing responsibilities (very important for exams)**: explain why **several drugs are taken together** and why the **full course (months) must be completed** even after the patient feels well; stress that stopping early causes relapse and resistance; **DOTS / directly observed treatment** — watching the patient swallow the dose — supports adherence; warn about the harmless **orange-red urine** with rifampicin; give **pyridoxine** with isoniazid to prevent neuropathy; ask about vision (ethambutol) and watch for jaundice, dark urine, nausea and loss of appetite (liver injury — report and the drugs may be held); teach infection control (cover the mouth, dispose of sputum safely, ventilation) and contact screening; support and counselling to prevent default.

## 7.9 Antileprosy drugs

Leprosy (Hansen's disease) is treated under the national programme with **multidrug therapy (MDT)** supplied free, for months, to cure it and prevent resistance and deformity.

| Drug | Points |
| --- | --- |
| **Dapsone** | Backbone of MDT; haemolysis in G6PD deficiency; rash; rarely a severe hypersensitivity syndrome |
| **Rifampicin** | Given (often once a month in MDT); orange-red secretions; enzyme inducer |
| **Clofazimine** | **Reddish-black discoloration of the skin** (reversible) — warn the patient; dryness, GI upset |

Standard WHO **MDT** combines these drugs; the exact drugs and length depend on whether the disease is paucibacillary or multibacillary — follow the current guideline. **Lepra reactions** (flare-ups) are treated with corticosteroids and, in some, clofazimine or thalidomide (thalidomide is strictly controlled — never in women who can become pregnant, as it causes severe birth defects). **Nursing**: explain the long course and the need to complete it; reassure that the patient becomes non-infectious soon after starting treatment; warn about skin discoloration (clofazimine) and orange secretions (rifampicin); care of anaesthetic hands and feet to prevent injury and ulcers; eye care; support against stigma.

## 7.10 Antimalarial drugs

Malaria is caused by *Plasmodium* parasites spread by the female *Anopheles* mosquito; in India *P. vivax* and *P. falciparum* are the main species. Treatment follows the **National Vector Borne Disease Control Programme** guideline and depends on the species and severity.

| Drug | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Chloroquine** | *P. vivax* in areas where it still works; now widely resistant in *P. falciparum* |
| **Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)** | First-line for *falciparum* malaria (an artemisinin drug combined with a partner drug) |
| **Primaquine** | Clears the liver stage of *vivax* to prevent relapse, and the gametocytes of *falciparum*; **causes haemolysis in G6PD deficiency (test first); not in pregnancy** |
| **Quinine, artesunate (IV)** | Severe/complicated malaria (artesunate IV is preferred) |
| **Doxycycline, mefloquine** | Prophylaxis for travellers; mefloquine can cause vivid dreams and mood changes |

**Adverse effects to note**: chloroquine — itching (common in dark skin), nausea, visual disturbance and retinal damage with long high use; quinine — "cinchonism" (ringing in the ears, headache, nausea, blurred vision), low blood sugar. **Nursing**: give antimalarials with or after food to reduce nausea; test for G6PD deficiency before primaquine and watch for dark urine; monitor blood glucose with quinine; in severe malaria monitor consciousness, urine output, blood glucose and temperature; teach prevention — mosquito nets (especially insecticide-treated), repellents, screens, removing stagnant water, and completing prophylaxis.

## 7.11 Antiretroviral drugs (for HIV)

HIV is treated with **antiretroviral therapy (ART)** — a **combination of at least three drugs** from different classes, taken **lifelong**, under the **National AIDS Control Programme** (free ART centres). ART does not cure HIV but controls the virus, restores immunity and prevents transmission.

| Class | How it acts | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NRTIs (nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors) | Block the enzyme that copies viral RNA into DNA | Tenofovir, lamivudine, zidovudine, abacavir |
| NNRTIs | Block the same enzyme at a different site | Efavirenz, nevirapine |
| Integrase inhibitors | Stop the viral DNA joining the host DNA | Dolutegravir |
| Protease inhibitors | Block the enzyme that cuts viral proteins | Lopinavir/ritonavir, atazanavir |

**Adverse effects** vary by drug: nausea, headache, anaemia (zidovudine), kidney and bone effects (tenofovir), rash and liver injury (nevirapine), vivid dreams (efavirenz), fat changes; many **drug interactions** (rifampicin lowers some levels — important in TB–HIV).

**Nursing responsibilities**: stress **strict daily adherence at the same times** — missed doses cause resistance and treatment failure; teach that ART is lifelong; support and confidentiality; monitor for side effects and adherence; **standard precautions** for all patients and **post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)** after a needle-stick injury (start ART drugs as soon as possible per protocol); **prevention of mother-to-child transmission** with ART in pregnancy and for the baby; counsel on safe sex and not sharing needles.

## 7.12 Antiviral drugs (other viruses)

| Drug | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Acyclovir, valacyclovir** | Herpes simplex (cold sores, genital herpes), chickenpox and shingles (varicella-zoster) |
| **Oseltamivir** | Influenza, including H1N1 (most effective when started early) |
| **Ganciclovir** | Cytomegalovirus infection in immunocompromised patients |
| **Tenofovir, entecavir** | Chronic hepatitis B |
| **Sofosbuvir and other direct-acting antivirals** | Hepatitis C (now curable in most people) |

Antivirals mostly **slow or control** viral multiplication rather than killing the virus outright; they work best when **started early**. Adverse effects include nausea, headache and (with IV acyclovir) kidney effects — ensure good hydration. **Nursing**: start as early as ordered; maintain hydration with acyclovir; standard precautions; vaccines prevent several viral diseases (hepatitis B, measles, influenza, chickenpox) — health promotion is part of the nurse's role.

## 7.13 Antifungal drugs

Fungal infections range from superficial (skin, nails, mouth, vagina) to deep/systemic (in immunocompromised and ICU patients).

| Group | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polyenes | **Amphotericin B** (IV); nystatin (local) | Amphotericin B for serious systemic fungal infection; nystatin for oral and vaginal thrush |
| Azoles | **Fluconazole, itraconazole, ketoconazole, voriconazole**; clotrimazole, miconazole (topical, Unit 3) | Candidiasis, ringworm, systemic fungal infections |
| Allylamine | **Terbinafine** | Nail and skin fungal infections |
| Echinocandins | Caspofungin | Serious *Candida* and *Aspergillus* infections |
| Others | Griseofulvin | Ringworm of skin, hair and nails (long courses) |

**Action**: most act on **ergosterol**, the fungal cell-membrane sterol — azoles block its synthesis; amphotericin and nystatin bind it and make the membrane leak. **Adverse effects**: **amphotericin B** — fever and chills during the infusion, **nephrotoxicity** and low potassium and magnesium (it is nicknamed "ampho-terrible"); **azoles** — liver injury and many interactions (they are enzyme inhibitors; ketoconazole especially raises other drug levels); terbinafine and griseofulvin — taste changes, GI upset, liver effects; griseofulvin interacts with alcohol and oral contraceptives. **Nursing**: for amphotericin B give premedication if ordered, infuse slowly, monitor temperature, kidney function, potassium and magnesium, and ensure hydration; check liver tests with oral azoles and terbinafine; teach that nail and ringworm courses are long and must be completed; topical antifungal teaching is in Unit 3.

## 7.14 Antihelminthic and antiscabies agents

### Antihelminthics (for worms)
Worm infestations (roundworm, hookworm, pinworm, tapeworm, *Ascaris*) are very common in India.

| Drug | Covers | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Albendazole, mebendazole** | Most common intestinal worms | Single-dose for many; chewed or taken with food; avoided in early pregnancy |
| **Pyrantel pamoate** | Roundworm, pinworm, hookworm | — |
| **Praziquantel** | Tapeworms, flukes | — |
| **Diethylcarbamazine (DEC)** | **Filariasis** (national programme), some others | — |
| **Ivermectin** | *Strongyloides*, filariasis, and **scabies** (below) | — |

Under the national **deworming (Albendazole) programme** children and adolescents are given a deworming dose on fixed days. **Nursing/teaching**: treat the whole family for pinworm; **hygiene prevents reinfection** — hand washing, short clean nails, washing vegetables, safe water and sanitation, wearing footwear (hookworm), washing bed linen in hot water (pinworm); give the dose as directed and report severe abdominal pain.

### Antiscabies and antilice agents
Scabies (the mite *Sarcoptes scabiei*) and head lice are treated with topical agents; this overlaps with Unit 3.
- **Permethrin cream** — first choice; applied to the whole body (neck down in adults), left on for the directed time and washed off.
- **Benzyl benzoate** emulsion — older agent; irritant on broken skin.
- **Gamma benzene hexachloride (lindane)** — neurotoxic if over-absorbed; avoided in infants, young children, pregnancy and broken skin, and is no longer the first choice.
- **Oral ivermectin** — for widespread or crusted scabies and for outbreaks.
- **Crotamiton** — an alternative; also relieves itching.

**Nursing for scabies**: **treat all close contacts at the same time**; wash clothes, towels and bed linen in hot water and dry in the sun, or seal unwashable items in a bag for a few days; trim nails and apply under them; explain that **itching may continue for 2–4 weeks after successful treatment** and does not mean the drug has failed.

## Remember

- Bactericidal: penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin, metronidazole. Bacteriostatic: sulfonamides, tetracyclines, macrolides.
- Take cultures before the first dose; complete the full course; resistance follows misuse; superinfection = thrush and *C. difficile* diarrhoea.
- Penicillins and cephalosporins block the cell wall; always ask about allergy and keep adrenaline ready — anaphylaxis can occur.
- Aminoglycosides (gentamicin): ototoxic and nephrotoxic; monitor hearing, balance and kidneys.
- Tetracyclines: not in children under ~8 or in pregnancy (teeth, bone); take apart from milk, iron, antacids; photosensitivity.
- Vancomycin: infuse slowly (red man syndrome); for MRSA and *C. difficile* (oral).
- Sulfonamides and quinolones: plenty of fluids (crystalluria with sulfa); haemolysis in G6PD with sulfa and primaquine; quinolones — tendon rupture, avoid in children/pregnancy.
- Metronidazole and furazolidone: no alcohol (disulfiram-like reaction).
- TB (RIPE + S): isoniazid → neuropathy (give pyridoxine); rifampicin → orange-red urine and enzyme inducer (contraceptive failure); ethambutol → optic neuritis; pyrazinamide → raised uric acid; DOTS and completing the course.
- Leprosy MDT: dapsone + rifampicin + clofazimine (reddish-black skin); long course.
- Malaria: ACT for falciparum; primaquine clears vivax relapse but not in G6PD deficiency or pregnancy; quinine → cinchonism and low sugar.
- HIV: lifelong three-drug ART; strict adherence; PEP after needle-stick; prevent mother-to-child transmission.
- Acyclovir for herpes/zoster, oseltamivir for influenza — start early; hydrate with IV acyclovir.
- Antifungals act on ergosterol; amphotericin B is nephrotoxic ("ampho-terrible"); long courses for nails and ringworm.
- Deworming with albendazole/mebendazole; hygiene prevents reinfection; treat the whole family for pinworm; scabies — treat all contacts, permethrin first, itching lasts weeks.
