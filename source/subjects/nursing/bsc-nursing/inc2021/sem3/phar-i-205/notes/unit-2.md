# Unit 2: Antiseptics, disinfectants and drugs acting on the GI system

INC Unit II (1 hour) — commonly used antiseptics and disinfectants — joined here with INC Unit III (2 hours) — emetics and antiemetics, laxatives and purgatives, antacids and antipeptic ulcer drugs, antidiarrhoeals with fluid and electrolyte therapy, furazolidone and dicyclomine. For every drug group learn the action, uses, contraindications, interactions, side effects and the nurse's role. Doses are not given here; follow the prescriber's order and your hospital's formulary.

## 2.1 Terms used in infection control

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Sterilisation | Killing or removal of all microorganisms, including spores |
| Disinfection | Killing of most pathogenic organisms (not necessarily spores) on non-living objects |
| Disinfectant | Chemical used on inanimate objects and surfaces (floors, instruments) |
| Antiseptic | Chemical safe enough to use on living tissue (skin, wounds, mucous membranes) |
| Germicide (-cidal) | Kills microbes: bactericidal, fungicidal, virucidal, sporicidal |
| Bacteriostatic | Stops multiplication without killing |
| Sanitiser | Lowers microbial numbers to a safe level (hand rub, dishwashing) |
| Asepsis | Absence of infection; techniques that prevent contamination |

The same chemical may be a disinfectant at a strong concentration and an antiseptic when diluted.

**An ideal antiseptic/disinfectant** has a wide spectrum, acts quickly, works in the presence of blood and pus, is not irritant or toxic to tissues, does not corrode instruments or stain, is stable, cheap and pleasant to use.

**Factors affecting action**: concentration, contact time, temperature, number and type of organisms (spores and mycobacteria resist most chemicals), presence of organic matter (blood and pus reduce action, so clean first), pH and compatibility (soap inactivates chlorhexidine and cetrimide).

**Spaulding classification** for equipment: critical items entering sterile tissue (surgical instruments) → sterilise; semi-critical items touching mucous membranes (endoscopes) → high-level disinfection (for example glutaraldehyde); non-critical items touching intact skin (BP cuffs, bed rails) → low-level disinfection.

## 2.2 Commonly used antiseptics and disinfectants

| Group | Examples | Action | Uses | Points to note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alcohols | Ethyl alcohol, isopropyl alcohol (70% is most effective) | Denature proteins | Skin before injection, hand rub, thermometers | Not sporicidal; flammable; dries skin; let it dry before injecting |
| Halogens – iodine | Tincture iodine, povidone-iodine | Oxidation of proteins | Pre-operative skin, wounds, surgical scrub | Stains; allergy; avoid large areas in newborns and in thyroid disease |
| Halogens – chlorine | Sodium hypochlorite (bleach), bleaching powder | Oxidation | Blood spills, surfaces, water disinfection, linen | Corrodes metal; inactivated by organic matter; never mix with acids (toxic chlorine gas) |
| Biguanides | Chlorhexidine (with cetrimide in Savlon) | Damages cell membrane | Hand wash, surgical scrub, mouthwash, catheter care | Inactivated by soap; may stain teeth; avoid eyes and ears |
| Quaternary ammonium | Cetrimide, benzalkonium chloride | Detergent, damages membrane | Wound cleaning, preservative | Inactivated by soap; weak against Gram-negatives |
| Phenols | Phenol, cresol (Lysol), chloroxylenol (Dettol) | Protein denaturation | Floors, excreta, linen (cresol); skin (chloroxylenol) | Phenol is corrosive to skin; standard for testing others (phenol coefficient) |
| Aldehydes | Glutaraldehyde (2%), formaldehyde (formalin) | Alkylate proteins | Endoscopes, heat-sensitive items; fumigation of rooms | Irritant to eyes and lungs; use in ventilated area with PPE; rinse items with sterile water |
| Oxidising agents | Hydrogen peroxide, potassium permanganate | Release oxygen | Wound cleaning (H₂O₂ froths and loosens debris); KMnO₄ soaks for weeping skin | Store away from light; KMnO₄ stains; use only freshly made, fully dissolved pale solutions |
| Dyes | Gentian violet, acriflavine | Act on cell wall | Fungal skin and mouth lesions, superficial wounds | Stains |
| Heavy metals | Silver nitrate, silver sulfadiazine | Bind proteins | Burns (silver sulfadiazine, Unit 3) | Silver nitrate stains black |
| Gas | Ethylene oxide | Alkylation | Sterilising heat-sensitive plastics | Toxic, explosive; items need aeration |
| Acids | Boric acid | Weak bacteriostatic | Old eye and ear washes | Rarely used now |

**Phenol coefficient (Rideal–Walker test)**: compares the killing power of a disinfectant with phenol under set conditions; a coefficient above 1 means it is stronger than phenol.

**Nursing responsibilities with antiseptics and disinfectants**

1. Use the agent and dilution set by the hospital infection control policy; label every container with name, strength, date and time of preparation.
2. Make dilutions freshly; discard old solutions (diluted solutions can grow bacteria such as Pseudomonas).
3. Clean before disinfecting — organic matter inactivates most agents.
4. Allow the full contact time; let skin antiseptics dry before a procedure.
5. Wear gloves, apron and eye protection with irritant chemicals (glutaraldehyde, phenol, bleach); keep the area ventilated.
6. Ask about allergy (iodine); watch for skin irritation and report.
7. Never use disinfectants on skin or wounds; never mix chemicals (bleach with acid or ammonia).
8. Store tightly closed, away from light and heat; keep out of reach of children and confused patients.

## 2.3 Emetics

Emetics produce vomiting. They act on the stomach (local irritants) or the chemoreceptor trigger zone. Inducing vomiting is now rarely recommended, because it delays better treatment and can cause aspiration. It must never be done in an unconscious or drowsy patient or after swallowing corrosives or kerosene. Ipecacuanha syrup and apomorphine are the classic examples you may be asked to name.

## 2.4 Antiemetics

Vomiting is coordinated by the **vomiting centre** in the medulla, which receives input from the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ), the gut (via 5-HT₃ receptors on vagal nerves), the inner ear (motion sickness) and the higher centres (smell, fear).

```text
 Gut irritation (chemotherapy, infection) → 5-HT₃ → vagus ─┐
 Drugs, toxins in blood → CTZ (D₂, 5-HT₃, NK₁) ──────────┼→ VOMITING CENTRE → vomiting
 Motion → inner ear (H₁, muscarinic) ────────────────────┤
 Anxiety, smell, pain → cortex ──────────────────────────┘
```

| Class | Examples | Main uses | Common side effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5-HT₃ antagonists | Ondansetron, granisetron | Chemotherapy, radiotherapy and post-operative vomiting | Headache, constipation, QT prolongation |
| D₂ antagonists (prokinetic) | Metoclopramide, domperidone | Gastritis, post-operative vomiting, migraine, gastroparesis | Metoclopramide: restlessness, acute dystonia (more in children and young adults); domperidone hardly enters the brain |
| Phenothiazines | Prochlorperazine, promethazine | Vertigo, drug-induced vomiting | Sedation, low BP, extrapyramidal effects |
| H₁ antihistamines | Promethazine, dimenhydrinate, meclizine, cyclizine | Motion sickness, vomiting of pregnancy (doxylamine with pyridoxine) | Drowsiness, dry mouth |
| Anticholinergic | Hyoscine (scopolamine), including a skin patch | Motion sickness | Dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention |
| NK₁ antagonist | Aprepitant | Chemotherapy (with ondansetron and dexamethasone) | Fatigue |
| Corticosteroid | Dexamethasone | Chemotherapy-induced vomiting (add-on) | See Unit 6 |

**Nursing responsibilities**: assess the cause, amount and character of vomit and record intake and output; give the antiemetic before the trigger (before chemotherapy, about half an hour before travel); keep the patient in a lateral position if drowsy to prevent aspiration; mouth care; monitor for dehydration and electrolyte loss; watch for abnormal movements after metoclopramide and report; warn about drowsiness (no driving).

## 2.5 Laxatives and purgatives

A **laxative** produces soft formed stool gently; a **purgative (cathartic)** produces more fluid, frequent evacuation. Many drugs are laxatives in small amounts and purgatives in larger amounts.

| Class | Examples | How they work | Points to note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk-forming | Ispaghula (psyllium) husk, methylcellulose, bran | Absorb water, increase stool bulk, stimulate peristalsis | Take with plenty of water, or they may obstruct; act in 1–3 days |
| Stool softeners / lubricants | Docusate, liquid paraffin | Soften and lubricate stool | Liquid paraffin: risk of lipid pneumonia if aspirated (not at bedtime lying down, not in children and the elderly); reduces vitamin A, D, E, K absorption |
| Osmotic | Lactulose, magnesium salts, polyethylene glycol, sodium phosphate (enema) | Hold water in the gut by osmosis | Lactulose also used in hepatic encephalopathy (reduces ammonia absorption); bloating; electrolyte disturbance; magnesium salts avoided in kidney failure |
| Stimulant | Bisacodyl, senna, castor oil | Irritate the bowel and increase motility | Cramps; long use can damage the colon |
| Others | Glycerin suppository, enemas | Local action in the rectum | Quick action |

**Uses**: constipation (after diet and fluid advice fail), bowel preparation before surgery or colonoscopy, after anal surgery or myocardial infarction to avoid straining, hepatic encephalopathy (lactulose), drug-induced constipation (opioids).

**Contraindications**: undiagnosed abdominal pain, suspected appendicitis, intestinal obstruction, inflammatory bowel disease in a flare.

**Laxative abuse**: dehydration, low potassium, weakness, dependence and an atonic ("lazy") colon.

**Nursing**: assess bowel habits, diet and fluids first; teach fibre, fluids, exercise and a regular toilet time; give bulk laxatives with a full glass of water; give stimulant laxatives at bedtime for a morning effect; record bowel movements; watch for diarrhoea, cramps and electrolyte loss.

## 2.6 Antacids and anti-peptic ulcer drugs

Peptic ulcer results from an imbalance between aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, *Helicobacter pylori*, NSAIDs, smoking) and protective factors (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, mucosal blood flow).

```text
 Parietal cell acid secretion is stimulated by:
   Histamine (H₂) ─┐
   Acetylcholine (M₃) ─┼→ PROTON PUMP (H⁺/K⁺-ATPase) → HCl
   Gastrin ─────────┘
 H₂ blockers act on one input; PPIs block the final common step.
```

| Group | Examples | Action | Important points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Antacids – systemic | Sodium bicarbonate | Neutralise acid quickly | Absorbed → alkalosis; CO₂ causes belching; sodium load (avoid in heart failure, hypertension) |
| Antacids – non-systemic | Magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, calcium carbonate | Neutralise acid | Mg causes diarrhoea, Al causes constipation, so they are combined; Al binds phosphate; calcium can cause rebound acid |
| H₂-receptor blockers | Famotidine, cimetidine | Block histamine on parietal cells | Cimetidine inhibits liver enzymes (interactions) and may cause gynaecomastia |
| Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) | Omeprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole, rabeprazole | Irreversibly block H⁺/K⁺-ATPase — the most powerful acid suppressors | Give before breakfast; long use: low magnesium, low vitamin B₁₂, fractures, gut infections |
| Mucosal protectives | Sucralfate, bismuth compounds | Coat the ulcer base | Sucralfate needs acid to act — give on an empty stomach and apart from other drugs; constipation. Bismuth blackens stool and tongue |
| Prostaglandin analogue | Misoprostol | Reduces acid, increases mucus | Prevents NSAID ulcers; diarrhoea; contraindicated in pregnancy (contracts the uterus) |
| Anti-*H. pylori* therapy | PPI + two antibiotics (amoxicillin with clarithromycin or metronidazole), usually for 14 days | Eradicates the bacterium; prevents relapse | Complete the course; metronidazole — no alcohol |

**Uses**: peptic ulcer, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD), Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, prevention of stress ulcers in ICU and of NSAID ulcers, before anaesthesia to reduce aspiration risk.

**Interactions**: antacids reduce absorption of tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, iron and digoxin — give them about 2 hours apart. Omeprazole may reduce the effect of clopidogrel.

**Nursing**: give antacids after meals and at bedtime (or as ordered); shake suspensions; give PPIs before breakfast; teach the patient to avoid smoking, alcohol and NSAIDs, to eat regular meals and to report black stools, vomiting blood or severe pain (signs of bleeding or perforation).

## 2.7 Antidiarrhoeals and fluid–electrolyte therapy

The main danger in diarrhoea is **dehydration and electrolyte loss**, especially in children. Replacing fluid is the most important treatment.

**Oral rehydration solution (ORS)**: a balanced mixture of glucose, sodium chloride, potassium chloride and trisodium citrate. Glucose helps sodium (and therefore water) to be absorbed from the gut by co-transport, which works even during diarrhoea. The WHO/UNICEF **low-osmolarity ORS** reduces stool output and vomiting. In children, **zinc** for 14 days is given along with ORS under the national guidelines (IMNCI).

**IV fluids** in severe dehydration or when the patient cannot drink: Ringer lactate (preferred) or normal saline, with potassium as needed.

**Drugs used in diarrhoea**

| Group | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antimotility | Loperamide, diphenoxylate with atropine | Slow the gut; not in children, bloody diarrhoea or high fever (can hold the infection in) |
| Antisecretory | Racecadotril | Reduces secretion into the gut |
| Adsorbents | Kaolin, pectin, activated charcoal | Little proven benefit |
| Probiotics | Lactobacillus, *Saccharomyces boulardii* | Restore normal flora; antibiotic-associated diarrhoea |
| Antimicrobials | Metronidazole (amoebiasis, giardiasis), ciprofloxacin or azithromycin (cholera, shigellosis), furazolidone | Only when there is a specific cause; most watery diarrhoea needs no antibiotic |

**Furazolidone**: a nitrofuran antimicrobial acting locally in the gut against many bacteria and *Giardia*; used in bacterial diarrhoea and giardiasis. Side effects: nausea, headache, haemolysis in G6PD deficiency, and a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol (flushing, vomiting) — the patient must avoid alcohol. It also weakly inhibits monoamine oxidase, so foods rich in tyramine and some drugs are avoided.

**Dicyclomine**: an anticholinergic antispasmodic that relaxes smooth muscle of the gut; used for abdominal colic, irritable bowel syndrome and dysmenorrhoea. Side effects: dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, fast heart rate. Contraindicated in glaucoma, enlarged prostate, intestinal obstruction and in infants under 6 months.

**Nursing in diarrhoea**: assess hydration (thirst, sunken eyes, skin pinch, urine output, weight, pulse, BP); keep an intake–output chart; teach mothers to prepare and give ORS correctly (clean water, whole packet in the stated volume, small frequent sips; continue breastfeeding and feeding); hand hygiene and safe disposal of stools; perianal skin care; observe for danger signs (lethargy, unable to drink, blood in stool).

## Remember

- Antiseptic = living tissue; disinfectant = objects. Clean before you disinfect.
- 70% alcohol works best; glutaraldehyde for endoscopes; hypochlorite for blood spills; soap inactivates chlorhexidine and cetrimide.
- Never induce vomiting in an unconscious patient or after corrosives or kerosene.
- Ondansetron (5-HT₃) for chemotherapy vomiting; hyoscine and antihistamines for motion sickness; metoclopramide can cause dystonia.
- Bulk laxatives need plenty of water; lactulose also treats hepatic encephalopathy; liquid paraffin risks lipid pneumonia.
- PPIs are the strongest acid suppressors — before breakfast; sucralfate on an empty stomach; misoprostol not in pregnancy.
- Mg antacids → diarrhoea, Al antacids → constipation; antacids bind tetracycline, iron, fluoroquinolones.
- ORS is the mainstay in diarrhoea; zinc for children; antimotility drugs not for children or bloody diarrhoea.
- Furazolidone and metronidazole: no alcohol. Dicyclomine: not in glaucoma or infants under 6 months.
