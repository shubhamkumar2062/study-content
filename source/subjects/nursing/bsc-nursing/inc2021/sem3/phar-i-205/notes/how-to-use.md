# How to use this guide

Pharmacology I is a 7-unit course taught in Semester 3 of the INC 2021 B.Sc Nursing programme. It covers the principles of pharmacology and the main drug groups that nurses use most frequently. It is not examined on its own at the end of Semester 3 — only internal (sessional/college) marks are recorded now. The university examination comes next semester.

## This is a "Part I" course — no university exam this semester

**There is no end-semester university theory examination for Pharmacology I in Semester 3.** Only internal/sessional marks are recorded this semester, and they are **carried forward**. Pharmacology is examined by the university **together with Pharmacology II (Semester 4) and Pathology I + II and Genetics**, as one written paper in Semester 4.

That Semester 4 combined paper is **75 marks, 3 hours**:

| Section | Subject | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Pharmacology (Part I + Part II) | 38 |
| B | Pathology (Part I + Part II) | 25 |
| C | Genetics | 12 |

Confirm the exact breakdown with your college, as universities may vary the internal split.

The pharmacology you learn now (Part I) is re-tested together with the Part II content next semester. **Do not treat this semester's work as "over" once the sessional is done.** Revise it again when you study Pharmacology II.

The practice paper in this pack is modelled on the **Section A pharmacology** questions of the Semester 4 combined paper.

## How the unit files are arranged

The INC syllabus for Pharmacology I has eight numbered units. Because Units VII and VIII together are large, they have been split to keep each notes file manageable. The seven files map to the INC units as follows:

| Notes file | INC unit(s) | Main content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit 1 | I | Introduction to pharmacology, pharmacodynamics (receptors, dose-response, efficacy, potency), pharmacokinetics (ADME), adverse drug reactions, drug interactions, pharmacovigilance |
| Unit 2 | II | Antiseptics and disinfectants; drugs acting on the GI system (antacids, PPIs, H2 blockers, antiemetics, laxatives, antidiarrhoeals) |
| Unit 3 | III | Drugs acting on the respiratory system (bronchodilators, antihistamines, corticosteroids, cough and cold preparations, oxygen); drugs acting on the skin (topical steroids, antifungals, antibacterials, retinoids) |
| Unit 4 | IV (a) | Autonomic nervous system drugs: cholinergic and anticholinergic; sympathomimetic and sympatholytic |
| Unit 5 | IV (b) | Cardiovascular drugs: antihypertensives, diuretics, anti-anginals, anti-arrhythmics, drugs for heart failure, anticoagulants |
| Unit 6 | V | Drugs for anaemia (iron, B12, folic acid, EPO), coagulation and bleeding, hypolipidaemic drugs, plasma expanders, drugs for shock |
| Unit 7 | VI / VII–VIII | Endocrine drugs (insulin, oral antidiabetics, thyroid, corticosteroids, sex hormones, oral contraceptives); drugs for infections (antibiotics, antitubercular, antifungal, antiviral, antiprotozoal, anthelmintic) |

## Probability ratings

- **High** — asked in most years; often as a short answer (5 marks) or as part of a 10-mark essay. Know the classification, mechanism, uses, adverse effects and nursing points.
- **Medium** — appears every few years or as part of a broader question.
- **Low** — less common; tested in MCQs and very short answers.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns in INC nursing pharmacology papers; they are not a promise.

## Scoring tips for this subject

1. **Classify drugs before describing them.** A classification table earns marks on its own and organises the rest of your answer.

2. **For every drug group, follow this structure**: Classification → Mechanism of action → Examples → Clinical uses → Adverse effects → Contraindications → Nursing responsibilities. Even a 5-mark answer improves when it follows this order.

3. **Know the high-alert drugs by heart**: insulin (types, hypoglycaemia), anticoagulants (bleeding risk, monitoring, reversal), antibiotics (allergy, culture before starting, complete the course), adrenaline (anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest doses and routes).

4. **Tables for comparisons score well**: H2 blockers vs PPIs, heparin vs warfarin, sedating vs non-sedating antihistamines, oral hypoglycaemic drug classes.

5. **Nursing responsibilities** must be included for every drug group. Examiners expect at least: check allergy, check relevant lab (BG for insulin, INR for warfarin), monitor for the drug's known adverse effect, teach the patient.

6. **For the MCQ section**, focus on: half-life, mechanism of action (which receptor or enzyme), one specific adverse effect per important drug, reversal agents (heparin → protamine, warfarin → vitamin K, opioids → naloxone, benzodiazepines → flumazenil).

7. **Exam reminder**: pharmacology is examined in Semester 4 as Section A (38 marks) of the combined 75-mark paper. The practice paper in this pack models that section.
