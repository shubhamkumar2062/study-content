# Unit 1: Introduction, asepsis, wound care, peri-operative care, fluid and electrolytes, shock and pain

This notes file covers INC Unit I (Introduction, 6 hours) and INC Unit III (common signs and symptoms — fluid and electrolyte imbalance, shock and pain, 6 hours). Both units give you the basic ideas you will use in every later system: the nursing process, asepsis, wound healing, pre- and post-operative care, fluids, shock and pain.

## 1.1 Evolution and trends of medical-surgical nursing

Medical-surgical nursing (now called adult health nursing) is the care of adults with illness or injury that is treated with medicines (medical) or with operations (surgical).

| Period | What changed |
| --- | --- |
| Early times | Care given by family members and religious orders; little knowledge of disease |
| Florence Nightingale (1850s) | Cleanliness, ventilation, nutrition and observation; nursing as a trained calling |
| Late 19th–early 20th century | Germ theory, antisepsis and anaesthesia make surgery safer; nurses assist in theatres |
| Mid 20th century | Antibiotics, intensive care units, specialised wards (cardiac, renal, neuro) |
| Present | Evidence-based practice, nursing process, critical care, day-care surgery, minimally invasive surgery, telehealth, nurse practitioners, patient safety programmes |

**Current trends**

- Shorter hospital stay and day-care (ambulatory) surgery → more discharge teaching and home care.
- Specialisation: critical care, oncology, cardiac, nephrology, orthopaedic nursing.
- Evidence-based practice and clinical protocols (care bundles, checklists).
- Patient safety: surgical safety checklist, infection-control programmes, fall prevention.
- Use of computers, electronic records and monitoring equipment.
- Holistic care, rehabilitation, palliative care and respect for patients' rights.

## 1.2 International classification of diseases (ICD)

- The **International Classification of Diseases** is published by the World Health Organization (WHO). It gives each disease and health problem a code, so that records from any hospital or country can be compared.
- Uses: hospital records, death and illness statistics, planning of services, research, insurance and billing.
- The current revision is **ICD-11** (in use from 2022); many Indian hospitals still use ICD-10 codes. Check which version your hospital record room uses.
- Nurse's part: write the diagnosis clearly and completely so that it can be coded correctly.

## 1.3 Roles and responsibilities of the nurse in medical-surgical settings

| Setting | Main roles |
| --- | --- |
| **Outpatient department (OPD)** | Receive and register patients, triage (who needs care first), check vital signs, weight and history, prepare for examination, assist in minor procedures, give injections and dressings, health education, follow-up advice and referral |
| **In-patient unit (ward)** | Admission and orientation, assessment, nursing care plan, giving medicines, monitoring, preparing for investigations and surgery, preventing complications (falls, pressure ulcers, infection), recording, discharge planning and teaching |
| **Intensive care unit (ICU)** | Continuous monitoring (ECG, saturation, blood pressure), care of ventilated patients, infusion pumps, emergency drugs and defibrillation, strict asepsis, family support, early detection of deterioration |

General roles in all settings: care giver, educator, counsellor, advocate, coordinator of the health team, manager, researcher.

### The nursing process (used throughout this course)

```text
 Assessment → Nursing diagnosis → Planning (goals) → Implementation → Evaluation
      ↑                                                                    │
      └───────────────────── reassess and modify ──────────────────────────┘
```

- **Assessment**: history, physical examination, investigation results.
- **Nursing diagnosis**: a statement of the patient's response to illness, e.g. "Ineffective airway clearance related to thick secretions as shown by noisy breathing and ineffective cough".
- **Planning**: list problems by priority (airway, breathing, circulation first), set measurable goals.
- **Implementation**: nursing actions with rationale.
- **Evaluation**: was the goal met? If not, change the plan.

## 1.4 Medical and surgical asepsis

**Asepsis** means freedom from disease-causing micro-organisms.

| Medical asepsis (clean technique) | Surgical asepsis (sterile technique) |
| --- | --- |
| Reduces the number and spread of micro-organisms | Removes all micro-organisms and spores from an area or object |
| Hand hygiene, clean gloves, cleaning, isolation, waste disposal | Sterile gloves, gowns, instruments, sterile field |
| Used for routine care, oral care, giving oral drugs | Used for surgery, catheterisation, dressings of surgical wounds, injections, IV lines |

**Principles of surgical asepsis**

1. Only sterile objects touch a sterile field.
2. If in doubt about sterility, consider it unsterile.
3. Sterile objects are kept above waist level and in sight.
4. A sterile field becomes contaminated if wet (strike-through).
5. The 2.5 cm border of a sterile drape is considered unsterile.
6. Do not talk, cough or reach across a sterile field.
7. Sterile persons face each other and face the sterile field.

### Inflammation

Inflammation is the body's protective response to injury or infection.

- **Cardinal signs**: redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor) and loss of function.
- **Vascular events**: brief narrowing of vessels → widening (vasodilatation) → more blood flow (redness, heat) → vessels become leaky → fluid and protein leave (swelling).
- **Cellular events**: white cells (first neutrophils, later macrophages) stick to the vessel wall, move out and eat bacteria and debris (phagocytosis).
- **Acute inflammation** lasts days; **chronic inflammation** lasts weeks to months, with lymphocytes, macrophages and fibrosis.

### Infection

Infection is the invasion and multiplication of micro-organisms that harm the body.

```text
Chain of infection:
 Infectious agent → Reservoir → Portal of exit → Mode of transmission
       → Portal of entry → Susceptible host → (back to agent)
```

Breaking any link prevents spread: hand hygiene breaks transmission; immunisation protects the host; cleaning removes the reservoir.

Signs of local wound infection: increasing pain, redness spreading, swelling, warmth, pus, foul smell, wound opening. General signs: fever, rapid pulse, raised white cell count.

## 1.5 Wound healing

A **wound** is a break in the continuity of skin or tissue.

### Types of healing

| Type | How it heals | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary intention** | Clean wound with edges brought together by sutures; little scar | Clean surgical incision |
| **Secondary intention** | Wound left open, fills from the base with granulation tissue; larger scar, slower | Infected wound, pressure ulcer, burns |
| **Tertiary intention (delayed primary closure)** | Left open for a few days (to drain infection), then closed | Contaminated traumatic wound |

### Stages of wound healing

| Stage | Time (approx.) | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Haemostasis | Minutes | Vessels narrow, platelets plug, clot forms |
| 2. Inflammatory | Up to about 4–6 days | Redness, swelling; neutrophils and macrophages clean the wound |
| 3. Proliferative (granulation) | About day 4 to 3 weeks | Fibroblasts make collagen, new capillaries form (red granulation tissue), epithelium covers the wound, wound contracts |
| 4. Maturation (remodelling) | 3 weeks to a year or more | Collagen reorganises, scar becomes pale and stronger |

### Factors affecting wound healing

- **Age**: older people heal more slowly.
- **Nutrition**: protein, vitamin C, zinc and calories are needed for collagen and new cells.
- **Blood supply and oxygen**: poor circulation, anaemia, smoking delay healing.
- **Infection**: prolongs inflammation.
- **Diseases**: diabetes mellitus, cancer, kidney failure, jaundice.
- **Drugs**: corticosteroids, anticancer drugs, immunosuppressants.
- **Obesity**: fatty tissue has poor blood supply; more tension on wound.
- **Local factors**: foreign body, dead tissue, tension on suture line, movement, oedema.

### Complications of wounds

- Haemorrhage, haematoma.
- Infection and abscess.
- **Dehiscence**: partial or full separation of wound layers.
- **Evisceration**: abdominal organs come out through the opened wound — an emergency: cover with sterile saline-soaked dressing, position with knees flexed, inform surgeon.
- Keloid and hypertrophic scar, contracture, incisional hernia, fistula.

## 1.6 Wound care and dressing technique

**Purposes of dressing**: protect from contamination and injury, absorb drainage, keep wound moist for healing, apply pressure, support or immobilise, apply medication, comfort.

**Types of dressing**: dry dressing, wet-to-dry (for debridement), moist dressings (hydrocolloid, hydrogel, foam, alginate), transparent film, pressure dressing.

### Procedure: surgical wound dressing (steps with rationale)

1. Check the doctor's order and the plan of care. — *Some wounds need special solutions or must not be disturbed.*
2. Explain the procedure; give pain relief if ordered before the dressing. — *Reduces anxiety and pain; gains cooperation.*
3. Arrange privacy; position the patient comfortably with the wound exposed. — *Dignity and easy access.*
4. Perform hand hygiene; wear clean gloves and remove the soiled dressing, peeling towards the wound. — *Prevents cross-infection and avoids pulling on the wound edges.*
5. Look at the old dressing (amount, colour, smell of drainage); discard it in the correct waste bag. — *Assessment and biomedical waste segregation.*
6. Remove gloves, perform hand hygiene, open the sterile dressing pack and put on sterile gloves (or use sterile forceps). — *Surgical asepsis.*
7. Assess the wound: size, edges, colour of tissue, drainage, suture line, signs of infection. — *Detects complications early.*
8. Clean with the ordered solution (often normal saline), one swab for one stroke, **from the cleanest area to the less clean**: from the incision line outward; for a drain site, in circles from the drain outward. — *Prevents carrying organisms into the wound.*
9. Dry the area, apply ordered medication and a sterile dressing; secure with tape or bandage. — *Protection and absorption.*
10. Dispose of waste correctly, clean equipment, perform hand hygiene. — *Infection control.*
11. Record: date, time, wound condition, drainage, dressing used and the patient's response. — *Legal record and continuity of care.*

**Wound drains**: open (Penrose) or closed suction (e.g. Jackson-Pratt, Romovac). Keep closed drains compressed for suction, record amount and type of drainage, keep the bag below the wound level.

## 1.7 Care of the surgical patient

Peri-operative care has three phases: **pre-operative** (from decision for surgery to arrival in theatre), **intra-operative** (Unit 2) and **post-operative** (from recovery room until full recovery).

### Pre-operative care

**Psychological preparation**: explain the surgery and what the patient will feel; answer questions; reduce fear; involve the family; respect religious wishes.

**Informed consent**: the surgeon explains the operation, risks, benefits and alternatives; the patient (adult, conscious, not sedated) signs freely; the nurse checks that the form is signed and witnessed. For a minor or an unconscious patient, the parent or legal guardian signs; in a life-threatening emergency, hospital policy is followed.

**Physical preparation**

| Area | Actions and reason |
| --- | --- |
| Assessment | History, allergies, current medicines, vital signs, weight, height |
| Investigations | Complete blood count, blood group and cross-match, blood sugar, kidney tests, ECG, chest X-ray, others as ordered |
| Nutrition | **Nil by mouth** for the period ordered (usually several hours for solids, less for clear fluids) — prevents vomiting and aspiration during anaesthesia |
| Bowel | Enema or bowel wash only if ordered (e.g. bowel surgery) |
| Skin | Bath, clean the operation site; hair removed only if needed, with clippers just before surgery, not by shaving — shaving causes small cuts that raise infection risk |
| Bladder | Ask the patient to void before going to theatre, or catheterise if ordered — an empty bladder is not injured and is out of the way |
| Valuables, prosthesis | Remove jewellery, dentures, contact lenses, hairpins, nail polish, make-up; hand over valuables — prevents loss, airway obstruction, burns from diathermy and allows nail-bed colour to be checked |
| Medicines | Give pre-medication as ordered (e.g. sedatives, anticholinergics, antacids); after sedation keep side rails up |
| Teaching | Deep breathing, coughing with splinting the wound, leg exercises, early ambulation, use of incentive spirometer |
| Identification | Identity band, site marking, checklist completed and signed |

### Post-operative care

**Immediate care (recovery room / post-anaesthesia care unit)**

1. **Airway**: position on the side or with head turned (until fully awake) to prevent tongue falling back and aspiration of vomit; keep suction ready; oral airway until gag reflex returns.
2. **Breathing**: watch rate, depth, oxygen saturation; give oxygen as ordered.
3. **Circulation**: pulse, blood pressure every 15 minutes at first; skin colour, capillary refill.
4. **Wound and drains**: look for bleeding.
5. **Level of consciousness** and return of reflexes and sensation (after spinal anaesthesia).
6. **Pain**, nausea and vomiting, temperature.
7. Discharge from recovery when stable and awake (using a scoring system such as the Aldrete score).

**Care in the ward**

- Vital signs as ordered; intake and output.
- Pain relief as ordered and regular pain assessment.
- Early ambulation as soon as allowed — prevents deep vein thrombosis, chest infection, constipation and urinary retention.
- Deep breathing, coughing and incentive spirometry every hour while awake.
- Diet: sips of water → liquids → soft → normal diet when bowel sounds return and as ordered.
- Wound care, drain care, catheter care.
- Hygiene, mouth care, positioning and pressure-area care.

### Post-operative complications

| Complication | Usual time | Nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemorrhage, shock | First 24 hours | Rapid weak pulse, falling BP, pallor, bleeding from wound or drain → inform surgeon, lie flat with legs raised, prepare fluids/blood |
| Airway obstruction, hypoxia | Immediate | Head tilt, suction, oxygen |
| Nausea and vomiting | Early | Turn to side, antiemetics as ordered |
| Urinary retention | 6–8 hours | Palpable bladder; privacy, running water, warm bedpan; catheterise if ordered |
| Atelectasis, pneumonia | 1–3 days | Fever, crepitations; deep breathing, coughing, ambulation |
| Paralytic ileus | 1–3 days | Distension, absent bowel sounds; nil orally, nasogastric drainage |
| Wound infection | 3–7 days | Fever, redness, discharge |
| Deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism | Days 5–14 | Calf pain, swelling; leg exercises, stockings, early walking |
| Dehiscence, evisceration | 5–8 days | Often after coughing or straining |

## 1.8 Alternative therapies in medical-surgical care

Alternative and complementary therapies are used along with standard treatment, not in place of it. In India, AYUSH systems (Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Homoeopathy) are recognised.

Examples useful to medical-surgical patients:

- **Relaxation, breathing exercises, yoga and meditation** — reduce anxiety and pain.
- **Music therapy, guided imagery, distraction** — reduce pain and anxiety around surgery.
- **Massage, therapeutic touch** — comfort, sleep.
- **Acupressure** — some patients use it for nausea.
- **Herbal products** — ask about them in the history; some affect bleeding or interact with prescribed drugs, so the doctor must know.

Nurse's role: ask about every therapy the patient uses, respect beliefs, encourage only safe practices, and report herbal products before surgery.

## 1.9 Fluid and electrolyte imbalance

Body water is about 60% of adult body weight: two-thirds inside the cells (intracellular fluid, ICF) and one-third outside (extracellular fluid, ECF — plasma and interstitial fluid).

| Electrolyte | Normal serum value (approx.) | Main role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium (Na⁺) | 135–145 mEq/L | Main ECF ion; water balance, nerve impulses |
| Potassium (K⁺) | 3.5–5.0 mEq/L | Main ICF ion; heart rhythm, muscle contraction |
| Calcium (Ca²⁺) | 8.5–10.5 mg/dL | Bones, clotting, nerve and muscle excitability |
| Magnesium (Mg²⁺) | 1.5–2.5 mEq/L | Enzymes, nerve and muscle function |
| Chloride (Cl⁻) | 96–106 mEq/L | Follows sodium |
| Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) | 22–26 mEq/L | Acid–base buffer |

Normal values differ slightly between laboratories; always use the laboratory's own range.

### Fluid volume deficit (dehydration, hypovolaemia)

- Causes: vomiting, diarrhoea, bleeding, burns, fever and sweating, poor intake, diuretics, drains.
- Signs: thirst, dry mouth and skin, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, weight loss, rapid weak pulse, low blood pressure, postural fall in BP, less urine (concentrated), raised haematocrit.
- Care: monitor intake and output, daily weight, vital signs; oral fluids or IV fluids as ordered; mouth care; prevent falls on standing.

### Fluid volume excess (overload)

- Causes: heart failure, kidney failure, too much IV fluid, liver cirrhosis.
- Signs: weight gain, oedema, distended neck veins, bounding pulse, high BP, breathlessness, crackles in the lungs.
- Care: fluid and salt restriction as ordered, diuretics as ordered, semi-Fowler's position, daily weight, intake and output, skin care of oedematous areas.

### Common electrolyte imbalances

| Imbalance | Common causes | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hyponatraemia** | Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics, excess water | Headache, confusion, muscle cramps, seizures |
| **Hypernatraemia** | Water loss, poor intake, fever | Thirst, restlessness, confusion, dry tongue |
| **Hypokalaemia** | Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics, nasogastric suction | Muscle weakness, cramps, constipation, ileus, arrhythmias |
| **Hyperkalaemia** | Kidney failure, crushing injury, burns, excess intake | Weakness, tall peaked T waves, dangerous arrhythmias, cardiac arrest |
| **Hypocalcaemia** | After thyroid or parathyroid surgery, kidney failure | Tingling around mouth and fingers, muscle spasm (tetany), positive Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs, seizures |
| **Hypercalcaemia** | Hyperparathyroidism, bone cancers | Weakness, constipation, kidney stones, confusion |

**Nursing point**: potassium given by vein is never given as a direct push; it is always diluted and given slowly through an infusion as ordered, with ECG monitoring if the dose is high, because a rapid rise in potassium can stop the heart.

### Intravenous therapy (overview)

| Type of fluid | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isotonic | Normal saline (0.9% NaCl), Ringer's lactate | Replace ECF loss, shock, surgery |
| Hypotonic | 0.45% saline | Cell dehydration |
| Hypertonic | 3% saline, 10% dextrose | Special uses under close watch |

Nursing responsibilities in IV therapy: check order and fluid, asepsis, correct flow rate, watch the site for **infiltration** (cool, swollen, pale) and **phlebitis** (red, warm, painful along the vein), watch for fluid overload, change sets and dressings as per policy, record.

Flow rate (drops per minute) = (volume in mL × drop factor) ÷ time in minutes.

## 1.10 Shock

**Shock** is a life-threatening condition in which blood flow to the tissues is not enough to meet their need for oxygen, leading to cell damage.

### Types

| Type | Cause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypovolaemic | Loss of blood or fluid | Haemorrhage, burns, severe diarrhoea |
| Cardiogenic | Failure of the heart as a pump | Myocardial infarction |
| Obstructive | Block to filling or outflow of heart | Cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive pulmonary embolism |
| Distributive — septic | Infection with widening of vessels | Severe infection |
| Distributive — anaphylactic | Severe allergy | Drug or insect-sting allergy |
| Distributive — neurogenic | Loss of sympathetic tone | High spinal cord injury |

### Stages

```text
 Initial → Compensatory → Progressive → Irreversible (refractory)
 (cells short   (body keeps BP     (compensation      (organ failure;
  of oxygen)     up: fast pulse,     fails: BP falls,   no response to
                 cool skin, less     acidosis, organ    treatment)
                 urine)              damage)
```

### Signs and symptoms

Restlessness and anxiety (early), rapid weak pulse, rapid breathing, cold clammy pale skin (warm and flushed in early septic shock), low blood pressure (later sign), reduced urine output (less than 0.5 mL/kg/hour), thirst, confusion, drowsiness.

### Management

- **Airway and breathing**: oxygen; prepare for ventilation if needed.
- **Circulation**: two large-bore IV cannulas; fluids and blood as ordered.
- **Position**: lie flat with legs raised (not with head injury or breathlessness).
- **Treat the cause**: stop bleeding, give antibiotics for sepsis, adrenaline for anaphylaxis as ordered, treat heart failure.
- **Drugs** (by class only): vasopressors and inotropes to support BP, as ordered.

### Nursing care

- Monitor vital signs every 15 minutes, oxygen saturation, level of consciousness.
- Hourly urine output through an indwelling catheter.
- Monitor central venous pressure if a line is in place.
- Keep warm with blankets (do not apply direct heat).
- Reassure the patient and family.
- Record intake, output and all observations.

**Nursing diagnoses**: decreased tissue perfusion; deficient fluid volume; anxiety.

## 1.11 Pain

**Pain** is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience linked with actual or possible tissue damage. Pain is whatever the patient says it is.

### Types

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Duration | **Acute** (sudden, short, has a cause, e.g. post-operative) and **chronic** (lasts more than 3–6 months, e.g. arthritis, cancer) |
| Origin | Somatic (skin, muscle, bone), visceral (organs, poorly localised), neuropathic (nerve damage, burning or shooting), **referred** (felt away from the source, e.g. heart pain in the left arm), phantom limb pain |

### Gate control theory (simple form)

Pain signals travel to the brain through a "gate" in the spinal cord. Rubbing, massage, heat and cold stimulate large nerve fibres that close the gate; anxiety opens it; the brain can also close it (distraction, relaxation, endorphins). This explains why non-drug methods help.

### Pain assessment

Use **PQRST**:

- **P** — provoking and palliating factors (what makes it worse or better)
- **Q** — quality (sharp, dull, burning, crushing)
- **R** — region and radiation
- **S** — severity, on a scale
- **T** — timing (onset, duration, pattern)

Pain scales: numeric rating scale (0 to 10), visual analogue scale, verbal descriptor scale, Wong-Baker faces scale (children, communication problems), behavioural scales for patients who cannot speak.

Also observe: facial expression, guarding, restlessness, sweating, rapid pulse and raised BP (acute pain).

### Management

**Drug methods (classes only)**: non-opioid analgesics (paracetamol, NSAIDs), opioid analgesics (for severe pain), adjuvants (some antidepressants and anticonvulsants for nerve pain), local anaesthetics (nerve blocks, epidural). WHO analgesic ladder: step 1 non-opioid → step 2 weak opioid → step 3 strong opioid, each with or without adjuvants. Patient-controlled analgesia is used after major surgery.

**Non-drug methods**: positioning and support, splinting the wound while coughing, heat or cold as ordered, massage, relaxation and breathing, distraction, music, guided imagery, TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation).

**Nursing responsibilities**: believe the patient's report; assess before and after each intervention; give analgesics on time and before pain becomes severe; watch for side effects of opioids (breathing slowing, drowsiness, constipation, nausea); record the pain score as the "fifth vital sign".

**Nursing diagnosis example**: Acute pain related to surgical incision as shown by pain score 7/10 and guarding of the abdomen. Goal: pain score below 3/10 within one hour.

## Remember

- Nursing process: assessment → diagnosis → planning → implementation → evaluation.
- ICD is published by WHO; current version ICD-11.
- Five signs of inflammation: redness, heat, swelling, pain, loss of function.
- Healing: primary (clean, sutured), secondary (open, granulation), tertiary (delayed closure).
- Stages: haemostasis → inflammation → proliferation → maturation.
- Clean a wound from clean to less clean, one swab per stroke.
- Evisceration: cover with sterile saline-soaked dressing, knees flexed, call surgeon.
- Pre-op: consent, nil by mouth, skin preparation with clippers, empty bladder, remove dentures and jewellery, teach exercises.
- Post-op priorities: airway, breathing, circulation; watch for haemorrhage and shock.
- Potassium is never given IV push.
- Hypocalcaemia → tetany, Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs.
- Shock: low BP is a late sign; restlessness and fast pulse come early; urine < 0.5 mL/kg/h.
- Pain is what the patient says it is; assess with PQRST and a 0–10 scale.
