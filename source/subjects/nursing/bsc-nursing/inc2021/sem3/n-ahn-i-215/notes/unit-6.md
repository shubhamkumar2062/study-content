## PART A — DISORDERS OF BLOOD

## 6.1 Review of anatomy and physiology of blood

- Blood is a connective tissue made of **plasma** (the fluid, about 55%) and **cells** (about 45%).
- Cells: **red blood cells (RBCs/erythrocytes)** carry oxygen using haemoglobin; **white blood cells (WBCs/leucocytes)** fight infection; **platelets (thrombocytes)** help clotting.
- Blood is made in the red bone marrow (haematopoiesis). RBCs live about 120 days and are broken down in the spleen and liver.
- Functions: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones), defence (WBCs, antibodies), clotting, and keeping body temperature and pH steady.

| Normal value (adult, approximate) | Range |
| --- | --- |
| Haemoglobin | Men about 13–17 g/dL; women about 12–15 g/dL |
| Total WBC count | 4,000–11,000 /µL |
| Platelet count | 1.5–4.0 lakh /µL (150,000–400,000) |

Use your own laboratory's reference range; values differ a little between labs.

## 6.2 Nursing assessment

**History**: tiredness, weakness, breathlessness, dizziness, bleeding or bruising easily, repeated infections, fever, bone pain, weight loss, diet (iron and vitamins), family history, blood loss (heavy periods, piles, ulcers).

**Physical assessment**: pallor (conjunctiva, nail beds, palms), jaundice, bruises (purpura, petechiae), bleeding gums, enlarged lymph nodes, enlarged spleen or liver, rapid pulse.

**Diagnostic tests**: complete blood count, peripheral blood smear, haemoglobin, reticulocyte count, bone marrow aspiration, clotting tests, blood grouping and cross-matching. Nurse's part: explain tests, label samples correctly, apply pressure after venepuncture, assist and care for the patient after bone marrow aspiration (pressure, watch for bleeding, rest).

## 6.3 Anaemia

**Anaemia** is a fall in haemoglobin (or RBCs) below normal, so less oxygen reaches the tissues. It is very common in India, especially in women.

| Type | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Iron-deficiency | Blood loss, poor iron intake, pregnancy — the commonest |
| Megaloblastic | Lack of vitamin B12 or folic acid |
| Haemolytic | RBCs broken down too fast (e.g. thalassaemia, sickle cell) |
| Aplastic | Bone marrow fails to make cells |
| Anaemia of chronic disease | Long illness, kidney failure, cancer |

- Features: tiredness, weakness, breathlessness on effort, pallor, dizziness, fast pulse, poor concentration; a smooth sore tongue and spoon-shaped nails in iron deficiency.
- Management: find and treat the cause; iron or vitamin supplements, diet rich in iron (green leafy vegetables, jaggery, dates, pulses, eggs, meat) and folate; blood transfusion if severe.
- Nursing: plan rest with activity, oxygen if very low, give iron with vitamin C for better absorption and warn that it darkens the stool, teach diet, treat the cause (deworming, treat ulcers, iron in pregnancy).

## 6.4 Polycythaemia

- Too many red cells make the blood thick, so it flows slowly and clots form.
- Features: red face, headache, dizziness, high blood pressure, risk of clots (stroke, DVT).
- Management: remove blood (venesection) as ordered, keep well hydrated, drugs as ordered.
- Nursing: encourage fluids, watch for signs of clotting, early movement.

## 6.5 Bleeding and clotting disorders

- **Clotting factor defects**: e.g. **haemophilia** (an inherited lack of a clotting factor) → bleeding into joints and muscles, prolonged bleeding after injury. Care: prevent injury, apply pressure and cold, give the missing factor as ordered, do not give intramuscular injections or aspirin, careful oral care.
- **Platelet defects** (**thrombocytopenia**): too few platelets → petechiae, bruises, bleeding gums, nose bleeds. Care: avoid injury and sharp objects, use a soft toothbrush, apply pressure after injections, watch for internal bleeding.
- **Thalassaemia**: an inherited disorder in which abnormal haemoglobin is made; children need repeated blood transfusions and iron is removed with chelating drugs. It is prevented by carrier testing and counselling before marriage/pregnancy.
- **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)**: widespread clotting and then bleeding at the same time, seen in severe illness — an emergency managed in intensive care.

## 6.6 White cell disorders and cancers of the blood

- **Leukaemia**: cancer of white cells; the marrow is filled with abnormal cells, so normal cells fall → anaemia, repeated infection, easy bleeding, enlarged liver, spleen and lymph nodes, bone pain. Acute types are rapid; chronic types are slower. Treatment: chemotherapy, sometimes bone marrow transplant.
- **Leukopenia / agranulocytosis**: too few white cells → high risk of infection; the patient is kept in protective (reverse barrier) isolation.
- **Lymphoma**: cancer of the lymph nodes (Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin) → painless swollen lymph nodes, fever, night sweats, weight loss; treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy.
- **Myeloma**: cancer of plasma cells in the marrow → bone pain, fractures, anaemia, kidney problems.

**Nursing care of the patient on chemotherapy or with low counts**: strict infection control and reverse barrier nursing, watch for fever and bleeding, mouth care, good nutrition, emotional support, safe handling of chemotherapy drugs and waste, manage nausea and hair loss with the patient's understanding.

**Health education on blood donation**: donors should be healthy adults within the accepted age and weight, with normal haemoglobin, no recent illness or risky exposure; donation is safe, the body replaces the blood, and one donation can help several patients. The nurse screens donors, cares for them during and after donation (rest, fluids, pressure on the site) and encourages voluntary donation.

## PART B — DISORDERS OF THE ENDOCRINE SYSTEM

## 6.7 Review of anatomy and physiology of the endocrine system

- Endocrine glands pour **hormones** straight into the blood to control growth, metabolism, water and salt balance, the stress response and reproduction.

| Gland | Main hormones and action |
| --- | --- |
| Pituitary (in the brain) | The "master gland"; growth hormone, and hormones that control the thyroid, adrenals and sex glands; ADH (controls water) and oxytocin |
| Thyroid (neck) | Thyroxine (T4) and T3 — control the body's metabolic rate; calcitonin lowers calcium |
| Parathyroid (4 small glands behind the thyroid) | Parathormone — raises blood calcium |
| Adrenal (on the kidneys) | Cortex: cortisol (stress, sugar), aldosterone (salt and water), sex hormones; medulla: adrenaline and noradrenaline |
| Pancreas (islets) | Insulin lowers blood sugar; glucagon raises it |

- Disorders are of two kinds: **too much** hormone (hyper-) or **too little** (hypo-), and sometimes **tumours** of the gland.

## 6.8 Nursing assessment

Ask about changes in weight, appetite, energy, mood, skin, hair, bowel habit, periods, tolerance of heat or cold, thirst and passing urine, and family history. On examination look at skin and hair, any neck swelling, weight, blood pressure, pulse and eyes. Tests include blood hormone levels, blood sugar, ultrasound and scans.

## 6.9 Disorders of the thyroid

### Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid; e.g. Graves' disease, goitre)

- Too much thyroid hormone speeds up the body.
- Features: weight loss with good appetite, fast pulse and palpitations, heat intolerance and sweating, nervousness and tremor, loose stools, bulging eyes (exophthalmos), a neck swelling (goitre).
- Management: anti-thyroid drugs, radioactive iodine, or surgery (thyroidectomy).
- **Thyroid storm** is a dangerous, sudden worsening with very high fever, fast heart and confusion — an emergency.

### Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid)

- Too little hormone slows the body.
- Features: weight gain, tiredness, cold intolerance, dry skin and hair, constipation, slow pulse, puffy face, slow thinking, heavy periods.
- Management: lifelong thyroid hormone replacement, taken daily; regular blood tests.

### Nursing care around thyroid surgery (thyroidectomy)

- **Before**: make the thyroid calm with drugs as ordered, teach the patient to support the neck when moving.
- **After**: semi-Fowler's position with the neck supported, watch for **bleeding** (check behind the neck and dressing), **breathing difficulty** (swelling pressing on the windpipe — keep a tracheostomy set ready), a **hoarse voice** (nerve injury), and **tetany** (tingling, spasm) from accidental removal of the parathyroids — keep calcium ready. Encourage gentle voice checks and later neck exercises.

## 6.10 Disorders of the parathyroid

- **Hyperparathyroidism**: too much parathormone → high blood calcium → weak bones, kidney stones, weakness, constipation, confusion.
- **Hypoparathyroidism**: too little → low calcium → tingling, muscle spasm (tetany), seizures; often after thyroid surgery. Managed with calcium and vitamin D.

## 6.11 Disorders of the adrenal and pituitary glands

- **Cushing's syndrome** (too much cortisol): round "moon" face, trunk obesity with thin limbs, purple stretch marks, high BP, high sugar, thin skin that bruises, weak bones, low mood.
- **Addison's disease** (too little adrenal hormone): weakness, weight loss, low BP, dark skin, salt craving; an **adrenal crisis** (sudden severe weakness, low BP, shock) is an emergency needing steroids and fluids.
- **Phaeochromocytoma** (adrenal medulla tumour): bursts of very high BP, headache, sweating, palpitations.
- **Pituitary disorders**: too much growth hormone causes gigantism (children) or acromegaly (adults, large hands, feet and jaw); too little ADH causes **diabetes insipidus** (passing large amounts of dilute urine and great thirst — different from diabetes mellitus).
- Nursing: monitor weight, BP, blood sugar and fluid balance; give hormone replacement exactly as ordered and teach that it must not be stopped suddenly; prepare for scans and surgery.

## 6.12 Diabetes mellitus (very important)

Diabetes mellitus is a long-term disorder in which blood sugar is high because the body makes too little insulin, or cannot use it well.

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Type 1** | The pancreas makes little or no insulin; usually in young people; needs insulin for life |
| **Type 2** | The body resists insulin; usually in adults, linked to obesity and inactivity; managed with diet, exercise, tablets, and sometimes insulin — the common type in India |
| **Gestational** | First appears in pregnancy |

- **Classic features**: passing a lot of urine (polyuria), great thirst (polydipsia), increased hunger (polyphagia), weight loss, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing wounds and repeated infections.
- **Diagnosis**: raised fasting blood sugar, raised post-meal sugar, raised HbA1c (average sugar over about three months). Nurses monitor sugar with a glucometer (GRBS).

### Acute complications

- **Hypoglycaemia (low sugar)**: sudden sweating, shaking, hunger, fast pulse, confusion, then unconsciousness. It can follow too much insulin, a missed meal or extra exercise. **Treat at once**: if the patient can swallow, give sugar/glucose by mouth; if unconscious, nothing by mouth — give glucose by vein or glucagon as ordered and call for help. Remember the rule "when in doubt, give sugar," because low sugar harms the brain quickly.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA, mainly type 1)**: very high sugar with ketones → deep rapid breathing, fruity breath, dehydration, vomiting, drowsiness; an emergency treated with IV fluids, insulin and potassium as ordered.
- **Hyperosmolar state (mainly type 2)**: very high sugar with severe dehydration and confusion.

### Chronic complications

- Eyes (retinopathy, may cause blindness), kidneys (nephropathy), nerves (neuropathy — numb feet), heart and vessels (heart attack, stroke, poor leg circulation), and the diabetic foot (ulcers and gangrene from numbness and poor blood flow).

### Management and nursing care

- **Diet**: regular meals, complex carbohydrates and fibre, limit sugar and fried food, control weight — the nurse teaches a simple, affordable diabetic diet plan.
- **Exercise**: regular activity helps lower sugar; carry sugar in case it drops.
- **Drugs**: oral drugs for type 2; insulin for type 1 and when needed in type 2 (described, not dosed).
- **Insulin teaching (self-administration)**: store insulin properly (cool, not frozen), roll cloudy insulin gently (do not shake), use the correct syringe or pen, give under the skin (subcutaneous) in the abdomen, thigh or arm, **rotate the sites** to prevent lumps, keep to the timing with meals, and recognise and treat low sugar.
- **Monitoring**: teach home blood-sugar testing and record keeping.
- **Foot care**: wash and dry feet daily, check for cuts and blisters, wear well-fitting footwear, never walk barefoot, cut nails straight, treat any wound early.
- **Sick-day rules and follow-up**: do not stop insulin when ill, drink fluids, check sugar often, attend regular check-ups of eyes, kidneys and feet.

## Remember

- Haemoglobin (adult): men ~13–17, women ~12–15 g/dL; platelets 1.5–4 lakh/µL.
- Iron-deficiency anaemia is the commonest; give iron with vitamin C; it darkens the stool.
- In thrombocytopenia and haemophilia: avoid injury and IM injections, use a soft toothbrush, apply pressure after venepuncture.
- Thalassaemia is prevented by carrier testing before marriage/pregnancy.
- Leukaemia and low white counts → reverse barrier (protective) nursing; watch for fever and bleeding.
- After thyroidectomy watch for: bleeding, breathing difficulty, hoarse voice and tetany; keep tracheostomy set and calcium ready.
- Hyperthyroidism speeds the body up (weight loss, fast pulse, heat intolerance); hypothyroidism slows it down.
- Diabetes classic signs: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss; HbA1c shows control over ~3 months.
- Hypoglycaemia is sudden and dangerous — when in doubt, give sugar; if unconscious, nothing by mouth.
- Insulin is given subcutaneously; rotate sites; store cool; roll cloudy insulin, do not shake.
- Diabetic foot care and daily foot checks prevent ulcers and amputation.
