# Unit 4: Nursing management of patients with disorders of the digestive system

This notes file covers INC Unit V (16 hours): GI assessment and investigations, disorders of the mouth, stomach, intestines, anus, pancreas, liver and gall bladder, and the GI procedures you must perform — gastric decompression, gavage (tube feeding), stoma care and feeding techniques. Drugs are named by class only.

## 4.1 Review of anatomy and physiology

```text
 Mouth → pharynx → oesophagus → stomach → duodenum → jejunum → ileum
       → caecum (appendix) → ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid colon
       → rectum → anal canal
 Accessory organs: salivary glands, liver, gall bladder, pancreas
```

- **Stomach**: stores food, secretes hydrochloric acid, pepsin and intrinsic factor (needed for vitamin B12 absorption).
- **Small intestine**: main site of digestion and absorption (villi).
- **Large intestine**: absorbs water and electrolytes, forms faeces.
- **Liver**: makes bile, proteins (albumin, clotting factors), stores glycogen, detoxifies drugs and ammonia.
- **Pancreas**: exocrine enzymes (amylase, lipase, trypsin) and endocrine hormones (insulin, glucagon).

## 4.2 Nursing assessment

### History

Appetite, weight change, difficulty in swallowing (dysphagia), pain on swallowing, nausea and vomiting (amount, colour — coffee-ground means altered blood), heartburn, abdominal pain (site, type, relation to food), bowel habits, diarrhoea, constipation, blood in stools (fresh red or black tarry = melaena), jaundice, alcohol intake, diet, past surgery, medicines (NSAIDs, steroids).

### Physical assessment of the abdomen

Order for the abdomen is **inspection → auscultation → percussion → palpation** — auscultation comes before palpation because touching changes bowel sounds.

| Step | Look for |
| --- | --- |
| Inspection | Shape (flat, distended), scars, visible peristalsis, dilated veins (caput medusae), hernias, jaundice of skin and eyes |
| Auscultation | Bowel sounds normally about 5–30 per minute; absent (paralytic ileus, peritonitis), high-pitched tinkling (obstruction) |
| Percussion | Tympany (gas), dullness (fluid, organs, masses); shifting dullness in ascites; liver span |
| Palpation | Tenderness, guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness, masses, enlarged liver or spleen |

Also examine the mouth, and the perianal area when indicated.

### Nine regions of the abdomen

```text
 Right hypochondrium | Epigastrium | Left hypochondrium
 Right lumbar        | Umbilical   | Left lumbar
 Right iliac (inguinal)| Hypogastrium (suprapubic) | Left iliac
```

## 4.3 GI investigations and nursing preparation

| Investigation | Preparation and after-care |
| --- | --- |
| **Upper GI endoscopy** (oesophagogastroduodenoscopy) | Nil by mouth before as ordered; remove dentures; throat spray; after: nil by mouth until gag reflex returns; watch for pain, bleeding, fever (perforation) |
| **Colonoscopy, sigmoidoscopy** | Clear liquid diet the day before; bowel preparation as ordered; after: watch for bleeding, abdominal pain; passing gas is normal |
| **Barium swallow / meal** | Nil by mouth before; after: fluids and laxative as ordered; stools will be white for a few days |
| **Barium enema** | Bowel preparation; after: fluids, laxative |
| Ultrasound abdomen | Nil by mouth for gall bladder studies; full bladder for pelvic scan |
| CT, MRI | Ask about contrast allergy and kidney function |
| **Liver function tests** | Bilirubin, liver enzymes (ALT, AST, ALP), albumin, prothrombin time |
| **Stool examination** | Occult blood, ova and cysts, culture; fresh sample |
| ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) | As for endoscopy; watch for pancreatitis after |
| **Liver biopsy** | Check clotting tests and platelets; after: lie on the **right side** for a few hours (pressure on the site), monitor pulse and BP for bleeding |
| **Paracentesis** (abdominal tap) | Empty the bladder (avoid puncture); sitting position; record weight and girth before and after; watch BP |

## 4.4 Disorders of the oral cavity — lips, gums and teeth

| Condition | Features | Care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stomatitis** (inflammation of mouth) | Painful ulcers; causes: infection, vitamin deficiency, chemotherapy | Soft bland food, mouth care with mild solutions, avoid spicy food |
| **Oral candidiasis (thrush)** | White patches that leave a raw area when scraped | Antifungal agents; denture hygiene |
| **Aphthous ulcer** | Painful shallow ulcers | Topical agents; avoid irritants |
| **Gingivitis, periodontitis** | Bleeding, swollen gums; tooth loss later | Brushing, flossing, dental care |
| **Dental caries** | Decay of teeth | Reduce sugar, fluoride toothpaste, dental visits |
| **Cleft lip/palate** (in adults: after repair) | Feeding and speech problems | Surgery and speech therapy |
| **Oral cancer** | Non-healing ulcer, white (leukoplakia) or red patches, lump; risk: tobacco chewing, smoking, betel quid, alcohol — a major cancer in India | Biopsy; surgery, radiotherapy; tobacco cessation, early screening |

## 4.5 GI bleeding

- **Upper GI bleeding** (above the ligament of Treitz): peptic ulcer, oesophageal varices, gastritis, Mallory-Weiss tear, cancer. Presents as **haematemesis** (vomiting blood) and **melaena** (black tarry stools).
- **Lower GI bleeding**: haemorrhoids, fissure, colon cancer, diverticular disease, inflammatory bowel disease. Presents as fresh red blood per rectum (haematochezia).
- **Management**: airway, two large IV lines, fluids and blood as ordered, nil by mouth, monitor pulse, BP and urine output, nasogastric tube if ordered, endoscopy to find and stop the bleed; acid-reducing drugs (proton pump inhibitors) as ordered; surgery if bleeding continues.
- **Nursing**: lie flat with legs raised if in shock; side position if vomiting; record amount and colour of blood loss; reassure; mouth care.

## 4.6 Infections and inflammation of the GI tract

- **Gastritis**: inflammation of the stomach lining — acute (alcohol, NSAIDs, food poisoning, stress) or chronic (*Helicobacter pylori*, autoimmune). Features: epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, indigestion. Care: remove the cause, small bland meals, acid-reducing drugs as ordered.
- **Gastroenteritis**: infection causing vomiting and diarrhoea (viral, bacterial, parasitic). Main risk is dehydration. Care: oral rehydration solution, IV fluids if severe, hygiene (see Unit 9 on diarrhoeal diseases).
- **Inflammatory bowel disease**:

| Feature | Ulcerative colitis | Crohn's disease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Rectum and colon only, continuous | Any part from mouth to anus, mostly terminal ileum; skip areas |
| Depth | Mucosa only | Full thickness of the wall |
| Main symptom | Bloody diarrhoea with mucus | Abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weight loss |
| Complications | Toxic megacolon, colon cancer | Strictures, fistulas, abscesses |
| Surgery | Removal of colon can cure | Not curative; done for complications |

Management (classes): anti-inflammatory agents (aminosalicylates), corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, biological agents; nutritional support; surgery (colectomy with ileostomy) when needed. Nursing: record stool frequency and character, fluids and electrolytes, perianal skin care, nutrition, emotional support.

## 4.7 Peptic and duodenal ulcer

- **Definition**: an erosion of the mucosa in areas exposed to acid and pepsin — stomach (gastric ulcer) or duodenum (duodenal ulcer, the commoner).
- **Causes**: *Helicobacter pylori* infection (main), NSAIDs, smoking, alcohol, stress, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (rare).
- **Pathophysiology**: imbalance between attacking factors (acid, pepsin, *H. pylori*, NSAIDs) and protective factors (mucus, bicarbonate, blood flow, prostaglandins) → mucosa erodes.

| Point | Gastric ulcer | Duodenal ulcer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain | Soon after meals | 2–3 hours after meals and at night; relieved by food |
| Weight | May lose weight (fear of eating) | Often normal or gain |
| Acid level | Normal or low | High |
| Cancer risk | Possible — biopsy needed | Very rare |

- **Tests**: upper GI endoscopy with biopsy; tests for *H. pylori* (urea breath test, stool antigen, biopsy); stool occult blood; CBC (anaemia).
- **Medical management (classes)**: proton pump inhibitors, H2-receptor blockers, antacids, mucosal protective agents; *H. pylori* eradication therapy (combination of a proton pump inhibitor with antibiotics); stop NSAIDs, smoking and alcohol.
- **Complications**: bleeding, **perforation** (sudden severe pain, board-like rigid abdomen, shock — surgical emergency), gastric outlet obstruction (pyloric stenosis — projectile vomiting of old food), malignancy (gastric).
- **Surgical management** (now less common): oversewing a perforation; vagotomy with pyloroplasty; partial gastrectomy (Billroth I, II).
- **After gastric surgery — dumping syndrome**: food passes quickly into the jejunum → faintness, sweating, palpitation, cramps, diarrhoea soon after meals. Teach small frequent dry meals, fluids between meals rather than with meals, more protein and fat, less simple sugar, lie down after meals.

**Nursing care**: regular small meals, avoid foods that cause pain, stop smoking and alcohol, avoid NSAIDs, take drugs as ordered and complete the eradication course, stress reduction, report black stools or vomiting blood.

## 4.8 Tumours of the GI tract

- **Oesophageal cancer**: progressive dysphagia (first solids, then liquids), weight loss; risk: tobacco, alcohol, hot drinks, achalasia, Barrett's oesophagus.
- **Gastric cancer**: indigestion, early fullness, weight loss, anaemia, mass, vomiting; risk: *H. pylori*, smoked and salted foods.
- **Colorectal cancer**: change in bowel habit, blood in stools, tenesmus, anaemia, weight loss, obstruction; risk: age, low-fibre high-fat diet, inflammatory bowel disease, polyps, family history.
- Tests: endoscopy and biopsy, CT, tumour markers (e.g. CEA for colorectal cancer, used for follow-up).
- Management: surgery (oesophagectomy, gastrectomy, hemicolectomy, abdominoperineal resection with colostomy), chemotherapy, radiotherapy, palliative care; nutritional support.

## 4.9 Intestinal obstruction

- **Definition**: blockage of the forward flow of intestinal contents.
- **Types**:
  - **Mechanical**: adhesions (commonest after surgery), hernia, tumour, volvulus (twisting), intussusception, faecal impaction, strictures.
  - **Non-mechanical (paralytic ileus)**: loss of peristalsis after abdominal surgery, peritonitis, hypokalaemia, spinal injury.
- **Pathophysiology**: blockage → gas and fluid collect above it → distension → vomiting and fluid loss → dehydration and electrolyte loss → pressure on bowel wall → reduced blood supply → strangulation, gangrene, perforation, peritonitis, shock.
- **Features**: colicky abdominal pain, vomiting (early and bilious in small-bowel obstruction; late and faeculent in large-bowel obstruction), distension, constipation with no gas passed (absolute constipation), high-pitched bowel sounds (silent in ileus).
- **Tests**: abdominal X-ray (dilated loops, fluid levels), CT, electrolytes.
- **Management**: "drip and suck" — nil by mouth, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids and electrolytes; surgery for strangulation or if not resolving (adhesiolysis, resection, colostomy).
- **Nursing**: nasogastric tube care, intake and output, monitor girth, vital signs, mouth care, pain assessment, prepare for surgery.

## 4.10 Perforation and peritonitis

- **Peritonitis**: inflammation of the peritoneum, usually from perforation of a hollow organ (ulcer, appendix, bowel, typhoid perforation of ileum) or infection.
- **Features**: severe abdominal pain worse on movement, patient lies still, **board-like rigidity**, rebound tenderness, absent bowel sounds, fever, rapid pulse, vomiting, shock.
- **Tests**: erect chest X-ray (gas under the diaphragm in perforation), CBC, CT.
- **Management**: nil by mouth, nasogastric suction, IV fluids, antibiotics, analgesia as ordered; emergency surgery (laparotomy, closure of perforation, peritoneal lavage).
- **Nursing**: semi-Fowler's position (pus collects in the pelvis rather than under the diaphragm), monitor vital signs and urine output, care of drains and wound.

## 4.11 Malabsorption

- Failure to absorb nutrients from the small intestine.
- Causes: coeliac disease (gluten intolerance), lactose intolerance, tropical sprue, chronic pancreatitis, short bowel after resection, Crohn's disease.
- Features: diarrhoea, bulky pale foul-smelling floating stools (steatorrhoea), weight loss, anaemia, vitamin and mineral deficiencies, oedema.
- Management: treat the cause — gluten-free diet in coeliac disease (avoid wheat, barley, rye), lactose-free diet, pancreatic enzyme replacement, vitamin and mineral supplements.

## 4.12 Appendicitis

- **Definition**: inflammation of the vermiform appendix, usually from blockage of its opening (faecolith, lymphoid swelling).
- **Features**: pain starts around the umbilicus and **shifts to the right iliac fossa**; tenderness at **McBurney's point** (one-third of the way from the anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus); rebound tenderness; low-grade fever, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite. Rovsing's sign: pressing on the left iliac fossa causes pain on the right.
- **Tests**: CBC (raised white cells), ultrasound, CT.
- **Management**: appendicectomy (open or laparoscopic); antibiotics.
- **Nursing before surgery**: nil by mouth, IV fluids, **no laxatives, no enema and no heat pad on the abdomen** (they can cause rupture), pain relief as ordered.
- **Complications**: perforation → peritonitis; appendicular mass or abscess.

## 4.13 Hernias

- **Definition**: protrusion of an organ or tissue through a weak area in the wall that normally contains it.
- **Types**: inguinal (indirect and direct — commonest in men), femoral (more in women), umbilical, paraumbilical, incisional (through an old scar), hiatus hernia (stomach through the diaphragm).
- **States**: reducible, irreducible (incarcerated), **obstructed**, **strangulated** (blood supply cut off — painful, tender, red, emergency).
- **Management**: hernia repair — herniorrhaphy, hernioplasty with mesh (open or laparoscopic).
- **After repair**: avoid straining, coughing with support of the wound, prevent constipation, no heavy lifting for the period advised (often several weeks), scrotal support and ice packs if there is scrotal swelling after inguinal repair.

## 4.14 Haemorrhoids, fissures and fistulas

| Condition | Features | Management |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Haemorrhoids (piles)** — dilated veins of the anal canal; internal (above dentate line) or external | Painless bright red bleeding with stools (internal), prolapse, itching; painful if thrombosed (external) | High-fibre diet, fluids, sitz baths, stool softeners; rubber band ligation, sclerotherapy; haemorrhoidectomy, stapler surgery |
| **Anal fissure** — tear in the lining of the anal canal | Severe pain during and after defecation, small amount of bright blood | Fibre, fluids, sitz baths, topical agents; lateral sphincterotomy if chronic |
| **Fistula-in-ano** — abnormal track from anal canal to perianal skin, usually after an abscess | Discharge of pus, recurrent abscesses | Fistulotomy, seton, other procedures |

**After anorectal surgery**: pain relief, **sitz bath** two to three times a day and after each bowel movement (cleans and relieves spasm), stool softeners, high-fibre diet and fluids so the first motion is soft, watch for bleeding and urinary retention.

## 4.15 Disorders of the pancreas

### Acute pancreatitis

- **Definition**: acute inflammation of the pancreas in which enzymes become active inside the gland and digest it (autodigestion).
- **Causes**: gallstones and alcohol (commonest), high triglycerides, trauma, ERCP, some drugs, mumps.
- **Features**: sudden severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, relieved partly by sitting and leaning forward; nausea, vomiting; fever; tachycardia; shock in severe cases; bruising in the flanks (Grey Turner's sign) or around the umbilicus (Cullen's sign) in severe haemorrhagic cases.
- **Tests**: serum amylase and lipase (raised; lipase more specific), ultrasound (gallstones), CT, calcium (low in severe disease), blood sugar.
- **Management**: nil by mouth initially, IV fluids (very important), analgesics as ordered, nasogastric suction if vomiting, early enteral feeding as ordered, treat the cause (ERCP for stones, later cholecystectomy), ICU care if severe; surgery for infected necrosis.
- **Nursing**: pain relief, position of comfort (sitting forward, knees flexed), fluid balance, monitor blood sugar and calcium (watch for tetany), breathing (risk of ARDS), no alcohol.

### Chronic pancreatitis

Repeated inflammation → fibrosis and loss of function. Mostly alcohol. Features: recurrent pain, steatorrhoea, weight loss, diabetes. Management: no alcohol, pain relief, pancreatic enzyme replacement with meals, low-fat diet, diabetes care.

### Pancreatic cysts and tumours

- **Pseudocyst**: fluid collection after pancreatitis; drained if large or symptomatic.
- **Carcinoma of the pancreas**: painless progressive jaundice (head of pancreas), weight loss, back pain, new diabetes; poor outlook. Whipple's operation (pancreaticoduodenectomy) when possible; palliative stenting of the bile duct.

## 4.16 Disorders of the liver

### Hepatitis (inflammation of the liver)

Viral hepatitis A to E is covered in Unit 9. Other causes: alcohol, drugs, autoimmune. Features: fatigue, loss of appetite, nausea, jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, tender liver.

### Liver abscess

- **Amoebic** (from *Entamoeba histolytica*; common in India) or **pyogenic** (bacterial).
- Features: fever, pain in the right upper abdomen, tender enlarged liver.
- Management: drugs against amoeba or antibiotics as ordered; aspiration or drainage for large abscesses.

### Liver cysts

Simple cysts (usually harmless) and **hydatid cyst** (dog tapeworm — anti-parasitic drugs and surgery; rupture can cause anaphylaxis).

### Cirrhosis of the liver

- **Definition**: chronic, irreversible liver disease in which normal tissue is replaced by fibrous scar and regenerating nodules, distorting liver structure and function.
- **Causes**: alcohol, chronic hepatitis B and C, fatty liver disease (obesity, diabetes), biliary obstruction, autoimmune and inherited disorders.
- **Pathophysiology**: repeated cell death → fibrosis and nodules → (1) loss of liver cell function and (2) obstructed blood flow through the liver → **portal hypertension**.

| Result of liver cell failure | Result of portal hypertension |
| --- | --- |
| Jaundice | Oesophageal varices (can bleed) |
| Low albumin → oedema and ascites | Splenomegaly (low platelets) |
| Low clotting factors → bleeding | Ascites |
| Raised ammonia → hepatic encephalopathy | Caput medusae, haemorrhoids |
| Spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia | |

- **Tests**: liver function tests, prothrombin time/INR, CBC (low platelets), ultrasound, endoscopy (varices), viral markers, liver biopsy or elastography.
- **Management**: stop alcohol; treat the cause (antiviral drugs for hepatitis B or C); diet; diuretics, salt restriction and paracentesis for ascites; beta-blockers and endoscopic band ligation for varices; lactulose and gut antibiotics for encephalopathy; liver transplantation for end-stage disease.

**Nursing diagnoses and care**

| Diagnosis | Interventions |
| --- | --- |
| Excess fluid volume (ascites, oedema) | Salt restriction (and fluid restriction if ordered), daily weight and abdominal girth, intake and output, semi-Fowler's position for breathing |
| Imbalanced nutrition | Small frequent meals, adequate calories; normal protein intake is generally advised, with limits only as ordered during acute encephalopathy; vitamins |
| Risk for bleeding | Watch gums, stools and vomit; soft toothbrush; avoid injections where possible; pressure after needle pricks |
| Risk for injury (confusion) | Watch for drowsiness, confusion, flapping tremor (asterixis); prevent constipation (lactulose as ordered); safety measures |
| Impaired skin integrity (itching, oedema) | Cool baths, mild soap, keep nails short, turn frequently |

### Portal hypertension and bleeding varices

Raised pressure in the portal vein → collateral veins swell, especially at the lower oesophagus. Variceal bleeding causes massive haematemesis. Emergency care: airway, IV lines, blood, drugs to reduce portal pressure (vasoconstrictors) as ordered, emergency endoscopic band ligation or sclerotherapy; balloon tamponade tube (e.g. Sengstaken-Blakemore) temporarily — keep scissors at the bedside to cut and remove it if it slips up and blocks the airway; TIPS (shunt) in selected patients.

### Hepatic failure (liver failure)

- Acute (e.g. viral hepatitis, drug overdose such as paracetamol overdose) or chronic (end-stage cirrhosis).
- Features: jaundice, bleeding, encephalopathy (stages from confusion to coma), kidney failure (hepatorenal syndrome), low blood sugar.
- Care: ICU, airway, blood sugar monitoring, measures to lower ammonia, avoid sedatives, transplantation.

### Liver tumours

Hepatocellular carcinoma (often on cirrhosis, hepatitis B and C), and secondaries from other organs (commoner). Features: weight loss, pain, mass, worsening liver function. Tests: alpha-fetoprotein, imaging, biopsy. Management: surgery, local ablation, chemoembolisation, targeted drugs, transplantation, palliation. Prevention: hepatitis B vaccination.

## 4.17 Disorders of the gall bladder

### Cholelithiasis and cholecystitis

- **Cholelithiasis**: stones in the gall bladder (cholesterol, pigment or mixed). Risk factors ("5 F": female, fat, fertile, forty, fair — a memory aid only), rapid weight loss, family history.
- **Cholecystitis**: inflammation of the gall bladder, usually from a stone blocking the cystic duct.
- **Features**: biliary colic — severe pain in the right upper abdomen or epigastrium, often after a fatty meal, radiating to the right shoulder or back; nausea, vomiting; fever and **Murphy's sign** (pain and catch in breathing when pressing under the right ribs) in cholecystitis; jaundice if a stone blocks the common bile duct (choledocholithiasis).
- **Tests**: ultrasound (test of choice), liver function tests, CBC, MRCP or ERCP for duct stones.
- **Management**: pain relief, antibiotics, IV fluids; **laparoscopic cholecystectomy** (standard treatment); ERCP to remove duct stones.
- **After cholecystectomy**: early ambulation; shoulder-tip pain after laparoscopy (gas) settles; if a T-tube is placed after open duct exploration: keep the bag below the wound, record the amount of bile, protect the skin; low-fat diet initially if advised.

### Tumours of the gall bladder

Carcinoma of the gall bladder (more common in parts of North and North-East India) — often found late with pain, mass, jaundice; surgery if early, otherwise palliation.

## 4.18 Gastric decompression, gavage, stoma care and feeding techniques

### Nasogastric (NG) tube insertion — steps with rationale

1. Check the order; explain; sit the patient upright (high-Fowler's) with head slightly flexed. — *Eases passage into the oesophagus, not the trachea.*
2. Measure the length: **tip of the nose → earlobe → xiphisternum (NEX)** and mark it. — *Places the tip in the stomach.*
3. Lubricate the tip with water-soluble jelly. — *Reduces trauma; a water-soluble gel is safe if aspirated.*
4. Insert through the more open nostril, aiming backwards along the floor of the nose; ask the patient to swallow (sips of water if allowed) as the tube passes the throat. — *Swallowing closes the airway.*
5. Stop and withdraw if the patient coughs, chokes or becomes cyanosed. — *The tube may be in the airway.*
6. Check position: aspirate gastric contents and **test with pH paper (gastric pH is usually 5.5 or below)**; X-ray confirmation when in doubt or for fine-bore tubes. Auscultating air ("whoosh test") alone is not reliable. — *Prevents feeding into the lungs.*
7. Secure with tape to the nose; record length, position check, and the patient's response.

**Gastric decompression**: the NG tube is connected to free drainage or intermittent low suction to remove gas and fluid (intestinal obstruction, ileus, after GI surgery). Nursing: keep the tube patent (irrigate with saline only if ordered), record drainage amount and colour, replace fluid and electrolytes as ordered, mouth and nostril care, watch for electrolyte imbalance.

### Gavage (tube feeding)

1. Check tube position before every feed and every medicine. — *Prevents aspiration.*
2. Patient sitting up at least 30–45° during feed and for 30–60 minutes after. — *Reduces regurgitation and aspiration.*
3. Check gastric residual volume if the hospital policy requires it.
4. Flush with water before and after the feed and medicines. — *Keeps the tube open.*
5. Give feed at room temperature, slowly by gravity or pump; never force. — *Prevents cramps, diarrhoea and vomiting.*
6. Close the tube after the feed; record amount and tolerance.

**Feeding techniques**: oral feeding with help; nasogastric and nasojejunal feeding; gastrostomy (PEG — through the abdominal wall) and jejunostomy for long-term feeding; bolus, intermittent or continuous feeds; **total parenteral nutrition (TPN)** through a central venous line when the gut cannot be used — strict asepsis, monitor blood sugar, weight and electrolytes, never stop suddenly without an order.

### Stoma care (colostomy, ileostomy)

| Point | Colostomy | Ileostomy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Large bowel | End of ileum |
| Output | Formed or semi-formed stool (more formed further along the colon) | Liquid, continuous, contains enzymes — irritates skin |
| Risks | Constipation | Dehydration, electrolyte loss, skin damage |

**Assess the stoma**: normal is moist, beefy red or pink, slightly raised. Dark red, purple or black means poor blood supply — report at once. Watch for retraction, prolapse, swelling and skin breakdown.

**Changing the pouch (steps)**

1. Explain; give privacy; encourage the patient to watch and take part. — *Promotes self-care and acceptance.*
2. Remove the old pouch gently, pushing the skin away from the wafer. — *Protects the skin.*
3. Clean the stoma and skin with warm water; pat dry. — *Soap residue can stop the wafer sticking.*
4. Measure the stoma; cut the wafer opening just slightly larger than the stoma (about 2–3 mm). — *Too large exposes skin to output; too small cuts the stoma.*
5. Apply skin barrier if needed; fit the wafer and pouch; hold for a minute (warmth helps adhesion).
6. Empty the pouch when about one-third to one-half full. — *Prevents leakage and the weight pulling it off.*
7. Record stoma appearance, output and skin condition.

**Patient teaching**: diet (avoid gas-forming foods if troublesome; ileostomy patients need plenty of fluids and salt), clothing, bathing, return to work, sexual concerns, support groups (ostomy associations), and when to seek help.

## 4.19 Drugs and alternative therapies in digestive disorders

| Drug class | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| Antacids, H2-receptor blockers, proton pump inhibitors | Acid disorders, ulcers, bleeding |
| Antiemetics | Nausea, vomiting |
| Laxatives and stool softeners | Constipation, before investigations, after anorectal surgery |
| Antidiarrhoeals, oral rehydration solution | Diarrhoea |
| Antispasmodics | Colicky pain |
| Antibiotics, anti-amoebic agents | Infections, *H. pylori*, abscesses |
| Aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants | Inflammatory bowel disease |
| Pancreatic enzymes | Pancreatic insufficiency |
| Diuretics, lactulose | Ascites, hepatic encephalopathy |

**Alternative therapies**: dietary approaches, yoga and relaxation for functional bowel symptoms, probiotics (curd), herbal remedies — always ask about herbs because some are harmful to the liver.

## Remember

- Abdomen: inspect → auscultate → percuss → palpate.
- Haematemesis and melaena = upper GI bleed.
- Duodenal ulcer pain is relieved by food and wakes the patient at night; *H. pylori* is the main cause.
- Perforation: sudden severe pain, board-like rigidity, gas under the diaphragm on erect X-ray.
- Intestinal obstruction: colicky pain, vomiting, distension, absolute constipation; "drip and suck".
- Appendicitis: McBurney's point; no enema, laxative or heat pad.
- Strangulated hernia is a surgical emergency.
- Acute pancreatitis: pain to the back, raised lipase and amylase; gallstones and alcohol.
- Cirrhosis → portal hypertension → varices, ascites, splenomegaly; watch for encephalopathy.
- Liver biopsy: lie on the right side after.
- Gallstones: ultrasound; laparoscopic cholecystectomy; Murphy's sign.
- NG tube length = nose → earlobe → xiphisternum; confirm position by pH test of aspirate.
- Healthy stoma is moist and red; dark or black stoma — report at once.
- Ileostomy output is liquid — watch fluids, electrolytes and skin.
