# Unit 8: Patients with musculoskeletal problems

INC Unit VIII covers anatomy and physiology of the musculoskeletal system, nursing assessment, and care of patients with fractures, dislocations, infections of bone, metabolic bone disease, and joint disorders.

---

## 8.1 Anatomy and physiology

### Bone
- Functions: support, protection, movement (with muscles), calcium/phosphate storage, haematopoiesis (red marrow).
- Types: long (femur, humerus), short (carpals, tarsals), flat (skull, scapula, ribs), irregular (vertebrae, pelvis), sesamoid (patella).
- Structure: outer **periosteum** (vascular, pain-sensitive, contains osteoblasts for healing) → compact cortical bone → cancellous (spongy/trabecular) bone → bone marrow cavity.
- Cells: **osteoblasts** (form bone), **osteocytes** (maintain matrix), **osteoclasts** (resorb bone).
- Blood supply: nutrient artery enters via nutrient foramen; periosteal vessels supply the outer layers — both are needed for healing.

### Joints
- **Fibrous** (sutures of skull — no movement), **cartilaginous** (symphysis pubis, intervertebral discs — slight movement), **synovial** (most limb joints — freely movable).
- Synovial joint: articular cartilage (smooth, avascular) + joint capsule + synovial membrane (makes synovial fluid — lubricates and nourishes cartilage) + ligaments.

### Muscles
- Skeletal (voluntary) muscle — movement; attached to bone via tendons.
- Cardiac (involuntary).
- Smooth (involuntary) — hollow organs.
- **Origin** (fixed end, less movement) and **insertion** (moving end).

### Normal musculoskeletal values
- Adult bone density: T-score >−1 (normal by DEXA); −1 to −2.5 = osteopenia; <−2.5 = osteoporosis.
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR): elevated in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), osteomyelitis, bone tumours.

## 8.2 Nursing assessment

**History**: onset (sudden = trauma/fracture vs gradual = arthritis), pain character (site, severity, radiation, what worsens/relieves), swelling, deformity, reduced range of motion, weakness, stiffness (morning stiffness of RA), previous fractures, occupational or sporting injury, osteoporosis risk (menopause, steroids, smoking, alcohol, low BMI, family history).

**Physical assessment**:
- Look: deformity, swelling, bruising, skin colour/integrity, posture, gait.
- Feel: warmth, tenderness (localised = fracture site), muscle spasm.
- Move: active then passive range of movement, crepitus (grating = bone ends), joint laxity.
- Special tests (by physiotherapist/doctor): valgus/varus stress, McMurray (knee meniscus), Lachman (ACL).
- Neurovascular status of limb distal to injury: **5 Ps** — Pain, Pulse, Pallor, Paraesthesia, Paralysis.

**Investigations**: X-ray (most fractures), CT (complex fractures, spine), MRI (soft tissue, ligament, meniscus, infection), bone scan, DEXA (bone density), ESR, CRP, RF (rheumatoid arthritis), uric acid (gout), blood culture (osteomyelitis).

## 8.3 Fractures

A **fracture** is a break in the continuity of bone. It is caused by trauma, repeated stress (stress fracture) or disease weakening the bone (pathological fracture).

### Types of fractures

| Type | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed (simple) | Skin intact over fracture site | Most common; safer |
| Open (compound) | Bone penetrates or is near a skin wound; infection risk | High-velocity injuries |
| Greenstick | Incomplete fracture; one cortex intact; like bending a green twig | Children |
| Comminuted | Bone shattered into multiple fragments | High-energy trauma |
| Transverse | Fracture line perpendicular to long axis | Direct blow |
| Oblique | Fracture at an angle | Rotational force |
| Spiral | Fracture winds around the bone | Twisting injury (classic in child abuse) |
| Compression | Vertebral body crushed | Osteoporosis, fall from height |
| Avulsion | Bone fragment pulled off by tendon/ligament | Ankle, pelvis |
| Stress (fatigue) | Repeated microtrauma — no single event | March fracture (metatarsal in soldiers/runners) |
| Pathological | Bone already weakened (osteoporosis, tumour, Paget's) | Minimal or no trauma |

### Fracture healing stages

```text
1. HAEMATOMA (hours–days)
   Blood fills the fracture gap → clots → inflammatory reaction

2. SOFT CALLUS (1–2 weeks)
   Fibroblasts and osteoblasts invade → fibrocartilage bridge forms
   The fracture is still mobile but no longer freely moving

3. HARD CALLUS (3–6 weeks)
   Fibrocartilage replaced by woven bone (primary callus)
   Visible on X-ray

4. BONE REMODELLING (months–years)
   Woven bone → mature lamellar bone; medullary canal reopens
   Shape returns to near-normal (Wolff's law — bone remodels along stress lines)
```

Healing is affected by: age (faster in children), blood supply, bone type (cancellous heals faster than cortical), infection, immobilisation (inadequate = non-union), nutrition (calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, protein), systemic diseases (diabetes, steroids).

### Fracture management

**Principles**: Recognition → Resuscitation → Reduction → Immobilisation → Rehabilitation.

**1. First aid**
- Do not move the patient until the fracture is splinted.
- Splint in the position found; use a padded board, SAM splint or improvised splint.
- Control bleeding with pressure.
- Cover open fractures with a clean damp dressing (do not push bone back).
- Monitor neurovascular status (5 Ps) before and after splinting.

**2. Reduction**: realigning bone ends.
- **Closed reduction**: manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA).
- **Open reduction** (ORIF — open reduction and internal fixation): surgical; screws, plates, intramedullary nail, wires.

**3. Immobilisation methods**

| Method | Use | Nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plaster of Paris (POP) cast | Simple fractures | Check circulation distal to cast; keep cast dry; do not put objects inside to scratch |
| Synthetic (fibreglass) cast | Simple fractures | Lighter, waterproof, sets faster |
| Traction | Femoral shaft, cervical spine, to reduce muscle spasm | Maintain correct weight and alignment; care for traction pin sites; prevent foot drop |
| External fixator | Open fractures, infected fractures, complex fractures | Pin site care (clean daily with saline/antiseptic, monitor for infection), keep fixator clean |
| Internal fixation (ORIF) | Displaced fractures, joint fractures | Post-operative nursing — wound, DVT prevention, early mobilisation |

**Cast nursing care**
- Elevate the limb above the heart for the first 24–48 hours to reduce swelling.
- **5 Ps** check every 1–2 hours initially; report pain unrelieved by analgesia (compartment syndrome).
- Keep cast dry (cover with plastic bag for showering).
- Do not use objects to scratch inside the cast.
- Report: increased pain, numbness, pallor, cold fingers/toes, cast cracking or smelling (possible wound infection or pressure sore under cast).
- Teach cast care: signs to report, activity restrictions, follow-up X-ray dates.

### Complications of fractures

| Complication | Signs | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compartment syndrome | Severe pain unrelieved by analgesia, pain on passive stretch of muscles, tight tense limb, paraesthesia, pallor | Emergency: notify surgeon immediately; remove all encircling bandages/cast; fasciotomy if pressure >30 mmHg |
| Fat embolism syndrome | 24–72 hours post-long-bone fracture or manipulation; petechial rash, confusion, hypoxia, tachycardia; fat globules in urine | Oxygen, supportive care, corticosteroids, early mobilisation or fixation |
| Avascular necrosis | Loss of blood supply to bone fragment (femoral head after hip fracture, scaphoid) | Surgical |
| Non-union / delayed union | Fracture not healed at expected time | Bone grafting, electrical stimulation |
| Malunion | Fracture heals in wrong position | Osteotomy |
| Infection (osteomyelitis) | In open fractures | Antibiotics, debridement |
| DVT / pulmonary embolism | Lower limb fractures, immobility | Anticoagulation, compression stockings, early mobilisation |

## 8.4 Dislocations

A **dislocation** is complete loss of contact between joint surfaces; a **subluxation** is partial loss.

Common sites: shoulder (commonest dislocation), elbow, hip (congenital hip dysplasia in infants), patella, ankle.

**Features**: deformity, pain, inability to move, swelling; loss of normal joint contour.

**Management**: X-ray first (rule out fracture), then reduction under analgesia/sedation; immobilise; physiotherapy to regain strength.

## 8.5 Osteomyelitis

**Osteomyelitis** is infection of bone. The commonest organism is **Staphylococcus aureus**.

**Routes**: haematogenous (blood-borne spread from skin boil, UTI, dental abscess — common in children, affects metaphysis of long bones), direct (open fracture, surgery, joint replacement), contiguous (from adjacent soft tissue infection).

**Types**

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Acute | Sudden onset; fever, local pain, warmth, swelling, reduced movement; child refuses to weight-bear |
| Chronic | Following incompletely treated acute; dead bone (sequestrum), shell of new bone (involucrum), sinus tract discharging pus |

**Investigations**: raised WBC, CRP, ESR; blood culture (take before antibiotics); X-ray (changes appear after 10–14 days — periosteal elevation), MRI (early detection), bone scan.

**Management**
- Prolonged IV antibiotics (4–6 weeks minimum; flucloxacillin for Staphylococcus); then oral.
- Rest and splinting of the affected limb.
- Surgical debridement (removal of dead bone/pus) for chronic osteomyelitis or if no response.
- Wound irrigation and dressings for open wounds.

**Nursing care**: immobilise and elevate the limb, strict asepsis during dressing changes, wound irrigation as ordered, monitor vital signs and inflammatory markers, ensure complete antibiotic course (never stop early), nutritional support, psychological support in long illness, teach signs of relapse.

## 8.6 Bone tumours

| Type | Features | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Osteosarcoma | Most common primary malignant bone tumour; adolescents; distal femur/proximal tibia; Codman's triangle on X-ray; "sunray" spicules | Limb-sparing surgery + chemotherapy |
| Ewing's sarcoma | Children; diaphysis of long bones; "onion skin" periosteal reaction on X-ray | Chemotherapy + surgery or radiotherapy |
| Osteochondroma | Commonest benign tumour; bony outgrowth with cartilage cap; near growth plates; most left alone unless symptomatic | Excision if symptomatic |
| Multiple myeloma | Malignant plasma cells in bone marrow; "punched-out" lesions on X-ray; in older adults; bone pain, anaemia, renal failure, hypercalcaemia | Chemotherapy, biphosphonates |

**Nursing**: pain management (opioids for malignant bone pain), handle bones carefully (pathological fracture risk), calcium monitoring, teach side effects of chemotherapy, psychological support.

## 8.7 Osteoporosis

**Osteoporosis** is reduced bone mass and strength leading to fragile bones and fracture risk.

**Risk factors**: female sex, post-menopause (oestrogen loss accelerates bone resorption), age >65, family history, low BMI, smoking, excess alcohol, low calcium/vitamin D intake, long-term corticosteroids, thyroid excess, sedentary lifestyle.

**Features**: often silent until a fracture occurs; typical fractures: wrist (Colles'), vertebral (wedge, can cause kyphosis and loss of height), hip (neck of femur — most dangerous; high mortality).

**Diagnosis**: DEXA scan — T-score ≤−2.5.

**Management**
- **Non-pharmacological**: weight-bearing exercise (walking, dancing — builds bone), fall prevention (home assessment, grab rails, good footwear, eye care), calcium (1000–1200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800–1000 IU/day).
- **Pharmacological**: bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate — take with full glass of water, sit upright for 30 min; osteonecrosis of jaw is a rare serious side effect), denosumab (RANK-L inhibitor, injection every 6 months), teriparatide (PTH analogue, stimulates new bone formation), HRT (post-menopausal women, short-term).

**Nursing**: teach fracture risk and prevention, medication compliance (bisphosphonate instructions are critical), calcium and vitamin D supplementation, home safety assessment, exercise programme, screen for depression (chronic pain, loss of independence).

## 8.8 Rheumatoid arthritis versus osteoarthritis

**Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)** is a systemic autoimmune disease causing symmetric inflammatory polyarthritis with joint destruction.

**Osteoarthritis (OA)** is a degenerative joint disease from cartilage breakdown, most common in weight-bearing joints.

| Feature | Rheumatoid Arthritis | Osteoarthritis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Autoimmune — synovitis; pannus erodes cartilage | Wear and tear; cartilage loss, subchondral sclerosis |
| Age | Any age; peak 30–50; more females | >50; both sexes equally (older age) |
| Joints | Symmetrical; small joints first (MCP, PIP, wrists) | Asymmetrical; weight-bearing (knees, hips), DIPs of hands, spine |
| Morning stiffness | >1 hour; relieved by activity | <30 minutes; "gelling" after rest |
| Swelling | Soft (synovitis, effusion) | Hard (osteophytes), Heberden's nodes (DIP), Bouchard's (PIP) |
| Systemic features | Fatigue, fever, anaemia, rheumatoid nodules, vasculitis, lung disease | None — purely articular |
| X-ray | Periarticular osteoporosis, joint space narrowing, erosions | Osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, joint space narrowing, subchondral cysts |
| Serology | RF positive (70%), anti-CCP (more specific) | Negative |
| Treatment | DMARDs (methotrexate first line), biologic agents (TNF inhibitors), NSAIDs, steroids | Simple analgesics (paracetamol), NSAIDs, physiotherapy, weight loss, joint replacement |

**Nursing care for RA**
- Rest affected joints during flares; splinting of wrists overnight.
- Gentle range-of-motion (ROM) exercises during remission; physiotherapy.
- Teach joint protection: use larger joints, avoid sustained grip, use aids (jar openers, raised toilet seat).
- Monitor for methotrexate toxicity: regular LFT and CBC; ensure folic acid supplementation; advise against alcohol.
- Support body image and functional independence; occupational therapy referral.

## 8.9 Low back pain and intervertebral disc herniation

**Low back pain** (LBP) is very common. Most (>90%) is mechanical (muscle strain, poor posture, disc degeneration); a few cases are due to serious causes (fracture, infection, malignancy) — red flags: age >50, history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, neurological deficit, bladder/bowel involvement (cauda equina emergency).

**Intervertebral disc herniation** ("slipped disc"): nucleus pulposus protrudes through the annulus fibrosus and presses on a nerve root → sciatica (pain radiating down the leg, usually below the knee in L4–L5/L5–S1 disc).

**Management of LBP**
- Short-term rest (not prolonged bed rest — worsens outcome); gentle early activity.
- Paracetamol, NSAIDs, muscle relaxants.
- Physiotherapy: core strengthening, posture correction, manual therapy.
- Epidural steroid injection for radicular pain.
- Surgery (discectomy, laminectomy) for persistent neurological deficit or cauda equina syndrome.

**Nursing**: encourage activity within pain limits, correct body mechanics (bend knees not waist, hold load close to body), mattress advice (medium-firm), pain management, encourage weight loss, occupational advice.

## 8.10 Rehabilitation principles

Rehabilitation begins the day after surgery or stabilisation of the fracture — not after full healing.

Goals: prevent complications of immobility (DVT, pressure ulcers, pneumonia, muscle wasting), restore range of motion, strength and function, enable return to independent living.

**Nurse's role**
- Passive and active ROM exercises.
- Positioning (foot drop prevention: foot board, calf rests).
- DVT prophylaxis: low-molecular-weight heparin, anti-embolism stockings, ankle pump exercises.
- Pressure ulcer prevention: turning, padding.
- Nutritional support.
- Encourage and supervise physiotherapy programme.
- Assistive devices: crutches, walking frame, wheelchair; teach safe use.
- Psychosocial support; referral to social work for home adaptations.

## 8.11 Remember

- **5 Ps** for neurovascular check: Pain, Pulse, Pallor, Paraesthesia, Paralysis.
- Fracture healing: haematoma → soft callus → hard callus → remodelling.
- Compartment syndrome = emergency: cut/bivalve cast, call surgeon for fasciotomy.
- Fat embolism: 24–72 h post-long-bone fracture; petechiae, confusion, hypoxia.
- Open fracture: cover with clean damp dressing; IV antibiotics; risk of osteomyelitis.
- Osteoporosis: DEXA T-score ≤−2.5; bisphosphonate — take with full glass of water and stay upright.
- RA vs OA: RA = autoimmune, symmetric, small joints, RF+, morning stiffness >1 h; OA = wear and tear, weight-bearing joints, Heberden's nodes.
- Osteomyelitis: Staphylococcus aureus; IV antibiotics 4–6 weeks; never stop early.
