# Unit 5: Nursing management of patients with cardiovascular problems (with BLS/BCLS)

This notes file covers INC Unit VI (cardiovascular disorders, 20 hours theory and 5 hours lab, including the Basic/Basic Cardiac Life Support module). It is the heaviest unit of the course, so plan two or three study sessions. Learn assessment first, then vascular disorders, then diseases of the heart itself, then rhythm problems and failure, and finish with cardiac arrest and life support.

## 5.1 Review of anatomy and physiology of the cardiovascular system

- The heart is a four-chambered muscular pump in the middle of the chest (mediastinum), apex pointing down and to the left at about the fifth intercostal space in the midclavicular line.
- Layers: pericardium (outer sac), myocardium (muscle), endocardium (inner lining). The pericardial sac holds a little fluid that lets the heart move without friction.
- Chambers and valves: right atrium and right ventricle (tricuspid valve between them, pulmonary valve to the lungs); left atrium and left ventricle (mitral valve between them, aortic valve to the body).
- Blood flow: body → vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → lungs (pick up oxygen) → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body.
- The heart's own blood comes from the right and left coronary arteries, which arise from the aorta. Blockage here causes angina and heart attack.
- Conduction system (the heart's wiring) makes it beat on its own:

```text
SA node (pacemaker, right atrium) → AV node → bundle of His
   → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres → ventricles contract
```

- **Cardiac output** = heart rate × stroke volume (the blood pumped per beat). It depends on preload (filling), afterload (resistance the heart pumps against) and contractility (strength of the beat).
- **Blood pressure** = cardiac output × resistance in the vessels. It is controlled by the heart, the vessels, the kidneys and hormones.

## 5.2 Nursing assessment

**History**: chest pain (site, type, radiation, triggers, relief), breathlessness (on effort, lying flat — orthopnoea, waking at night — paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea), palpitations, swelling of feet, fainting, tiredness, cough; risk factors (smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, obesity, family history, stress, lack of exercise); past rheumatic fever; current medicines.

**Physical assessment**: general look (distress, cyanosis, pallor), pulse (rate, rhythm, volume, equality in both arms), blood pressure (both arms, lying and standing), neck veins (raised in heart failure), oedema of feet and sacrum, apex beat, heart sounds (normal S1 and S2; added S3, S4, murmurs), lung bases (crackles in failure), peripheral pulses and capillary refill, weight.

```text
Chest pain of cardiac origin — classic pattern:
 Central/left chest, crushing or heavy, may spread to left arm, jaw or back,
 with sweating, nausea and breathlessness; brought on by effort or stress.
```

## 5.3 Invasive and non-invasive cardiac procedures

| Procedure | What it is | Nursing points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ECG (electrocardiogram)** | Records the heart's electrical activity; non-invasive | Clean dry skin, correct lead placement, patient relaxed and still |
| **Echocardiography** | Ultrasound of the heart; shows chambers, valves, pumping | No preparation; explain it is painless |
| **Stress test (TMT)** | ECG during exercise on a treadmill | Light meal, comfortable shoes; stop if chest pain or arrhythmia |
| **Chest X-ray** | Heart size, lung congestion | Remove metal objects |
| **Cardiac catheterisation / angiography** | A catheter passed from the groin or wrist to the coronary arteries, with dye | **Before**: consent, nil by mouth, check kidney function and allergy to contrast, mark peripheral pulses, shave/clean site. **After**: bed rest with the limb straight, pressure over the site, watch for bleeding and haematoma, check distal pulse, colour and warmth, encourage fluids to flush the dye |
| **Angioplasty (PTCA) and stenting** | The blocked artery is widened with a balloon and a stent | As for catheterisation; continue antiplatelet drugs as ordered |
| **Blood tests** | Cardiac markers (troponin, CK-MB) after chest pain; lipid profile; electrolytes | Correct timing of troponin samples |

## 5.4 Disorders of the vascular system

### Hypertension (high blood pressure)

- Persistently raised blood pressure. It is called the "silent killer" because it often has no symptoms until it damages organs.
- **Primary (essential)**: no single cause; linked to age, salt, obesity, stress, family history, alcohol, smoking. **Secondary**: due to another disease (kidney disease, endocrine disorders, pregnancy, some drugs).
- Effects if untreated: stroke, heart attack, heart failure, kidney failure, eye damage.
- Features when present: headache, dizziness, nose bleeds, blurred vision.
- Management: lifestyle change (less salt, weight loss, exercise, no smoking, less alcohol, stress control) and antihypertensive drugs (by class: diuretics, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers), taken regularly for life.
- Nursing: measure BP correctly (rested, correct cuff, both arms), teach the patient to take drugs daily even when feeling well, teach home monitoring, diet counselling, report very high readings and warning signs.

### Arteriosclerosis and atherosclerosis

- **Arteriosclerosis**: thickening and hardening of artery walls with age.
- **Atherosclerosis**: fatty plaques (cholesterol) build up inside arteries, narrowing them and reducing blood flow; the main cause of coronary and peripheral artery disease.
- Risk factors: high cholesterol, smoking, diabetes, high BP, obesity, inactivity.

### Raynaud's disease

- Sudden narrowing of small arteries of the fingers and toes, usually brought on by cold or stress; the fingers turn white, then blue, then red, with pain and numbness.
- Nursing: keep hands and body warm, avoid cold and smoking, protect fingers from injury.

### Aneurysm

- A weak, bulging part of an artery wall (commonly the aorta). Danger is rupture with severe bleeding.
- Features: a pulsating swelling, pain; sudden tearing pain and shock if it ruptures.
- Management: control blood pressure; surgical repair or stent graft for large aneurysms.
- Nursing: avoid straining, monitor BP and pulses, watch for signs of rupture (sudden pain, falling BP) — an emergency.

### Peripheral vascular (arterial and venous) disorders

- **Peripheral arterial disease**: narrowed leg arteries → pain in the calf on walking that eases with rest (intermittent claudication), cold pale foot, weak pulses, poor healing, gangrene. Nursing: keep legs slightly dependent, foot care, no smoking, graded walking, protect from injury.
- **Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)**: clot in a deep leg vein → calf pain, swelling, warmth, redness. Risk after surgery and long bed rest. Prevention: early movement, leg exercises, anti-embolism stockings, hydration. Danger: the clot can travel to the lung (pulmonary embolism). Do not massage the leg.
- **Varicose veins**: dilated twisted superficial veins, aching, swelling. Nursing: elevate legs, support stockings, avoid long standing.

## 5.5 Coronary artery disease

Coronary atherosclerosis narrows the coronary arteries so the heart muscle gets too little oxygen.

### Angina pectoris

- Chest pain from a temporary shortage of blood to the heart muscle, usually during effort or stress; it is relieved by rest and by nitroglycerine under the tongue.
- Pain is central or left, pressing, may spread to the arm or jaw, lasts a few minutes.
- Nursing during an attack: stop activity, sit the patient up, give oxygen and nitroglycerine as ordered, reassure, record. Teach the patient to avoid triggers and to carry the medicine.

### Myocardial infarction (heart attack)

- Death of part of the heart muscle because a coronary artery is completely blocked.
- Features: severe crushing central chest pain lasting more than 20–30 minutes, **not relieved by rest or nitroglycerine**, sweating, nausea, breathlessness, fear; sometimes silent in older people and diabetics.
- Diagnosis: ECG changes, raised cardiac markers (troponin), history.
- Immediate management (remember **MONA**, as ordered): Morphine for pain, Oxygen, Nitroglycerine, Aspirin (chewed); plus drugs to dissolve the clot (thrombolytics) or urgent angioplasty to open the artery; bed rest, continuous ECG monitoring.
- Nursing: relieve pain, complete rest to reduce the heart's work, continuous monitoring for arrhythmias, oxygen, calm reassurance, watch for complications (arrhythmia, heart failure, shock), gradual return to activity (cardiac rehabilitation), health teaching on diet, smoking, exercise and drugs.

## 5.6 Valvular disorders

- Valves may be **narrowed (stenosis)** so blood cannot pass easily, or **leaky (regurgitation/incompetence)** so blood flows backward.
- **Congenital**: present at birth. **Acquired**: most often from rheumatic heart disease; also from infection or ageing.
- Common lesions: mitral stenosis, mitral regurgitation, aortic stenosis, aortic regurgitation.
- Features: breathlessness, tiredness, palpitations, a murmur heard on listening, and later heart failure.
- Management: drugs for symptoms and heart failure, prevention of infective endocarditis, valve repair or replacement surgery.
- Nursing: activity as tolerated, monitor for failure, teach about drugs and the need to tell dentists and surgeons about the valve problem.

## 5.7 Rheumatic heart disease and inflammation of the heart

- **Rheumatic fever** follows a throat infection with group A streptococcus; the body's own defence damages the heart valves. **Rheumatic heart disease** is the lasting valve damage. It is prevented by treating sore throats properly and by long-term penicillin prophylaxis as ordered.
- **Pericarditis**: inflammation of the pericardium → sharp chest pain that is worse on lying down and on deep breathing, a rubbing sound on listening, fever. Danger: fluid can collect and press on the heart (tamponade).
- **Myocarditis**: inflammation of the heart muscle → tiredness, breathlessness, chest pain, arrhythmias.
- **Endocarditis (infective)**: infection of the heart's inner lining and valves → fever, murmur, small emboli; needs long antibiotic treatment. People with valve disease take antibiotics before dental and surgical procedures to prevent it.
- **Cardiomyopathy**: disease of the heart muscle itself (dilated, hypertrophic or restrictive) → heart failure and arrhythmias.
- Nursing for all: rest, monitor vital signs and rhythm, give drugs on time, watch for failure and emboli, teach prevention.

## 5.8 Cardiac dysrhythmias and heart block

- **Dysrhythmia (arrhythmia)**: an abnormal heart rhythm — too fast, too slow or irregular.
- Examples: sinus tachycardia (fast), sinus bradycardia (slow), **atrial fibrillation** (fast irregular atria, risk of clots and stroke), ventricular tachycardia and **ventricular fibrillation** (life-threatening — the ventricles quiver and do not pump).
- **Heart block**: the signal from the atria to the ventricles is delayed or blocked; complete heart block gives a very slow pulse and fainting and may need a pacemaker.
- Nursing: continuous ECG monitoring, record the rhythm, note the pulse and blood pressure, keep emergency drugs and the defibrillator ready, care of a patient with a pacemaker (teach to avoid strong magnetic fields and to report dizziness or hiccups).

```text
Dangerous rhythms to recognise at once:
 Ventricular fibrillation → no effective pulse → start CPR, defibrillate
 Ventricular tachycardia without a pulse → treat like VF
 Asystole (flat line) → start CPR
```

## 5.9 Heart failure and related emergencies

### Congestive heart failure (CHF)

- The heart cannot pump enough blood for the body's needs, so blood backs up.
- **Left-sided failure**: blood backs up into the lungs → breathlessness, orthopnoea, night breathlessness, cough with frothy sputum, crackles.
- **Right-sided failure**: blood backs up into the body → swollen feet and sacrum, raised neck veins, enlarged tender liver, weight gain.
- Management: rest, oxygen, drugs as ordered (diuretics to remove fluid, drugs to strengthen or unload the heart), salt and fluid restriction.
- Nursing: sit the patient up (Fowler's position), oxygen, daily weight and strict intake–output, watch breathing and oxygen saturation, give drugs on time, low-salt diet, skin and pressure-area care, teach the patient to weigh daily and report weight gain and swelling.

### Cor pulmonale

- Right-sided heart failure caused by chronic lung disease (such as COPD). Care includes treating the lung disease, oxygen and the usual heart-failure care.

### Acute pulmonary oedema

- A sudden flooding of the lungs with fluid — an emergency. Severe breathlessness, pink frothy sputum, gasping, restlessness, fast pulse.
- Nursing: sit the patient upright with legs down, high-flow oxygen, call for help, give drugs as ordered, reassure, continuous monitoring.

### Cardiogenic shock

- Shock because the heart cannot pump (often after a large heart attack): low blood pressure, cold clammy skin, confusion, little urine. Needs intensive care, oxygen, drugs to support the heart and blood pressure, and continuous monitoring.

### Cardiac tamponade

- Fluid or blood collects in the pericardial sac and squeezes the heart so it cannot fill. Features (Beck's triad): low blood pressure, muffled heart sounds, raised neck veins. It is relieved by draining the fluid (pericardiocentesis). Nursing: recognise it, call for help, assist with drainage, monitor.

## 5.10 Cardiopulmonary arrest and BLS/BCLS

**Cardiopulmonary arrest** is the sudden stopping of effective breathing and heartbeat. Without blood flow the brain is damaged within minutes, so action must be immediate.

### Chain of survival

```text
Early recognition and call for help → Early CPR → Early defibrillation
   → Advanced care → Post-resuscitation care
```

### Basic Life Support (BLS) — the DRCAB/CAB approach

1. **Danger**: check the area is safe for you and the patient.
2. **Response**: tap and shout "Are you all right?"
3. **Call for help**: shout for help and the emergency team; send someone for the defibrillator (AED).
4. **Check breathing and pulse** (take no more than about 10 seconds). If not breathing normally and no pulse → start CPR.
5. **C — Compressions** (begin with these in an adult):
   - Place the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone (centre of the chest), the other hand on top.
   - Push hard and fast: about 100–120 compressions a minute, depth about 5 cm, let the chest come fully back up each time.
   - Change the person giving compressions about every 2 minutes to avoid tiring.
6. **A — Airway**: open the airway by head tilt and chin lift (jaw thrust if neck injury is suspected).
7. **B — Breathing**: give rescue breaths with a bag-mask or mouth-to-mask; the chest should rise.
   - One rescuer, adult: 30 compressions to 2 breaths.
8. Continue CPR until the AED arrives, the patient recovers, or help takes over.

### Using an AED (automated external defibrillator)

- Switch it on and follow the spoken instructions; dry the chest, attach the pads as shown; stand clear while it analyses; if it advises a shock, make sure no one is touching the patient, then press shock; resume compressions at once.

### Basic Cardiac Life Support (BCLS) / advanced points

- Good-quality compressions with the fewest breaks are the most important factor in survival.
- A shockable rhythm (ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia) is treated with defibrillation; a non-shockable rhythm (asystole, pulseless electrical activity) is treated with continued CPR and emergency drugs by the team.
- Emergency drugs are given by the advanced team as per protocol (named by class only, not by dose here).
- After return of the heartbeat: keep the airway and oxygen, monitor, find and treat the cause, and transfer to intensive care.

**Nursing role in arrest**: recognise it early, call the code, start compressions without delay, bring the crash cart and defibrillator, assist the team, record times and actions, and support the family afterwards.

## Remember

- Blood flow: body → RA → RV → lungs → LA → LV → aorta → body. Pacemaker = SA node.
- After cardiac catheterisation: bed rest, pressure on the site, check distal pulse, colour and warmth; encourage fluids.
- Hypertension is the "silent killer"; take drugs for life even when feeling well; measure BP correctly.
- Angina pain is relieved by rest and nitroglycerine; heart attack pain is not.
- Heart attack first aid as ordered: MONA — Morphine, Oxygen, Nitroglycerine, Aspirin; continuous ECG, rest.
- Atrial fibrillation carries a risk of clots and stroke.
- Ventricular fibrillation and pulseless VT are shockable; asystole is not — but all need CPR.
- Left heart failure → lungs (breathlessness); right heart failure → body (oedema, raised neck veins). Daily weight is a key measure.
- Cardiac tamponade = Beck's triad: low BP, muffled heart sounds, raised neck veins.
- Adult CPR: push on the lower half of the sternum, 100–120/min, about 5 cm deep, 30 compressions to 2 breaths; change rescuers every 2 minutes; use the AED as soon as it arrives.
