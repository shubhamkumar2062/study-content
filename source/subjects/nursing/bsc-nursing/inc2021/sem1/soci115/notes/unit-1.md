# Unit 1: Introduction to sociology, basic social concepts and social groups

This notes file covers INC Unit I (Introduction, 1 hour) and the first part of INC Unit II (Social structure): society, community, association, institution, the individual and society, personal disorganization and social groups. The rest of Unit II is in notes file 2.

## 1.1 What sociology is

The word **sociology** comes from the Latin *socius* (companion, associate) and the Greek *logos* (study or science). So, word for word, it is "the study of companionship", that is, of people living together.

The term was first used by the French thinker **Auguste Comte** (1798–1857), who is called the **father of sociology**. He wanted a science of society that studied social life by observation, the way natural sciences study nature.

**Definitions** (learn one or two and write them with the name):

| Writer | Definition in short |
| --- | --- |
| Auguste Comte | The science of social phenomena, studied by observation and classification |
| Max Weber | The science that tries to understand social action and so explain its course and effects |
| Émile Durkheim | The science of social institutions and social facts |
| MacIver and Page | Sociology is about social relationships; the network of relationships is called society |
| Ginsberg | The study of human interactions and inter-relations, their conditions and consequences |

A simple working definition for the exam: **sociology is the scientific study of human society, social relationships, social groups and social institutions, and of how they change.**

## 1.2 Nature of sociology

1. **An independent social science** – it has its own subject matter (social relationships) and is not a branch of philosophy or history.
2. **A social science, not a physical science** – it studies people and their relations, not matter.
3. **A categorical (positive) science, not a normative one** – it describes what *is*, not what *ought to be*; it does not pass moral judgement.
4. **A pure science, not an applied one** – its main aim is knowledge; but its findings are applied in nursing, medicine, social work, law and planning.
5. **An abstract science** – it studies patterns (family, caste, marriage), not one particular family or event.
6. **A generalizing science** – it looks for general rules that hold across many societies.
7. **Both rational and empirical** – it uses reasoning and also facts collected by observation, surveys and case studies.

## 1.3 Scope of sociology

There are two classic views of the scope.

**1. Formal (specialistic) school** – Georg Simmel, Max Weber, Tönnies, Vierkandt. Sociology should study only the *forms* of social relationships (cooperation, competition, conflict, domination, subordination), not their content, so that it stays a separate, specialised science.

**2. Synthetic school** – Émile Durkheim, L. T. Hobhouse, Sorokin, Ginsberg. Society is a whole; all parts are related, so sociology should be a general science that brings together the findings of all social sciences.

Most textbooks combine the two. In practical terms the scope covers:

- social structure – groups, communities, associations, institutions
- social processes – cooperation, competition, conflict, accommodation, assimilation
- social institutions – family, marriage, religion, education, economy, government
- social stratification – caste, class, race, social mobility
- culture and socialization
- social control and social change
- social problems and social disorganization
- special fields: medical sociology (sociology of health and illness), rural and urban sociology, sociology of the family, criminology, clinical sociology

## 1.4 Significance of sociology in nursing

Illness is never only a biological event; it happens to a person who lives in a family, a caste, a religion, a village or a slum. Sociology helps the nurse see that person.

| How sociology helps | Example in nursing |
| --- | --- |
| Understanding the patient as a social being | A daily-wage worker may leave hospital early because the family has no other earner |
| Understanding family and its role in care | Joint families often take decisions together; the nurse involves the right family member |
| Respecting culture, beliefs and customs | Food taboos after delivery, fasting, preference for a female nurse |
| Understanding social causes of disease | Poverty, overcrowding, illiteracy, unsafe water, addiction |
| Community health nursing | Knowing village structure, leaders, caste groups and local health practices helps in planning programmes |
| Health education | Messages are shaped to the group's language, values and literacy |
| Working in the hospital as a social system | Nurses understand roles, status, hierarchy and teamwork |
| Identifying vulnerable groups | Elderly, disabled, women, children, migrants, tribal people |
| Rehabilitation | Helping a patient return to family, work and community after illness or disability |
| Professional adjustment | Nursing itself is a social role with expectations, ethics and status |
| Social change | Nurses adapt to new technology, urbanization and changing family forms |

Remember the one-line idea: **sociology helps the nurse give holistic, culturally appropriate, family- and community-centred care.**

## 1.5 Society

**Society** is a web (network) of social relationships. MacIver and Page called it "a web of social relationships" that keeps changing. People living together over time, with a common culture and a sense of belonging, form a society.

**Characteristics of society**

1. **Likeness** – members share some common features (language, needs, culture).
2. **Difference** – there is also variety (age, sex, occupation); differences make division of labour possible.
3. **Interdependence** – no member is self-sufficient; the farmer, doctor, nurse and teacher need each other.
4. **Cooperation and conflict** – both exist; cooperation holds society together, controlled conflict brings change.
5. **Society is abstract** – relationships cannot be seen; only people and their behaviour can.
6. **Society is dynamic** – it changes all the time.
7. **Society has culture** – each society has its own way of life.
8. **Society is wider than any group** – it includes many groups, communities and institutions.

## 1.6 Community

A **community** is a group of people living in a **definite locality** who share a **common life** and have a **sense of belonging** ("we-feeling"). A village, a town, a tribal hamlet and a housing colony are communities.

Characteristics:

- a definite **locality** (territory)
- **community sentiment** – we-feeling, role feeling and dependency feeling
- common life, common customs, often a common language or dialect
- it is **natural** – people are born into it; it is not created for a purpose
- it is **permanent** and has its own name
- it is **wider** than an association; it meets most of a person's needs

## 1.7 Association

An **association** is a group **deliberately organised** to pursue one or more **specific common interests**. Examples: Trained Nurses' Association of India, a trade union, a cricket club, a political party, a women's self-help group.

Characteristics:

- formed for a definite purpose
- has rules, a constitution and office bearers
- membership is voluntary (in most cases)
- it is temporary – it may end when its aim is met
- it is a concrete group of people

## 1.8 Institution

An **institution** is an established, approved **way (procedure) of doing things** in a society. It is a system of rules and norms, not a group of people. Marriage, family, religion, education, property and government are institutions.

Characteristics: built around a basic social need; relatively permanent; has rules and procedures; has symbols (wedding ring, flag, uniform); passed from one generation to the next; abstract.

### Difference between community, association and institution

| Point | Community | Association | Institution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | People in a locality with a common life | People organised for a specific purpose | Established procedure or set of rules |
| Nature | Concrete | Concrete | Abstract |
| Origin | Grows naturally | Deliberately formed | Grows slowly through custom and law |
| Membership | By birth or residence | Voluntary | One does not "join" it; one follows it |
| Duration | Permanent | Can be temporary | Long lasting |
| Example | A village | Trained Nurses' Association of India | Marriage |

A useful link: **a hospital is an association; the system of hospital care and its rules is an institution; the town in which it stands is a community.**

## 1.9 Individual and society

The relationship between the individual and society is two-way.

**Theories**

- **Social contract theory** (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau): people originally lived in a "state of nature" and made an agreement (contract) to form society for protection. It is criticised because no such historical contract is known.
- **Organic theory** (Herbert Spencer): society is like a living body; individuals are its cells and institutions its organs. It shows interdependence but ignores that individuals have free will.
- **Group mind theory**: society has a mind of its own beyond individual minds; it is largely rejected.
- **Modern view**: the individual and society **make each other**. Society shapes the individual through socialization, and individuals shape society through their actions.

**Evidence that a person becomes "human" only in society** – reported cases of children reared in isolation (such as the girls known as Kamala and Amala found in Bengal, and the American child Anna) showed that without social contact a child does not learn language, emotional bonds or ordinary human behaviour.

**What society gives the individual**: protection, food and shelter through cooperation, language, culture, education, identity, status and role.

**What the individual gives society**: work, new ideas, leadership, and the transmission of culture to the next generation.

## 1.10 Personal disorganization

**Personal disorganization** is a state in which an individual's behaviour **deviates from the accepted norms** of the group so that he or she cannot fulfil social roles, and personal life and relationships break down. Elliott and Merrill describe it as the opposite of personal organization.

**Forms (examples)**: alcoholism and drug addiction, crime and delinquency, suicide and attempted suicide, prostitution, mental illness, vagrancy and beggary, gambling.

**Causes**

| Group | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| Personal | Physical or mental illness, disability, frustration, low self-esteem |
| Family | Broken home, death or desertion of a parent, domestic violence, neglect |
| Economic | Poverty, unemployment, debt |
| Social | Rapid social change, migration, loss of traditional controls, bad company |
| Cultural | Conflict between old and new values, culture shock |

**Role of the nurse**: identify early signs (withdrawal, absence from work, injuries, substance use), listen without judging, refer to counsellor, de-addiction centre or social worker, involve the family, and teach healthy coping.

## 1.11 Social groups

A **social group** is two or more people who **interact** with one another, share a **sense of unity** and have **common interests or goals**. A crowd waiting at a bus stop is an aggregate, not a group, because there is no interaction or we-feeling.

**Characteristics of a social group**

1. Two or more members
2. Reciprocal relationships and interaction
3. Sense of unity ("we-feeling")
4. Common interest or goal
5. Group norms that regulate behaviour
6. A structure – roles, statuses, often a leader
7. Groups are dynamic; they change over time

**Classification of social groups**

| Basis | Types | Given by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closeness of contact | **Primary** and **secondary** groups | C. H. Cooley (primary); secondary added later |
| Sense of belonging | **In-group** ("we") and **out-group** ("they") | W. G. Sumner |
| Way of formation | **Voluntary** and **involuntary** groups | – |
| Size | Small and large groups | Simmel |
| Organisation | Formal and informal groups | – |
| Life of the group | Temporary and permanent | – |
| Point of reference | **Reference group** – a group whose standards a person uses to judge himself | Herbert Hyman, R. K. Merton |
| Type of bond | **Gemeinschaft** (community, close ties) and **Gesellschaft** (association, impersonal ties) | Ferdinand Tönnies |

### Primary and secondary groups

| Feature | Primary group | Secondary group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Example | Family, play group, close neighbours | Hospital, university, trade union, political party |
| Size | Small | Large |
| Contact | Face to face, intimate | Indirect, formal |
| Relationship | Personal, for its own sake | Impersonal, for a purpose |
| Duration | Long lasting | May be temporary |
| Control | Informal (praise, blame) | Formal (rules, law) |
| Role in life | Shapes personality; "nursery of human nature" (Cooley) | Meets special needs |

### Importance of groups in nursing

- The family (primary group) is the first caregiver and decision maker.
- Patients in a ward form small groups; group health teaching and support groups (diabetes, cancer, de-addiction) use group strength.
- The nursing team is a formal group; good teamwork depends on clear roles and communication.
- Reference groups influence health behaviour – adolescents copy peers in smoking or dieting.

## Remember

- Sociology = scientific study of society and social relationships; **Auguste Comte** is the father of sociology and coined the term.
- Nature: independent, social, categorical, pure, abstract, generalizing, rational and empirical science.
- Scope: formal school (Simmel, Weber) vs synthetic school (Durkheim, Hobhouse).
- Society = web of social relationships; community = locality + we-feeling; association = organised for a purpose; institution = established procedure.
- Personal disorganization = an individual's failure to follow norms and fulfil roles.
- Primary group (Cooley): small, face to face, intimate – the family is the best example.
- In-group/out-group (Sumner); Gemeinschaft/Gesellschaft (Tönnies); reference group (Hyman, Merton).
