# Unit 8: Clinical sociology

This notes file covers INC Unit VII (Clinical sociology, 5 hours): introduction to clinical sociology, sociological strategies for developing services for the abused, and the use of clinical sociology in crisis intervention in the hospital and the community.

## 8.1 Introduction to clinical sociology

**Clinical sociology** is the **applied, intervention-oriented** branch of sociology. It uses sociological knowledge, theories and methods to **assess and change** situations of individuals, families, groups, organizations and communities, in order to solve problems and improve lives.

Ordinary (academic) sociology mainly **studies and explains** society; clinical sociology **acts** on it. The word "clinical" is used in its original sense of working directly with people to help them, not only in a hospital.

**Origin**: the term "clinical sociology" was used by **Louis Wirth** in 1931 in an article on the subject, and earlier ideas came from sociologists who worked on child guidance and social reform. The field grew after the 1970s, when sociologists began working as counsellors, mediators, planners and consultants.

### Features

1. **Applied and practical** – aims at change, not only knowledge.
2. **Holistic** – sees the problem in its social setting (family, workplace, community, culture).
3. **Multi-level** – works at micro, meso and macro levels.
4. **Client-centred** – the client may be a person, family, organization or community.
5. **Interdisciplinary** – works with medicine, nursing, psychology, social work and law.
6. **Evidence-based** – uses assessment, research methods and evaluation.
7. **Ethical** – respects confidentiality, consent and dignity.

### Levels of intervention

| Level | Client | Example in health care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro** | Individual, couple, family | Counselling a family caring for a patient with stroke |
| **Meso** | Groups and organizations | Improving teamwork and communication in a hospital ward; support groups |
| **Macro** | Community, society, policy | Community programme against domestic violence; advice on health policy |

### Roles of a clinical sociologist (and of the nurse using these skills)

- **Assessor** – studies the social causes of a problem.
- **Counsellor** – helps clients understand their social situation and make changes.
- **Mediator** – resolves conflicts between family members, staff or groups.
- **Facilitator and group leader** – runs support and self-help groups.
- **Change agent and planner** – designs and runs programmes.
- **Advocate** – speaks for the rights of the weak.
- **Evaluator** – checks whether an intervention worked.

### Process of clinical sociological intervention

```text
1. Identify the problem and the client (who is affected?)
2. Assess the social setting – family, roles, norms, culture,
   resources, support networks, power relations
3. Plan the intervention with the client (set goals together)
4. Intervene – counselling, group work, mediation,
   linking with services, changing rules or structures
5. Evaluate the results and follow up
```

### Uses in nursing

- understanding how family structure, culture and social class influence illness and recovery
- dealing with conflict among relatives, or between relatives and staff
- planning discharge and rehabilitation with the family and community
- supporting patients facing stigma (HIV, leprosy, mental illness, disability)
- organizing community programmes and support groups
- improving the hospital as a social system – roles, teamwork, communication

## 8.2 The abused – who they are

**Abuse** is any behaviour by a person in a position of trust or power that causes harm or distress to another. Types: physical, sexual, emotional (psychological), financial (economic) abuse and neglect.

Common groups who are abused:

| Group | Common forms of abuse |
| --- | --- |
| Children | Physical punishment, sexual abuse, neglect, child labour, trafficking |
| Women | Domestic violence, dowry harassment, sexual violence, workplace harassment |
| Elderly | Neglect, financial exploitation, emotional abuse, abandonment |
| Persons with disabilities and mental illness | Neglect, confinement, exploitation |
| Others | Migrant workers, transgender persons, persons in institutions |

**Social factors that increase abuse**: unequal power (gender, age), poverty and unemployment, alcohol and drug use, social isolation, overcrowding, cultural acceptance of violence, family history of violence, weak law enforcement and silence due to shame or fear.

## 8.3 Sociological strategies for developing services for the abused

Clinical sociology looks at abuse as a **social** problem, not only an individual one, and plans services at all levels.

### 1. Needs assessment and research

- Find out how common abuse is, who is affected and why (surveys, case records, community meetings).
- Map existing services and gaps.

### 2. Prevention at three levels

| Level | Aim | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Stop abuse before it starts | Awareness campaigns, parenting education, school programmes on safe and unsafe touch, gender equality education, laws against dowry and child marriage |
| Secondary | Identify abuse early | Screening of women in antenatal clinics and emergency departments, training health workers to recognise signs, helplines |
| Tertiary | Reduce harm and prevent repetition | Treatment, counselling, shelter, legal help, rehabilitation, support groups |

### 3. Building an integrated service network

- **One-stop services** that bring medical care, police help, legal aid, counselling and shelter under one roof (in India, One Stop Centres / Sakhi Centres under Mission Shakti for women; Child Welfare Committees and child protection units for children).
- **Helplines** – the Women Helpline 181 and child helplines.
- **Shelters and short-stay homes** for women and children who cannot go home safely.
- **Referral pathways** between hospitals, police, courts, NGOs and welfare departments.

### 4. Empowerment

- Education, vocational training and livelihood support so that the victim is not economically dependent on the abuser.
- Self-help groups and women's collectives.
- Teaching people about their rights and the laws that protect them.

### 5. Community mobilisation

- Involve panchayats, religious leaders, schools, youth clubs and NGOs.
- Change community norms that accept violence.
- Form vigilance groups against child marriage and trafficking.

### 6. Multidisciplinary teamwork

Doctors, nurses, counsellors, social workers, police, lawyers and teachers work as one team with clear roles.

### 7. Advocacy and policy

Press for better laws, enforcement, budgets and services; monitor implementation.

### 8. Training and sensitisation

Train health workers, police and teachers in recognising abuse, trauma-informed care, confidentiality and legal duties.

### 9. Rehabilitation and follow-up

Long-term counselling, reintegration with family where safe, alternative care (foster care, adoption) for children, support groups for survivors.

### Role of the nurse with abused clients

1. Give immediate care for injuries in a private, safe place.
2. Believe and listen; do not blame the victim.
3. Assess safety – is it safe to go home?
4. Document injuries and statements accurately (medico-legal record).
5. Preserve evidence as per protocol in sexual assault cases.
6. Report where the law requires (for example sexual offences against children under the POCSO Act).
7. Refer to counselling, One Stop Centres, child protection services, police and legal aid.
8. Follow up and support rehabilitation.
9. Maintain confidentiality.

## 8.4 Crisis and crisis intervention

### Crisis

A **crisis** is a state of **acute emotional upset** that occurs when a person faces a stressful event and his or her **usual coping methods fail**. Gerald **Caplan**, who developed crisis theory, described it as a short period of disequilibrium when a person is faced with an obstacle to important life goals that cannot be overcome by usual problem-solving methods. A crisis is **time-limited** – it usually resolves within about four to six weeks, for better or worse.

**Types of crisis**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Maturational (developmental)** | Expected transitions in the life cycle | Adolescence, marriage, birth of a child, retirement, old age |
| **Situational** | Unexpected events in a person's life | Sudden illness, accident, death of a family member, loss of job, divorce, diagnosis of cancer or HIV |
| **Adventitious (social)** | Unplanned disasters affecting many people | Floods, earthquakes, riots, terrorist attacks, epidemics such as COVID-19 |

**Phases of crisis (Caplan)**

```text
Phase 1  Rising tension; usual coping is tried
Phase 2  Coping fails; anxiety and disorganization increase
Phase 3  Emergency resources tried (new methods, help-seeking);
         the problem may be solved here
Phase 4  If not solved: severe anxiety, disorganization,
         possible panic, depression or self-harm
```

**Balancing factors (Aguilera)** that decide whether a stressful event becomes a crisis:

1. **Realistic perception** of the event
2. **Adequate situational support** (family, friends, community)
3. **Adequate coping mechanisms**

If all three are present the person regains balance; if one or more is missing, a crisis develops.

### Crisis intervention

**Crisis intervention** is **short-term, focused help** given to a person, family or group in crisis to **restore at least the pre-crisis level of functioning** and to prevent long-term harm.

**Principles**: immediate help; focus on the present problem; time-limited; active and directive approach; use of the person's own strengths and social supports; realistic goals; safety first.

**Steps of crisis intervention (Aguilera's model)**

1. **Assessment** – what happened, how the person sees it, available supports, coping methods, risk of suicide or harm.
2. **Planning** – decide goals and actions with the client.
3. **Intervention** – help the person understand the crisis, express feelings, explore coping methods, mobilise support, give practical help.
4. **Resolution** – reinforce new coping skills, review progress.
5. **Anticipatory planning** – prepare for future stress so that a similar event does not cause another crisis.

## 8.5 Use of clinical sociology in crisis intervention

Psychology looks mainly at the person's mind; clinical sociology adds the **social side** of the crisis.

| Sociological contribution | How it helps in crisis intervention |
| --- | --- |
| Seeing the person within family and community | Identifies who can give support and who may increase stress |
| Social support networks | Mobilises family, neighbours, religious groups, self-help groups, NGOs |
| Understanding roles and role change | Helps a family re-assign roles when the earner is ill or dies |
| Understanding culture | Respects mourning rituals, beliefs about illness and death; uses culturally acceptable help |
| Group methods | Group debriefing and support groups for disaster survivors or bereaved families |
| Linking with institutions | Connects people with welfare schemes, legal aid, shelter, employment |
| Community-level response | Organises relief, rebuilding and community mental health after disasters |
| Fighting stigma | Reduces social rejection after HIV diagnosis, suicide attempt or sexual assault |

### Examples

- **In the hospital**: a young man becomes paraplegic after a road accident. The nurse uses crisis intervention to support the patient and family, mediates their worries about income and care, links them with disability certificates and schemes, and plans rehabilitation and return to the community.
- **In the community**: after floods, health teams set up relief camps, reunite families, run group sessions for survivors, identify the elderly and disabled who need special help, and work with panchayats to restore normal life.
- **Abuse**: a woman comes to the emergency department after domestic violence – immediate care, safety assessment, documentation, referral to a One Stop Centre and police, and follow-up support.

### Role of the nurse in crisis intervention

- recognises people in crisis early (sudden diagnosis, bereavement, violence, disaster)
- ensures safety and assesses suicide risk
- listens and allows expression of feelings
- gives clear, honest information
- mobilises family and community support
- teaches simple coping skills
- refers to counsellors, psychiatrists, social workers and welfare services
- follows up and helps in anticipatory planning

## Remember

- Clinical sociology = applied sociology that assesses and changes social situations; term used by Louis Wirth (1931).
- Levels: micro (person, family), meso (groups, organizations), macro (community, policy).
- Services for the abused: needs assessment, primary–secondary–tertiary prevention, one-stop integrated services, helplines, shelters, empowerment, community mobilisation, multidisciplinary teams, advocacy, rehabilitation.
- Crisis (Caplan) = acute upset when usual coping fails; time-limited.
- Types: maturational, situational, adventitious.
- Aguilera's balancing factors: realistic perception, situational support, coping mechanisms.
- Crisis intervention steps: assessment, planning, intervention, resolution, anticipatory planning.
