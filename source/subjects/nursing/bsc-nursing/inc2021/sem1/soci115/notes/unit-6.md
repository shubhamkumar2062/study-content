## 6.1 Social organization

**Social organization** is the arrangement of the parts of society – individuals, groups, roles and institutions – into an orderly pattern, so that they work together and society runs smoothly. Ogburn and Nimkoff call it an articulation of different parts which perform various functions; it is an active group device for getting something done.

Put simply: **social organization exists when people and groups do their roles in harmony, following accepted norms, so that the needs of society are met.** It is the opposite of social disorganization.

### Elements of social organization

| Element | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Individuals and groups | The members who carry out roles |
| Roles and statuses | Positions and the behaviour expected in them |
| Norms and values | Rules and ideals that guide behaviour |
| Institutions | Established ways of meeting needs (family, education, health system) |
| Social control | Means that keep behaviour in line |
| Communication | Sharing of information and meaning |
| Common goals and consensus | Agreement on what is desirable |
| Division of labour and coordination | Each part has a task and the tasks are linked |

### Types of social organization

- **Formal organization** – deliberately created with written rules, a clear hierarchy, defined roles and impersonal relations; for example a hospital, a government health department, a nursing council. Max Weber's **bureaucracy** is the model: hierarchy of authority, written rules, specialisation, appointment by merit, records, impersonality.
- **Informal organization** – grows naturally from personal relations, friendships and customs within or outside formal bodies; for example a group of nurses who help each other cover duties, village elders, a neighbourhood group.

Other ways of classifying: by purpose (economic, political, religious, educational, health), by size (small, large), and by membership (voluntary or compulsory).

## 6.2 Voluntary associations

A **voluntary association** is a group that people **join by their own choice**, usually **without profit motive**, to serve a common interest or a social cause. It is managed by its members through elected office bearers.

**Characteristics**: voluntary membership; a definite aim; a constitution and rules; democratic management; mainly non-profit; often registered under law (as a society or trust); funded by members, donations and government grants.

**Functions**: filling gaps left by government services, reaching remote and poor people, raising awareness, advocacy for rights, research and innovation, disaster relief, training volunteers and health workers.

**Examples of voluntary and non-governmental bodies working in health and welfare in India**

| Organization | Field |
| --- | --- |
| Indian Red Cross Society | Disaster relief, blood banks, first aid, home nursing |
| Tuberculosis Association of India | TB awareness and care |
| Hind Kusht Nivaran Sangh | Leprosy relief |
| Family Planning Association of India | Family planning and reproductive health |
| Indian Council for Child Welfare | Child welfare |
| Kasturba Gandhi National Memorial Trust | Rural women and children |
| HelpAge India | Elderly care |
| Self Employed Women's Association (SEWA) | Working women, livelihood, health |
| Rotary and Lions clubs | Eye camps, polio and other health drives |
| Women's self-help groups | Savings, credit, health awareness in villages |
| Trained Nurses' Association of India | Professional association of nurses |

**Role of the nurse**: work with NGOs in camps and campaigns, refer patients to suitable organizations, join professional associations, and encourage community members to form self-help and support groups.

## 6.3 Social system

A **social system** is a set of **interacting individuals or groups** whose relationships are organised around shared norms, roles and goals, forming a **whole** in which every part affects the others. **Talcott Parsons** described it as a plurality of individual actors interacting with each other in a situation, guided by a shared system of culturally structured symbols.

**Characteristics**

- it has **parts** (people, roles, groups) that are **interrelated**
- it has a **goal** or purpose
- the parts are held together by **norms and values**
- it tends towards **equilibrium (balance)**; change in one part brings adjustment in the others
- it has **boundaries** but interacts with its environment
- it is dynamic and adapts over time

**Types**

- **Simple and complex** – a tribal community vs a modern city.
- **Open and closed** – class society (open) vs caste society (closed).
- By function, Parsons' four sub-systems: **economic** (adaptation), **political** (goal attainment), **integrative** (law, social control) and **pattern maintenance** (family, religion, education) – the "AGIL" scheme.

**Elements of a social system** (Loomis lists): beliefs, sentiments, goals, norms, **status and role**, rank, power, sanctions and facilities.

### Role and status as structural elements

Status and role are the **building blocks of any social system**: the system is a network of positions (statuses), and the behaviour attached to each position (role) is what keeps it running.

- **Status** – the position a person holds (patient, staff nurse, ward sister, doctor).
- **Role** – the expected behaviour attached to that position (the staff nurse gives care, records, reports).
- **Role set** – the set of roles linked to one status (a staff nurse relates to patients, doctors, relatives, students, supervisors).
- **Role conflict** – incompatible demands from two roles; **role strain** – difficulty meeting the demands of one role.
- **Sick role** (Talcott Parsons): the sick person is (1) excused from normal duties, (2) not held responsible for being ill, (3) expected to want to get well and (4) expected to seek competent help and cooperate with treatment.

          Hospital as a social system

          \            |          |            /
           \           +-- Patients --+       /

## 6.4 Interrelationship of institutions

Social institutions do not work alone; each depends on and influences the others.

| Institution | Linked with | Example of the link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family | Economy | Family earns; job loss affects nutrition and health |
| Family | Education | Parents' education decides children's schooling |
| Religion | Family and marriage | Rites of marriage, birth and death; food rules |
| Economy | Politics (government) | Government policies decide employment, prices, welfare |
| Education | Economy | Education provides skilled workers; jobs give value to education |
| Health system | All others | Health depends on income, education, family care, religion and policy |

So a change in one institution sets off changes in others: for example, women's education → later marriage → smaller families → better child health.

## 6.5 Social control

**Social control** is the way society **regulates the behaviour** of its members so that they conform to accepted norms and values, and order is maintained. **E. A. Ross** made the term popular. Ogburn and Nimkoff describe it as the patterns of pressure that a society exerts to maintain order and established rules.

### Aims of social control

1. To maintain **social order** and stability
2. To ensure **conformity** to norms and values
3. To protect society and the weak from harmful behaviour
4. To bring **unity and solidarity**
5. To pass on culture and continuity
6. To guide social change in a desired direction

### Process and types

| Type | Agencies or means | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Informal** (unwritten) | Family, peer group, neighbourhood, religion, folkways, mores, customs, public opinion, praise, ridicule, gossip | A family disapproves of smoking |
| **Formal** (written) | Law, police, courts, government, rules of institutions, education system | Fine for spitting in public; hospital code of conduct |
| Positive | Rewards – praise, promotion, awards | Best nurse award |
| Negative | Punishments – blame, fines, suspension, jail | Disciplinary action for negligence |

The **process** works through socialization (learning norms so that people control themselves), surveillance and feedback, sanctions (rewards and punishments) and, when needed, formal enforcement.

## 6.6 Social norms, morals and values

- **Norms** – shared rules of expected behaviour in a group. They include folkways, mores, customs and laws.
  - **Folkways** – everyday habits; breaking them causes mild disapproval (table manners).
  - **Mores** – strongly held moral norms; breaking them causes severe disapproval (honesty, respect for parents).
  - **Customs** – long-established practices handed down.
  - **Laws** – formal norms made and enforced by the state.
  - **Taboo** – a strong prohibition (incest).
- **Morals** – principles of right and wrong conduct held by individuals and society; in nursing they show in the code of ethics (truthfulness, confidentiality, beneficence).
- **Values** – collective ideas of what is good, desirable and worth striving for (health, equality, compassion, family welfare). Values are the **basis** of norms: the value "respect for life" leads to the norm "do not harm patients".

## 6.7 Social disorganization

**Social disorganization** is a condition in which the **parts of a social system stop working in harmony**, rules lose their hold, and **social relationships break down**, so that the society cannot meet the needs of its members. Elliott and Merrill describe it as the process by which relationships among members of a group are broken or dissolved. Thomas and Znaniecki described it as a decrease in the influence of existing social rules of behaviour upon individual members of the group.

### Symptoms (signs)

- conflict between old and new values (generation gap)
- breakdown of family – divorce, desertion, neglect of elders
- rising crime, delinquency, addiction, suicide
- corruption, violence, communal and caste riots
- unemployment and poverty
- loss of trust in institutions; mob behaviour
- **anomie** – a state of normlessness (Émile Durkheim)

### Causes

| Cause | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Rapid social change | Old rules break before new ones are accepted (**cultural lag**) |
| Industrialization and urbanization | Migration, slums, impersonal life, loss of family control |
| Conflict of values | Traditional vs modern values; different groups pull in different directions |
| Economic causes | Poverty, unemployment, inflation, inequality |
| Natural calamities and epidemics | Floods, earthquakes, pandemics disrupt life (as COVID-19 did) |
| War and political instability | Displacement, violence, refugees |
| Weak social control | Family, religion and community lose influence |
| Population growth | Pressure on housing, jobs, health services |
| Individual causes | Mental illness, addiction, personal disorganization |

### Personal, family and community disorganization

- **Personal** – individual fails to follow norms (addiction, crime, suicide) – see notes file 1.
- **Family** – breakdown of family roles: divorce, desertion, domestic violence, neglect of children and elders.
- **Community** – breakdown of community life: riots, slums, crime, corruption, loss of cooperation.

### Control and planning of social disorganization

1. **Strengthening the family** – family counselling, family courts, support to single parents and elders.
2. **Education** – values education, literacy, skill training.
3. **Economic measures** – employment schemes, poverty reduction, social security.
4. **Housing and urban planning** – slum improvement, affordable housing, basic amenities.
5. **Social legislation** – laws against dowry, child marriage, child labour, trafficking, domestic violence; enforcement.
6. **Health services** – mental health and de-addiction services, HIV prevention, maternal and child care.
7. **Recreation and youth programmes** – sports, clubs, constructive use of leisure.
8. **Role of voluntary agencies and religion** – moral guidance and community support.
9. **Social welfare planning** – coordinated planning by government and communities, research and evaluation.

### Role of the nurse

- identifies families and individuals at risk during home visits
- gives counselling and health education on addiction, violence, mental health
- refers to social workers, legal aid, de-addiction and mental health services
- helps in rehabilitation and community support
- takes part in planning and implementing welfare programmes

## Remember

- Social organization = parts of society working in harmony; formal (bureaucracy – Weber) and informal.
- Voluntary association = joined by choice, non-profit, for a cause – Indian Red Cross Society, HelpAge India, SEWA.
- Social system (Parsons) = interacting actors with shared norms; tends to equilibrium; AGIL sub-systems.
- Status = position; role = expected behaviour; Parsons' sick role has four parts.
- Social control (E. A. Ross) – formal and informal; aims: order, conformity, unity.
- Folkways (mild) < mores (strong) < laws (formal); values are the basis of norms.
- Social disorganization = breakdown of harmony; anomie (Durkheim); causes: rapid change, cultural lag, urbanization, poverty, disasters.
