# Unit 5: Social stratification – caste, class and race

This notes file covers INC Unit V (Social stratification, 8 hours): characteristics, forms and functions of stratification; the Indian caste system; class and status; social mobility; race; and the influence of class, caste and race on health.

## 5.1 Meaning of social stratification

**Social stratification** is the division of society into **layers (strata) arranged one above the other** according to wealth, power, prestige, birth or occupation, so that people in different layers have **unequal** access to resources and rewards.

The word comes from geology, where rocks lie in layers (strata). Ogburn and Nimkoff describe it as the process by which individuals and groups are ranked in a more or less lasting hierarchy of status. Sorokin describes it as the differentiation of a population into hierarchically superposed classes.

## 5.2 Characteristics of stratification

1. **It is social** – based on social rules, not only on natural differences of age or strength.
2. **It is universal** – found in all societies, simple or complex.
3. **It is ancient** – present since early societies.
4. **It takes diverse forms** – slavery, estate, caste, class.
5. **It is consequential** – it decides life chances (health, education, life expectancy) and lifestyle.
6. **It involves inequality** – of wealth, power and prestige.
7. **It persists over generations** – children usually inherit their parents' position.
8. **It is supported by beliefs** – ideas that make the inequality look natural or just.

## 5.3 Forms of stratification

| Form | Basis | Mobility | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Slavery** | Ownership of one person by another | Almost none | Ancient Greece and Rome; slave trade |
| **Estate** | Land ownership and law; rights fixed by law | Very limited | Feudal Europe – clergy, nobility, commoners |
| **Caste** | Birth; hereditary and closed | Very limited (closed system) | Indian caste system |
| **Class** | Wealth, income, occupation, education | Possible (open system) | Upper, middle and lower class in modern societies |

## 5.4 Functions and theories of stratification

**Functionalist view (Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore)**: some positions are more important and need rare skills and long training (a surgeon, a nurse specialist). Higher rewards attract the most able people to fill them, so stratification motivates people and keeps society working.

**Conflict view (Karl Marx)**: society is divided into those who own the means of production (bourgeoisie) and those who sell their labour (proletariat). Stratification benefits the powerful and causes exploitation and conflict.

**Max Weber**: stratification has three dimensions – **class** (economic position), **status** (social honour or prestige) and **party** (power).

**Functions (positive)**

- motivates people to work hard and acquire skills
- places the right people in important positions
- gives order and clear roles
- provides identity and a sense of belonging to a group

**Dysfunctions (negative)**

- inequality in income, education and health
- exploitation and discrimination
- limits talent among the poor
- causes tension, conflict and social unrest

## 5.5 Indian caste system

**Caste** is a **hereditary, endogamous** social group with a traditional occupation and a fixed rank in a hierarchy, membership of which is decided by **birth**. The word caste comes from the Portuguese *casta* (breed, race). In India, the ideas of **varna** (four broad orders: Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra) and **jati** (thousands of local endogamous groups) are both used.

### Origin – main theories

| Theory | Main idea | Associated with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Traditional (divine) | Varnas came from different parts of the Creator (Purusha Sukta of the Rigveda) | Religious texts |
| Racial | Arrival of people of different racial types; colour (varna) became a basis of rank | H. H. Risley |
| Political | Devised by priests to keep their supremacy | Abbé Dubois |
| Occupational | Occupations of different prestige became hereditary castes | Nesfield |
| Evolutionary / multiple factor | Many causes over a long time – race, occupation, religion, geography, rules of purity | J. H. Hutton |

No single theory explains caste fully; most sociologists accept that many factors worked together.

### Characteristics (G. S. Ghurye's six features)

1. **Segmental division of society** – each caste is a distinct group with its own rules and council (caste panchayat).
2. **Hierarchy** – castes are ranked from high to low.
3. **Restrictions on feeding and social intercourse** – rules about who may eat or drink with whom (ideas of purity and pollution).
4. **Civil and religious disabilities and privileges** – some castes were denied entry to temples, wells and schools; others had privileges.
5. **Lack of unrestricted choice of occupation** – occupation was hereditary.
6. **Restrictions on marriage** – **endogamy** (marriage within the caste), with exogamy for gotra.

Other features: membership by birth; caste names; strong sense of belonging.

### Positive and negative impact of caste

| Positive (traditional functions) | Negative |
| --- | --- |
| Gave identity and security to members | Inequality and exploitation of lower castes |
| Caste council helped in disputes and needy times | **Untouchability** – now abolished by the Constitution (Article 17) |
| Passed on skills of hereditary occupations | Blocked social mobility and talent |
| Preserved culture, customs and purity of traditions | Hindered national unity; casteism in politics |
| Gave a sense of social order | Caste violence and honour crimes; opposes inter-caste marriage |
| | Barriers in health care – refusal of touch, segregated wells, unequal access |

### Changes in the caste system

Factors bringing change: the Constitution (equality, abolition of untouchability, reservations), education, urbanization and industrialization, new occupations, transport and eating in public places, democracy and voting, reform movements (Ambedkar, Gandhi, Jyotiba Phule, Narayana Guru), inter-caste marriage, Sanskritization and Westernization (M. N. Srinivas).

Even now, caste survives strongly in marriage choices and politics, and **dominant caste** (Srinivas) still controls many villages.

## 5.6 Class system and status

A **social class** is a group of people with a similar position in terms of **wealth, income, occupation and education**, who share a similar lifestyle. Class is an **open** system; a person can move up or down.

Common divisions: **upper class, middle class (upper-middle, lower-middle) and lower (working) class.** In public health, socio-economic scales such as the **Kuppuswamy scale** (education, occupation and family income) and the **B. G. Prasad scale** (per capita income) are used to grade families; their income cut-offs are revised regularly, so learn the components rather than the figures.

### Caste vs class

| Point | Caste | Class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basis | Birth | Wealth, occupation, education |
| Nature | Closed | Open |
| Mobility | Very little | Possible |
| Marriage | Endogamous | No fixed rule |
| Occupation | Traditionally hereditary | Free choice |
| Religious sanction | Yes | No |
| Class consciousness | Caste feeling strong | Class consciousness weaker in India |

### Status and role

- **Status** is a position in a group or society (nurse, mother, patient). **Role** is the expected behaviour attached to that status (Ralph Linton: role is the dynamic aspect of status).
- **Ascribed status** – given by birth (caste, sex, age, race).
- **Achieved status** – earned by effort (registered nurse, doctor, officer).
- **Role conflict** – two roles make opposite demands (a nurse who is also a mother with a sick child at home).
- **Status symbols** – uniform, cap, title, car, house.

## 5.7 Social mobility

**Social mobility** is the movement of a person or group from one social position to another. The concept was developed by **P. A. Sorokin**.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Horizontal** | Change of position at the same level | A staff nurse moves from one hospital to another as a staff nurse |
| **Vertical – upward** | Rise to a higher position | A staff nurse becomes nursing superintendent |
| **Vertical – downward** | Fall to a lower position | A business owner becomes a labourer after losses |
| **Intergenerational** | Change between parents and children | A labourer's daughter becomes a doctor |
| **Intragenerational** | Change within one person's lifetime | A ward assistant studies and becomes a nurse |
| Individual / group | One person or a whole group moves | A caste rises through Sanskritization |

Factors helping mobility: education, new occupations, industrialization, urbanization, migration, democracy, reservation policies, marriage, personal ability. Factors limiting it: caste, poverty, poor health, illiteracy, gender discrimination, rural isolation.

## 5.8 Race

**Race** is a large group of people who share certain **inherited physical characteristics**. Older anthropology classified people into races; modern genetics shows that human genetic variation is continuous and that biological "races" are not sharply separate. Race is now studied mainly as a **social category** that has been used to rank and discriminate.

### Criteria used in racial classification

- **Skin colour** (most visible, but least reliable)
- **Hair** – form (straight, wavy, woolly), colour
- **Head form** – cephalic index (long, medium or broad head)
- **Nose form** – nasal index
- **Face and eye form** (e.g. the epicanthic fold)
- **Stature** (height)
- **Blood groups** and other genetic markers

### Classifications

- The broad older grouping into **Caucasoid, Mongoloid and Negroid** types.
- For India, **H. H. Risley** described seven racial types, and **B. S. Guha** described six main racial elements: Negrito, Proto-Australoid, Mongoloid, Mediterranean, Western Brachycephals and Nordic.

### Racism

**Racism** is the belief that one race is superior to another, leading to prejudice and discrimination (apartheid in South Africa, slavery). Nursing ethics require equal care for all, whatever their race, colour, caste or creed.

## 5.9 Influence of class, caste and race on health

**Class**

- Poor families have more malnutrition, infections, TB, maternal and infant deaths, and shorter lives.
- They live in overcrowded, unsanitary housing and do risky jobs.
- They delay seeking care because of cost and loss of wages; they depend on public facilities.
- Richer groups have more lifestyle diseases (obesity, diabetes, heart disease) but better access to care.

**Caste**

- Historically, lower castes were denied clean water, schooling and sometimes health care.
- Occupation-linked hazards – manual scavenging, leather work and sanitation work expose workers to infections and injuries.
- Purity–pollution beliefs may affect who touches whom, who donates or receives blood, and diet.
- Endogamy and close-kin marriage within small groups may increase inherited disorders.
- Government schemes and reservations aim to reduce these gaps.

**Race and ethnicity**

- Some inherited disorders are more common in certain populations – sickle cell disease in some tribal groups of central and western India; thalassaemia in some communities.
- Discrimination and language barriers reduce access to care.
- Skin colour affects how some signs look (cyanosis and jaundice are harder to see in dark skin; check lips, nail beds, conjunctiva and palms).

**Role of the nurse**: give equal, non-judgemental care; identify the poorest and marginal families during community surveys; link them to free schemes; screen high-risk groups (sickle cell, thalassaemia); and fight discrimination in the health system.

## Remember

- Stratification = ranking of people in layers with unequal rewards; universal and ancient.
- Forms: slavery, estate, caste (closed), class (open).
- Davis–Moore (functional), Marx (conflict), Weber (class, status, party).
- Ghurye's six features of caste: segmental division, hierarchy, feeding restrictions, disabilities and privileges, hereditary occupation, endogamy.
- Untouchability abolished by Article 17.
- Ascribed vs achieved status (Linton); social mobility – horizontal, vertical, intergenerational (Sorokin).
- Race criteria: skin, hair, head (cephalic index), nose (nasal index), stature, blood groups; Guha's six elements.
- Low class and caste position → more disease and less access to care.
