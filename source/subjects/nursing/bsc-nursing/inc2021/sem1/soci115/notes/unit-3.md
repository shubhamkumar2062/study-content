# Unit 3: Culture

This notes file covers INC Unit III (Culture, 8 hours): nature, characteristics and evolution of culture, diversity and uniformity, culture and civilization, culture and socialization, transcultural society, and culture, modernization and their impact on health and disease.

## 3.1 Meaning of culture

In daily speech "cultured" means refined or educated. In sociology **culture** means the **whole way of life** of a people – everything they learn and share as members of a society.

**E. B. Tylor** gave the classic definition: culture is that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society.

Other views: Malinowski called culture the handiwork of man and the means by which he achieves his ends; Herskovits called it the man-made part of the environment.

Simple exam definition: **culture is the learned, shared and transmitted way of life of a group – its ideas, values, norms, language, customs and material objects.**

## 3.2 Components of culture

```text
                        Culture
                           |
         +-----------------+------------------+
         |                                    |
 Material culture                     Non-material culture
 (things made by people)              (ideas and behaviour)
 - houses, clothes, food              - knowledge and beliefs
 - tools, vehicles, machines          - values and morals
 - medicines, hospitals,              - norms: folkways, mores,
   instruments                          customs, laws
                                      - language and symbols
                                      - art, music, religion
```

Elements of non-material culture:

| Element | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Beliefs | Ideas people hold to be true | Illness caused by the "evil eye" |
| Values | Ideas of what is good and desirable | Respect for elders, hospitality |
| Norms | Rules of expected behaviour | Removing shoes before entering a temple |
| Folkways | Everyday customs; mild disapproval if broken | Greeting with "Namaste" |
| Mores | Strong moral norms; serious disapproval if broken | Not stealing, caring for parents |
| Laws | Formal written rules enforced by the state | Ban on dowry |
| Language and symbols | Means of sharing meaning | Red cross as a sign of medical help |

## 3.3 Nature and characteristics of culture

1. **Culture is learned** – not inherited biologically; a child learns it through socialization.
2. **Culture is shared** – it belongs to a group, not to one person.
3. **Culture is transmitted** – passed from generation to generation, mainly through language.
4. **Culture is social** – it exists only in society.
5. **Culture is ideational** – it is a system of ideas and patterns.
6. **Culture gratifies needs** – food habits, marriage and religion meet biological, social and emotional needs.
7. **Culture is adaptive** – it adjusts to the environment (dress in the hills vs the plains).
8. **Culture is dynamic** – it changes slowly over time.
9. **Culture is integrated** – its parts fit together; change in one part affects others.
10. **Culture varies** from society to society.
11. **Culture is superorganic** – it continues even when individuals die.

## 3.4 Evolution of culture

Culture has grown over a very long period. In broad outline:

```text
Hunting and food gathering → domestication of animals → settled agriculture
→ villages and crafts → towns, trade and writing → industry and science
→ information and digital society
```

Ways culture grows and spreads:

- **Invention** – creating something new (vaccines, the wheel).
- **Discovery** – finding something that already existed (a medicinal plant, a germ).
- **Diffusion** – spread of traits from one society to another (tea, cricket, allopathic medicine).
- **Acculturation** – one culture borrowing many traits from another through long contact.
- **Accumulation** – new traits are added to old ones, so culture keeps growing.

Early writers (Morgan, Tylor) described culture moving from "savagery" to "barbarism" to "civilization". These labels are now rejected because they judge other cultures as inferior; modern sociology treats every culture as meaningful in its own setting (**cultural relativism**).

## 3.5 Diversity and uniformity of culture

### Cultural diversity

Different groups have different ways of life. India is a classic example: many languages, religions, castes, tribes, food habits, dress and festivals.

Causes of diversity: geography and climate, history, religion, migration and invasions, isolation, economy.

### Cultural uniformity

Behind the diversity, all cultures share certain **cultural universals**, because all humans have similar needs: family, marriage rules, language, religion or belief, rules about property, food sharing, care of the sick, rites at birth and death, art and music.

In India, uniformity is also seen in shared values (respect for elders, hospitality), common festivals across regions, a common Constitution and national symbols – **unity in diversity**.

### Related terms

- **Ethnocentrism** – judging other cultures by the standards of one's own and seeing one's own as superior. In nursing it leads to disrespect and poor care.
- **Cultural relativism** – judging a practice in the context of its own culture.
- **Culture shock** – confusion and anxiety on entering an unfamiliar culture (a rural patient in a city hospital; a nurse posted to a tribal area).
- **Subculture** – a smaller group with its own distinct ways within a larger culture (adolescents, a tribe, the nursing profession).
- **Counterculture** – a group that rejects the main values of society.

## 3.6 Culture and civilization

**Civilization** is the advanced stage of material and technical development of a society – its technology, cities, science, political organisation. MacIver: civilization is what we *use* (means), culture is what we *are* (ends).

| Point | Culture | Civilization |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Way of life, values, ideas | Material and technical achievement |
| Nature | Internal, an end in itself | External, a means to an end |
| Measurement | Hard to measure | Can be measured (efficiency, speed) |
| Transmission | Not easily passed on; must be learned | Easily borrowed by other societies |
| Progress | Does not always advance | Generally advances steadily |
| Example | Religion, art, values | Machines, hospitals, transport |

## 3.7 Culture and socialization

Socialization is the **channel** through which culture is passed on. The family, school, religion, peers and media teach the child the language, values and norms of the culture. In turn, culture decides **what** is taught: in one culture children learn to obey elders without question, in another to speak their mind. So culture is the content and socialization the process.

## 3.8 Transcultural society

A **transcultural (multicultural) society** is one in which people of many cultures live and work together – common in Indian cities and hospitals, and growing with migration within India and abroad.

**Transcultural nursing**, founded by **Madeleine Leininger** (Culture Care theory, "Sunrise model"), means care that is **sensitive to and congruent with** the patient's culture. Leininger described three modes of nursing action:

1. **Culture care preservation or maintenance** – keep practices that are helpful or harmless (prayer, family presence).
2. **Culture care accommodation or negotiation** – adjust care to fit a practice (allowing a vegetarian or religious diet, female staff for a woman patient).
3. **Culture care repatterning or restructuring** – gently change harmful practices (prelacteal feeds, applying cow dung to the cord).

**Cultural competence** of a nurse includes: awareness of one's own biases, knowledge of other cultures, skill in culturally sensitive assessment (asking about beliefs, diet, healers), using interpreters, and respecting privacy, modesty and religious needs.

## 3.9 Culture, modernization and their impact on health and disease

### How culture affects health

| Area | Positive examples | Harmful examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Food habits | Vegetarian diets, fermented foods, breastfeeding | Discarding colostrum, food taboos in pregnancy, excess oil and sugar |
| Personal hygiene | Hand washing before meals, daily bathing | Open defecation, unsafe disposal of waste |
| Childbirth and child care | Rest after delivery, family support | Unhygienic cord care, prelacteal feeds, home delivery by untrained persons |
| Beliefs about illness | Prayer and family support give comfort | Belief in evil eye or spirits delays medical care |
| Health-seeking | Use of yoga and some safe traditional remedies | Going first to quacks or faith healers |
| Social customs | Fasting can promote discipline | Child marriage, dowry, son preference, tobacco and alcohol at social events |
| Stigma | – | Stigma of leprosy, TB, HIV, mental illness, infertility, disability |

### Modernization

**Modernization** is the process by which a traditional society takes on scientific knowledge, technology, rational thinking, industrial economy, democracy and secular values.

Features: use of science and technology, urbanization, industrialization, spread of education and media, mobility, nuclear families, rational and secular outlook.

**Positive impact on health**

- control of communicable diseases through vaccines, antibiotics, safe water and sanitation
- fall in maternal and infant deaths with institutional delivery
- longer life expectancy
- better communication and health education; telemedicine
- women's education and employment improve family health

**Negative impact on health**

- **lifestyle (non-communicable) diseases** – diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, cancers, obesity from sedentary work and fast food
- stress, anxiety, depression and suicide
- substance abuse and new addictions (internet, gaming)
- road traffic and industrial accidents, occupational diseases
- pollution of air, water and soil
- breakdown of joint families – loneliness of the elderly, less family support in illness
- migration leading to slums, overcrowding, TB and HIV

### Role of the nurse

- Do a cultural assessment as part of every history.
- Respect beliefs; do not ridicule traditional practices.
- Encourage harmless practices, negotiate where possible, repattern harmful ones with patience and evidence.
- Use local language, examples and trusted community members in teaching.
- Promote healthy modern practices (immunization, institutional delivery) while warning about modern hazards (junk food, tobacco, sedentary life).

## Remember

- Tylor: culture is the complex whole of knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom and habits acquired as a member of society.
- Culture is learned, shared, transmitted, adaptive, dynamic and integrated.
- Material vs non-material culture; folkways, mores and laws.
- Culture = what we are (ends); civilization = what we use (means) – MacIver.
- Ethnocentrism vs cultural relativism; culture shock.
- Leininger: preservation, accommodation, repatterning.
- Modernization reduces infections but raises lifestyle disease, stress, pollution and loneliness.
