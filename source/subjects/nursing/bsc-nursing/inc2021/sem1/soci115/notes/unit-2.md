## 2.1 Social processes

**Social interaction** is the mutual action and reaction between people or groups. When interaction takes a repeated, recognisable form, it is called a **social process**. Ginsberg describes social processes as the various modes of interaction between individuals or groups, including cooperation, conflict, differentiation and integration.

Social processes are of two broad kinds:

```text
                   Social processes
                         |
        +----------------+----------------+
        |                                 |
  Associative (conjunctive)        Dissociative (disjunctive)
  bring people together            push people apart
  - cooperation                    - competition
  - accommodation                  - conflict
  - assimilation                   - (isolation, the absence
                                      of interaction)
```

### Cooperation

**Cooperation** is working together for a common goal. It is the most basic process; family, society and every health team depend on it.

- **Direct cooperation** – people do the same work together (villagers building a well).
- **Indirect cooperation** – people do different but related work for a common end (division of labour in a hospital: doctor, nurse, pharmacist, laboratory technician).
- **Primary** (in primary groups), **secondary** (in organisations) and **tertiary** (temporary cooperation between groups with different aims, such as two political parties joining for an election) – classification by Ogburn and Nimkoff.

Nursing example: the nurse, patient and family cooperate in a home care plan.

### Competition

**Competition** is a struggle between people for things that are limited (jobs, seats, prizes, status), usually **impersonal, continuous, unconscious** of the rival and **governed by rules**.

- Effects: it can raise efficiency and progress, but too much competition causes anxiety, frustration and conflict.
- Example: competition for admission to nursing colleges.

### Conflict

**Conflict** is a struggle in which people or groups try to achieve their ends by **opposing or harming the rival**, directly and consciously.

- Characteristics: personal and conscious, intermittent (not continuous), may involve threat or violence.
- Types (Gillin and Gillin): personal, racial, class, political and international conflict.
- Effects: harmful when violent, but controlled conflict can lead to change and greater unity within each group.
- Example: a quarrel between the patient's relatives and hospital staff; land disputes in a village.

### Accommodation

**Accommodation** is the process of **adjusting to a conflict** so that people with differences can live and work together, without the differences disappearing.

Methods: coercion (force), compromise, arbitration and conciliation, toleration, conversion, sublimation (redirecting energy into acceptable work), rationalization.

Example: a new nurse adjusts to the routines of a ward; two quarrelling departments agree to a shared roster.

### Assimilation

**Assimilation** is a slow, **unconscious** process by which individuals or groups with different cultures **merge** and take on a common culture, so that the original differences fade.

- Factors that help: tolerance, long contact, intermarriage, common economic life, education, cultural similarity.
- Factors that hinder: isolation, prejudice, very different cultures, physical differences, feelings of superiority.
- Example: migrant families in a city slowly adopt city language, dress and food.

**Accommodation vs assimilation**: accommodation is quicker and conscious, and differences remain; assimilation is slow and unconscious, and differences disappear.

### Isolation

**Isolation** is the absence of social contact or communication. It may be physical (geographical remoteness, hospital isolation ward), social (untouchability, stigma of leprosy, HIV or mental illness) or psychological (loneliness of the elderly). Prolonged isolation harms personality and mental health. The nurse reduces isolation by communication, involving family and support groups, and fighting stigma.

## 2.2 Socialization

**Socialization** is the lifelong process by which an individual **learns the culture, norms, values and roles** of the society and becomes a functioning member of it. It turns a biological infant into a social being.

### Characteristics

1. It is **lifelong** – from birth to death (childhood, school, marriage, work, old age).
2. It **transmits culture** from one generation to the next.
3. It is learned through **interaction**.
4. It **varies** between societies and over time.
5. It creates **self and personality**.
6. It can be **conscious** (teaching) or **unconscious** (imitation).

### Process (stages)

Two widely taught explanations:

**Charles Horton Cooley – the "looking-glass self"**: a person develops a self-image by imagining (1) how he appears to others, (2) how others judge that appearance, and (3) feeling pride or shame as a result.

**George Herbert Mead – development of self**:

```text
I (spontaneous self) ⇌ Me (social self, the attitudes of others taken in)

Stage 1 Imitation (preparatory) – the child copies actions without understanding
Stage 2 Play stage – the child plays one role at a time ("mother", "doctor")
Stage 3 Game stage – the child understands many roles at once and the rules
        (the "generalized other": the expectations of society as a whole)
```

**Sigmund Freud** – personality grows through the id, ego and superego; the superego (conscience) is formed by taking in the parents' and society's rules.

Stages across life (common textbook form): primary socialization in infancy and childhood, secondary socialization in school and peer groups, adult socialization (marriage, work, parenthood), **anticipatory socialization** (learning a role before taking it, e.g. a student nurse learning professional behaviour) and **resocialization** (learning new ways after a major change – retirement, disability, prison, migration).

### Agencies of socialization

| Agency | What it teaches |
| --- | --- |
| **Family** – the first and most important agency | Language, habits, hygiene, values, gender roles, love and trust |
| Peer group (play group) | Cooperation, competition, equality, social skills |
| School and teachers | Knowledge, discipline, citizenship, punctuality |
| Religion | Morals, rituals, beliefs about life, illness and death |
| Neighbourhood and community | Customs, festivals, local norms |
| Mass media and the internet | Ideas, fashion, attitudes – both good and harmful (advertising of junk food, tobacco) |
| Occupation and workplace | Professional roles, ethics (nursing code of conduct) |
| State and law | Rights, duties, discipline |

### Nurse and socialization

- Professional socialization turns a student into a nurse – learning skills, ethics, uniform, accountability.
- A patient admitted to hospital goes through **resocialization** – learning the "sick role", ward routines, a new diet or insulin.
- The nurse teaches families healthy habits and so becomes an agent of socialization for health.

## 2.3 Social change

**Social change** is a **change in the social structure and social relationships** of a society over time – in its institutions, roles, values and way of life. (MacIver and Page: changes in social relationships.)

### Nature (characteristics)

- It is **universal** – every society changes.
- It is **continuous** but its **speed varies** between societies and periods.
- It is **community change**, not change in one person.
- It is **unpredictable** in detail.
- It can be **planned** (family planning, literacy campaigns) or **unplanned** (natural disaster, epidemic).
- It may be **progress** or **regress**; change itself is value-neutral.

### Factors (causes) of social change

| Factor | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Demographic | Population growth, falling death rate, ageing, migration |
| Technological | Vaccines, mobile phones, telemedicine, mechanised farming |
| Economic | Industrialization, new jobs, income changes |
| Cultural | New ideas, education, values |
| Political and legal | New laws on marriage, dowry, child labour |
| Biological and natural | Epidemics such as COVID-19, floods, droughts |
| Education and communication | Literacy, media, internet |

### Process of social change

Change usually spreads through **invention** (a new idea or tool), **discovery**, **diffusion** (spread from one group to another) and **acculturation** (one culture taking traits from another). Common processes in India are **Sanskritization** (a lower caste adopting customs of higher castes to raise its status – M. N. Srinivas), **Westernization**, **modernization**, **urbanization** and **industrialization**.

**Cultural lag** (W. F. Ogburn): material culture (technology) changes faster than non-material culture (beliefs, customs), creating a gap – for example, hospital delivery is available but some families still prefer untrained home delivery.

### Theories in brief

- **Linear (evolutionary)** – society moves forward in stages (Comte, Spencer).
- **Cyclical** – societies rise and fall in cycles (Spengler, Toynbee, Sorokin).
- **Conflict** – change comes from class struggle (Karl Marx).
- **Functional** – change is a slow adjustment that keeps balance (Talcott Parsons).

### Role of the nurse in social change

1. **Change agent** – introduces healthy practices (hand washing, breastfeeding, immunization).
2. **Health educator** – removes harmful beliefs gently while respecting culture.
3. **Researcher** – finds out which practices help or harm.
4. **Adapting to change** – learns new technology, digital records, new disease patterns.
5. **Advocate** – supports laws and programmes for women, children and the elderly.
6. **Counsellor** – helps families cope with change (migration, nuclear family stress, ageing parents living alone).
7. **Leader** – plans change step by step: understand the community, involve leaders, start small, evaluate.

## 2.4 Urban, rural and tribal communities

### Rural community

Characteristics: small population living in villages; agriculture and allied work as the main occupation; close relation with nature; primary relationships (everyone knows everyone); joint families more common; strong caste, religion and tradition; panchayat and elders exercise control; simple living; slower social change; lower literacy and fewer services.

### Urban community

Characteristics: large, dense, mixed population; non-agricultural occupations (industry, trade, services); division of labour and specialisation; secondary, impersonal relationships; nuclear families more common; social mobility and individualism; formal control (police, law); faster change; better access to schools and hospitals, but also slums and pollution.

### Rural vs urban

| Point | Rural | Urban |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size and density | Small, scattered | Large, dense |
| Occupation | Agriculture | Industry, trade, services |
| Relationships | Primary, personal | Secondary, impersonal |
| Family | Joint more common | Nuclear more common |
| Control | Informal – customs, elders | Formal – law, police |
| Mobility | Low | High |
| Change | Slow | Fast |
| Services | Fewer | More, but unequal |

### Tribal community

Tribes (Scheduled Tribes in the Constitution) are communities that usually live in forests, hills or remote areas. Characteristics: a common territory and a common name; their own dialect; strong kinship and clan ties; endogamy within the tribe; simple economy (hunting, gathering, shifting cultivation, forest produce); own customs, beliefs and traditional healers; a tribal council or chief for control; relative isolation from the mainstream.

### Major health problems

| Community | Major health problems |
| --- | --- |
| Rural | Communicable diseases (diarrhoea, typhoid, tuberculosis), malnutrition and anaemia, maternal and child health problems, unsafe water and open defecation, snake bite and farm injuries, pesticide poisoning, delayed care because of distance, reliance on untrained practitioners |
| Urban | Lifestyle diseases (diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, cancer), obesity, air and noise pollution, road traffic accidents, stress and mental illness, substance abuse, slum problems (overcrowding, tuberculosis, vector-borne disease), HIV and STIs, occupational diseases |
| Tribal | Malaria and other vector-borne disease, malnutrition, anaemia, genetic disorders such as sickle cell disease in some groups, high maternal and infant illness, skin and worm infections, alcohol use, poor access to services, mistrust of outsiders |

## 2.5 Importance of social structure in nursing

**Social structure** is the relatively stable pattern of social relationships, roles, statuses, groups and institutions in a society. Knowing it helps the nurse to:

- plan care that fits the family, caste, religion and community of the patient
- identify leaders and decision makers for community programmes
- understand why some groups use services less (distance, cost, caste barriers, gender norms)
- find vulnerable groups and target help to them
- work within the hospital's own structure of roles and authority
- use local institutions – panchayats, self-help groups, schools, ASHAs and anganwadis – to spread health messages

## Remember

- Associative processes: cooperation, accommodation, assimilation. Dissociative: competition, conflict.
- Competition = impersonal, continuous; conflict = personal, conscious, intermittent.
- Accommodation adjusts to conflict (differences stay); assimilation merges cultures (differences fade).
- Socialization = lifelong learning of culture; family is the first and most important agency.
- Cooley – looking-glass self; Mead – I and me, play and game stages, generalized other.
- Social change is universal and continuous; cultural lag (Ogburn); Sanskritization (M. N. Srinivas).
- Nurse = change agent, health educator, advocate, counsellor.
- Rural – agriculture, primary ties, communicable disease; urban – industry, impersonal ties, lifestyle disease; tribal – isolation, malaria, malnutrition, sickle cell in some groups.
