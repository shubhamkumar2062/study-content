# Unit 2: The respiratory system

INC Unit II (6 hours): structure of the organs of respiration, the muscles of respiration, and how this applies in nursing. The functions (gas exchange, regulation) are taught in Applied Physiology.

## 2.1 Overview

The respiratory system brings air close to the blood so oxygen can enter and carbon dioxide can leave.

```text
UPPER TRACT                      LOWER TRACT
Nose → Nasal cavity → Pharynx →  Larynx → Trachea → Main bronchi → Lobar bronchi
                                 → Segmental bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles
                                 → Respiratory bronchioles → Alveolar ducts → Alveoli
```

- **Conducting zone** (nose to terminal bronchioles): warms, moistens and filters air; no gas exchange. Its volume is the anatomical dead space.
- **Respiratory zone** (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveoli): gas exchange happens here.

## 2.2 Nose and nasal cavity

- **External nose**: framework of nasal bones above and hyaline cartilage below; openings are the nostrils (anterior nares).
- **Nasal cavity**: divided into right and left halves by the **nasal septum** (perpendicular plate of the ethmoid, vomer, and septal cartilage). Roof formed by the cribriform plate of the ethmoid; floor by the hard and soft palate.
- Each lateral wall has three curled bones — **superior, middle and inferior conchae (turbinates)** — that increase surface area and make air swirl.
- Lining: the front part (vestibule) has skin with coarse hairs; the rest has pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells and a rich blood supply. The roof has the **olfactory epithelium** for smell.
- **Paranasal sinuses**: air spaces in the frontal, maxillary, sphenoid and ethmoid bones; they lighten the skull and give resonance to the voice. They drain into the nasal cavity, so a blocked nose can lead to sinusitis.
- The **nasolacrimal duct** opens under the inferior concha; this is why tears make the nose run.

Functions: warming, humidifying and filtering air; smell; voice resonance.

## 2.3 Pharynx

A muscular tube about 12–14 cm long, from the base of the skull to the level of C6, behind the nose, mouth and larynx.

| Part | Position | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nasopharynx | Behind the nasal cavity | Air only; has the openings of the auditory (Eustachian) tubes and the pharyngeal tonsil (adenoids) |
| Oropharynx | Behind the mouth | Air and food; palatine tonsils on the side walls |
| Laryngopharynx | Behind the larynx | Continues as the oesophagus below |

The opening of the auditory tube explains why throat infections spread to the middle ear, especially in children.

## 2.4 Larynx (voice box)

Lies in the front of the neck from C3 to C6, connecting the pharynx and trachea.

**Cartilages**

| Cartilage | Type | Feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thyroid | Hyaline; single | Largest; forms the "Adam's apple" (laryngeal prominence) |
| Cricoid | Hyaline; single | Complete ring, below the thyroid cartilage; level of C6 |
| Epiglottis | Elastic; single | Leaf-shaped lid that covers the laryngeal inlet during swallowing |
| Arytenoid | Hyaline; paired | Vocal cords attach; move the cords |
| Corniculate, cuneiform | Elastic; paired | Small supporting cartilages |

- **Vocal cords (folds)**: two folds of mucous membrane stretched from the thyroid cartilage in front to the arytenoids behind. The gap between them is the **glottis**. Air passing makes them vibrate to produce sound. Above them are the false (vestibular) folds.
- The **cricothyroid membrane** joins the thyroid and cricoid cartilages; it is the site for an emergency airway opening (cricothyroidotomy).
- Nerve supply: recurrent laryngeal nerves (branches of the vagus) supply almost all the muscles of the larynx. Injury during thyroid surgery causes hoarseness.

Functions: air passage, protection of the lower airway (cough, closing during swallowing), voice production.

## 2.5 Trachea

- Tube about **10–12 cm long and 2–2.5 cm wide**, from the lower border of the cricoid cartilage (C6) to its division at the level of the **sternal angle (T4–T5)**.
- Wall supported by **16–20 C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage**, open at the back. The gap is closed by the trachealis muscle, which lets the oesophagus behind it expand when food passes.
- Lining: pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells. Cilia beat upward and carry mucus and trapped dust to the pharynx (the **mucociliary escalator**). Smoking paralyses the cilia.
- The **carina** is the ridge at the bifurcation; it is very sensitive and its touch causes coughing (felt during suctioning).

## 2.6 Bronchi and bronchial tree

- **Right main bronchus**: shorter, wider and more vertical (about 2.5 cm). **Inhaled foreign bodies and a too-deep endotracheal tube usually go into the right bronchus.**
- **Left main bronchus**: longer (about 5 cm), narrower and more horizontal; it passes under the arch of the aorta.
- Each main bronchus divides into **lobar bronchi** (3 on the right, 2 on the left), then **segmental bronchi** (one for each bronchopulmonary segment), then bronchioles.
- As tubes get smaller, cartilage becomes irregular plates and then disappears (bronchioles have none), smooth muscle increases, and epithelium changes from ciliated columnar to cuboidal. Spasm of this smooth muscle narrows the airways in asthma.

## 2.7 Lungs

Two cone-shaped spongy organs in the thoracic cavity, one on each side of the mediastinum.

**Parts of each lung**: apex (rises about 2.5 cm above the medial third of the clavicle), base (rests on the diaphragm), costal surface (against the ribs), mediastinal (medial) surface with the **hilum** (root of the lung, where the bronchus, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, bronchial vessels, nerves and lymphatics enter and leave).

| Feature | Right lung | Left lung |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lobes | 3 (upper, middle, lower) | 2 (upper, lower) |
| Fissures | Oblique and horizontal | Oblique only |
| Size | Shorter (liver pushes the diaphragm up) and wider | Longer and narrower |
| Special features | — | Cardiac notch and lingula (heart lies to the left) |
| Bronchopulmonary segments | 10 | Usually 8–10 (some segments fuse) |

**Bronchopulmonary segment**: a wedge of lung supplied by one segmental bronchus and its artery. Each segment can be removed surgically, and postural drainage positions are chosen segment by segment.

**Blood supply**

- Pulmonary arteries bring deoxygenated blood for gas exchange; pulmonary veins (four) return oxygenated blood to the left atrium.
- Bronchial arteries (from the aorta) nourish the lung tissue itself.

## 2.8 Alveoli and the respiratory membrane

- About **300 million alveoli** give a surface area of around 70 m² (figures vary between textbooks; quote as approximate).
- Alveolar walls contain:
  - **Type I pneumocytes** — thin flat cells forming most of the surface; gas exchange.
  - **Type II pneumocytes** — make **surfactant**, which lowers surface tension and stops alveoli collapsing. Premature babies may lack surfactant (respiratory distress syndrome).
  - **Alveolar macrophages (dust cells)** — clean out particles and microbes.
- **Respiratory membrane** (where gases cross): alveolar epithelium, fused basement membranes, capillary endothelium — under 1 µm thick in total.

```text
 Alveolar air | Type I cell | basement membrane | capillary endothelium | blood
     O2  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────►
     CO2 ◄───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
```

## 2.9 Pleura

The **pleura** is a serous membrane around each lung.

- **Visceral pleura** covers the lung surface and dips into the fissures.
- **Parietal pleura** lines the inner chest wall, the diaphragm and the mediastinum.
- Between them is the **pleural cavity**, a potential space with a thin film of fluid that lets the layers glide and holds the lung to the chest wall.
- The parietal pleura is sensitive to pain (intercostal and phrenic nerves); the visceral pleura is not. Pleuritic chest pain is sharp and worse on breathing.
- Air in the cavity = pneumothorax; fluid = pleural effusion; pus = empyema; blood = haemothorax.

## 2.10 The thoracic cage and mediastinum

- Thoracic cage: 12 thoracic vertebrae, 12 pairs of ribs with costal cartilages, sternum (manubrium, body, xiphoid).
- Ribs 1–7 are **true ribs** (join the sternum directly); 8–10 are **false ribs** (join the cartilage above); 11–12 are **floating ribs**.
- The **mediastinum** is the middle space between the two pleural sacs; it contains the heart, great vessels, trachea, oesophagus, thymus, nerves and lymph nodes.

## 2.11 Muscles of respiration

| Phase | Muscles | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quiet inspiration | **Diaphragm** (main muscle) and **external intercostals** | Diaphragm contracts and flattens → vertical size of thorax increases. External intercostals lift the ribs up and out ("bucket-handle" and "pump-handle") → side-to-side and front-to-back size increase |
| Forced inspiration (accessory) | Sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, pectoralis minor, serratus anterior | Raise the sternum and upper ribs further |
| Quiet expiration | No muscle needed | Passive elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall |
| Forced expiration | **Internal intercostals**, abdominal muscles (rectus abdominis, external and internal obliques, transversus abdominis) | Pull ribs down; push abdominal organs up against the diaphragm |

**The diaphragm**

- Dome-shaped sheet of skeletal muscle separating the thorax from the abdomen; central tendon in the middle.
- Openings: **caval opening (T8)** for the inferior vena cava, **oesophageal opening (T10)**, **aortic opening (T12)**. (Memory: "I 8 10 eggs at 12" — IVC 8, oesophagus 10, aorta 12.)
- Nerve supply: **phrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5)** — "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive". A high cervical spinal injury above this level stops breathing.

```text
 Inspiration: diaphragm contracts ↓, ribs ↑ → chest volume ↑ → pressure ↓ → air in
 Expiration:  diaphragm relaxes ↑, ribs ↓  → chest volume ↓ → pressure ↑ → air out
```

## 2.12 Application and implication in nursing

- **Positioning**: Fowler's or high-sitting position lets the diaphragm descend more easily, helping breathless patients.
- **Suctioning**: the catheter more easily enters the right bronchus; touching the carina causes coughing. Suction is applied only while withdrawing, for a short time, to limit oxygen loss.
- **Endotracheal tube**: an over-advanced tube enters the right main bronchus and only the right lung is ventilated — check equal air entry on both sides.
- **Chest drain insertion**: usually in the "safe triangle" in the 4th–5th intercostal space in the mid-axillary line; instruments pass just above the upper border of the rib below, because the intercostal vessels and nerve run under the lower border of each rib.
- **Postural drainage** uses gravity to drain individual bronchopulmonary segments.
- **Tracheostomy**: opening in the trachea below the cricoid; the airway then bypasses the nose, so inspired air must be humidified.
- **Breathing exercises** (deep breathing, incentive spirometry) after abdominal or chest surgery use the diaphragm to prevent alveolar collapse (atelectasis).
- Children have shorter Eustachian tubes and smaller airways, so infections and obstruction are more dangerous.

## Remember

- Conducting zone = no gas exchange; respiratory zone begins at respiratory bronchioles.
- Trachea: C6 to T4–T5, C-shaped hyaline rings, open behind.
- Right bronchus: short, wide, vertical — foreign bodies go there.
- Right lung 3 lobes, 2 fissures; left lung 2 lobes, 1 fissure, cardiac notch, lingula.
- Type II pneumocytes make surfactant.
- Diaphragm: main muscle of inspiration, phrenic nerve C3–C5; openings at T8, T10, T12.
- Quiet expiration is passive; forced expiration uses internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.
- Epiglottis is elastic cartilage; cricoid is the only complete ring.
