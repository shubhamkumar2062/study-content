# Unit 2: Mental health, mental hygiene, defence mechanisms, frustration and conflict

This notes file covers INC Unit III (5 hours). It is one of the most examined units: the mentally healthy person, defence mechanisms, and frustration and conflict appear again and again as essay and short-answer questions.

## 2.1 Concept of mental health

**Mental health** is a state of wellbeing in which a person realises his or her own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and is able to contribute to the community. This is the idea behind the World Health Organization's description; learn it in your own words.

Points to stress in an answer:

- Mental health is **more than the absence of mental illness**. A person with no diagnosis may still be unhappy and unable to cope.
- It is a **continuum**, not two boxes. Everyone moves along it.

```text
Mental health continuum
Flourishing ── Coping ── Struggling (stress, worry) ── Mental illness
   (well)                                                (needs treatment)
```

- It includes the **emotional** (feelings), **psychological** (thinking, self-image) and **social** (relationships) sides of a person.
- It is affected by biological factors (genes, brain chemistry, physical illness), psychological factors (early experiences, coping style) and social factors (poverty, family conflict, discrimination, support).

## 2.2 Mental hygiene

**Mental hygiene** is the science and practice of **preserving mental health and preventing mental illness**. Just as physical hygiene keeps the body clean and healthy, mental hygiene keeps the mind healthy.

Aims of mental hygiene:

1. **Preservative:** keep healthy people healthy (good upbringing, education, recreation).
2. **Preventive:** prevent mental illness by reducing risk factors and detecting problems early.
3. **Curative/remedial:** early treatment and rehabilitation of those who become ill.

Mental hygiene works at every level: the individual, the home, the school, the workplace and the community.

## 2.3 Characteristics of a mentally healthy person

Learn at least ten, with a one-line explanation each.

| Characteristic | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Self-acceptance and self-esteem | Knows own strengths and limits; does not feel worthless |
| Realistic perception of reality | Sees situations as they are, not distorted by fear or wishful thinking |
| Emotional balance | Feels emotions but controls their expression; not easily upset |
| Ability to cope with stress | Faces problems instead of running away from them |
| Good interpersonal relationships | Can give and receive love, trust and respect |
| Independence with healthy dependence | Takes own decisions but can ask for help |
| Sense of responsibility | Accepts consequences of own actions |
| Productive work | Uses abilities, finds satisfaction in work |
| Adaptability (flexibility) | Adjusts to change — new job, illness, loss |
| Purpose and direction in life | Has goals and values |
| Integrated personality | Thoughts, feelings and actions fit together |
| Sense of humour | Can laugh, including at self |
| Social interest | Concern for others and community |
| Tolerance of frustration | Can wait, accept delay and failure |

## 2.4 Warning signs of poor mental health

Changes from the person's **usual** behaviour that last for some weeks are the clue.

- Persistent sadness, hopelessness, crying spells.
- Excessive worry, fear or restlessness.
- Marked change in sleep (too little or too much) or appetite.
- Withdrawal from friends, family and activities once enjoyed.
- Fall in work or school performance; poor concentration.
- Extreme mood swings; irritability, anger outbursts.
- Unusual beliefs or hearing/seeing things others do not.
- Increased use of alcohol, tobacco or other substances.
- Many physical complaints with no clear cause.
- Neglect of personal hygiene.
- Talk of death, being a burden, or self-harm.

**Nursing point:** any talk of suicide or self-harm is taken seriously, the person is not left alone, and it is reported at once to the senior nurse or doctor.

## 2.5 Promotive and preventive mental health strategies and services

### Levels of prevention

| Level | Aim | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Promotion | Build mental wellbeing in everyone | Life-skills education, good parenting, yoga, sports, safe workplaces |
| Primary prevention | Stop illness occurring | Antenatal care (prevent birth injury), reduce substance use, school counselling, stress management |
| Secondary prevention | Early detection and prompt treatment | Screening, crisis intervention, early referral |
| Tertiary prevention | Reduce disability; rehabilitation | Vocational training, day care, family support, prevent relapse |

### Strategies at different levels

- **Individual:** balanced diet, exercise, sleep, hobbies, relaxation, meaningful relationships, avoiding alcohol and drugs.
- **Family:** warm, consistent parenting; open communication; avoiding violence.
- **School:** life-skills, anti-bullying policies, counselling, recognising learning difficulties.
- **Workplace:** reasonable workload, support, recognition, employee assistance.
- **Community:** awareness programmes, reducing stigma, self-help groups, support for vulnerable groups.

### Services in India (check current details on the MoHFW / DGHS site)

- **National Mental Health Programme (NMHP)**, started in 1982, to make basic mental health care available to all, especially the poor, and to integrate it with general health care.
- **District Mental Health Programme (DMHP)**, added in 1996, brings community-based services to the district level: outpatient and inpatient care, outreach, awareness, training of general health staff.
- **Tele-MANAS**, the national tele-mental-health service started in October 2022, gives free round-the-clock counselling by phone; the helpline number is **14416**.
- **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** — a rights-based law that protects the dignity of persons with mental illness, gives the right to access mental health care, and allows an advance directive. Read the current Act for details before quoting sections.
- Mental hospitals and institutes, psychiatry departments of general hospitals, counselling centres, de-addiction centres, NGOs and self-help groups.

## 2.6 Defence mechanisms

**Defence mechanisms** (ego defence or mental mechanisms) are mostly **unconscious** mental processes that protect a person from anxiety, guilt or loss of self-esteem. The idea comes from Sigmund Freud and was developed by Anna Freud.

Key points:

- Everyone uses them; they are **normal** in moderate amounts.
- They **distort or deny reality** to some extent and do not solve the real problem.
- Used **too much or rigidly**, they become unhealthy and can be part of mental illness.

| Mechanism | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repression | Pushing painful thoughts or memories into the unconscious (involuntary) | Cannot recall details of a traumatic accident |
| Suppression | Consciously putting a worry aside (voluntary; healthy) | A nurse decides not to think about a family problem while on duty |
| Denial | Refusing to accept a painful reality | A patient told of cancer says, "The report must be wrong" |
| Projection | Blaming others for one's own unacceptable feelings | A student who did not study says the teacher hates him |
| Rationalisation | Giving a logical-sounding excuse for behaviour | "I failed because the paper was out of syllabus" |
| Displacement | Transferring feelings to a safer target | Angry with the doctor, the patient shouts at the nurse |
| Regression | Returning to behaviour of an earlier stage | A toilet-trained 4-year-old wets the bed in hospital |
| Reaction formation | Showing the opposite of the real feeling | Being extra polite to a disliked colleague |
| Sublimation | Channelling unacceptable drives into socially useful activity (healthy) | Aggression channelled into sport |
| Compensation | Making up for a weakness by excelling in another area | A student weak in sports works hard to top the class |
| Identification | Taking on the qualities of an admired person | A student nurse copies a respected senior |
| Introjection | Taking in the values of others as one's own | Child adopting parents' rules |
| Fantasy (daydreaming) | Satisfying wishes in imagination | Imagining being a famous doctor |
| Conversion | Anxiety turned into a physical symptom without a physical cause | Sudden loss of voice before a feared exam |
| Undoing | Doing something to cancel a guilty act | Bringing gifts after shouting at a child |
| Isolation of affect | Separating a feeling from a memory | Describing a relative's death without emotion |
| Intellectualisation | Using reasoning to avoid feeling | A patient talks only about statistics of the disease |
| Symbolisation | An object or act stands for a hidden idea | Repeated hand-washing standing for guilt |
| Substitution | Replacing an unreachable goal with a reachable one | Not getting MBBS, joins nursing |
| Withdrawal | Avoiding situations that cause anxiety | Staying alone in the room |

### Implications for nursing

- Recognise the mechanism; it is a **sign of anxiety**, not stubbornness or bad character.
- **Do not attack or tear down** a defence suddenly (for example arguing with a patient in denial); it may increase anxiety.
- Give time, accept the person, listen, and gently help them face reality step by step.
- **Encourage healthy mechanisms** (suppression, sublimation, compensation) and discourage harmful ones (excessive denial, projection).
- Check your own defences: a nurse who displaces anger on patients or rationalises poor care needs self-awareness.

## 2.7 Frustration

**Frustration** is the feeling of tension, disappointment and irritation when a person is blocked from reaching a goal or satisfying a need.

```text
Need/motive → Goal-directed behaviour → BARRIER → Frustration
                                                     ↓
                     Reactions: aggression, withdrawal, regression,
                     apathy, anxiety, defence mechanisms – or new solution
```

### Sources (causes) of frustration

| Source | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Environmental (external) – physical | Floods, traffic, illness, lack of money |
| Environmental – social | Rules, discrimination, family restrictions |
| Personal (internal) | Lack of ability, physical disability, unrealistic goals, low confidence |
| Conflict | Two opposing motives at the same time (see below) |

### Reactions to frustration

- **Direct approach:** more effort, a new method, changing the goal — healthy.
- **Aggression:** direct (hitting, shouting) or displaced on someone else.
- **Withdrawal or apathy:** giving up, "learned helplessness".
- **Regression, fixation:** childish or repetitive behaviour.
- **Psychosomatic symptoms:** headache, acidity.
- **Excessive use of defence mechanisms.**

## 2.8 Conflict and its types

**Conflict** is a state in which a person faces two or more opposing motives, needs or goals at the same time and must choose. Kurt Lewin described the basic types.

| Type | Choice | Example | Usual result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Approach–approach | Between two attractive goals | Two good job offers | Easiest to solve; mild stress |
| Avoidance–avoidance | Between two unpleasant choices | Painful surgery or continuing pain | Hard; person may delay, "leave the field" |
| Approach–avoidance | One goal has both attractive and unattractive sides | Wants sweets but fears gaining weight | Vacillation; anxiety |
| Double (multiple) approach–avoidance | Two or more goals, each with good and bad sides | Job in a city (good pay, away from family) vs village (less pay, near family) | Most common in real life; most stressful |

### Measures to overcome frustration and conflict

1. Identify the real problem and the needs behind it.
2. Set realistic goals that match abilities.
3. Gather information; list options, pros and cons; choose and act.
4. Accept what cannot be changed; change what can.
5. Talk to a trusted person, counsellor or teacher.
6. Use relaxation, exercise, hobbies to reduce tension.
7. Develop frustration tolerance and patience.
8. Learn from failure instead of blaming self or others.
9. Seek professional help if distress persists.

## 2.9 Role of the nurse in reducing frustration and conflict and enhancing coping

Patients are frustrated by pain, loss of independence, hospital rules, waiting, uncertainty and cost. Many face conflicts such as "surgery or no surgery", "hospital or home".

- **Assess** the source of frustration: pain, fear, unmet basic needs, poor information.
- **Meet basic needs promptly** — comfort, pain relief as ordered, food, sleep, privacy.
- **Explain procedures and rules** in simple language; uncertainty is a major frustration.
- **Allow choices** wherever possible (timing of bath, food preferences) to restore a sense of control.
- **Listen** and allow expression of feelings; accept anger without retaliating.
- **Help in decision-making** in conflicts: give information, discuss options; the decision belongs to the patient.
- **Involve the family** and support systems.
- **Teach coping skills:** relaxation breathing, problem-solving, positive self-talk.
- **Set achievable goals** in rehabilitation, praise progress.
- **Refer** to counsellor, psychologist or psychiatrist when needed.
- **Look after own stress** so that the nurse is not frustrated with patients.

**Coping** is the effort a person makes to manage stress. Problem-focused coping changes the situation; emotion-focused coping manages the feelings (more in Unit 7).

## 2.10 Dealing with ego

In Freud's structure of personality the **ego** is the realistic part of the mind that balances the demands of the **id** (instinctive wants) and the **superego** (conscience, moral rules). In everyday language "ego" also means pride or self-importance. Both meanings matter in nursing.

- **Healthy ego:** strong enough to face reality, delay gratification, solve problems, and accept criticism.
- **Ego problems:** a weak ego (easily hurt, defensive, dependent) or an inflated ego (arrogant, cannot accept mistakes or advice).

Dealing with ego in self and others:

1. **Self-awareness:** notice when hurt pride is driving your reaction.
2. **Accept feedback** as information, not as attack; separate the act from the person.
3. **Admit mistakes** early — essential for patient safety.
4. **Respect others' self-esteem:** correct in private, praise in public; never humiliate a patient.
5. **Avoid power struggles** with patients or colleagues; focus on the goal (the patient's wellbeing).
6. **Assertiveness, not aggression:** state needs clearly and politely.
7. **Humility and teamwork:** value every member of the team.

## Remember

- Mental health = wellbeing, coping, productivity, contribution; not just absence of illness; a continuum.
- Mental hygiene = preserve mental health, prevent illness, early treatment.
- Defence mechanisms are mostly unconscious, protect against anxiety, normal in moderation; suppression and sublimation are healthy.
- Do not break a patient's defence suddenly; reduce anxiety first.
- Frustration = blocked goal; conflict = opposing goals (approach–approach, avoidance–avoidance, approach–avoidance, double approach–avoidance).
- NMHP 1982, DMHP 1996, Tele-MANAS 14416, Mental Healthcare Act 2017.
- Ego = realistic mediator between id and superego; nurses accept feedback and protect patients' self-esteem.
