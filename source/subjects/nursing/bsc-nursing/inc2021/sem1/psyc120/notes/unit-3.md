# Unit 3: Developmental psychology and vulnerable groups

This notes file covers INC Unit IV (7 hours): development across the life span, the nurse's role in supporting normal development, psychological needs of each age group in health and sickness, an introduction to child psychology, and the psychology of vulnerable individuals.

## 3.1 Growth and development: basic ideas

- **Growth** = increase in size (height, weight); it is quantitative and measurable.
- **Development** = increase in skill and complexity of function (walking, speech, reasoning); it is qualitative.
- **Developmental psychology** studies how people change physically, cognitively, emotionally and socially from conception to death.

### Principles of development

1. Development is **continuous** — it goes on throughout life.
2. It follows a **predictable sequence** (sit before stand, babble before words).
3. **Cephalocaudal** direction: head to tail (head control before walking).
4. **Proximodistal** direction: centre to periphery (arm movements before finger skills).
5. **General to specific:** whole-body movements before precise ones.
6. Each person develops at their **own rate** (individual differences).
7. It is the result of **heredity and environment** together.
8. There are **critical (sensitive) periods** when certain skills develop best (language in early childhood).
9. All areas of development are **interrelated**: a sick child may lag in social and cognitive development too.

## 3.2 Theories you should name in answers

**Erikson's psychosocial stages** — each stage has a "crisis" to resolve.

| Age | Crisis | Successful outcome (virtue) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infancy (0–1 yr) | Trust vs mistrust | Hope |
| Toddler (1–3 yr) | Autonomy vs shame and doubt | Will |
| Preschool (3–6 yr) | Initiative vs guilt | Purpose |
| School age (6–12 yr) | Industry vs inferiority | Competence |
| Adolescence (12–18 yr) | Identity vs role confusion | Fidelity |
| Young adult | Intimacy vs isolation | Love |
| Middle adult | Generativity vs stagnation | Care |
| Older adult | Integrity vs despair | Wisdom |

**Piaget's stages of cognitive development:**

| Stage | Age (approx.) | Key features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sensorimotor | 0–2 yr | Learns through senses and movement; object permanence develops |
| Preoperational | 2–7 yr | Symbols and language; egocentric; magical thinking; no conservation |
| Concrete operational | 7–11 yr | Logical thinking about real objects; conservation; classification |
| Formal operational | 11 yr onwards | Abstract and hypothetical thinking |

**Freud's psychosexual stages** (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital) are also asked occasionally as a short note.

## 3.3 Development across the life span

### Prenatal period (conception to birth)

- Physical: germinal, embryonic and fetal stages; organs form in the first trimester, which is the most sensitive time.
- Influences: mother's nutrition, infections, smoking, alcohol, some medicines, radiation, maternal stress, age.
- Psychological point: mother's emotional state and acceptance of the pregnancy matter.

### Infancy and early childhood (birth to about 6 years)

- **Physical:** rapid growth; motor milestones — social smile, head control, sitting, crawling, standing, walking, running.
- **Cognitive:** sensorimotor then preoperational; first words around one year, short sentences by two to three years; curiosity, "why" questions.
- **Psychosocial:** attachment to mother or main caregiver (bonding); stranger anxiety and separation anxiety; autonomy (toilet training, "no"); play moves from solitary to parallel to cooperative; initiative in preschool years.

### Middle and late childhood (6 years to puberty)

- **Physical:** slower, steady growth; fine motor skills (writing).
- **Cognitive:** concrete operational thinking; school learning; memory improves.
- **Psychosocial:** peers become important; industry (achievement) vs inferiority; rules and fairness; gender role learning.

### Adolescence (about 12 to 18 years)

- **Physical:** puberty — growth spurt, secondary sexual characters, menarche in girls; body image concerns.
- **Cognitive:** abstract thinking, idealism; "personal fable" (feeling unique, invulnerable) leads to risk-taking.
- **Psychosocial:** search for identity; peer pressure; wish for independence; mood swings; career choices; risk of substance use, eating problems, depression.

### Early and middle adulthood (about 18 to 60 years)

- **Physical:** peak physical ability in early adulthood; gradual decline later; menopause in women around the late forties to early fifties.
- **Cognitive:** practical problem-solving and expertise grow.
- **Psychosocial:** career, marriage, parenthood (intimacy); in middle age generativity — guiding the next generation, caring for both children and ageing parents ("sandwich generation"); possible mid-life review of achievements.

### Late adulthood (about 60 years onwards)

- **Physical:** reduced sensory acuity (vision, hearing), slower reactions, chronic illnesses, reduced mobility.
- **Cognitive:** slower processing and recall of new information, but knowledge and wisdom remain; dementia is a disease, **not** normal ageing.
- **Psychosocial:** retirement, loss of spouse and friends, dependence, loneliness; integrity vs despair — looking back on life with satisfaction or regret.

### Death and dying

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross described five stages that dying people (and the bereaved) may pass through. They do not always occur in order, and people may move back and forth.

| Stage | Typical words | Nursing response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Denial | "Not me, it can't be true." | Accept; do not force the truth; stay available |
| Anger | "Why me?" | Do not take it personally; allow expression |
| Bargaining | "If I recover, I will…" | Listen; support spiritual needs |
| Depression | Sadness, withdrawal | Sit with the person; allow grief; touch, presence |
| Acceptance | Calm, peaceful | Support wishes, dignity, family time |

Understanding of death also develops with age: young children see it as temporary, by about 9–10 years most understand it is permanent and universal.

## 3.4 Role of the nurse in supporting normal growth and development

1. **Assess** development at every contact using milestones and growth charts; identify delays early and refer.
2. **Promote health:** antenatal care, breastfeeding, nutrition, immunisation, safety, hygiene.
3. **Educate parents** on stimulation, play, toilet training, discipline without harshness, and expected behaviour at each age.
4. **Support attachment:** early skin-to-skin contact, rooming-in, involve parents in care of sick children.
5. **Adapt care to the stage:** explain in age-appropriate language; allow choices; respect privacy of adolescents.
6. **Adolescent health:** counselling on puberty, nutrition, substance use, safe relationships, mental health.
7. **Adult health:** stress management, screening, work–life balance.
8. **Older adults:** prevent falls, maintain independence, social engagement, early recognition of memory problems.
9. **End of life:** comfort, dignity, honest communication, bereavement support for the family.

## 3.5 Psychological needs of various age groups in health and sickness

| Age group | Needs in health | Effect of illness / hospital | Nursing approach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Infant | Love, security, consistent caregiver, feeding, touch | Separation distress, disturbed trust | Mother to stay; same nurse; cuddle, comfort; routine as at home |
| Toddler / preschool | Autonomy, play, routine, parental presence | Separation anxiety (protest, despair, detachment), fear of injury, regression, sees illness as punishment | Parents stay; favourite toy; simple honest explanations; play; allow small choices |
| School-age child | Achievement, peers, information, privacy | Fear of missing school, loss of control, fear of body injury | Explain using diagrams; schoolwork; contact with friends; involve in own care |
| Adolescent | Identity, independence, privacy, peer acceptance, body image | Worry about appearance, loss of independence, embarrassment | Privacy; talk directly with them; keep confidentiality where safe; peer contact |
| Adult | Work, family role, financial security, self-esteem | Worry about job, money, family; role change; anger, depression | Information; involve in decisions; social work help; family support |
| Older adult | Respect, dignity, independence, companionship, security | Confusion in new places, fear of dependence and death, loneliness | Address respectfully; orient; aids (glasses, hearing aid); unhurried care; family visits |

## 3.6 Introduction to child psychology and the nurse's role

**Child psychology** studies the behaviour, thinking and emotions of children from birth to adolescence.

Important ideas:

- **Attachment** (Bowlby): a secure bond with a caregiver is the base for later confidence and relationships.
- **Play** is the child's work and language: physical, social, intellectual and emotional development happen through play. **Therapeutic play** in hospital lets children act out fears (for example giving an injection to a doll).
- **Separation anxiety in hospital** has three phases: protest (crying, clinging), despair (quiet, sad, withdrawn), detachment (seems to accept strangers but has emotionally withdrawn). It is reduced by keeping parents with the child.
- Common **behaviour problems:** temper tantrums, thumb-sucking, bed-wetting, nail-biting, fears, school refusal, lying. Most are part of development or a reaction to stress; harsh punishment worsens them.
- **Discipline:** consistent rules, praise for good behaviour, calm consequences; avoid physical punishment.

**Nurse's role in meeting children's psychological needs:**

- Prepare the child for procedures with honest, simple words, just before the event for young children.
- Never threaten ("If you cry I'll give you an injection").
- Use distraction, toys, stories, music during painful procedures.
- Keep routines (sleep, meals) close to home routine.
- Praise cooperation; give stickers or small rewards.
- Provide a play corner; allow parents to participate in care.
- Watch for signs of abuse or neglect and report as per hospital policy and law.

## 3.7 Psychology of vulnerable individuals

**Vulnerable individuals** are people at higher risk of harm, neglect or poor health because of their physical, mental, social or economic situation.

| Group | Psychological concerns |
| --- | --- |
| Persons with disabilities (physically or mentally challenged) | Low self-esteem, dependence, social stigma, frustration, isolation; family burden |
| Women | Role overload (home and work), gender discrimination, domestic violence, stress of pregnancy and childbirth, postnatal low mood, menopause |
| The sick, especially chronically ill | Anxiety, depression, loss of role, fear of death, financial worry |
| Older adults | Loneliness, neglect, abuse, dependence |
| Children, especially orphans and street children | Insecurity, exploitation, abuse |
| Persons with mental illness | Stigma, discrimination, rejection |
| Poor, homeless, migrants, refugees | Insecurity, poor access to care |
| Persons with HIV, leprosy, TB | Stigma, fear of rejection |
| Victims of disasters, violence, abuse | Fear, trauma, grief |

## 3.8 Role of the nurse with vulnerable groups

1. **Respect and dignity:** treat every person equally, use their name, avoid labels.
2. **Non-judgemental attitude:** accept the person; avoid blaming.
3. **Assess needs** — physical, psychological, social, economic — and risks (abuse, suicide).
4. **Empower:** encourage independence, teach self-care, use aids and appliances.
5. **Advocacy:** speak up for their rights; ensure informed consent and privacy.
6. **Link with services:** social welfare schemes, disability certificates, women's helplines, shelters, NGOs, rehabilitation (check current schemes with the local health or social welfare office).
7. **Family support and education.**
8. **Fight stigma** through community awareness.
9. **Safeguarding:** recognise and report abuse or violence as per law and hospital policy.

## Remember

- Growth = size; development = function; development is sequential, cephalocaudal, proximodistal.
- Erikson: trust, autonomy, initiative, industry, identity, intimacy, generativity, integrity.
- Piaget: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational.
- Separation anxiety in hospital: protest → despair → detachment; keep parents with the child.
- Kübler-Ross: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance.
- Vulnerable groups need respect, advocacy, empowerment and links to services.
