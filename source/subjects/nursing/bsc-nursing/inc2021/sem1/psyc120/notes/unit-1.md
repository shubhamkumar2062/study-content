# Unit 1: Introduction to psychology and the biological basis of behaviour

This notes file covers INC Unit I (Introduction, 2 hours) and INC Unit II (Biological basis of behaviour, 4 hours). Both are short, and Unit II explains the "machinery" that Unit I's definitions talk about, so they are read together.

## 1.1 Meaning of psychology

The word comes from two Greek words: *psyche* (soul, mind) and *logos* (study, knowledge). So, word for word, psychology was "the study of the soul". Over time the meaning changed as the subject became scientific.

| Period | Psychology was seen as | Problem with the idea |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early Greek philosophy | Study of the soul | The soul cannot be observed or measured |
| 17th–18th century | Study of the mind | "Mind" was vague; no agreed method |
| Late 19th century | Study of consciousness | Ignored unconscious and animal behaviour |
| Early 20th century (behaviourism) | Study of behaviour | Ignored thoughts and feelings |
| Present day | Scientific study of behaviour and mental processes | Accepted working definition |

**Modern definition (in plain words):** psychology is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes of humans and animals, and of how these are affected by the body, the environment and other people.

- **Behaviour** = anything a person does that can be observed: walking, crying, talking, refusing food.
- **Mental processes** = inner activities we cannot see directly: thinking, remembering, feeling, perceiving, dreaming.
- **Scientific** = it uses systematic observation, measurement and testing of ideas rather than opinion.

### Goals of psychology

1. **Describe** behaviour accurately (what is happening).
2. **Explain** it (why it happens).
3. **Predict** it (what will happen next).
4. **Control or change** it (how to improve it — for example reduce anxiety before surgery).

## 1.2 Development of psychology

```text
Philosophy (Plato, Aristotle)  →  Physiology of the senses
             ↓
1879: Wilhelm Wundt opens the first psychology laboratory (Leipzig)
             ↓
Schools of thought:
  Structuralism (Wundt, Titchener)  – elements of conscious experience
  Functionalism (William James)     – what the mind does, adaptation
  Behaviourism (Watson, Skinner)    – only observable behaviour
  Gestalt (Wertheimer, Köhler)      – the whole is more than its parts
  Psychoanalysis (Sigmund Freud)    – unconscious mind, early childhood
  Humanistic (Maslow, Rogers)       – growth, free will, self-actualisation
  Cognitive                          – thinking, memory, information processing
  Biological / neuroscience          – brain, hormones, genes
```

Wundt is called the "father of experimental psychology", and 1879 is usually taken as the birth of psychology as a separate science. Modern psychology is **eclectic**: it uses whichever approach best explains a problem.

## 1.3 Scope of psychology

Scope means the areas in which psychology is studied and used. It is very wide because wherever there are people, there is behaviour.

- Individual: personality, intelligence, emotions, learning.
- Family and society: relationships, groups, attitudes, prejudice.
- Health: illness behaviour, coping, pain, compliance with treatment.
- Education: how students learn, motivation, guidance.
- Work: selection of staff, job satisfaction, teamwork.
- Law and crime, advertising, sports, the armed forces, space travel.

## 1.4 Branches of psychology

**Pure (theoretical) branches** — build knowledge:

| Branch | What it studies |
| --- | --- |
| General psychology | Basic principles of behaviour in normal adults |
| Developmental psychology | Changes from conception to death (child, adolescent, ageing psychology) |
| Abnormal psychology | Causes and nature of abnormal behaviour |
| Social psychology | Behaviour of the individual in groups |
| Physiological (bio)psychology | Brain, nerves, hormones and behaviour |
| Experimental psychology | Behaviour studied under controlled laboratory conditions |
| Personality psychology | Individual differences in traits and character |

**Applied branches** — use the knowledge:

| Branch | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Clinical psychology | Assessment and therapy of mental disorders |
| Counselling psychology | Helping normal people with personal, educational, career problems |
| Health psychology | Behaviour in health, illness, prevention and recovery |
| Educational psychology | Teaching and learning |
| Industrial / organisational | Workplace, selection, productivity |
| Community psychology | Mental health of whole communities |
| Forensic / legal | Crime, witnesses, courts |
| Nursing psychology | Patient behaviour, nurse–patient relationship, nurse's own wellbeing |

## 1.5 Methods of psychology

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Introspection | A person looks inward and reports own thoughts and feelings | Only way to know private experience | Subjective; children and ill people cannot do it well |
| Observation (naturalistic) | Watch behaviour in its natural setting, record it | Real-life behaviour | Observer bias; cannot show cause |
| Experimental method | Change one factor (independent variable) and measure its effect (dependent variable), keeping others constant | Shows cause and effect | Artificial setting; ethical limits |
| Case study (case history) | Detailed study of one person over time | Deep understanding | Cannot generalise |
| Survey / questionnaire | Ask many people set questions | Large data quickly | People may not answer honestly |
| Interview | Face-to-face questioning, structured or unstructured | Flexible, rich information | Time-consuming, interviewer bias |
| Correlational method | Measures how two variables move together | Useful when experiments are not possible | Correlation is not causation |
| Psychological tests | Standardised tools to measure intelligence, personality, attitude | Objective scores | Must be valid, reliable, culturally fair |

In an experiment, the group that receives the change is the **experimental group** and the group that does not is the **control group**.

**Nursing example of observation:** a nurse records that a child cries and refuses food every evening after parents leave — this is naturalistic observation, and it helps plan care (for example a familiar toy, flexible visiting).

## 1.6 Relationship with other subjects

| Subject | Link with psychology |
| --- | --- |
| Physiology and anatomy | Nervous system and hormones are the physical basis of behaviour |
| Sociology | Both study people; sociology studies groups, psychology the individual within them |
| Medicine and psychiatry | Mind affects illness and illness affects mind (psychosomatic disorders) |
| Education | Learning theories guide teaching, including health teaching |
| Anthropology | Culture shapes behaviour, beliefs about illness |
| Philosophy | Psychology grew out of philosophy; ethics and values |
| Nursing | Holistic care needs understanding of the patient's mind as well as body |

## 1.7 Significance of psychology in nursing

1. **Understanding the patient as a whole person** — fear, anxiety, denial, anger and depression are common responses to illness.
2. **Nurse–patient relationship** — empathy, trust and therapeutic communication come from psychological principles.
3. **Understanding illness behaviour** — why some people delay treatment, stop medicines, or keep coming back with vague complaints.
4. **Patient education** — learning and memory principles make health teaching effective (short, repeated, practical).
5. **Care across age groups** — a toddler, a teenager and an elderly patient need different approaches.
6. **Pain and stress** — attention, perception and emotion change how much pain is felt; distraction and reassurance help.
7. **Mental health promotion** — early recognition of warning signs and referral.
8. **The nurse's own wellbeing** — managing stress, preventing burnout, handling death of patients.
9. **Teamwork and leadership** — working with doctors, colleagues and families.
10. **Rehabilitation** — motivating patients after stroke, amputation, long illness.

## 1.8 Applied psychology to solve everyday issues

Psychology is not only for patients; it helps in daily life:

- **Study habits:** spaced revision, self-testing, sleep before exams.
- **Time management:** priority lists, breaking big tasks into small steps.
- **Relationships:** listening, assertive (not aggressive) communication, resolving conflicts.
- **Stress:** recognising signs early, relaxation, exercise, talking to someone.
- **Habits:** replacing a bad habit by a new one with reward (for example quitting tobacco).
- **Decision-making:** listing options, pros and cons, avoiding hasty decisions under strong emotion.

## 1.9 Body–mind relationship

The body and mind are not separate; each affects the other.

```text
        MIND  ⇄  BODY
  worry, fear      →  fast heartbeat, sweating, acidity, headache
  chronic stress   →  high BP, lowered immunity, poor sleep
  pain, illness    →  anxiety, irritability, depression
  hormonal change  →  mood change (puberty, pregnancy, menopause)
  exercise, sleep  →  better mood and concentration
```

- **Psychosomatic (psychophysiological) disorders:** physical illnesses in which psychological factors play a big part in onset or worsening, for example bronchial asthma, peptic ulcer, essential hypertension, tension headache, some skin disorders.
- **Somatopsychic effects:** physical disease causing psychological change, for example hypothyroidism causing slowness and low mood, or a long hospital stay causing depression.
- The **biopsychosocial model** says health and illness come from the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors. Nursing assessment follows this model.

## 1.10 Genetics and behaviour; inheritance of behaviour

- **Genes** are units of heredity on chromosomes (46 in 23 pairs in humans). They carry instructions for body structure and also influence the nervous system and hormones, and so behaviour.
- **Heredity** sets the potential (the range); **environment** decides how much of it is reached. Behaviour comes from their interaction.

How scientists study inheritance of behaviour:

| Method | Idea |
| --- | --- |
| Family studies | Does a trait occur more often in close relatives? |
| Twin studies | Identical (monozygotic) twins share all genes; fraternal (dizygotic) share about half. If identical twins are more alike, genes matter |
| Adoption studies | Is an adopted child more like the biological parents (genes) or adoptive parents (environment)? |

Traits with a clear genetic influence include intelligence, temperament (easy or difficult baby), and the risk of some mental illnesses such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. "Risk" is the key word: genes increase vulnerability; stress and environment often trigger the illness. Some chromosomal conditions affect behaviour and intelligence, for example **Down syndrome** (trisomy 21).

**Nursing point:** a family history of mental illness is part of the history; it is a reason for watchfulness and support, never for blaming or labelling.

## 1.11 Brain and behaviour

The nervous system controls all behaviour.

```text
Nervous system
├── Central (CNS): brain + spinal cord
│     Brain: cerebrum, cerebellum, brainstem (midbrain, pons, medulla),
│            diencephalon (thalamus, hypothalamus), limbic system
└── Peripheral (PNS): cranial and spinal nerves
      ├── Somatic – voluntary muscles, sensations
      └── Autonomic – sympathetic ("fight or flight"),
                      parasympathetic ("rest and digest")
```

| Part | Main behavioural role |
| --- | --- |
| Frontal lobe | Planning, judgement, personality, voluntary movement, speech production (Broca's area) |
| Parietal lobe | Touch, pain, temperature, body position |
| Temporal lobe | Hearing, memory, understanding speech (Wernicke's area) |
| Occipital lobe | Vision |
| Limbic system (hippocampus, amygdala) | Memory formation; emotions such as fear and anger |
| Hypothalamus | Hunger, thirst, temperature, sleep, sex drive; controls pituitary |
| Thalamus | Relay station for sensations |
| Cerebellum | Balance, coordination |
| Brainstem / reticular formation | Breathing, heartbeat, alertness and wakefulness |
| Corpus callosum | Connects left and right hemispheres |

- **Neurons** pass messages using electrical impulses and chemical **neurotransmitters**. Some linked with behaviour: dopamine (reward, movement), serotonin (mood, sleep), noradrenaline (alertness, stress), acetylcholine (memory, muscle action), GABA (calming).
- **Endocrine glands** also shape behaviour: adrenaline in fear; thyroid hormones in energy level; sex hormones in puberty.
- **Damage changes behaviour:** a head injury to the frontal lobe may make a person impulsive or rude; stroke may cause speech loss; dementia damages memory.

## 1.12 Psychology and sensation

**Sensation** is the process by which sense organs receive stimuli (light, sound, pressure) and change them into nerve impulses sent to the brain. **Perception** (Unit 5) is the brain's interpretation of these impulses.

### The sensory process

```text
Stimulus → Receptor (sense organ) → Transduction (change to nerve impulse)
        → Sensory nerve → Thalamus (relay) → Sensory cortex → Sensation
```

| Sense | Receptor organ | Stimulus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vision | Eye (rods, cones in retina) | Light |
| Hearing | Ear (cochlea) | Sound waves |
| Smell | Nose (olfactory epithelium) | Chemicals in air |
| Taste | Tongue (taste buds) | Chemicals in food |
| Touch (skin senses) | Skin receptors | Pressure, pain, heat, cold |
| Kinaesthetic | Muscles, joints | Body position and movement |
| Vestibular | Inner ear (semicircular canals) | Balance |
| Organic (visceral) | Internal organs | Hunger, thirst, fullness |

Key terms:

- **Absolute threshold:** the smallest stimulus that can be detected half the time.
- **Difference threshold (just noticeable difference):** the smallest change in a stimulus that can be detected.
- **Sensory adaptation:** sensitivity falls when a stimulus stays constant (we stop noticing a ward smell after some time).
- **Sensory deprivation:** too little stimulation (isolation rooms, ICU at night, patients with eye patches) can cause confusion, restlessness and even hallucinations.
- **Sensory overload:** too much stimulation (alarms, lights, noise in ICU) causes irritability, poor sleep and confusion ("ICU delirium" risk).

### Abnormal sensation

| Abnormality | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anaesthesia | Loss of sensation | Nerve injury |
| Hyperaesthesia | Increased sensitivity | Burns, some nerve diseases |
| Hypoaesthesia | Reduced sensitivity | Diabetic feet |
| Paraesthesia | Abnormal sensation without stimulus (tingling, pins and needles) | Vitamin B12 deficiency |
| Blindness, deafness | Loss of a special sense | Cataract, ageing |
| Phantom limb sensation | Feeling in a limb that has been amputated | After amputation |

### Nursing role in sensation

- Check that glasses and hearing aids are worn and working.
- Avoid both deprivation and overload: orientation clocks, window light, dim lights and less noise at night.
- Protect patients with reduced sensation: check bath water temperature, inspect diabetic feet daily, prevent pressure sores.
- Speak clearly, face the patient, use touch appropriately.

## Remember

- Psychology = scientific study of behaviour and mental processes; goals: describe, explain, predict, control.
- Wundt, 1879, Leipzig = first psychology laboratory.
- Methods: introspection, observation, experiment, case study, survey, interview, tests, correlation.
- Body and mind affect each other: psychosomatic disorders; biopsychosocial model.
- Heredity sets potential; environment shapes it — twin and adoption studies show this.
- Limbic system = emotion and memory; hypothalamus = drives; frontal lobe = judgement and personality.
- Sensation (receiving) comes before perception (interpreting); avoid sensory deprivation and overload in patients.
