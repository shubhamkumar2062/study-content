# Unit 7: Motivation, emotion, stress and attitudes

This notes file covers INC Unit VII (6 hours): motivation, emotions, stress and adaptation, attitudes, an introduction to psychometric assessment of emotions and attitudes, and the role of the nurse in caring for the emotionally sick client. Maslow's hierarchy and the general adaptation syndrome are favourite exam topics.

## 7.1 Motivation: meaning and concept

**Motivation** is the inner force or process that starts, directs and keeps up goal-directed behaviour. The word comes from the Latin *movere*, "to move".

Related terms:

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Need | A state of lack or deprivation | Body lacks water |
| Drive | The aroused state of tension produced by a need that pushes to action | Thirst |
| Motive | The general term for any need or drive directing behaviour towards a goal | Desire to drink |
| Incentive (goal) | The external object or reward that satisfies the need and pulls behaviour | Water; a prize |

## 7.2 The motivation cycle

```text
      NEED (lack)
         ↓
      DRIVE (tension)
         ↓
  GOAL-DIRECTED BEHAVIOUR
         ↓
  GOAL / INCENTIVE reached
         ↓
  NEED REDUCED (relief) ──→ new need arises later ──┐
         ↑______________________________________________┘
```

If the goal is blocked, frustration results (Unit 2).

## 7.3 Types of motives

**By source:**

- **Intrinsic motivation:** the activity is rewarding in itself (a nurse who enjoys caring).
- **Extrinsic motivation:** an outside reward or pressure (salary, marks, praise, fear of punishment).

**By origin:**

| Biological (primary, physiological) motives | Social / special (secondary, acquired, psychological) motives |
| --- | --- |
| Hunger | Achievement – to succeed, excel |
| Thirst | Affiliation – to belong, be with others |
| Need for oxygen | Power – to influence and control |
| Sleep and rest | Approval and recognition |
| Temperature regulation | Curiosity and exploration |
| Elimination | Self-esteem and status |
| Avoidance of pain | Security |
| Sex drive | Self-actualisation |
| Maternal drive | Aggression |

Biological motives are inborn and keep the body in balance (**homeostasis**). Social motives are learned through upbringing and culture.

## 7.4 Theories of motivation

**1. Maslow's hierarchy of needs** — needs are arranged in a pyramid; lower needs must be reasonably met before higher ones become strong motivators.

```text
               /\
              /SA\        Self-actualisation – reaching one's full potential
             /----\
            /Esteem\      Self-respect, achievement, recognition
           /--------\
          / Love and \    Belonging, family, friends, acceptance
         / belonging  \
        /--------------\
       /    Safety      \  Security, protection, freedom from fear
      /------------------\
     /   Physiological    \ Food, water, oxygen, sleep, elimination
    /______________________\
```

Nursing use: priority setting — airway, breathing and circulation first; then safety; then psychological and social needs. A patient in pain cannot attend to health teaching.

**2. Instinct theory (William McDougall):** behaviour is driven by inborn instincts, each with an emotion. Criticised because naming an instinct does not explain behaviour.

**3. Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull):** a need creates a drive; behaviour aims to reduce the drive and restore homeostasis.

**4. Incentive theory:** external goals pull behaviour (rewards, praise).

**5. Arousal theory:** people seek an optimum level of arousal. The **Yerkes–Dodson law** says performance is best at a moderate level of arousal; too little (boredom) or too much (panic) lowers performance.

```text
Performance
   ▲      ___
   │    /     \
   │  /         \
   │/             \
   └────────────────► Arousal
   low   moderate   high
```

**6. McClelland's need theory:** achievement, affiliation and power motives.

**7. Herzberg's two-factor theory (work):** hygiene factors (pay, conditions) prevent dissatisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) produce satisfaction — useful in nursing management.

## 7.5 Emotions

### Meaning

An **emotion** is a complex state of feeling that includes physiological arousal, a subjective experience (feeling), a mental appraisal and outward expression, and that prepares the person for action. Examples: fear, anger, joy, sadness, disgust, surprise, love.

### Components of emotion

| Component | Example in fear |
| --- | --- |
| Subjective feeling | "I am scared" |
| Physiological changes (mainly sympathetic) | Fast heart, raised BP, rapid breathing, dilated pupils, sweating, dry mouth, raised blood sugar, gooseflesh |
| Cognitive appraisal | "This is dangerous" |
| Expression / behaviour | Wide eyes, trembling, running away or freezing |

**Mood** is a milder, longer-lasting emotional state; **affect** is the observable expression of emotion.

### Theories of emotion (short note level)

| Theory | Idea |
| --- | --- |
| James–Lange | Bodily changes come first; the feeling is our perception of them ("we feel afraid because we tremble") |
| Cannon–Bard | The thalamus sends signals at the same time to the cortex (feeling) and the body (arousal) |
| Schachter–Singer (two-factor) | Arousal plus the cognitive label given to it produces the emotion |

### Development of emotions

- At birth, the infant shows **general excitement**.
- In the first months this separates into **distress** and **delight**; the social smile appears.
- During the first two years more specific emotions appear: anger, fear, disgust, joy, affection, jealousy.
- Self-conscious emotions (shame, guilt, pride) develop in the toddler and preschool years as the child becomes aware of self and rules.
- Emotional control develops through childhood with maturation, learning, imitation of parents and conditioning.
- Adolescence brings intense, changing emotions; adulthood usually brings greater stability and control.

Maturation (brain and glands) and learning (conditioning, imitation, training) both shape emotional development.

### Alteration of emotion

| Alteration | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Anxiety | Persistent fear and worry out of proportion to the threat |
| Depression | Persistent low mood, loss of interest, hopelessness |
| Euphoria / elation | Exaggerated happiness out of proportion (mania, some drugs) |
| Irritability | Easily angered |
| Labile affect | Rapid, unpredictable mood swings |
| Blunted or flat affect | Reduced or absent emotional expression |
| Inappropriate affect | Emotion not matching the situation (laughing at sad news) |
| Apathy | Lack of feeling and interest |

### Emotions in sickness

- Illness brings **fear** (of pain, procedures, diagnosis, death), **anxiety** about family and money, **anger** at loss of control, **guilt** (blaming self), **sadness and grief** for lost health or a body part, **shame** (stigmatised illnesses), and sometimes **denial**.
- Emotions affect the body: anxiety increases pain, delays sleep and healing; prolonged stress weakens immunity.
- Hospitalisation itself — strange place, loss of privacy, separation from family — is emotionally stressful.

### Handling emotions in self and others

**In self (the nurse):**

1. Recognise and name the emotion.
2. Pause before reacting (count, breathe slowly).
3. Express feelings appropriately to a colleague or supervisor rather than to the patient.
4. Use healthy outlets: exercise, hobbies, prayer or meditation, rest.
5. Reflect after difficult events (debriefing).
6. Seek help when distress persists.

**In others (patients, relatives):**

1. Notice verbal and non-verbal signs.
2. Accept the emotion without judging ("It is natural to feel afraid").
3. Listen actively; allow crying and ventilation of feelings.
4. Show empathy; use touch where culturally acceptable.
5. Give honest information to reduce fear of the unknown.
6. Help with problem-solving and coping.
7. Ensure safety if anger becomes aggression; stay calm, keep a safe distance, call for help.

## 7.6 Stress and adaptation

- **Stress** is the body's and mind's response to any demand that is felt to exceed a person's ability to cope.
- A **stressor** is anything that causes stress: physical (heat, injury, infection, noise), psychological (exams, fear), social (conflict, loss of job), and life events (death of spouse, marriage, divorce).
- **Eustress** = positive, motivating stress (preparing for a wedding, a challenge); **distress** = harmful stress.

### General adaptation syndrome (Hans Selye)

```text
Stressor → 1. ALARM REACTION → 2. RESISTANCE → 3. EXHAUSTION
           (shock, then         (body adapts,     (resources used up:
            countershock:        hormones high,    illness, collapse,
            fight or flight)     coping effort)    in extreme cases death)
```

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Alarm | Sympathetic activation and adrenaline; cortisol released; fast heart, raised BP and blood sugar |
| Resistance | Body tries to adapt; arousal stays high; if the stressor ends, recovery |
| Exhaustion | Prolonged stress drains resources; disease of adaptation (hypertension, ulcers, infections, depression) |

### The stress cycle (in daily life)

```text
Stressor → Appraisal ("Is this a threat? Can I cope?")
        → Stress response (body + mind) → Coping effort
        → Outcome: adaptation (stress reduced) or maladaptation (stress continues)
```

### Effects of stress

| Area | Effects |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Headache, palpitations, muscle tension, fatigue, acidity, poor sleep, lowered immunity |
| Emotional | Anxiety, irritability, anger, sadness, feeling overwhelmed |
| Cognitive | Poor concentration, forgetfulness, indecision, negative thinking |
| Behavioural | Over- or under-eating, withdrawal, smoking, alcohol, absenteeism, conflicts |

### Adaptation and coping

- **Adaptation:** the process of adjusting to a stressor so that balance is regained.
- **Coping:** the conscious efforts used to manage stress. Richard Lazarus described **primary appraisal** (is this a threat?) and **secondary appraisal** (what resources do I have?).
  - **Problem-focused coping:** change the situation (plan, seek information, act).
  - **Emotion-focused coping:** manage the feelings (relaxation, prayer, talking, reframing).
- **Healthy coping:** exercise, adequate sleep, balanced diet, relaxation (deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, yoga), time management, social support, humour, hobbies, counselling.
- **Unhealthy coping:** alcohol, tobacco, drug use, overeating, aggression, withdrawal, excessive defence mechanisms.

## 7.7 Attitudes

### Meaning and nature

An **attitude** is a learned, relatively lasting tendency to respond favourably or unfavourably towards a person, object, group, idea or situation.

**Components (ABC model):**

- **Affective:** feelings ("I am afraid of hospitals").
- **Behavioural:** tendency to act ("I avoid going to hospital").
- **Cognitive:** beliefs ("Hospitals are where people die").

**Nature:** attitudes are learned, not inborn; relatively stable but can change; have direction (positive/negative) and strength; relate to an object; influence perception and behaviour; may be held consciously or unconsciously.

### Factors affecting attitude formation

- Family (the earliest and strongest influence).
- Personal experience (a good or bad hospital visit).
- Peers and social groups.
- Education and information.
- Mass media and social media.
- Culture, religion and tradition.
- Conditioning and imitation of models.

### Attitudinal change

Attitudes change through:

- **New information and education** (health campaigns).
- **Persuasive communication:** credible source, clear message, suited to the audience.
- **Direct experience and contact** (meeting people with HIV reduces prejudice).
- **Cognitive dissonance (Leon Festinger):** when beliefs and actions conflict, the discomfort pushes a change in attitude or behaviour (a smoking nurse who knows its harm).
- **Group influence and role-play.**
- **Reward and punishment.**

Resistance to change is strong when an attitude is linked to identity, culture or strong emotion.

### Role of attitude in health and sickness

- Positive attitudes encourage health-seeking, early treatment, compliance and rehabilitation; negative ones lead to delay, refusal of vaccination, stopping medicines, stigma against mental illness, TB, leprosy or HIV.
- Patients' attitudes to illness (fatalism, hope) affect coping and recovery.
- **The nurse's attitude matters:** a caring, non-judgemental attitude builds trust; prejudice towards certain patients (alcohol users, people with mental illness) harms care. Nurses should examine their own attitudes.

## 7.8 Psychometric assessment of emotions and attitude – introduction

| What is measured | Tools (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| Attitudes | **Likert scale** (strongly agree … strongly disagree on a 5-point scale); Thurstone equal-appearing interval scale; **semantic differential** (pairs of opposites such as good–bad); Bogardus social distance scale |
| Anxiety | Self-report anxiety scales (for example the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory), clinician-rated anxiety scales |
| Depression | Self-report and clinician-rated depression scales; short screening questionnaires used in primary care |
| Emotional intelligence | EI questionnaires |
| Physiological signs of emotion | Heart rate, BP, galvanic skin response (sweating), respiration |
| Observation and interview | Facial expression, tone, posture |

Rating scales are only as good as their reliability and validity; use them as trained and report scores; they support but do not replace clinical judgement.

## 7.9 Role of the nurse in caring for the emotionally sick client

1. **Establish a therapeutic relationship** built on trust, respect and genuineness.
2. **Assess** mood, anxiety, sleep, appetite, risk of self-harm, and support systems; record and report.
3. **Active listening and empathy;** allow ventilation of feelings.
4. **Provide a safe, calm environment:** reduce noise, remove harmful objects if there is self-harm risk, observe as ordered.
5. **Meet basic needs** (Maslow): nutrition, hygiene, rest, which emotionally sick clients often neglect.
6. **Give simple, honest information** to reduce fear.
7. **Teach coping and relaxation skills;** encourage problem-solving.
8. **Encourage social contact** and meaningful activity.
9. **Involve and educate the family;** reduce stigma.
10. **Administer prescribed treatment** and observe its effects and side effects as per orders.
11. **Refer** to psychiatrist, psychologist or counsellor; give helpline information (Tele-MANAS 14416).
12. **Maintain confidentiality** and dignity.
13. **Care for self:** debrief, supervision, stress management.

## Remember

- Motivation cycle: need → drive → goal-directed behaviour → goal → need reduction.
- Biological motives (hunger, thirst, sleep, oxygen) vs social motives (achievement, affiliation, power).
- Maslow: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualisation.
- Yerkes–Dodson: best performance at moderate arousal.
- Emotion = feeling + physiological change + appraisal + expression; James–Lange, Cannon–Bard, Schachter–Singer.
- Selye's GAS: alarm → resistance → exhaustion; eustress vs distress.
- Coping: problem-focused vs emotion-focused.
- Attitude = affective + behavioural + cognitive; changed by information, contact, dissonance; measured by Likert, Thurstone, semantic differential.
