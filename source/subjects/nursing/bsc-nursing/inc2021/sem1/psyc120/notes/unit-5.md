# Unit 5: Cognitive processes I – attention, perception, intelligence and aptitude

INC Unit VI (Cognitive process) is the largest unit of the course (16 hours), so it is split into two notes files. This file covers attention, perception, intelligence (with mental deficiency) and aptitude. Unit 6 covers learning, memory, thinking, psychometric assessment of cognitive processes and alterations in cognition.

**Cognition** means all the mental processes by which we take in, store, change and use information: attending, perceiving, learning, remembering, thinking, reasoning and problem-solving.

## 5.1 Attention

**Attention** is the process of focusing consciousness on one object, idea or activity while ignoring others. It works like a torch: what is in the beam is clear, the rest is dim.

### Types of attention

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Involuntary (non-volitional) | Drawn automatically by a strong stimulus, without effort | Turning at a loud crash |
| Voluntary (volitional) | Deliberately directed with effort and will | Concentrating on a lecture |
| Habitual | Voluntary attention that has become automatic through habit and interest | A mother waking at her baby's faint cry; an ICU nurse noticing a monitor alarm |
| Selective | Picking one stimulus from many | Following one conversation in a noisy ward |
| Divided | Attending to two tasks at once | Talking to the patient while checking the drip rate |
| Sustained (vigilance) | Keeping attention over a long time | Watching an anaesthesia monitor |

### Determinants (factors) of attention

**External (objective) factors — in the stimulus:**

- Intensity and size (loud sound, bright light, big headline)
- Novelty (something new)
- Change and movement (flashing light)
- Repetition (an advertisement repeated)
- Contrast (red word on white page)
- Colour, position (top-left of a page) and duration

**Internal (subjective) factors — in the person:**

- Interest and motives (a hungry person notices food smells)
- Needs and drives
- Mental set or expectation (waiting for one's name to be called)
- Emotional state and mood
- Fatigue, illness, drugs, alcohol (reduce attention)
- Past experience and training

### Span, duration and degree of attention

- **Span of attention (span of apprehension):** the number of items a person can grasp in one brief glance — usually about six to seven separate items for adults; fewer for children.
- **Duration:** attention cannot stay perfectly steady; it fluctuates every few seconds and tires with time. In a lecture, attention drops after about 15–20 minutes unless the activity changes.
- **Degree:** attention can be full and concentrated or partial and scattered, depending on interest, health and distractions.
- **Distraction:** any stimulus that pulls attention away. Shifting of attention is normal; distractibility is excessive shifting.

### Alterations in attention

| Alteration | Description | Seen in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distractibility | Attention drawn by every trivial stimulus | Mania, delirium, anxiety |
| Inattention / poor concentration | Cannot focus | Depression, fatigue, ADHD |
| Hypervigilance | Over-alert, scanning for danger | Anxiety, PTSD, paranoia |
| Narrowing of attention | Focus only on one thing, misses others | Severe anxiety, pain |
| Clouding of consciousness | Reduced awareness | Delirium, head injury, drug effects |
| Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder | Inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity from childhood | Children, may continue into adult life |

### Nursing applications

- Health teaching: short sessions, one main point, use colour and demonstration, repeat, avoid noise.
- Pain: distraction (music, talking, play) shifts attention and reduces pain felt.
- Patient safety: reduce distraction during drug preparation (quiet zone), double-check.
- Note attention on mental state examination (ask the patient to count backwards).

## 5.2 Perception

**Perception** is the process of selecting, organising and interpreting sensations so that they have meaning. Sensation gives raw data; perception gives the meaning.

```text
Stimulus → Sensation (receptor) → Attention (selection)
        → Organisation → Interpretation (with past experience) → Meaningful percept → Response
```

Example: a nurse hears a sound (sensation), attends to it, recognises it as the pulse oximeter alarm (perception) and checks the patient (response).

### Principles of perceptual organisation (Gestalt laws)

| Principle | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Figure–ground | We see an object (figure) standing out from a background (ground) |
| Proximity | Items close together are seen as a group |
| Similarity | Similar items (shape, colour) are grouped |
| Closure | We fill gaps to see a complete figure |
| Continuity | We follow smooth, continuous lines |
| Simplicity (good form) | We see the simplest possible shape |

```text
Proximity:   ●●  ●●  ●●     → seen as three pairs
Closure:     ( _  _ )       → seen as a complete circle
```

### Perceptual constancy

We see objects as stable even when the image on the eye changes:

- **Size constancy:** a person walking away does not seem to shrink.
- **Shape constancy:** a door seen at an angle is still seen as rectangular.
- **Colour and brightness constancy:** a white sheet looks white in dim light.

**Depth perception** uses binocular cues (two eyes seeing slightly different images) and monocular cues (relative size, overlap, shading, linear perspective).

### Factors affecting perception

| Factor | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Characteristics of the stimulus | Clarity, intensity, familiarity |
| Attention and interest | We notice what interests us |
| Past experience and learning | An experienced nurse recognises subtle cyanosis |
| Motivation and needs | A thirsty person sees water everywhere |
| Mental set and expectation | Seeing what we expect to see |
| Emotions | Fear makes shadows look like threats |
| Culture | Interpretation of symptoms, gestures, colours |
| Physical state | Fever, fatigue, sensory loss, drugs, alcohol |
| Age | Children and older adults may misinterpret more |

### Errors of perception (alteration)

| Error | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Illusion | Misinterpretation of a real stimulus | A rope seen as a snake in dim light; a frightened, feverish child sees a shadow as a ghost |
| Hallucination | Perception without any external stimulus | Hearing voices when no one is speaking (schizophrenia, alcohol withdrawal, delirium) |
| Agnosia | Cannot recognise objects despite intact senses | After certain brain injuries |
| Depersonalisation / derealisation | Feeling of self or surroundings being unreal | Severe anxiety |

**Nursing role:** good lighting at night, reduce shadows and noise, orient confused patients, never argue with a hallucinating patient or pretend to share the experience — acknowledge the feeling ("I don't hear voices, but I can see you are frightened"), keep them safe and report.

## 5.3 Intelligence

### Meaning

There is no single agreed definition. In plain words, **intelligence** is the general mental ability to learn from experience, understand ideas, reason, solve problems and adjust to new situations. David Wechsler described it as the global capacity to act purposefully, think rationally and deal effectively with the environment.

### Theories (name in answers)

| Theorist | Theory |
| --- | --- |
| Charles Spearman | Two-factor theory: a general factor *g* in all tasks plus specific factors *s* for each task |
| L. L. Thurstone | Primary mental abilities: verbal meaning, word fluency, number, space, memory, perceptual speed, reasoning |
| J. P. Guilford | Structure of intellect: many abilities from operations × contents × products |
| Howard Gardner | Multiple intelligences: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, naturalistic |
| Robert Sternberg | Triarchic: analytical, creative, practical intelligence |
| Daniel Goleman (popularised) | Emotional intelligence: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy, social skills |

Emotional intelligence is especially useful for nurses: understanding and managing one's own feelings and responding to patients' feelings.

### Effect of heredity and environment on intelligence

- **Heredity:** twin studies show identical twins have closer intelligence scores than fraternal twins, even when reared apart; genetic conditions such as Down syndrome lower intelligence.
- **Environment:** nutrition (iodine, iron, protein), prenatal care, birth complications, stimulating home, schooling, poverty, language, culture, and emotional security all affect intelligence. Children moved from deprived to enriched homes show gains.
- **Conclusion:** heredity sets the upper and lower limits; environment decides where within these limits a person reaches. They interact; it is not "either–or".

### Measurement of intelligence – introduction

- **Alfred Binet** and Théodore Simon prepared the first practical intelligence test (1905) in France to identify children needing extra help in school. The idea of **mental age (MA)** came from this work.
- Lewis Terman revised it as the **Stanford–Binet** test and used the **intelligence quotient**:

```text
IQ = (Mental age ÷ Chronological age) × 100
Example: a 10-year-old who performs like an average 12-year-old
IQ = (12 ÷ 10) × 100 = 120
```

- Modern tests (Wechsler scales — WAIS for adults, WISC for children) use a **deviation IQ**: the person's score compared with others of the same age, with the average set at 100.
- Other tests: Raven's Progressive Matrices (non-verbal, culture-reduced); performance tests such as form boards; Indian tests and adaptations exist, such as Bhatia's battery of performance tests and Malin's adaptation of the Wechsler scale for Indian children.

### Classification of intelligence (commonly used IQ ranges)

| IQ | Classification |
| --- | --- |
| 130 and above | Very superior (gifted) |
| 120–129 | Superior |
| 110–119 | High average (bright normal) |
| 90–109 | Average |
| 80–89 | Low average (dull normal) |
| 70–79 | Borderline |
| Below 70 | Intellectual disability (mental deficiency) |

Ranges vary slightly between textbooks and tests; learn the pattern, with average around 90–110.

### Mental deficiency (intellectual disability)

The older term "mental deficiency" or "mental retardation" has been replaced by **intellectual disability**: significantly below-average intellectual functioning **together with** difficulty in everyday adaptive skills (self-care, communication, social skills), beginning before 18 years of age.

| Degree | Approximate IQ | Typical abilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild | 50–69 | Can learn basic reading and arithmetic, many live independently with some support |
| Moderate | 35–49 | Can learn self-care and simple work under supervision |
| Severe | 20–34 | Limited speech, needs help with most activities |
| Profound | Below 20 | Needs constant care |

**Causes:** genetic (Down syndrome, fragile X), prenatal (infections such as rubella, maternal alcohol use, iodine deficiency), perinatal (birth asphyxia, prematurity), postnatal (meningitis, head injury, severe malnutrition, hypothyroidism untreated), and social deprivation.

**Prevention and nursing role:** antenatal care, iodised salt, immunisation (rubella), safe delivery, newborn screening for hypothyroidism where available, early stimulation; early identification of developmental delay; training in self-care with small steps and rewards; support and counselling of parents; inform families about special education and disability benefits (check current schemes locally); protect from abuse.

## 5.4 Aptitude

**Aptitude** is a person's natural capacity or potential to learn a particular skill quickly and do well in it with training. Intelligence is general ability; aptitude is specific potential; achievement is what has already been learned.

| Term | Looks at |
| --- | --- |
| Intelligence | General mental ability |
| Aptitude | Future potential in a specific area |
| Achievement | Present level of learning |
| Interest | Liking for an activity |

### Types of aptitude

- Verbal (language), numerical, abstract reasoning, spatial (visualising shapes), mechanical, clerical (speed and accuracy), musical, artistic, scientific, teaching, manual dexterity, social (dealing with people).

**Aptitude tests:** for one area (special aptitude tests) or several (batteries, e.g. the Differential Aptitude Tests, General Aptitude Test Battery). Nursing entrance assessments often look at reasoning, language and interpersonal aptitude.

### Individual differences and variability

- People differ from each other (**inter-individual differences**) in intelligence, aptitude, personality and interests.
- One person's abilities also differ from area to area (**intra-individual differences**) — good at language, weak in mathematics.
- Abilities are distributed in the population in a **normal (bell-shaped) curve**: most people are near the average, few at the extremes.

```text
           frequency
              ▲        ___
              │      /     \
              │    /         \
              │___/           \___
              └─────────────────────► ability
               low   average    high
```

- **Causes of individual differences:** heredity, environment, age, sex-role training, education, socio-economic status, culture, health.
- **Uses for nurses:** teach patients at their level, use strengths in rehabilitation, place staff in suitable areas, avoid comparing children harshly.

## Remember

- Attention: involuntary, voluntary, habitual; determinants external (intensity, novelty, movement, contrast) and internal (interest, need, set).
- Span of attention ≈ six to seven items; attention fluctuates and tires.
- Perception = organising and interpreting sensations; Gestalt laws; constancy; illusion (real stimulus misread) vs hallucination (no stimulus).
- IQ = MA ÷ CA × 100; average 90–110; below 70 with adaptive problems = intellectual disability.
- Binet (first test, 1905), Terman (IQ), Wechsler (deviation IQ).
- Heredity sets limits; environment decides development within them.
- Aptitude = potential in a specific area; differences follow a normal curve.
