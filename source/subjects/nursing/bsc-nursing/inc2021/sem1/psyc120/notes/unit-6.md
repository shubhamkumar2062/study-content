# Unit 6: Cognitive processes II – learning, memory and thinking

This notes file is the second half of INC Unit VI. It covers learning and habit formation, memory and forgetting, thinking, reasoning and problem-solving, psychometric assessment of cognitive processes, and alterations in cognitive processes.

## 6.1 Learning

**Learning** is a relatively permanent change in behaviour (or in the potential for behaviour) that results from experience or practice. Changes due to growth, fatigue, illness or drugs are not learning.

Key words in the definition: *relatively permanent*, *change in behaviour*, *experience or practice*.

### Types of learning

**1. Classical conditioning (Ivan Pavlov)**

A neutral stimulus, after being paired many times with a stimulus that naturally produces a response, comes to produce that response by itself.

```text
Before:  Food (UCS) ──────────────→ Salivation (UCR)
         Bell (neutral) ──────────→ no salivation
During:  Bell + Food, repeated ───→ Salivation
After:   Bell (CS) ───────────────→ Salivation (CR)
```

- UCS = unconditioned stimulus, UCR = unconditioned response, CS = conditioned stimulus, CR = conditioned response.
- **Extinction:** the CR fades if the CS is given repeatedly without the UCS.
- **Spontaneous recovery:** an extinguished response returns briefly after rest.
- **Generalisation:** similar stimuli also produce the response; **discrimination:** responding only to the exact stimulus.
- **Nursing example:** a child who had painful injections cries at the sight of any white coat (generalisation). Anticipatory nausea before a chemotherapy session is another conditioned response.

**2. Trial-and-error learning (Edward Thorndike)**

A hungry cat in a puzzle box makes many random movements, accidentally opens the latch, and gets food; over trials, useless movements drop and the right one comes faster. Thorndike's laws:

- **Law of readiness:** learning is better when the learner is ready.
- **Law of exercise:** practice strengthens connections; disuse weakens them.
- **Law of effect:** responses followed by satisfaction are strengthened; those followed by discomfort are weakened.

**3. Operant (instrumental) conditioning (B. F. Skinner)**

Behaviour is shaped by its consequences.

| Consequence | Effect on behaviour | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive reinforcement (something pleasant added) | Increases | Praise when a patient walks with the walker |
| Negative reinforcement (something unpleasant removed) | Increases | Pain goes away after the patient changes position, so he repeats it |
| Punishment (unpleasant added or pleasant removed) | Decreases | Fine for late arrival |
| Extinction (no reinforcement) | Decreases | Ignoring attention-seeking tantrums |

**Shaping** = reinforcing small steps towards the final behaviour; used in rehabilitation and in training children with intellectual disability.

**4. Insight learning (Wolfgang Köhler)**

Sudden understanding of the relationship between parts of a problem ("aha!"). Köhler's chimpanzee Sultan joined two sticks to reach a banana outside the cage. Insight learning is quick, remembered well and transferred to new problems.

**5. Observational (social) learning (Albert Bandura)**

Learning by watching others (models) and imitating them. Bandura's "Bobo doll" studies showed children copy aggressive behaviour they observe. Steps: attention → retention → reproduction → motivation. Student nurses learn many skills by watching senior nurses — so seniors must model correct practice.

### Factors influencing learning

| Learner factors | Material / method factors | Environment factors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age and maturation | Meaningfulness of material | Quiet, well-lit, ventilated room |
| Intelligence and aptitude | Length and difficulty | Freedom from distraction |
| Motivation and interest | Spaced vs massed practice | Teacher's attitude |
| Physical health, fatigue, sleep | Whole vs part method | Group atmosphere |
| Emotional state, anxiety | Use of senses (audiovisual aids) | Time of day |
| Previous knowledge | Feedback and reinforcement | Culture and language |

### The learning process and learning curve

```text
Motive (need) → Goal → Activity (trying) → Reinforcement → Learning → Fixation (practice)
```

- A **learning curve** plots progress against practice. It often rises quickly at first, then flattens into a **plateau** (no apparent progress for a while, due to fatigue, loss of interest or the need to combine sub-skills), then rises again with continued practice.
- **Transfer of learning:** learning in one situation helps (positive transfer) or hinders (negative transfer) learning in another. Learning aseptic technique for dressing helps in learning catheterisation (positive transfer).

### Habit formation

A **habit** is an acquired behaviour pattern that, through repetition, becomes automatic and is performed with little conscious effort.

**Forming a good habit (classic maxims, often attributed to William James):**

1. Start with a strong, clear decision and announce it.
2. Never allow an exception until the habit is firmly fixed.
3. Act on every opportunity to practise.
4. Practise regularly at the same time and place (cue).
5. Reward yourself for each success.

**Breaking a bad habit:** become aware of the cue, avoid triggers, replace with an alternative behaviour, gradual reduction or sudden stop as suitable, remove reward, get support and keep a record.

**Nursing value:** good habits (hand hygiene, checking patient identity, documentation) protect patients; health teaching aims at new habits — exercise, medication timing, tobacco cessation.

## 6.2 Memory

**Memory** is the mental ability to encode, store and later retrieve information and experiences.

### Stages of memory

```text
Encoding (registration) → Storage (retention) → Retrieval (recall/recognition)
```

### Types (stores) of memory

| Store | Capacity | Duration | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sensory memory | Large | Less than 1–2 seconds | Brief trace of what the senses received; lost unless attended |
| Short-term (working) memory | About 7 ± 2 items | About 20–30 seconds without rehearsal | Holding a phone number while dialling; improved by chunking |
| Long-term memory | Practically unlimited | Minutes to a lifetime | Needs meaningful encoding and rehearsal |

Long-term memory has kinds:

- **Explicit (declarative):** episodic (personal events — "my first injection") and semantic (facts — normal pulse rate).
- **Implicit (non-declarative):** procedural (skills — riding a bicycle, giving an injection), conditioned responses.

### Measuring memory (methods of retention)

- **Recall:** reproduce information without cues (essay answer).
- **Recognition:** identify the correct item from choices (MCQ) — easier than recall.
- **Relearning (savings) method:** time saved in learning the material again.
- **Reconstruction:** rearranging items into their original order.

### Factors influencing memory

- Attention and interest at the time of learning.
- Meaningfulness and organisation of material.
- Repetition, revision and overlearning.
- Spacing of practice; sleep after learning.
- Emotional state: mild arousal helps, severe anxiety hinders.
- Health: fatigue, illness, alcohol, some drugs, head injury and ageing reduce memory.
- Interference from similar material.

### Methods to improve memory

1. **Understand** before memorising; link new facts to what you know.
2. **Organise** in headings, tables, flow charts.
3. **Chunking:** group items (a 10-digit number in 3–3–4 groups).
4. **Mnemonics:** acronyms (OCEAN for Big Five), rhymes, visual images.
5. **Spaced revision** rather than cramming; revise within a day, a week, a month.
6. **Recitation / self-testing:** close the book and recall.
7. **Overlearning:** practise beyond the first correct recall.
8. **Use many senses:** read, write, speak, draw.
9. **Whole method** for short meaningful material, **part method** for long material.
10. **Sleep, exercise, nutrition** and avoiding stress.

**Teaching patients:** older or anxious patients remember little of what is said in a busy clinic, so give one or two key points, repeat, give written or picture instructions, ask them to repeat back ("teach-back"), and involve the family.

### Forgetting

**Forgetting** is the loss or inability to retrieve information that was once learned. Hermann Ebbinghaus showed that forgetting is fastest soon after learning and then slows (the **curve of forgetting**) — the reason for early revision.

```text
Retention
100% ●
     │ \
     │  \
     │    \___
     │        ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾────── 
     └──────────────────────► time (hours → days)
```

**Causes (theories) of forgetting:**

| Theory | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Decay (disuse) | Memory traces fade if not used |
| Interference – proactive | Old learning interferes with new (old phone number blocks new one) |
| Interference – retroactive | New learning interferes with old |
| Retrieval failure | Information is stored but cannot be accessed without the right cue ("tip of the tongue") |
| Motivated forgetting / repression | Painful memories pushed out of awareness |
| Organic causes | Brain injury, dementia, alcohol, ECT, ageing |

Forgetting is not always bad: it clears unnecessary detail and painful experiences.

## 6.3 Thinking

**Thinking** is the mental manipulation of information — images, symbols, concepts and language — to understand, decide, plan and solve problems. It is "behaviour carried on inside the mind".

### Elements (tools) of thinking

Images, concepts, symbols and signs, language, and propositions (statements).

### Types of thinking

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Perceptual (concrete) thinking | Based on objects present; seen in young children |
| Conceptual (abstract) thinking | Using ideas and concepts not physically present |
| Reflective thinking | Careful, logical thinking to solve a problem |
| Creative thinking | Producing new and original ideas |
| Critical thinking | Questioning, analysing evidence, judging before accepting — essential in nursing |
| Convergent thinking | Arriving at one correct answer |
| Divergent thinking | Producing many possible answers (creativity) |
| Autistic (fantasy) thinking | Daydreaming, wish-fulfilling, not reality-based |

### Levels of thinking

Thinking develops from the **perceptual** level (things present) to the **conceptual** level (ideas and classes) to the **abstract or symbolic** level (principles, hypotheses) — matching Piaget's stages.

### Reasoning

- **Inductive reasoning:** from specific observations to a general rule ("Several patients with these signs had dengue; this pattern suggests dengue").
- **Deductive reasoning:** from a general rule to a specific case ("All patients on bed rest are at risk of pressure sores; Mr A is on bed rest; so Mr A is at risk").

### Problem-solving

Steps:

1. Identify and define the problem.
2. Collect information.
3. Form possible solutions (hypotheses).
4. Evaluate and choose the best.
5. Act.
6. Evaluate the result and modify if needed.

These steps are the same as the **nursing process**: assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation and evaluation.

**Strategies:** trial and error, algorithms (step-by-step rules that always work, such as a protocol), heuristics (rules of thumb, quick but may err), insight.

**Barriers:** mental set (sticking to one approach), functional fixedness (seeing objects only in their usual use), lack of information, emotional stress, fatigue, bias.

## 6.4 Psychometric assessment of cognitive processes – introduction

Tests that measure cognitive functions:

| Function | Examples of tools |
| --- | --- |
| Overall cognition (screening) | Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), clock-drawing test, bedside orientation questions |
| Intelligence | Wechsler scales, Stanford–Binet, Raven's matrices, Indian adaptations |
| Memory | Wechsler Memory Scale; Indian memory scales such as the PGI Memory Scale |
| Attention and concentration | Digit span, serial subtraction (100 − 7), letter cancellation tests |
| Aptitude | Differential Aptitude Tests |
| Neuropsychological | Batteries testing language, planning, visuo-spatial ability after brain injury |

Screening tests are quick and can be used by trained nurses; detailed tests are given and interpreted by psychologists.

**Simple bedside cognitive assessment by a nurse:** orientation to time, place and person; attention (spell a word backwards, serial sevens); immediate and delayed recall of three words; simple commands; judgement ("What would you do if you smelt smoke?").

## 6.5 Alterations in cognitive processes

| Process | Alteration | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consciousness / orientation | Disorientation; clouding; delirium (acute, fluctuating confusion) | Post-operative older adult, high fever, infection |
| Attention | Distractibility, poor concentration | Anxiety, mania |
| Perception | Illusions, hallucinations | Delirium, schizophrenia, alcohol withdrawal |
| Memory | Amnesia — anterograde (cannot form new memories after an event) and retrograde (loss of memories before it); confabulation (filling gaps with made-up stories) | Head injury; alcohol-related memory disorder |
| Thinking – form and stream | Flight of ideas, thought block, poverty of thought, perseveration | Mania, schizophrenia, dementia |
| Thinking – content | Delusions (fixed false beliefs not shared by culture), obsessions (unwanted repeated thoughts) | Psychosis, obsessive-compulsive disorder |
| Intelligence | Intellectual disability (from childhood); dementia (acquired decline in adults) | Down syndrome; Alzheimer's disease |
| Judgement and insight | Poor judgement, lack of insight into illness | Severe mental illness, frontal lobe damage |

### Nursing role in altered cognition

- Assess and record orientation, attention and memory regularly; report sudden change (delirium is a medical emergency).
- Reorient: clocks, calendars, name boards, family photos, introduce yourself each time.
- Keep a safe environment: bed rails as per policy, night light, remove hazards, supervision.
- Simple, short instructions, one at a time; give time to respond.
- Routine and the same caregivers reduce confusion.
- Memory aids: lists, pill organisers, alarms.
- Do not argue with delusions; do not agree with them either; focus on feelings and reality.
- Support and teach the family.

## Remember

- Learning = relatively permanent change in behaviour through experience.
- Pavlov (classical), Thorndike (trial and error; laws of readiness, exercise, effect), Skinner (operant; reinforcement), Köhler (insight), Bandura (observation).
- Plateau = flat part of the learning curve; transfer of learning positive or negative.
- Memory: encoding → storage → retrieval; STM about 7 ± 2 items, about 20–30 seconds.
- Ebbinghaus: forgetting is fastest soon after learning — revise early and spaced.
- Interference: proactive (old blocks new), retroactive (new blocks old).
- Problem-solving steps = nursing process.
- Delirium (acute, fluctuating) vs dementia (gradual, progressive).
