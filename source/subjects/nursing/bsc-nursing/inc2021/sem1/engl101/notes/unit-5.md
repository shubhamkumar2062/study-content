# Unit 5: Reading — strategies, medical vocabulary, abbreviations, visuals, reports and remedial grammar

This unit covers INC Unit V (5 hours). It teaches how to read quickly and accurately — notes, messages, articles, charts, patient documents, doctors' prescriptions of care and reports — and revises the grammar points that most often cause errors.

## 5.1 Why reading accuracy matters

A nurse reads all day: case sheets, orders, medicine labels, lab reports, previous notes, policies and books. Misreading one letter or digit can lead to a wrong patient, wrong drug, wrong dose or wrong time. The rule is simple: **if you cannot read it clearly or do not understand it, ask — never guess.**

## 5.2 Reading strategies

| Strategy | What you do | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Skimming** | Read quickly for the main idea: title, headings, first and last lines of paragraphs, bold words | Deciding if an article is useful; getting the gist of a circular |
| **Scanning** | Run your eyes over the text looking for one specific item — a name, number, date, word | Finding a patient's allergy, a result, a phone extension |
| **Intensive reading** | Read slowly and carefully, every word, checking meaning | Orders, consent forms, procedures, exam questions |
| **Extensive reading** | Read longer texts for general understanding and pleasure | Novels, magazines, news — builds vocabulary and speed |
| **Critical reading** | Question the text: who wrote it, evidence, bias, fact or opinion | Health news, advertisements, research articles |

### The SQ3R method for study reading

1. **Survey** — look over headings, pictures, summaries.
2. **Question** — turn headings into questions ("What are the barriers to communication?").
3. **Read** — read actively to answer the questions.
4. **Recite** — say or write the answers without looking.
5. **Review** — go over the whole chapter again later.

### Guessing meaning from context

When you meet an unknown word, look at the words around it before reaching for a dictionary.

> "The patient was **lethargic**; she slept most of the day and responded slowly when spoken to."
> The sentence explains the word: lethargic = very drowsy and slow.

Clues: definitions ("that is", "which means"), examples ("such as"), contrast ("but", "unlike"), and word parts (prefix, root, suffix — Unit 2).

## 5.3 Reading notes and messages

Notes and messages are short and often abbreviated. Read for: **who** it is from and for, **what** is needed, **when** (deadline), and **any action** for you.

> *Note left at the nurses' station:* "Sr. Mary — Bed 14 relatives want to meet the doctor re: discharge. Dr. on OT till 2. Pls inform after 2 pm. — Ravi (8 am shift)"
> Meaning: Bed 14's family wants to talk to the doctor about discharge; the doctor is in the operation theatre until 2 pm; tell the doctor after 2 pm.

## 5.4 Reading articles and news items

- Read the **headline** and **first paragraph** — news puts the most important facts first (the inverted pyramid).
- Find the **5 Ws and 1 H**: who, what, where, when, why, how.
- Separate **facts** (numbers, dates, named sources) from **opinions** (adjectives, "experts feel", "it is believed").
- Check the **source** and **date** — health information changes; prefer official health department and journal sources over forwarded messages.
- Summarise in two or three sentences of your own.

## 5.5 Vocabulary for everyday activities and medical vocabulary

Words for daily care that patients and nurses use:

| Activity | Useful words |
| --- | --- |
| Hygiene | bed bath, sponge bath, mouth care, oral hygiene, shampoo, perineal care, nail care |
| Elimination | bedpan, urinal, commode, pass urine (void), motion/stool, constipation, incontinence |
| Mobility | turn, reposition, sit up, dangle legs, walk (ambulate), wheelchair, walking frame |
| Nutrition | diet, feed, appetite, swallow, sips, fluids, nasogastric tube |
| Comfort | pillow, backrest, side rails, call bell, blanket, fan |

Medical word building (prefix + root + suffix) is in Unit 2. Practise with matching exercises: match *nephrectomy* → removal of a kidney, *gastroscopy* → looking into the stomach, *dyspnoea* → difficult breathing.

## 5.6 Abbreviations

Hospitals keep an **approved abbreviation list**; use only those. Common ones:

| Abbreviation | Meaning | Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| BP | blood pressure | I/O | intake and output |
| TPR | temperature, pulse, respiration | NBM / NPO | nil by mouth |
| SpO2 | oxygen saturation by pulse oximeter | Stat | immediately |
| IV / IM / SC | intravenous / intramuscular / subcutaneous | PRN / SOS | when required |
| PO | by mouth | HS | at bedtime |
| BD / TDS / QID | twice / three times / four times a day | OD | once a day (but also "right eye" — ambiguous) |
| CBC | complete blood count | ECG | electrocardiogram |
| Hb | haemoglobin | ICU / OPD / OT | intensive care unit / outpatient department / operation theatre |
| c/o | complains of | h/o | history of |
| Dx / Rx / Tx | diagnosis / prescription, treatment / treatment | k/c/o | known case of |

**Dangerous abbreviations** — many hospitals forbid these because they are easily misread:

- "U" for units (can look like 0 or 4) — write "units".
- "IU" (can look like IV or 10) — write "international units".
- "QD" and "QOD" (daily and every other day are confused) — write "daily", "every other day".
- A trailing zero ("5.0" can be read as 50) — write "5".
- A missing leading zero (".5" can be read as 5) — write "0.5".
- Abbreviated drug names (two drugs may share the same letters) — write the full name.

If an abbreviation in an order has two possible meanings, **clarify with the prescriber before acting**.

## 5.7 Understanding visuals: graphs, tables, figures and flow sheets

Steps for reading any visual:

1. Read the **title** — what does it show?
2. Read the **labels and units** on each axis or column (°C, beats/min, ml, hours).
3. Note the **time scale** (hourly, 4-hourly, daily).
4. Look for the **trend**: rising, falling, steady, fluctuating.
5. Note **highest and lowest** values and anything **outside the normal range**.
6. Say in one or two sentences what it means and what action is needed.

```text
TEMPERATURE CHART (Bed 11)        °C
  39 |            *
  38 |       *         *
  37 | *  *                 *   *
     +-----------------------------
       6am 10am 2pm 6pm 10pm 2am 6am
Reading: temperature rose from 37 °C to a peak of 39 °C at 2 pm,
then fell back to 37 °C by 10 pm and stayed there overnight.
```

**Tables** — read across rows and down columns; check the row and column headings meet at the right cell.

**Flow sheets** — sheets on which repeated observations are entered at set times (vital-sign chart, intake–output chart, diabetic chart, neurological observation chart, fluid balance). Read the latest entry, then compare with earlier entries for change.

**Figures and diagrams** — read the caption and labels; connect each part to the text.

**Notes on instructions** — equipment labels and package inserts: read warnings, storage conditions, expiry date and steps in order.

## 5.8 Reading the patient document

The **case sheet / patient record** usually contains: identification data (name, age, sex, hospital number, ward, bed), admission notes and history, doctor's progress notes, **orders**, medicine administration record, nursing notes, vital-sign and intake–output charts, investigation reports, consent forms, and discharge summary.

When reading, check:

- **Right patient** — name and hospital number on every page.
- **Allergies** — usually flagged on the front.
- **Latest entries** — date and time order.
- **Legibility and signatures** — an unsigned or unclear order is clarified before acting.

## 5.9 Reading the doctor's prescription of care

A complete medicine order contains:

1. Patient's full name and identification.
2. Date and time of the order.
3. Name of the medicine (generic name preferred), written clearly.
4. Dose and units.
5. Route.
6. Frequency or exact times.
7. Duration or stop date (or "until review").
8. Special instructions (before food, after food, with water).
9. Prescriber's name and signature.

Other orders: diet ("soft diet", "diabetic diet"), activity ("bed rest", "ambulate"), position, investigations, observations ("hourly BP"), wound care.

The nurse checks the order against the **rights of medication administration** (right patient, drug, dose, route, time, and right documentation) — studied in detail in Nursing Foundations and Pharmacology. From an English point of view: read every word, check every abbreviation, and ask when in doubt.

## 5.10 Reading reports and interpreting them

**Laboratory report** — read: patient identifiers, test name, the patient's result, units, **reference (normal) range** column, and flags (H for high, L for low, often in bold or a different colour). Compare with the previous result to see the trend. Critical results are reported to the doctor at once.

**Radiology or other reports** — have two parts: **findings** (what was seen) and **impression / conclusion** (what it means). Read the impression first, then the findings.

**Incident or departmental reports** — find the event, time, people involved, action taken and recommendations.

Interpretation means not just reading the words but saying what they mean for the patient: "The haemoglobin is lower than the reference range and lower than last week's report — the doctor should be informed."

## 5.11 Idioms and phrases

Idioms are expressions whose meaning is not the sum of their words. Use them sparingly in speech; avoid them in records and with patients who may not understand them.

| Idiom / phrase | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| under the weather | slightly unwell | He's feeling under the weather today. |
| on the mend | recovering | The wound is on the mend. |
| back on one's feet | recovered | She'll be back on her feet in a week. |
| a bitter pill to swallow | an unpleasant fact to accept | Losing his job was a bitter pill to swallow. |
| in a nutshell | in brief | In a nutshell, the patient is stable. |
| keep an eye on | watch carefully | Keep an eye on his drip. |
| touch and go | uncertain, risky | It was touch and go during the night. |
| on duty / off duty | working / not working | I'm on night duty this week. |
| at the eleventh hour | at the last moment | The surgery was postponed at the eleventh hour. |
| break the ice | start a friendly conversation | Small talk helps break the ice with new patients. |
| hit the nail on the head | say exactly the right thing | Your answer hit the nail on the head. |
| burn the midnight oil | work late at night | Students burn the midnight oil before exams. |

## 5.12 Vocabulary for presentations

- Opening: "I'd like to talk about…", "The aim of my talk is…"
- Structuring: "firstly", "secondly", "moving on to", "let's now look at"
- Referring to visuals: "As you can see in this chart…", "This graph shows…", "The figures here indicate…"
- Describing trends: rise, increase, go up, peak; fall, decrease, drop, decline; remain steady, level off, fluctuate; sharply, gradually, slightly.
- Concluding: "To sum up…", "The key message is…", "Thank you for your attention."

## 5.13 Remedial grammar and spotting errors

### Articles

- **a** before a consonant **sound**, **an** before a vowel **sound**: a patient, a university (/j/ sound), an injection, an hour (silent h), an MRI ("em").
- **the** for something specific or already mentioned: "I gave her a tablet. **The** tablet was for pain."
- No article with general plural or uncountable nouns: "Nurses work hard." "Water is essential."

### Prepositions

| Correct | Common error |
| --- | --- |
| suffering **from** fever | suffering with fever |
| admitted **to** hospital **on** Monday **at** 6 pm | admitted in hospital at Monday |
| allergic **to** penicillin | allergic from penicillin |
| discuss the plan | discuss **about** the plan |
| cope **with** stress | cope up with stress |
| reply **to** the letter | reply the letter |
| comply **with** the rules | comply to the rules |

### Subject–verb agreement

- "Each of the patients **has** a call bell." (each = singular)
- "The team **is** ready." / "Neither the nurse nor the doctors **were** informed." (verb agrees with the nearer subject)
- "Mathematics / news **is**…"; "Scissors **are**…".

### Common Indian English usages to avoid in formal writing

| Avoid | Use |
| --- | --- |
| revert back | reply / reply to |
| do the needful | please take the necessary action (say what) |
| prepone | bring forward |
| return back | return |
| I am having fever | I have a fever |
| Myself Anu | I am Anu / My name is Anu |
| one of my friend | one of my friends |
| He is knowing | He knows |
| cousin brother | cousin |

### Spotting errors — practice with answers

1. "The patient have been vomiting since morning." → **has** been vomiting.
2. "She is suffering with diabetes since five years." → has been suffering **from** diabetes **for** five years.
3. "I have given the injection yesterday." → I **gave** the injection yesterday.
4. "Please discuss about the plan with the family." → discuss the plan.
5. "He is an unique case." → **a** unique case (/j/ sound).
6. "Each patients need a name band." → Each **patient needs**.
7. "The datas shows improvement." → The **data show** improvement.

## Remember

- Never guess an unclear order or abbreviation — ask.
- Skim for the main idea, scan for a detail, read intensively for orders and forms; SQ3R for study.
- Guess word meaning from context and word parts.
- Use only approved abbreviations; avoid U, IU, QD, QOD, trailing zeros and missing leading zeros.
- Visuals: title → labels and units → time scale → trend → highs/lows → meaning.
- A complete medicine order: patient, date/time, drug, dose, route, frequency, duration, instructions, signature.
- Lab report: result, units, reference range, flags, trend.
- Remedial grammar: articles by sound, correct prepositions, subject–verb agreement, no "discuss about", "cope up", "revert back".
