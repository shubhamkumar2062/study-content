# Unit 2: Introduction to LSRGW — listening, speaking sounds, medical vocabulary, grammar and simple writing

This unit covers INC Unit II (5 hours). LSRGW stands for **L**istening, **S**peaking, **R**eading, **G**rammar and **W**riting. It also covers the language points named in the course competencies: pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, paraphrasing, voice modulation, spelling, pause and silence.

## 2.1 L — Listening: different types of listening

**Hearing** is the physical act of sound reaching the ear. **Listening** is the active mental process of attending to, understanding, remembering and responding to what is heard. A nurse may hear a monitor all night without listening to it; she listens when she notices the rate has changed.

| Type of listening | Purpose | Clinical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discriminative | Telling sounds apart | Noticing a change in a patient's breathing sound or a different alarm tone |
| Comprehensive (informational) | Understanding and remembering facts | Listening to a lecture or a handover |
| Critical (evaluative) | Judging whether a message is sound or true | Deciding if a medicine advertisement makes believable claims |
| Empathetic (therapeutic) | Understanding feelings from the speaker's point of view | Listening to a mother anxious about her baby's surgery |
| Appreciative | Enjoying | Listening to music or a good speech |
| Selective | Hearing only what interests us (a fault) | Catching the time of the next class but missing the room change |
| Passive | Hearing without responding or engaging | Half-listening while writing notes |
| Active | Full attention, with verbal and non-verbal responses and checking | Nodding, paraphrasing and asking a question in a patient interview |

Practice material for this unit: news bulletins, hospital announcements, telephone conversations and instructions. While listening, note **who, what, where, when, how much** and any **number**.

## 2.2 S — Speaking: the sounds of English

English spelling and English sounds do not match one-to-one. "Cough", "though", "through" and "rough" all end in *-ough* but sound different. So we learn **sounds (phonemes)**, written in phonetic symbols between slashes, for example /p/.

Standard British English (Received Pronunciation, the model used in most Indian textbooks) has **44 sounds: 24 consonants and 20 vowels (12 pure vowels and 8 diphthongs).**

### Consonants

A **consonant** is a sound made when the air is stopped or narrowed somewhere in the mouth or throat.

| Group | Sounds | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plosives (stops) — air stopped then released | /p/ /b/ /t/ /d/ /k/ /g/ | pain, blood, temperature, dose, cough, gauze |
| Fricatives — air squeezed through a narrow gap | /f/ /v/ /θ/ /ð/ /s/ /z/ /ʃ/ /ʒ/ /h/ | fever, vein, thermometer, the, swab, zinc, shock, measure, heart |
| Affricates — stop + friction | /tʃ/ /dʒ/ | chest, jaundice |
| Nasals — air through the nose | /m/ /n/ /ŋ/ | mouth, nurse, lung |
| Lateral | /l/ | liver |
| Approximants (semi-vowels) | /r/ /w/ /j/ | rest, ward, yellow |

**Voiced** consonants make the vocal cords vibrate (/b/, /d/, /v/, /z/); **voiceless** ones do not (/p/, /t/, /f/, /s/). Put your fingers on your throat while saying "vvv" and "fff" to feel the difference.

Common Indian learner difficulties and fixes:

- **/v/ and /w/** — "vein" (top teeth on lower lip) vs "wound" (rounded lips, no teeth).
- **/s/ and /ʃ/** — "sip" vs "ship"; "sore" vs "shore". Saying "sugar" correctly needs /ʃ/.
- **/z/** — "zinc", "dose" (/s/) vs "doze" (/z/).
- **/θ/ and /ð/** — "thermometer", "three" (tongue between the teeth, no voice); "the", "this" (with voice). Avoid replacing them with /t/ or /d/.
- **Silent letters** — psychology, pneumonia, psoriasis (silent p); knee, knife (silent k); haemorrhage, rhythm (silent h after r); mortgage (silent t); honest (silent h).

### Vowels

A **vowel** is a sound made with the air flowing freely through the mouth; the tongue and lips shape it.

- **Short pure vowels**: /ɪ/ (bit), /e/ (bed), /æ/ (cat), /ʌ/ (cup), /ɒ/ (hot), /ʊ/ (put), /ə/ (the first sound of "about" — called **schwa**).
- **Long pure vowels**: /iː/ (feed), /ɑː/ (heart), /ɔː/ (ward), /uː/ (food), /ɜː/ (nurse).
- **Diphthongs** (a glide from one vowel to another): /eɪ/ (pain), /aɪ/ (eye), /ɔɪ/ (boil), /əʊ/ (dose), /aʊ/ (mouth), /ɪə/ (ear), /eə/ (care), /ʊə/ (pure).

Length changes meaning: "ship" /ɪ/ vs "sheep" /iː/; "full" /ʊ/ vs "fool" /uː/. The **schwa** is the most common sound in English because most unstressed syllables use it: a**bout**, nur**ses**, **hos**pit**al**.

## 2.3 Word stress

In every English word of two or more syllables, one syllable is said **louder, longer and higher** — the stressed syllable. Wrong stress can make a correct word hard to understand.

| Word | Stressed syllable | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **HOS**-pi-tal | 1st | |
| **PA**-tient | 1st | Not "pa-TIENT" |
| **ME**-di-cine | 1st | A common Indian slip is "me-DI-cine" |
| **TEM**-pe-ra-ture | 1st | |
| di-ag-**NO**-sis | 3rd | |
| hy-per-**TEN**-sion | 3rd | Words ending in *-sion*, *-tion* stress the syllable before |
| in-**JEC**-tion | 2nd | Same *-tion* rule |
| a-**NA**-ly-sis / a-na-**LY**-ti-cal | moves | Stress can move when the word changes form |
| **PRE**-sent (noun) / pre-**SENT** (verb) | 1st / 2nd | Also **RE**-cord / re-**CORD**, **IN**-crease / in-**CREASE** |

Simple rules (with exceptions):

1. Most two-syllable nouns and adjectives stress the first syllable: **NUR**ses, **DOC**tor, **HAP**py.
2. Most two-syllable verbs stress the second: re**PORT**, ad**MIT**, ex**PLAIN**.
3. Words ending in *-tion, -sion, -ic, -ical, -ity, -ian* stress the syllable just before the ending: in-**FEC**-tion, pae-di-**A**-tric, ac-**TI**-vi-ty, phy-**SI**-cian.
4. A dictionary marks stress with ˈ before the stressed syllable: /ˈtemprətʃə/.

## 2.4 Sentence stress and rhythm

In a sentence, **content words** carry stress; **function words** are usually weak and quick.

- Content words: nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs, question words, negatives (not, never).
- Function words: articles (a, the), prepositions (in, at), auxiliaries (is, have, can), pronouns, conjunctions.

Example: "The **PAtient** in **BED FOUR** has a **HIGH FEver**."

Moving the stress changes the meaning (contrastive stress):

- "**I** gave the medicine at 8." (not someone else)
- "I gave the medicine at **8**." (not at 9)

English is **stress-timed**: stressed syllables come at roughly regular intervals and the weak ones are squeezed between them. Many Indian languages are syllable-timed (every syllable gets equal time), which is why Indian English can sound fast or flat to outsiders. Weakening the function words makes speech clearer, not less clear.

## 2.5 Intonation

**Intonation** is the rise and fall of the voice (pitch) across a sentence. It shows the type of sentence and the speaker's attitude.

| Pattern | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Falling ↘ | Statements, commands, *wh*-questions (what, where, when) | "Your test is at ten. ↘" "Where is the pain? ↘" |
| Rising ↗ | Yes/no questions, checking, showing interest | "Are you comfortable? ↗" "Did you sleep? ↗" |
| Fall-rise ↘↗ | Doubt, politeness, something left unsaid | "It's not too bad… ↘↗ (but I'm worried)" |
| Rise-fall ↗↘ | Strong feeling, surprise | "Really! ↗↘" |
| List intonation | Rise on each item, fall on the last | "Bring the gauze ↗, the tape ↗ and the scissors ↘." |

A flat or falling tone on "Can I help you?" can sound bored or rude; a gentle rise sounds caring.

## 2.6 Voice modulation, pause and silence

**Voice modulation** is changing the voice to suit the message and the listener. Its parts:

- **Pitch** — high or low. Lower, steady pitch sounds calm and confident.
- **Volume** — loud enough to be heard, never shouting; softer for private matters.
- **Pace (rate)** — slower for instructions, older people, anxious patients and numbers; slower still in a second language.
- **Tone** — warm, firm, urgent, sympathetic as the situation needs.
- **Emphasis** — stressing key words ("do **not** eat after midnight").

**Pause** — a short break in speech. It separates ideas, lets the listener think, highlights what comes next, and gives the speaker time to breathe. Pause after giving a number, a time or an instruction.

**Silence** — deliberately saying nothing. In therapeutic communication, a few seconds of silence after a patient shares bad news gives them time to feel and to continue. Silence must be attentive (with eye contact and open posture), not awkward or cold. Too long a silence in a formal talk, however, looks like forgetting.

## 2.7 R — Reading: medical vocabulary

Most medical words are built from Greek and Latin parts. Learn the parts and you can work out thousands of words.

```text
   PREFIX      +      ROOT (combining form)     +      SUFFIX
   brady-             card(i)/o                         -ia
   (slow)             (heart)                           (condition)
   → bradycardia = condition of slow heart rate
```

| Prefix | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a-, an- | without | anaemia, apnoea |
| brady- | slow | bradycardia |
| tachy- | fast | tachypnoea |
| dys- | difficult, painful | dysphagia, dyspnoea |
| hyper- | above, excess | hypertension |
| hypo- | below, deficient | hypoglycaemia |
| peri- | around | pericardium |
| endo- | within | endoscopy |
| post- / pre- | after / before | postoperative, prenatal |
| poly- | many | polyuria |

| Root | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cardi(o) | heart | cardiology |
| gastr(o) | stomach | gastritis |
| hepat(o) | liver | hepatomegaly |
| nephr(o), ren- | kidney | nephritis, renal |
| neur(o) | nerve | neurology |
| oste(o) | bone | osteoporosis |
| pulmon(o), pneum(o) | lung | pulmonary, pneumonia |
| derm(at)(o) | skin | dermatitis |
| haem(at)(o) | blood | haematuria |
| cyst(o) | bladder | cystitis |

| Suffix | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -itis | inflammation | appendicitis |
| -ectomy | surgical removal | appendicectomy |
| -otomy | cutting into | tracheotomy |
| -ostomy | making an opening | colostomy |
| -algia | pain | neuralgia |
| -megaly | enlargement | splenomegaly |
| -scopy | looking inside with an instrument | bronchoscopy |
| -emia / -aemia | blood condition | septicaemia |
| -uria | urine condition | haematuria |
| -plasty | surgical repair | rhinoplasty |

Use a **medical dictionary or glossary**: check the meaning, the pronunciation (stress mark) and the plural. Some plurals are irregular: diagnosis → diagnoses, bacterium → bacteria, vertebra → vertebrae, ovum → ova, nucleus → nuclei, criterion → criteria.

## 2.8 Gr — Grammar: tenses

A **tense** shows the time of an action. English has three times (present, past, future), each with four aspects (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous) — 12 forms.

| Tense | Form | Nursing example | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simple present | V1 / V1+s | The patient **complains** of headache. | Facts, habits, current state |
| Present continuous | is/am/are + V-ing | The drip **is running** well. | Action happening now |
| Present perfect | has/have + V3 | She **has vomited** twice since morning. | Past action linked to now; no exact time |
| Present perfect continuous | has/have been + V-ing | He **has been coughing** for three days. | Action that began in the past and continues |
| Simple past | V2 | The doctor **saw** the patient at 10 am. | Finished action at a stated time |
| Past continuous | was/were + V-ing | She **was sleeping** when I checked. | Action in progress at a past moment |
| Past perfect | had + V3 | The fever **had settled** before the doctor came. | The earlier of two past actions |
| Past perfect continuous | had been + V-ing | He **had been bleeding** for an hour before admission. | Duration up to a past moment |
| Simple future | will + V1 | I **will check** the dressing at 2 pm. | Decision, promise, prediction |
| Future continuous | will be + V-ing | We **will be shifting** her to the ward at noon. | Action in progress at a future time |
| Future perfect | will have + V3 | The drip **will have finished** by 6 pm. | Completed before a future time |
| Future perfect continuous | will have been + V-ing | By tomorrow he **will have been fasting** for 12 hours. | Duration up to a future time |

Rules nurses often break:

- Use the **simple past** with a stated past time: "I **gave** the injection at 8 am" — not "I have given the injection at 8 am".
- Nurses' notes are written in the **past tense** for what was observed and done ("Patient **ate** half of lunch"); the **present** for the present state ("Patient **is** drowsy but rousable").
- Third person singular takes **-s** in the simple present: "The patient **feels** dizzy" (not "feel").
- Irregular verbs: give–gave–given, take–took–taken, eat–ate–eaten, sleep–slept–slept, bleed–bled–bled, feel–felt–felt.

## 2.9 Linkers (connectives)

Linkers join ideas and show how they relate. They make writing and speech coherent.

| Purpose | Linkers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Addition | and, also, in addition, moreover, furthermore | The wound is clean; moreover, there is no swelling. |
| Contrast | but, however, although, whereas, on the other hand | Although she slept, she still feels tired. |
| Cause | because, since, as, due to | He was restless because of pain. |
| Result | so, therefore, as a result, hence | His pulse rose; therefore, I informed the doctor. |
| Sequence | first, then, next, after that, finally | First wash your hands, then put on gloves. |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as | Use a soft diet, for example khichdi or porridge. |
| Condition | if, unless, provided that | Call me if the pain increases. |
| Summary | in short, to sum up, overall | In short, the patient is stable. |

Punctuation note: **however** and **therefore** in the middle of a sentence need a semicolon before and a comma after: "The patient was febrile; however, the pulse was normal."

## 2.10 W — Writing simple sentences and short paragraphs

A **simple sentence** has one subject and one main verb and expresses one complete idea: "The patient slept well."

Word order in English: **Subject – Verb – Object – Place – Time**: "The nurse (S) checked (V) the blood pressure (O) in the ward (P) at 6 am (T)."

Common faults:

- **Fragment** (incomplete sentence): "Because the patient was in pain." → "The patient could not sleep because he was in pain."
- **Run-on**: "The patient was in pain he could not sleep." → split with a full stop or a linker.
- **Subject–verb agreement**: "The list of patients **is** on the table" (the subject is "list").

A **paragraph** develops one idea: a **topic sentence** (main idea), **supporting sentences** (facts, examples) and a **concluding sentence**.

Model paragraph:

> Hand washing is the simplest way to prevent infection in hospital. Germs spread easily from one patient to another on the hands of staff. Washing with soap and water, or rubbing with an alcohol hand rub, removes most of these germs. Nurses should clean their hands before and after touching a patient, before a clean procedure and after contact with body fluids. If every member of the team follows this habit, infections in the ward fall sharply.

## 2.11 Paraphrasing and spelling

**Paraphrasing** means expressing someone's meaning in your own words without changing it. It is used to check understanding ("So you are saying the pain wakes you at night?"), to explain medical terms to patients, and to write assignments without copying.

- Original (doctor): "Keep the patient nil by mouth after midnight; surgery is planned for tomorrow."
- Paraphrase to the patient: "Please don't eat or drink anything after 12 tonight, because your operation is tomorrow."

Steps: read or listen until you understand → set the source aside → say it in your own words → compare to make sure the meaning is the same.

**Spelling** in records must be correct — a wrong spelling can change a medicine or a diagnosis. Words often misspelled: diarrhoea, haemorrhage, oesophagus, pneumonia, rhythm, temperature, separate, receive, occurred, accommodate, necessary, privilege, guarantee, unconscious. Indian records generally follow **British spelling**: haemoglobin (American: hemoglobin), oedema (edema), paediatric (pediatric), colour (color), centre (center), programme (program). Be consistent within one document.

## Remember

- LSRGW = listening, speaking, reading, grammar, writing.
- Hearing is passive; listening is active. Types: discriminative, comprehensive, critical, empathetic, appreciative, (selective, passive, active).
- 44 sounds in RP English: 24 consonants, 20 vowels (12 pure + 8 diphthongs). Schwa /ə/ is the commonest vowel.
- Word stress: nouns usually first syllable, verbs second; *-tion/-sion/-ic* words stress the syllable before.
- Content words are stressed; function words are weak. Falling intonation for statements and *wh*-questions, rising for yes/no questions.
- Voice modulation = pitch, volume, pace, tone, emphasis; pause separates ideas; silence gives space.
- Medical words = prefix + root + suffix.
- 12 tenses; nurses' notes use past tense for actions and present for current state.
- Linkers make writing coherent; paraphrase = same meaning, own words; use British spelling consistently.
