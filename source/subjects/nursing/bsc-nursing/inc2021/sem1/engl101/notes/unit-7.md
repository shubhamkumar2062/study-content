# Unit 7: LSRW skills in combination — critical thinking, oral reports, presentations, instructions and error analysis

This unit covers INC Unit VII (8 hours). Real work never uses one skill alone: a nurse **listens** to a patient, **reads** the chart, **speaks** to the doctor and **writes** the note — often in ten minutes. This unit brings the four skills together and adds critical thinking, group discussion, role play and the analysis of errors.

## 7.1 Integrating the four skills

```text
   LISTEN to the patient's complaint
        |
        v
   READ the chart, orders and previous notes
        |
        v
   THINK: what does this mean? what is the priority?
        |
        v
   SPEAK: report to the doctor / explain to the patient (SBAR, plain words)
        |
        v
   WRITE: document findings, action and response
        |
        v
   LISTEN again: feedback, new orders (read back)
```

Example — an admission:

1. **Listen** to the patient and family give the history; note key facts and the patient's own words.
2. **Read** the referral letter, previous reports and the admission orders.
3. **Speak** to welcome the patient, explain the ward routine, and report findings to the doctor.
4. **Write** the admission assessment, nurses' notes and care plan.

## 7.2 Critical thinking strategies for listening and reading

**Critical thinking** is careful, purposeful thinking that questions information, examines evidence and reaches reasoned judgements instead of accepting things at face value. Nurses use it every time they decide whether a finding is normal, urgent or needs action.

### Questions a critical listener or reader asks

| Question | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Who is the source? Are they qualified and reliable? | Credibility |
| What is the main claim? | Understanding |
| What evidence supports it — data, studies, observation? | Evidence |
| Is it fact or opinion? | Objectivity |
| Is anything missing, exaggerated or one-sided? | Bias |
| Is it current? | Relevance |
| Does it agree with other trusted sources? | Verification |
| What follows from it — what should I do? | Application |

### Fact, opinion and inference

- **Fact** — can be checked or measured: "The temperature is 38.5 °C."
- **Opinion** — a personal belief or judgement: "This hospital has the best nurses."
- **Inference** — a conclusion drawn from facts: "Her temperature is high and she has chills, so she may have an infection."

Signal words of opinion: *best, worst, should, I believe, clearly, everyone knows, amazing*. Be careful with health messages forwarded on social media that use these words without evidence.

### Evaluating options and decisions

When there are several possible answers, compare them:

| Option | Advantages | Disadvantages | Risk to patient |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | | | |
| B | | | |

Choose the option that is **safest for the patient**, supported by **evidence and policy**, and **practical**. Then explain the decision in clear language.

**Situational activity** — "A patient's relative asks you to give the patient a herbal remedy he brought from home." Options: give it, refuse rudely, or explain politely that only prescribed medicines can be given and inform the doctor so it can be discussed. The third is safest and most respectful.

## 7.3 Oral reports

An **oral report** presents information, findings or an account to listeners — at handover, rounds, a staff meeting or in class.

Structure:

1. **Opening** — topic and purpose.
2. **Main body** — facts in logical order (time order, priority order or SBAR).
3. **Closing** — conclusion, recommendation, invitation to questions.

Qualities: brief, accurate, organised, clear voice, numbers given slowly, no unnecessary detail, confirms understanding.

Model oral report at a staff meeting:

> "Good morning. This is the infection-control report for March on our ward. We had 42 admissions. Two patients developed urinary infections linked to catheters. In both cases the catheters had been in place for more than five days. I recommend a daily check of every catheter with the question 'Is it still needed?' and recording the answer in the notes. Any questions?"

## 7.4 Presentations

(See Unit 4 for delivery.) In this unit, presentations are combined with reading and writing:

- **Read** sources critically and choose reliable content.
- **Write** an outline, slide text (key phrases, not paragraphs) and speaker notes.
- **Speak** to the audience with signposting.
- **Listen** to questions and feedback; respond thoughtfully.

A good health-education talk for patients: one clear message, local language support when needed, pictures or demonstrations, a return demonstration, and a short handout.

## 7.5 Writing instructions

Instructions tell a reader how to do something safely and correctly — for patients (home care), for colleagues (how to use equipment) or for students (how to do a task).

Rules:

1. A clear **title** that says what the instructions are for.
2. A list of **what is needed** first.
3. **Numbered steps** in the right order.
4. Each step starts with an **action verb** in the imperative ("Wash", "Check", "Press").
5. **One action per step**; short sentences.
6. **Warnings** in a separate line and highlighted ("Do not…", "Stop and call the nurse if…").
7. **Plain words** for patients; pictures where helpful.
8. End with **what to do if there is a problem** and a contact.

Model — instructions for a patient going home:

> **How to care for your wound at home**
>
> You will need: soap, clean water, a clean towel.
>
> 1. Wash your hands with soap and water before touching the dressing area.
> 2. Keep the dressing clean and dry.
> 3. Bathe carefully; do not soak the wound in water until the doctor says it is healed.
> 4. Look at the wound every day.
> 5. Eat a healthy diet with enough protein and drink plenty of water unless the doctor has told you to limit fluids.
> 6. Come to the dressing clinic on the date written on your card.
>
> **Contact the hospital at once if** the wound becomes red, swollen, very painful, smells bad, leaks pus, or you get a fever.

Check understanding with **teach-back**: "To make sure I explained clearly, can you tell me what you'll look for each day?"

## 7.6 Letters and reports in combination

Many tasks need all four skills: a nurse **listens** to a complaint, **reads** the record to check facts, **speaks** to the person to resolve it, and **writes** a report or reply letter. Formats are in Unit 6; the critical-thinking step is to check facts before writing and to keep the tone objective and polite.

## 7.7 Group discussion

A **group discussion (GD)** is a structured conversation where a group shares views on a topic to reach understanding or a decision. It is used in teaching, team meetings and job selection.

Skills assessed: content knowledge, clarity, logical reasoning, listening to others, leadership, teamwork, body language.

Do's:

- Start if you have a clear point; otherwise listen first.
- Support points with facts and examples.
- Listen and build on others' ideas: "Adding to what Ravi said…"
- Disagree politely: "I see it differently because…"
- Bring a drifting group back: "Coming back to the topic…"
- Summarise at the end if asked.

Don'ts: shouting, interrupting, dominating, personal remarks, staying silent throughout, going off-topic.

Useful phrases: "I'd like to begin by…", "In my view…", "May I add something?", "Could you explain what you mean by…?", "To sum up our discussion…".

## 7.8 Role play

**Role play** is acting out a situation to practise communication safely before meeting it in real life. Common nursing role plays:

- Admitting a new patient and orienting the family.
- Explaining a procedure and getting cooperation.
- Handling an angry relative who has waited long.
- Giving handover with SBAR.
- Teaching a mother about oral rehydration at home.
- A telephone call to a doctor about a deteriorating patient.

After each role play, the group discusses: what went well, what language was effective, what could be said differently.

## 7.9 Demonstration

A **demonstration** shows how to do something while explaining it. The nurse uses all skills: reads the procedure, explains aloud step by step with rationale, watches the learner, listens to questions, and asks for a **return demonstration** (the learner does it back). This is the main way patients learn skills such as using an inhaler or checking blood sugar at home.

## 7.10 Error analysis regarding LSRW

**Error analysis** means identifying your errors, finding their cause and correcting them systematically. Errors are normal while learning; the aim is to notice and reduce them.

| Skill | Common errors | Cause | Correction strategy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Listening | Missing numbers, mishearing names, getting only part of the message | Distraction, unfamiliar accent, no notes | Note while listening, read back, ask for repetition, practise with news and recordings |
| Speaking | Wrong word stress (meDIcine), /v/–/w/ confusion, mother-tongue sentence patterns, too fast, flat intonation | First-language influence, nervousness | Listen to good models, record and replay yourself, practise stress patterns, slow down, pause |
| Reading | Misreading similar words or numbers, skipping words, guessing abbreviations | Haste, poor handwriting in the source | Read orders twice, check abbreviations, clarify unclear writing, use a dictionary |
| Writing | Tense errors, subject–verb agreement, wrong prepositions, spelling, run-on sentences, missing time and signature | Translation from first language, hurry | Plan, re-read before signing, keep a personal error list, learn standard formats |

### Types of errors

- **Grammatical** — "The patient have pain" → "has pain".
- **Lexical (vocabulary)** — wrong word: "The patient's pressure is very bad" → "The patient's blood pressure is very high".
- **Pronunciation** — "wein" for "vein".
- **Spelling** — "temprature" → "temperature".
- **Pragmatic (appropriateness)** — correct English but wrong for the situation: "Sit down!" to an elderly patient → "Please have a seat."
- **Discourse** — ideas out of order; a handover that starts with minor details and ends with the critical change.

A **personal error log** helps: write each error you make, the correct form, and one example sentence. Review it weekly.

### Error-analysis exercise with answers

Find and correct the errors in this nurses' note:

> "Patient is complaining of severe headache since morning, he is vomited two times. Doctor was informed and he told to give medicine. Patient now feel better."

Corrected:

> "**11.00 am** — Patient has been complaining of severe headache since 7 am. He has vomited twice (about 100 ml each, clear fluid). Dr Gupta informed at 10.15 am; medicine given as ordered (see medicine chart). **11.00 am:** patient says the headache is better, 3/10. — *Signature, name, designation*"

Errors fixed: tense (*is complaining since* → *has been complaining since*), run-on sentence, "is vomited" → "has vomited", vague timing and amount made specific, doctor's name and time added, "told to give medicine" made precise without guessing, subject–verb agreement (*feel* → *says … is better*), time and signature added.

## 7.11 Consolidated practice: one clinical situation, four skills

**Situation:** A diabetic patient on your ward tells you he feels shaky and sweaty before lunch.

- **Listen:** "I'm shaking, sweating, and I feel strange." Note his exact words and that it is before lunch.
- **Read:** check the chart — when he last ate, when his last medicine was given, his last blood sugar.
- **Think:** these signs suggest low blood sugar — a priority.
- **Speak:** reassure him; inform the doctor using SBAR; follow the hospital's protocol for low blood sugar.
- **Write:** time, symptoms in his words, blood sugar reading, action taken, doctor informed, and the response 15 minutes later.
- **Teach:** later, give him clear written and spoken instructions on recognising and preventing low sugar at home, and check with teach-back.

## Remember

- Real nursing uses LSRW together: listen → read → think → speak → write → listen again.
- Critical thinking: question the source, evidence, bias, fact vs opinion, currency; choose the safest evidence-based option.
- Oral report: opening, ordered facts, conclusion; brief and accurate.
- Instructions: title, things needed, numbered imperative steps, warnings, contact; check with teach-back.
- Group discussion: contribute facts, listen, build on others, disagree politely, stay on topic.
- Role play and demonstration (with return demonstration) build confidence.
- Error analysis: identify → find the cause → correct → keep a personal error log. Types: grammatical, lexical, pronunciation, spelling, pragmatic, discourse.
