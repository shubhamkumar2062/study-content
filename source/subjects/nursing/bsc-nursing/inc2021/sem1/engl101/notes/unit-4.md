# Unit 4: Speaking — effective conversation, telephone talk, handover and presentations

This unit covers INC Unit IV (9 hours), the largest unit. It gives the phrases and patterns a nurse needs to speak well with patients, relatives, colleagues and doctors, face to face and on the telephone, and to present cases and give handover.

## 4.1 Conversation situations: formal, informal and neutral

| Situation | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Formal** | Polite forms, full sentences, titles (Sir, Madam, Dr), no slang, set structure | Speaking to the Principal, a ward round, a viva, an interview |
| **Neutral** | Polite but relaxed; standard English; common in work | Talking to a patient, a colleague from another ward, a pharmacist |
| **Informal** | Short forms, first names, casual words | Chatting with a classmate or a close friend |

The same request at three levels:

- Formal: "Would you mind checking the drug chart for Bed 5, Sister?"
- Neutral: "Could you check the drug chart for Bed 5, please?"
- Informal: "Can you look at Bed 5's chart?"

**Register** is the choice of words and tone suited to the situation. Using an informal register with a senior or a patient's elderly relative can sound disrespectful; using a very formal register with a frightened child can sound cold.

## 4.2 Factors influencing the way we speak

1. **Setting** — place and time: an ICU at night needs a low voice; a classroom presentation needs a clear, carrying voice.
2. **Topic** — sensitive topics (death, sex, money, diagnosis) need gentle, private, careful language.
3. **Social relationship** — age, rank, familiarity: a senior consultant, a new patient, a peer.
4. **Attitude** — of the speaker and listener: respect, anger, anxiety, impatience change tone and words.
5. **Language** — shared language, vocabulary level, accent; use plain words with patients and technical words with professionals.

Other influences: culture (eye contact, touch, use of first names), the listener's health (hearing loss, pain, confusion), and the channel (face to face vs telephone).

## 4.3 Everyday speaking functions with useful phrases

### Greetings and introductions

- "Good morning, Mr Sharma. My name is Priya. I'm the nurse looking after you today."
- Introducing others: "Dr Mehta, this is Mrs Fernandes, the patient's daughter."
- Replying: "Pleased to meet you." / "Nice to meet you too."
- Leaving: "I'll be back at ten to check your drip. Press the call bell if you need me."

Always say your **name and role** and confirm the **patient's name** (two identifiers such as name and date of birth or hospital number before procedures).

### Requesting

- Polite: "Could you…, please?" "Would you mind…ing?" "I wonder if you could…"
- Example: "Would you mind turning onto your left side, please?"
- Replying: "Of course." / "Sure." / "I'm sorry, I can't right now, but…"

### Asking for and giving permission

- Asking: "May I check your blood pressure?" "Is it all right if I open the window?" "Do you mind if I sit here?"
- Giving: "Yes, of course." "Please go ahead."
- Refusing politely: "I'm afraid visitors are not allowed in the ICU now; visiting time is 4 to 6 pm."

Asking permission before touching or examining is part of respect and consent.

### Speaking personally and casual conversation

Small talk builds trust and gives clues about the patient's life: "How was your night?" "Is your family visiting today?" "What kind of work do you do?" Keep it brief, respectful and focused on the patient; avoid gossip, personal opinions on religion or politics, and sharing details of other patients.

### Asking for information

- Open: "Can you tell me about the pain?" "What happened before you fell?"
- Closed: "Do you have any allergies?" "Did you pass urine today?"
- Indirect (softer): "Could you tell me when you last ate?"

### Giving instructions and directions

Instructions — use the **imperative**, one step at a time, with sequence words:

> "First, wash your hands with soap. Then take the strip out of the box. Next, prick the side of your fingertip. After that, place the drop of blood on the strip. Finally, note the reading in your diary."

Directions — give landmarks and turns:

> "Go straight down this corridor, take the lift to the second floor, turn left, and the X-ray department is the second door on your right, opposite the pharmacy."

Check understanding: "Could you tell me how you'll do it at home?"

### Agreeing, disagreeing and giving opinions

| Function | Phrases |
| --- | --- |
| Giving an opinion | In my opinion…, I think…, I feel…, As far as I can see… |
| Agreeing | I agree., That's right., Exactly., I think so too. |
| Partly agreeing | That's true, but…, I agree up to a point, however… |
| Disagreeing politely | I'm not sure I agree., I see your point, but…, With respect, I think… |
| Asking for opinions | What do you think?, How do you feel about…? |

Disagreeing politely with a senior about patient safety is a duty, not rudeness: "Doctor, I'm concerned — the patient's BP is low; could you please review before we give the next dose?"

### Describing people, places, events and things

- **People**: age, build, height, clothes, distinguishing marks, behaviour. "An elderly man, about 70, thin, wearing a white kurta, walks with a stick."
- **Places**: location, size, layout, condition. "The isolation room is at the end of the ward; it has its own toilet and an anteroom."
- **Events**: what, when, where, who, how, result.
- **Things**: size, shape, colour, material, use. "A clear plastic bag with a tube and a clamp, used to collect urine."
- **Clinical descriptions**: a wound (site, size in cm, colour, discharge, smell), sputum (colour, amount, consistency), pain (site, type, severity, duration).

### Narrating, reporting and reaching conclusions

- **Narrating** — telling events in order with past tenses and time linkers: "At 2 am the patient woke up confused. He tried to climb out of bed. I raised the side rails and stayed with him, and then I informed the duty doctor."
- **Reporting** — passing on what others said (reported speech): "The patient said that he had not slept." "The doctor asked me to recheck the sugar at 6."
- **Reaching conclusions** — "From these readings, it seems that…", "So we can conclude that…", "Overall…".

Reported speech changes: present → past ("I am tired" → he said he **was** tired); *will* → *would*; *today* → *that day*; *here* → *there*.

### Evaluating and comparing

- Comparatives and superlatives: "Her pain is **less** today **than** yesterday." "This dressing is **more comfortable than** the old one." "This is **the best** position for her breathing."
- Weighing options: "On one hand… on the other hand…", "The advantage of… is…, but the drawback is…"

### Complaints and suggestions

**Receiving a complaint** (from a patient or relative) — five steps:

1. **Listen** without interrupting.
2. **Empathise**: "I understand how frustrating it is to wait so long."
3. **Apologise** for the experience (without blaming others): "I'm sorry this happened."
4. **Resolve** or explain what you will do: "I'll find out about the report and come back to you within half an hour."
5. **Follow up** and inform the in-charge if needed.

**Making a complaint** (for example about equipment) — be specific and calm: "The suction machine in Bed 3 isn't working; this is the second time this week. Could it be repaired today?"

**Suggestions** — "Why don't we…?", "How about…?", "It might help if…", "Shall we…?", "I suggest that…"

## 4.4 Telephone conversations

Telephone talk has no face or gestures, so voice and clarity matter more.

**Making a call**

1. Plan what you will say; keep the chart and a pen ready.
2. Greet and identify yourself and your area: "Good evening, this is Staff Nurse Anu from Medical Ward 3."
3. Check you have the right person: "Am I speaking to Dr Rao?"
4. State the purpose briefly (use SBAR for a clinical call — see 4.6).
5. Note the reply and **read back** any order or result.
6. Close politely: "Thank you, doctor. I'll do that now."
7. Document the call: time, person, message, response.

**Receiving a call**

- Answer promptly: "Medical Ward 3, Staff Nurse Anu speaking. How can I help you?"
- Take messages with: date and time, caller's name and number, message, action needed, your name.
- Never give confidential patient information to an unidentified caller; follow hospital policy.

**Clear telephone speech**: speak slowly; spell names using a phonetic alphabet ("R for Romeo, A for Alpha…"); say numbers digit by digit; avoid eating or side conversations; hold the receiver close.

Model message slip:

```text
Telephone message
Date/time: 12 March, 3.15 pm
For: Ward Sister, Surgical Ward
From: Mr Iqbal, Blood Bank, ext. 245
Message: Two units for Bed 18 (Mrs Kavita) are ready for collection.
Action: Send a staff member with the requisition slip.
Taken by: N. Joseph
```

## 4.5 Talking to patients, attenders and other professionals

**To patients and attenders** — use plain language, short sentences, no jargon; explain before acting; check understanding by teach-back; respect privacy; keep promises about time.

| Jargon | Plain words |
| --- | --- |
| NPO / nil by mouth | Nothing to eat or drink |
| Ambulate | Walk |
| Void | Pass urine |
| Hypertension | High blood pressure |
| Intravenous | Into a vein |

**To other professionals** — be brief, factual, organised, and use correct medical terms and units.

## 4.6 Handoff (handover) and SBAR

**Handover (handoff)** is the transfer of responsibility and information about a patient from one nurse or team to another at shift change, transfer between units, or for a procedure. Errors at handover are a major cause of missed care, so a **standard structure** is used.

### SBAR

| Letter | Meaning | What to say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S** | Situation | Who you are, which patient, what the problem is now |
| **B** | Background | Diagnosis, date of admission, relevant history, treatment so far |
| **A** | Assessment | Current findings: vital signs, observations, your judgement of the problem |
| **R** | Recommendation (Request) | What needs to be done, by whom, by when; what you are asking for |

Some hospitals use **ISBAR** (adding **I**dentify yourself and the patient first).

**Model SBAR call to a doctor**

> **S:** "Dr Rao, this is Staff Nurse Anu from Medical Ward 3. I'm calling about Mr Prakash Singh, Bed 9. His breathing has become fast in the last hour."
> **B:** "He is 58, admitted two days ago with pneumonia, on intravenous antibiotics and oxygen by nasal prongs."
> **A:** "His respiratory rate is 30 per minute, oxygen saturation has dropped to 89% on oxygen, pulse 118, temperature 38.6 °C, BP 110/70. He is anxious and using his neck muscles to breathe. I think his breathing is getting worse."
> **R:** "Could you please come and see him now? Should I increase the oxygen or prepare anything before you arrive?"
> (Doctor gives instructions; the nurse **reads them back**.)

**Model bedside shift handover (morning to afternoon)**

> "This is Mrs Radha Iyer, 45, Bed 4, second day after an abdominal hysterectomy. She has no known allergies. Overnight and this morning her vital signs were stable — last at 12 noon: temperature 37.2 °C, pulse 84, BP 120/80, respirations 18, pain score 3 out of 10 after her pain relief at 10 am. The wound dressing is dry and intact. She passed urine after the catheter was removed at 8 am — 300 ml at 11 am. She has started oral fluids and tolerated a light breakfast. She walked to the bathroom with help. Outstanding: blood test report for haemoglobin is awaited; next pain relief due at 4 pm if needed; she is anxious about going home and would like to talk to the dietitian. Any questions?"

Good handover is: at the bedside where possible (the patient can join), structured, up to date, focused on **changes, risks and pending tasks**, confidential, and ends with a chance to ask questions.

## 4.7 Reporting in doctors' and nurses' rounds

On rounds, the nurse reports briefly for each patient:

1. Name, bed, diagnosis, day of stay or day after surgery.
2. Overnight events and current condition (vital signs, intake–output, pain, sleep, wound).
3. Investigations done and results received.
4. Problems and concerns of the patient and nurse.
5. Questions for the doctor (clarify orders, discharge plans).

Speak clearly, use the chart, and note new orders immediately; confirm any order you are unsure about before the team leaves.

## 4.8 Presenting a situation in emergency and routine

**Emergency** — short, loud enough, facts first, clear request:

> "Emergency in Bed 7! Patient unresponsive, not breathing normally. Starting compressions — call the resuscitation team and bring the crash cart."

Use closed-loop communication: the leader gives a named instruction ("Ritu, bring the defibrillator"), the person repeats it ("Bringing the defibrillator") and confirms when done.

**Routine** — fuller, calmer, structured (SBAR or a ward-round report), with time for questions.

## 4.9 Case presentation

A **case presentation** is a structured oral report about one patient, given to teachers and peers.

Outline:

1. **Introduction** — patient's initials (keep identity private), age, sex, ward, date of admission, diagnosis.
2. **Chief complaints** with duration.
3. **History** — present illness, past, family, personal and social history.
4. **Physical examination** findings (head to toe, positive findings first).
5. **Investigations** and their results.
6. **Medical/surgical management** — the treatment plan (no need to recite every dose).
7. **Nursing assessment, nursing diagnoses, care plan** and evaluation.
8. **Health education** given and discharge plan.
9. **Summary** and questions.

Language tips: use past tense for history ("He was admitted on…"), present for current state ("He is now afebrile"); use signposts ("Moving on to the investigations…"); speak to the audience, not to your notes.

## 4.10 Delivering presentations

**Preparation**

- Know your **purpose** and **audience** (classmates, patients, a community group).
- Collect content; choose **3–5 main points**.
- Structure: **introduction** (greet, topic, why it matters, outline) → **body** (main points with examples) → **conclusion** (summary, key message, thank you, questions).
- Visuals: simple slides or charts — few words per slide, large font, one idea each; pictures and models help health talks.
- Rehearse aloud and time yourself.

**Delivery**

- Stand straight, smile, make eye contact around the room.
- Speak slowly and clearly, vary pitch and pause after key points.
- Use gestures naturally; avoid reading slides word for word.
- Handle questions: listen fully, repeat the question, answer briefly; if you don't know: "That's a good question; I'll find out and let you know."

**Useful signposting phrases**: "Good morning, everyone. Today I'm going to talk about…", "I've divided my talk into three parts…", "Let's start with…", "Moving on to…", "This brings me to my last point…", "To sum up…", "Thank you for listening. Are there any questions?"

## 4.11 Face-to-face oral communication — model nurse–patient conversation

> **Nurse:** "Good morning, Mr Das. I'm Meena, your nurse today. Could you tell me your full name and date of birth, please?"
> **Patient:** "Arun Das, fifth June, 1967."
> **Nurse:** "Thank you. The doctor has asked for a blood sample this morning. It's to check your sugar and kidney function. May I take it now?"
> **Patient:** "Will it hurt?"
> **Nurse:** "You'll feel a small prick, like an ant bite, for a second. I'll be quick and gentle."
> **Patient:** "All right."
> **Nurse:** (after the procedure) "All done. Please keep pressing the cotton for two minutes. Is there anything else I can do for you?"
> **Patient:** "No, thank you."
> **Nurse:** "Your breakfast will come at eight. I'll be back at nine to check on you."

The nurse introduced herself, identified the patient, explained the purpose and sensation, asked permission, reassured honestly, gave after-care instructions and told the patient when she would return.

## Remember

- Situations: formal, neutral, informal — choose the right register.
- Factors: setting, topic, social relationship, attitude, language.
- Functions: greet/introduce, request, permission, ask/give information, instruct/direct, agree/disagree, describe, narrate/report, evaluate/compare, complain/suggest.
- Telephone: identify self and area, state purpose, read back, note and document; spell names and say numbers digit by digit.
- Handover and calls to doctors: **SBAR** — Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (ISBAR adds Identify).
- Rounds: name, diagnosis, events, findings, results, concerns, questions.
- Case presentation: identification → complaints → history → examination → investigations → management → nursing care → education → summary.
- Presentation: introduction, 3–5 points, conclusion; signposts, eye contact, pace, pauses.
