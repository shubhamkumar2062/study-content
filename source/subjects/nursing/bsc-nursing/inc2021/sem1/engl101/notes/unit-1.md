# Unit 1: Communication and the roles of listeners, speakers, readers and writers

This unit covers INC Unit I (3 hours). It explains what communication is, how it works, what can go wrong, and why a nurse needs good English for each of the four skills: listening, speaking, reading and writing (LSRW).

## 1.1 What is communication?

**Communication** is the process of sending and receiving messages — ideas, information, feelings and instructions — between two or more people so that both reach a shared understanding.

The word comes from the Latin *communis*, meaning "common". Communication has worked only when the meaning in the receiver's mind is the same as the meaning the sender intended. Speaking or writing alone is not communication; the message must be understood.

Key points in the definition:

- It is a **two-way process** — there is always a sender and a receiver, and the roles keep changing.
- It is **continuous** — even silence, a frown or a delay sends a message.
- It is **purposeful** — to inform, to instruct, to persuade, to comfort or to build a relationship.
- It is **contextual** — the place, time, culture and relationship change how a message is understood.

**Communicative English** means English used to get things done with other people: to ask, explain, report, reassure, complain, agree or record. The focus is on clear, correct and suitable language rather than on literature.

## 1.2 The process of communication

```text
  SENDER --> encodes --> MESSAGE --> CHANNEL --> decodes --> RECEIVER
    ^          (words, gestures)   (speech, chart,             |
    |                               phone, letter)             |
    |                                                          |
    +---------------------- FEEDBACK <-------------------------+

           NOISE (anything that disturbs the message) acts at every stage
           CONTEXT (setting, culture, relationship) surrounds the whole process
```

| Element | Meaning | Ward example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sender (source) | The person who starts the message | The staff nurse giving a handover |
| Encoding | Turning an idea into words, signs or symbols | Choosing clear words: "Bed 4 needs a fresh dressing at 2 pm" |
| Message | The content — facts, feelings, orders | The patient's condition and plan |
| Channel (medium) | The route the message travels by | Face-to-face talk, telephone, case sheet, text message |
| Decoding | The receiver working out the meaning | The incoming nurse understanding "fresh dressing at 2 pm" |
| Receiver | The person for whom the message is meant | The nurse coming on duty |
| Feedback | The receiver's response that shows how the message was understood | "So the dressing is due at 2 pm — I'll do it." |
| Noise | Anything that distorts the message | Monitor alarms, poor handwriting, jargon, fatigue |
| Context | The surrounding situation | An emergency, a night shift, a crowded OPD |

**Feedback** is what makes communication two-way. Without it the sender cannot know whether the message was received correctly. In clinical work feedback is made deliberate: the receiver **reads back** or **repeats** important information (closed-loop communication).

## 1.3 Types of communication

### By the means used

| Type | Description | Nursing examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbal – oral | Spoken words | Handover, explaining a procedure, telephone call |
| Verbal – written | Written or typed words | Nurses' notes, incident report, discharge instructions |
| Non-verbal | Messages without words: face, eyes, posture, gestures, touch, distance, tone, silence | A reassuring touch, eye contact, sitting at the bedside |
| Visual | Charts, graphs, signs, pictures, colours | TPR chart, fall-risk sign above a bed, pictorial pain scale |

Non-verbal signs often carry more emotion than words. If words and body language disagree ("I'm fine" with a grimace), people usually believe the body language, so a nurse must observe both.

**Paralanguage** is *how* something is said — tone, pitch, loudness, speed, pauses. "Take your medicine" can sound kind or rude depending on paralanguage alone.

### By formality and direction

- **Formal** — follows official lines and set formats: duty rosters, circulars, reports, ward rounds.
- **Informal** — casual talk between colleagues; quick, but can spread rumours ("grapevine").
- **Downward** — from senior to junior: the nursing superintendent's instructions.
- **Upward** — from junior to senior: reporting a change in a patient to the ward in-charge or doctor.
- **Horizontal (lateral)** — between people of the same level: nurse to nurse, unit to unit.

### By the number of people

- **Intrapersonal** — talking to oneself, thinking, reflecting.
- **Interpersonal** — between two people, for example nurse and patient.
- **Group** — a team meeting or a health talk to a small group.
- **Mass / public** — a talk to a large audience, posters, radio.

## 1.4 Principles of effective communication: the 7 Cs

| C | Meaning | Weak | Better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clear | One idea at a time, simple words | "Patient not OK, check" | "Bed 6's pulse is 128 and irregular; please review" |
| Concise | No unnecessary words | "I would like to inform you that it seems the patient may have…" | "The patient has vomited twice" |
| Concrete | Specific facts, figures, times | "Drank a lot" | "Drank 1,200 ml since 8 am" |
| Correct | Right facts, right grammar, right spelling | "Patient is hypertention" | "The patient has hypertension" |
| Coherent | Ideas follow a logical order | Jumping between topics | Situation → background → assessment → request |
| Complete | Everything the receiver needs | Missing the patient's name or bed | Name, bed, problem, action needed |
| Courteous | Polite and respectful | "Sit there and wait" | "Please have a seat; I'll call you in a few minutes" |

Some books add **Considerate** (thinking of the receiver's needs) instead of one of the above; the idea is the same.

## 1.5 Barriers to communication

| Barrier | Examples in hospital | How a nurse overcomes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical / environmental | Noise, poor light, distance, a mask, a closed door | Move closer, reduce noise, face the person |
| Physiological | Hearing loss, pain, fatigue, sedation, speech defect | Speak slowly and clearly, use writing or pictures, wait for comfort |
| Language and semantic | Different mother tongue, jargon, abbreviations, words with two meanings | Use simple words, avoid jargon, use an interpreter, check understanding |
| Psychological / emotional | Fear, anxiety, anger, grief, prejudice | Acknowledge feelings, listen first, be calm |
| Cultural | Different beliefs about illness, gender rules, eye contact norms | Respect customs, ask preferences, involve family appropriately |
| Organisational | Too many levels, unclear roles, rushed handovers | Use standard formats (SBAR), written records |
| Personal | Poor listening, assumptions, interrupting, talking down | Practise active listening and respect |

A useful habit: after giving important information, ask the patient to **say it back in their own words** (the teach-back method). This checks understanding without making the patient feel tested.

## 1.6 Why English matters for a nurse in India

- **Medical records** — case sheets, nurses' notes, medicine charts, consent forms and reports are mostly kept in English.
- **Education** — textbooks, journals, guidelines and examinations are in English.
- **Team work** — doctors, nurses and technicians from different states may share no regional language; English becomes the common one.
- **Technology** — hospital information systems, equipment manuals and drug labels use English.
- **Career** — higher studies, nursing jobs abroad and international examinations test English.
- **Patient safety** — a misheard or misread word (a wrong name, wrong side, wrong time) can harm a patient. Many errors in hospitals are communication errors.

A nurse also needs the **patient's language**. Good communicative English does not replace the local language; it lets the nurse move accurately between the record (English) and the patient (often a regional language).

## 1.7 Communication roles of listeners, speakers, readers and writers as healthcare professionals

The four skills are grouped as **receptive** (listening, reading — taking in) and **productive** (speaking, writing — giving out). They are also grouped as **oral** (listening, speaking) and **written** (reading, writing).

```text
                 RECEPTIVE          PRODUCTIVE
   ORAL          Listening          Speaking
   WRITTEN       Reading            Writing
```

### The nurse as a listener

- Listens to the patient's complaints, worries and history — often the first clue to a problem.
- Listens to handover, doctors' instructions, telephone orders and announcements.
- Listens with attention, without interrupting, and checks meaning ("Do you mean the pain started after food?").
- Listens to non-verbal cues — sighs, silence, a shaky voice.
- Repeats critical information back to confirm it.

### The nurse as a speaker

- Greets, introduces self and explains procedures before doing them.
- Gives health education in simple words the patient can follow.
- Reports changes in a patient's condition to doctors and seniors clearly and quickly.
- Gives handover at the end of a shift and speaks during rounds and case presentations.
- Speaks politely to relatives, calms anxious people and handles complaints.
- Uses a suitable tone, speed, volume and level of language for each listener.

### The nurse as a reader

- Reads case sheets, doctors' orders, prescriptions of care, lab reports and previous nurses' notes.
- Reads charts, flow sheets, graphs and tables (TPR, intake–output, partograph).
- Reads hospital policies, circulars, equipment manuals and medicine labels.
- Reads textbooks, journals and news to keep knowledge up to date.
- Reads carefully and asks when something is unclear — never guesses at an unclear order.

### The nurse as a writer

- Writes nurses' notes, observations, intake–output and care plans.
- Writes handover notes, incident reports, requisitions and letters.
- Writes patient education leaflets and discharge instructions in plain language.
- Writes assignments, reflective journals, projects and research abstracts as a student.
- Writes legibly, accurately and promptly; a record is a legal document ("not written = not done").

## 1.8 Communicative competence and LSRW strategies

**Communicative competence** is the ability to use language not just correctly but **appropriately** for the situation. It has four parts:

| Part | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grammatical (linguistic) | Correct words, grammar, pronunciation | "The patient has slept well" (not "has sleeped") |
| Sociolinguistic | Right level of formality for the person and place | "Good morning, sir" to a senior doctor; first name with a close colleague |
| Discourse | Linking sentences into a connected whole | A handover that flows from situation to plan |
| Strategic | Repairing a breakdown in communication | "Sorry, could you repeat the last dose time?" or drawing a picture |

**Communicative strategies** for each skill:

| Skill | Useful strategies |
| --- | --- |
| Listening | Predict the topic, listen for key words, note numbers and names, ask for repetition, read back |
| Speaking | Plan the main point, use short sentences, check the listener's understanding, use examples |
| Reading | Skim for the main idea, scan for a detail, guess words from context, check abbreviations |
| Writing | Plan, write, then revise; use headings and lists; keep to facts; check spelling and grammar |

## 1.9 Therapeutic and social communication (a preview)

- **Social communication** — everyday conversation between equals for friendship or business.
- **Therapeutic communication** — purposeful, patient-centred communication that helps the patient express feelings, understand care and cope. The nurse listens more than she speaks, uses open questions, reflects feelings, and keeps the focus on the patient.

This idea is studied in detail in Nursing Foundations and Psychology; in English you practise the **language** for it (open questions, reflecting, summarising — see Units 3 and 4).

## Remember

- Communication = sending and receiving a message with **shared understanding**; feedback makes it two-way.
- Elements: sender, encoding, message, channel, decoding, receiver, feedback, noise, context.
- Types: verbal (oral, written), non-verbal, visual; formal/informal; upward, downward, horizontal.
- 7 Cs: clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, courteous.
- Barriers: physical, physiological, language, psychological, cultural, organisational, personal.
- LSRW: listening and reading are receptive; speaking and writing are productive.
- Nurses are listeners, speakers, readers and writers every shift — and every record is a legal document.
- Communicative competence = grammatical + sociolinguistic + discourse + strategic.
