# Unit 6: Writing skills — patient history, notes, letters, reports, journals, abstracts and CV

This unit covers INC Unit VI (5 hours). It gives the formats and model examples for the writing a nurse does in practice and as a student. Writing is assessed for **task fulfilment, coherence and cohesion, appropriate vocabulary and correct grammar** — keep these four in mind for every answer.

## 6.1 Qualities of good writing

| Quality | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Task fulfilment | Answers exactly what was asked, in the right format and length |
| Coherence | Ideas in a logical order; each paragraph has one main idea |
| Cohesion | Sentences linked with linkers (however, therefore, then) and pronouns that refer back clearly |
| Appropriate vocabulary | Words suited to the reader: technical for professionals, plain for patients |
| Correct grammar and spelling | Tenses, agreement, punctuation, spelling (Units 2 and 5) |

The writing process: **plan** (purpose, reader, points) → **draft** → **revise** (content, order) → **edit** (grammar, spelling) → **final copy**.

## 6.2 Principles of documentation

Nursing records are **legal documents**. In a dispute, the record is the evidence of care: "if it was not written, it was not done."

- **Factual** — record what you see, hear, measure and do; not guesses or labels ("patient is rude" ✗; "patient shouted, 'Leave me alone'" ✓).
- **Accurate** — exact measurements, times and spelling; use approved abbreviations only.
- **Complete** — all care, observations, responses and communication with others.
- **Current (timely)** — write as soon as possible after the event; never before doing it.
- **Organised** — in time order, with date and time for every entry.
- **Legible and permanent** — clear handwriting in ink (or the hospital's electronic system).
- **Signed** — name, designation and signature.
- **Confidential** — records are shared only with those involved in care.

Errors: draw a **single line** through the mistake so it is still readable, write "error" (or the hospital's term), initial and date it, then write the correct entry. Never use correction fluid, never overwrite, never tear out pages, and never leave blank lines (draw a line through empty space).

## 6.3 Writing patient history

A **patient history** is a written account of the patient's health information collected by interview.

Format:

```text
IDENTIFICATION DATA: Name, age, sex, hospital no., ward/bed, date of admission,
                     address, religion, education, occupation, informant
CHIEF COMPLAINTS (with duration): in the patient's words where possible
HISTORY OF PRESENT ILLNESS: onset, duration, course, associated symptoms,
                     treatment taken
PAST HISTORY: illnesses, surgeries, admissions, allergies, immunisation
FAMILY HISTORY: similar illness, hereditary conditions
PERSONAL HISTORY: diet, sleep, bowel and bladder habits, habits (tobacco, alcohol)
SOCIO-ECONOMIC HISTORY: family type, income, housing, support
```

Model (history of present illness):

> Mrs S, 54 years, was admitted on 10 March with complaints of "burning while passing urine for four days" and fever with chills for two days. She also reports passing urine frequently, about every hour. She took paracetamol from a local shop with temporary relief. There is no history of blood in urine or loin pain. She is a known case of diabetes for eight years, on tablets.

Write history in the **past tense**, report the patient's words in quotation marks, and keep opinions out.

## 6.4 Nurses' notes and written handover

**Nurses' notes** record the patient's condition, the care given and the response. Common formats:

| Format | Parts |
| --- | --- |
| Narrative | Time-ordered paragraphs of observations and care |
| SOAP / SOAPIE | Subjective (what the patient says), Objective (what you observe/measure), Assessment (your judgement), Plan, Intervention, Evaluation |
| DAR (focus charting) | Data, Action, Response — under a focus heading such as "Pain" |

Model narrative entry:

> **14/03, 2.00 pm** — Patient complains of pain at the operation site, 6/10 on the pain scale, "like a pulling feeling." Grimacing on moving. T 37.4 °C, P 96/min, R 20/min, BP 130/84 mmHg. Dressing dry and intact. Pain relief given as ordered (see medicine chart). Positioned on the left side with a pillow supporting the abdomen.
> **2.45 pm** — Pain now 2/10; patient resting comfortably. — *Signature, N. Kaur, Staff Nurse*

Model SOAPIE entry (focus: breathing):

> **S:** "I feel short of breath when I walk to the toilet."
> **O:** RR 26/min, SpO2 92% on room air, using accessory muscles after walking 10 metres.
> **A:** Breathlessness on exertion.
> **P:** Reduce exertion, oxygen as ordered, inform doctor.
> **I:** Placed in high Fowler's position, oxygen started as ordered, doctor informed at 10.20 am, commode placed at bedside.
> **E:** At 10.45 am RR 20/min, SpO2 96%, patient says breathing is easier.

**Written handover note** (shift report) — for each patient: name, age, bed, diagnosis, day of stay; current condition and vital signs; care given; changes and incidents; investigations pending; medicines or procedures due; risks (falls, pressure injury, infection); family concerns. Use SBAR headings for clarity (Unit 4).

## 6.5 Note taking and summarising

**Note taking** — writing down key points from a lecture, book or meeting for later use. Use headings, short phrases, abbreviations, numbering and symbols; leave space; use the Cornell or mind-map format (Unit 3).

**Summarising** — reducing a text to its main ideas in your own words, usually one-third or less of the original.

Steps:

1. Read the whole text to understand it.
2. Underline the main idea of each paragraph.
3. Leave out examples, repetition and minor details.
4. Write the main ideas in your own words, linked logically.
5. Check that the meaning is unchanged and nothing important is missing.
6. Give a title.

Model — original (about 110 words):

> Falls are a common and serious problem among elderly patients in hospital. A fall may cause fractures, head injuries, fear of walking and a longer stay. Many factors increase the risk: weakness, dizziness, poor eyesight, confusion, the need to pass urine at night, wet floors, poor lighting and unfamiliar surroundings. Some medicines that cause drowsiness also raise the risk. Nurses can prevent most falls by assessing every patient's risk on admission, keeping the bed low with the brakes on, placing the call bell and personal items within reach, helping patients to the toilet, keeping floors dry and lights on at night, and teaching patients to rise slowly.

Summary (about 40 words):

> **Preventing falls in elderly patients.** Falls in hospital cause injury and longer stays. Weakness, confusion, poor vision, night-time toileting, drowsy medicines and unsafe surroundings increase risk. Nurses prevent falls by assessing risk, making the bedside safe, helping with toileting and teaching patients.

## 6.6 Anecdotal records

An **anecdotal record** is a brief, factual written description of a specific incident of a student's (or patient's) behaviour, written soon after it is observed, with the observer's comment kept separate. Teachers use it to assess students' clinical performance; nurses may use similar records for patient behaviour.

Features: one incident per record, objective description, date, time and place, separate interpretation, signed.

Model:

```text
ANECDOTAL RECORD
Student: Ms R. Thomas        Year: 1st B.Sc Nursing     Area: Female Medical Ward
Date/time: 18/03, 10.15 am   Observer: Clinical Instructor
Incident: While giving a bed bath to an elderly patient, the student closed the
curtains, explained each step, covered parts of the body not being washed, and
asked the patient if the water was warm enough. When the patient said she felt
cold, the student added a blanket at once.
Comment: Shows respect for privacy and dignity and responds to patient needs.
Recommendation: Continue; encourage her to share this practice with peers.
Signature of observer: ________   Signature of student: ________
```

## 6.7 Letter writing

### Formal letter format

```text
Sender's address
Date

Receiver's designation and address

Subject: (one line saying what the letter is about)

Salutation (Respected Sir/Madam, / Dear Sir/Madam,)

Body: paragraph 1 — purpose
      paragraph 2 — details
      paragraph 3 — request or action and thanks

Closing (Yours faithfully, / Yours sincerely,)
Signature
Name and designation
```

Use **"Yours faithfully"** when the salutation is "Dear Sir/Madam" (name not known); **"Yours sincerely"** when you address the person by name. Keep it short, polite and to the point.

**Model 1 — Leave application**

> To
> The Principal,
> College of Nursing, …
>
> 22 March 2026
>
> Subject: Application for leave for three days
>
> Respected Madam,
>
> I am a first-year B.Sc Nursing student. I request leave from 24 to 26 March 2026 to attend my sister's wedding in my home town.
>
> I will complete the missed classes and clinical assignments on my return.
>
> Kindly grant me leave for these three days.
>
> Thanking you,
> Yours faithfully,
> (Signature)
> Anjali Verma, Roll No. 12, 1st year B.Sc Nursing

**Model 2 — Letter to the Nursing Superintendent requesting equipment**

> To
> The Nursing Superintendent, District Hospital, …
>
> Subject: Request for replacement of two non-working suction machines in the Paediatric Ward
>
> Respected Madam,
>
> Two of the three suction machines in the Paediatric Ward have not been working since 15 March. The biomedical department has examined them and reported that they cannot be repaired.
>
> With an average of 25 children admitted, several of them with respiratory problems, one machine is not enough, especially at night and in emergencies.
>
> I request that two new suction machines be supplied as early as possible. The biomedical report is attached.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> (Signature)
> Ward In-charge, Paediatric Ward

Other letters students should practise: letter to a patient's family about a follow-up visit, a complaint about poor hostel facilities, a requisition to the store, a letter of thanks to a hospital for a field visit, and a job application with CV.

**Informal letter** (to a friend or family member): address and date, "Dear …", friendly paragraphs, "With love / Yours affectionately", first name. No subject line.

**E-mail** follows the formal letter in tone: clear subject line, greeting, short paragraphs, sign-off with name and designation, check before sending.

## 6.8 Diary and journal writing

A **diary** is a personal day-to-day record. A **reflective journal** is a written reflection on learning experiences — used in nursing education to link theory and practice and to grow professionally.

A common structure for reflection is **Gibbs' reflective cycle**:

1. **Description** — what happened?
2. **Feelings** — what were you thinking and feeling?
3. **Evaluation** — what was good and bad about the experience?
4. **Analysis** — what sense can you make of it? (link to theory)
5. **Conclusion** — what else could you have done?
6. **Action plan** — what will you do if it happens again?

Model reflective journal entry (short):

> **Date: 5 April.** Today I took the blood pressure of an elderly patient for the first time without help. My hands shook and I had to repeat it twice. I felt nervous because the patient's son was watching. Afterwards, the patient thanked me and said I was gentle. I realise I was worried about being judged rather than focusing on the technique. Explaining the procedure first, as we learned in class, would have relaxed both of us. Next time I will explain each step aloud and practise more on classmates during lab hours.

Write in the **first person** and **past tense** for events; be honest and keep patient identities confidential.

## 6.9 Report writing

A **report** is a formal, factual account of an event, activity or investigation, written for a specific reader, usually with headings.

General format:

```text
Title
To / From / Date
1. Introduction (purpose, background)
2. Details / Findings (what happened, in order)
3. Action taken
4. Conclusion / Recommendations
Signature, name, designation
```

**Model — Incident report (patient fall)**

> **Incident report**
> **Patient:** Mr K. Nair, 76 years, Bed 21, Male Medical Ward, Hosp. No. 30718
> **Date and time of incident:** 9 April, 3.10 am
> **Description:** At 3.10 am the patient was found sitting on the floor beside his bed. He said, "I wanted to go to the toilet and my legs gave way." The side rails were down; the call bell was on the bedside table out of his reach.
> **Assessment after the fall:** Conscious and oriented. Complained of pain in the right hip. No visible bleeding. T 36.8 °C, P 92/min, R 20/min, BP 140/90 mmHg.
> **Action taken:** Patient lifted back to bed with the help of two staff. Duty doctor informed at 3.15 am and examined him at 3.25 am; X-ray of the right hip ordered. Side rails raised, call bell placed within reach, urinal kept at bedside. Family informed at 7 am.
> **Recommendations:** Fall-risk assessment for all elderly patients on admission; night-time toileting rounds.
> **Reported by:** S. Pillai, Staff Nurse — 9 April, 4.00 am

Other reports: ward (shift) report, report of a health camp or field visit, report on a seminar, monthly census report. Use the **past tense**, the **passive voice** where the doer is not important ("The patient was shifted to ICU"), and objective language.

## 6.10 Paper writing skills

A **paper** (assignment, term paper or research article) presents information or findings on a topic in an organised, referenced way.

1. **Choose and narrow the topic** — "Hand hygiene compliance among nursing students" rather than "Infection".
2. **Search the literature** — textbooks, journals, official guidelines; note references as you read.
3. **Outline** — introduction, main sections, conclusion.
4. **Write the draft** in your own words — **paraphrase and cite**; copying without citing is **plagiarism**.
5. **Cite sources** in the text and list them in references in the style your college uses (for example Vancouver or APA).
6. **Revise** for logic and **edit** for language; read aloud; check figures and tables are numbered and titled.

Academic style: formal, objective, precise; avoid "I think", slang and contractions ("don't" → "do not"); define terms when first used; one idea per paragraph.

## 6.11 Abstract writing

An **abstract** is a short, complete summary of a paper (usually 150–300 words; follow the limit given) placed at the beginning so readers can decide whether to read the whole paper.

A **structured abstract** follows **IMRaD**:

- **Background / Introduction** — the problem and aim.
- **Methods** — design, setting, sample, tools.
- **Results** — the main findings with key figures.
- **Discussion / Conclusion** — what the results mean and recommendations.
- **Keywords** — 3–5 terms.

Model structured abstract (fictitious study for practice):

> **Background:** Hand hygiene prevents hospital infections, but students may not follow it consistently. This study aimed to assess hand hygiene practice among first-year nursing students.
> **Methods:** A descriptive study was conducted with 60 first-year students in a teaching hospital. Practice during clinical postings was observed with a checklist based on the five moments of hand hygiene.
> **Results:** Students cleaned their hands most often after body-fluid exposure and least often before touching a patient. Practice improved when hand-rub dispensers were placed at the bedside.
> **Conclusion:** Hand hygiene before patient contact needs reinforcement through teaching and reminders.
> **Keywords:** hand hygiene, nursing students, infection prevention

Write it **last**, in the past tense for methods and results, with no references or abbreviations that are not explained.

## 6.12 Resume / curriculum vitae

A **resume** is a short (1–2 page) summary of qualifications for a job; a **CV** is a fuller academic record. For a fresh nurse they look similar.

```text
NAME
Address | Phone | E-mail
CAREER OBJECTIVE: one or two lines about the role you want
EDUCATION: degree, college, university, year, grade (latest first)
REGISTRATION: State Nursing Council registration number (when obtained)
CLINICAL EXPERIENCE: areas of posting / work, duration
SKILLS: clinical skills, computer skills, languages known
TRAINING / CERTIFICATES: e.g. life-support courses, workshops
ACHIEVEMENTS AND ACTIVITIES: awards, positions, community work
REFERENCES: two referees with designation and contact (or "available on request")
DECLARATION, place, date, signature
```

Tips: latest information first; action words (assisted, managed, organised, conducted); no spelling errors; honest; send with a short covering letter.

## 6.13 Verbatim reproducing and presenting a situation in writing

- **Verbatim** writing quotes exactly what was said or written, in quotation marks — patients' statements, telephone orders (also read back), critical results. Do not "correct" the patient's grammar inside a quotation.
- **Presenting a situation** in writing (for example a change in a patient's condition) is best done with the SBAR headings, so the reader sees the problem, background, findings and request at once.

## Remember

- Writing is judged on task fulfilment, coherence and cohesion, vocabulary and grammar.
- Documentation: factual, accurate, complete, timely, organised, legible, signed, confidential; single-line corrections.
- History: identification → chief complaints → present, past, family, personal, socio-economic history.
- Nurses' notes: narrative, SOAPIE, DAR; record the patient's words in quotation marks.
- Summary = main ideas in your own words, about one-third length.
- Anecdotal record: one incident, objective, dated, with a separate comment.
- Formal letter: address, date, receiver, subject, salutation, body, "Yours faithfully/sincerely", signature.
- Reflective journal: Gibbs — description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan.
- Report: title, introduction, details, action, recommendations, signature; past tense, objective.
- Abstract: IMRaD in 150–300 words; written last. CV: latest first, honest, error-free.
