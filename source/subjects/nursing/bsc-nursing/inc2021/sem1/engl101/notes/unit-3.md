# Unit 3: Attentive listening

This unit covers INC Unit III (5 hours). It teaches how to listen with full attention in different situations — announcements, descriptions, stories, instructions, discussions, demonstrations, lectures and presentations — and how to reproduce what was heard exactly when it matters.

## 3.1 What is attentive listening?

**Attentive (active) listening** is giving the speaker your full attention, understanding both the words and the feelings behind them, and showing through your responses that you have understood.

A nurse spends more of her working time listening than speaking, reading or writing. Patients often say the most important thing at the end of a conversation ("…and one more thing, doctor, I get chest pain when I climb stairs"). Only an attentive listener catches it.

Benefits of attentive listening:

- Accurate history and assessment — fewer missed symptoms.
- Fewer errors when receiving orders and handover.
- The patient feels respected and is more likely to trust and cooperate.
- Conflicts and complaints are calmed early.
- Better learning in class and in clinical teaching.

## 3.2 The listening process

```text
  RECEIVING  →  UNDERSTANDING  →  REMEMBERING  →  EVALUATING  →  RESPONDING
  (hear and      (attach meaning   (store key      (judge what    (feedback:
   attend)        to words, tone)   points)         is important)  nod, reply, act)
```

1. **Receiving** — hearing the sound and choosing to attend to it.
2. **Understanding** — working out the meaning of words, tone and non-verbal signs.
3. **Remembering** — keeping key facts long enough to use them (notes help).
4. **Evaluating** — separating fact from opinion, the important from the unimportant.
5. **Responding** — giving feedback: a nod, a question, a summary, or an action.

## 3.3 Barriers to attentive listening

| Barrier | Example | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noise and interruptions | Phones, alarms, visitors | Choose a quieter place, close the curtain |
| Fatigue, hunger, worry | End of a night shift | Ask for a short repeat; write down key facts |
| Thinking ahead | Planning your reply instead of listening | Wait until the speaker finishes |
| Prejudging | "This patient always complains" | Listen fresh each time |
| Selective listening | Hearing only the part you expected | Listen for the whole message, then summarise |
| Language difficulty | Unfamiliar accent or word | Ask politely: "Could you say that again, please?" |
| Information overload | Long list of instructions | Ask the speaker to slow down; note as you listen |
| Distracting habits | Looking at a phone, fiddling with a pen | Put things down and face the speaker |

## 3.4 Skills of the active listener

### Non-verbal attending: SOLER

A simple guide to body language while listening:

- **S** — **S**it squarely facing the person (or at a slight angle).
- **O** — **O**pen posture: arms and legs uncrossed.
- **L** — **L**ean slightly towards the person.
- **E** — **E**ye contact: comfortable, not staring (adjust to culture).
- **R** — **R**elaxed manner.

### Verbal responses

| Technique | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimal encouragers | Short sounds or words that invite the speaker to continue | "Mm-hm", "I see", "Go on" |
| Open questions | Questions that cannot be answered yes/no | "How have you been sleeping?" |
| Closed questions | Yes/no or one-word answers, to check facts | "Did you take your morning tablet?" |
| Clarifying | Asking about something unclear | "When you say dizzy, do you mean the room spins?" |
| Paraphrasing | Restating the content in your own words | "So the pain began after lunch and is getting worse." |
| Reflecting feelings | Naming the emotion you notice | "You sound worried about going home alone." |
| Summarising | Pulling the main points together at the end | "Let me check I've got everything: …" |
| Silence | Giving time to think or feel | A pause after the patient becomes tearful |

Avoid **blocking responses**: false reassurance ("Don't worry, everything will be fine"), changing the subject, giving advice too quickly, judging ("You shouldn't feel like that"), and asking "why" in a way that sounds like blame.

## 3.5 Listening in different situations

### Announcements

Short, public and often repeated — at the hospital, railway station or over a public-address system. Listen for: **who** it concerns, **what** action is needed, **where** and **when**.

> "Attention please. All final-year students are to report to the Conference Hall on the second floor at 2 pm today for the infection control workshop."
> Key facts: final-year students · Conference Hall, 2nd floor · 2 pm today · infection control workshop.

### Descriptions

Describe a person, place, object or process. Listen for **features in order** — size, shape, colour, position, use. Example: a patient describing a wound or a relative describing a missing elderly patient.

### Narratives

Stories told in time order — a patient's history of illness or a colleague's account of an incident. Listen for **sequence words** (first, then, after that, finally) and **time expressions** (two days ago, last night). Draw a quick timeline in your notes.

### Instructions

Step-by-step directions. Listen for **action verbs**, **numbers**, **sequence words** and **warnings** ("do not", "never", "before"). Repeat the steps back before acting.

### Discussions

Several speakers giving views — a team meeting or a case conference. Listen for the **topic**, each speaker's **position**, the **reasons** given, points of **agreement**, and the **decision** reached.

### Demonstrations

A procedure shown and explained together — for example the clinical instructor demonstrating catheter care. Match each **spoken step** with the **action seen**; notice the rationale ("we clean from the meatus outwards because…"). Note the safety points.

## 3.6 Listening for different purposes

| Purpose | What you do | Example task |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gist (overall meaning)** | Ignore details; get the main idea | "What is this news item about?" |
| **Specific information** | Listen only for the needed detail | "What time is the next dose?" "Which bed?" |
| **Detailed understanding** | Follow every point | Receiving handover or a telephone order |
| **Attitudes and opinions** | Notice tone, word choice and signal phrases | "I'm not very happy with…", "To be honest…", "I strongly feel…" |
| **Inference** | Work out what is meant but not said | "I don't want to be a burden" may hide fear of dependence |

Signals of **opinion**: I think, I feel, in my view, it seems, probably, I'm sure, I doubt. Signals of **fact**: dates, numbers, measurements, names, direct observations.

Listening tasks in class include multiple-choice, yes/no, true/false and fill-in-the-blank questions on announcements, news and short talks. Check your answers individually against the key, then listen again to find what you missed.

## 3.7 Reproducing verbatim

**Verbatim** means "word for word", exactly as spoken. Some information must never be summarised or changed:

- **Telephone or verbal orders** — written down at once and **read back** to the speaker word for word for confirmation. Hospitals limit verbal orders to emergencies and require the prescriber to sign them later; follow your hospital's policy.
- **Critical test results** reported by telephone — write them down and read them back.
- **A patient's own words** about symptoms, threats, refusals or complaints — recorded in quotation marks in the notes.
- **Names and numbers** — patient name, hospital number, bed number, times.
- **Statements in legal or incident situations** — what each person actually said.

Example of a verbatim entry in nurses' notes:

> 11.30 pm — Patient says, "I can't breathe properly when I lie flat." Propped up to 45°, respiratory rate 26/min. Doctor informed at 11.35 pm.

Read-back example on the telephone:

> Lab: "Bed 12, Mr Ravi Kumar, hospital number 4521, potassium is critical."
> Nurse: "Let me read that back: Bed 12, Mr Ravi Kumar, hospital number 4521, critical potassium result. I'm informing the doctor now. May I have your name, please?"

Tips: write while listening; use the speaker's exact words for anything you will quote; read back numbers digit by digit ("one-five, not fifty") because *fifteen* and *fifty*, *thirteen* and *thirty* are easily confused.

## 3.8 Listening to academic talks and lectures

Before the lecture:
- Read the topic in advance and revise the last class.
- Prepare your notebook with the date and title.

During the lecture:
- Listen for **signposting** language that shows the structure: "Today I'll cover three points…", "Firstly…", "Another important factor…", "To sum up…".
- Note **main ideas and key words**, not every word. Use abbreviations and symbols (↑ increase, ↓ decrease, → leads to, ∴ therefore, e.g., i.e., pt for patient).
- Mark points the teacher **repeats**, **writes on the board** or says are important — these are exam points.
- Note questions to ask at the end.

After the lecture: within a day, rewrite or tidy the notes, fill gaps from the textbook, and summarise in five lines.

### Note-taking formats

```text
CORNELL METHOD                         MIND MAP
+-----------+----------------------+         Barriers
| Cues /    | Notes taken during   |            |
| questions | the lecture          |  Process--COMMUNICATION--Types
|           |                      |            |
+-----------+----------------------+          7 Cs
| Summary (written after class)    |
+----------------------------------+
```

- **Outline method** — headings and indented sub-points.
- **Cornell method** — a narrow left column for key words and questions, a wide right column for notes, and a summary at the bottom.
- **Mind map** — the topic in the centre with branches for sub-topics; good for revision.

## 3.9 Listening to presentations

When a classmate or a speaker presents:

- Identify the **purpose** (inform, persuade, teach) and the **main message**.
- Follow the **structure**: introduction, main points, conclusion.
- Look at the **visuals** and connect them with what is said.
- Note **evidence** given for claims — figures, studies, examples.
- Give respectful **feedback**: one thing done well, one thing to improve, a question.

## 3.10 Attentive listening at the bedside — a model

> **Patient (Mrs Lakshmi, 62):** "I don't know… they said the operation went well, but I keep feeling something is wrong."
> **Nurse:** (sits beside the bed, eye contact) "You keep feeling something is wrong?" *(reflecting)*
> **Patient:** "My leg feels heavy and it's swollen since this morning."
> **Nurse:** "Tell me more about the swelling." *(open request)*
> **Patient:** "It's only the left leg, and it hurts in the calf when I move my foot."
> **Nurse:** "So since this morning your left leg is swollen and the calf hurts when you move your foot. Is that right?" *(paraphrasing and checking)*
> **Patient:** "Yes, exactly."
> **Nurse:** "Thank you for telling me. I'll check your leg now and inform the doctor straight away." *(responding with action)*

The nurse heard a worry, explored it, confirmed the facts and acted — a possible complication was reported early because she listened.

## Remember

- Hearing is physical; listening is active: receive → understand → remember → evaluate → respond.
- SOLER: Sit squarely, Open posture, Lean in, Eye contact, Relaxed.
- Active listening tools: encouragers, open questions, clarifying, paraphrasing, reflecting feelings, summarising, silence.
- Situations: announcements (who, what, where, when), descriptions, narratives (sequence), instructions (verbs, numbers, warnings), discussions, demonstrations.
- Purposes: gist, specific information, detail, attitude/opinion, inference.
- Verbatim = word for word: verbal orders and critical results are read back; patients' words go in quotation marks.
- Lectures: listen for signposts, note key words, use Cornell / outline / mind map, review within a day.
