# Unit 3: Communication, nurse–patient relationship, documentation and reporting

INC Unit IV (8 hours theory + 3 skill lab): communication and the nurse–patient relationship. INC Unit V (4 hours theory + 2 skill lab): documentation and reporting. They are joined because both are about passing information accurately — by talking and by writing.

## 3.1 Communication: meaning

**Communication** is the exchange of information, thoughts and feelings between people through words, signs, writing and behaviour, so that both understand the same meaning. In nursing it is the tool for assessment, teaching, comfort and teamwork.

## 3.2 Levels of communication

| Level | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intrapersonal | Talking to oneself, inner thoughts | "I can do this injection safely" |
| Interpersonal | Between two people | Nurse talking with a patient |
| Transpersonal | Communication with a higher power or the spiritual self | Prayer, meditation |
| Small group | Among a few people | Ward meeting, health team conference |
| Public | One person to a large audience | Health talk in a village |

## 3.3 Elements and process of communication

```text
          ┌─────────── Feedback ◄────────────┐
          ▼                                   │
     SENDER ──encodes──► MESSAGE ──channel──► RECEIVER ──decodes
     (nurse)            (words, gestures)     (patient)
                       ↑
             CONTEXT / environment (noise, privacy, culture)
```

| Element | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Referent (stimulus) | What starts the communication — a pain, a need, a question |
| Sender (encoder) | Person who forms and sends the message |
| Message | The information, verbal or non-verbal |
| Channel | The route: speech, sight, touch, writing, phone, email |
| Receiver (decoder) | Person who receives and interprets the message |
| Feedback | The receiver's response, showing whether the message was understood |
| Environment / context | Setting in which it happens — time, place, privacy, noise |

The process is a cycle: the sender forms an idea, puts it in words or signs, sends it; the receiver takes it in, interprets it and responds with feedback.

## 3.4 Types and modes of communication

| Type | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbal (spoken or written) | Uses words; should be simple, clear, brief, well-timed, suited to the listener, credible | Explaining a procedure; written instructions |
| Non-verbal | Body language; often more honest than words | Facial expression, eye contact, posture, gestures, touch, tone of voice, silence, appearance, personal space |
| Formal | Through official channels | Shift report, written order |
| Informal | Casual, personal | Chat at the bedside |
| One-way / two-way | Only sender speaks / both speak and give feedback | A notice board / a counselling session |

**Personal space** (distance zones): intimate (touching to about 45 cm — used in procedures, so explain first), personal (about 45 cm–1.2 m — most nurse–patient talks), social (about 1.2–3.6 m), public (beyond 3.6 m).

## 3.5 Factors influencing communication

- **Perception** — each person sees things through their own experience.
- **Values and beliefs**, **culture and language**.
- **Emotions** — anxiety, anger and pain block listening.
- **Developmental stage** — children and the elderly need adapted language.
- **Gender and social roles**.
- **Knowledge level** — avoid medical jargon.
- **Physical condition** — hearing loss, speech problems, sedation, pain.
- **Environment** — noise, lack of privacy, interruptions.
- **Space and territory**, and **relationship** between the people.

## 3.6 Therapeutic communication techniques

**Therapeutic communication** is purposeful, patient-centred communication that helps the patient express feelings, understand the illness and cope.

| Technique | What the nurse does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Active listening | Gives full attention, maintains eye contact, nods | (Sits down, faces the patient) |
| Broad opening | Lets the patient choose the topic | "What would you like to talk about?" |
| Open-ended question | Invites a full answer, not yes/no | "How have you been feeling since the surgery?" |
| Silence | Gives time to think and feel | (Pauses quietly) |
| Restating / paraphrasing | Repeats the main idea in other words | "You are worried about going home alone." |
| Clarifying | Asks to make the meaning clear | "Do you mean the pain is worse at night?" |
| Reflecting | Returns the feeling to the patient | "You sound frightened." |
| Focusing | Keeps the talk on one important point | "Let's talk more about your breathlessness." |
| Sharing observations | States what the nurse notices | "You seem tense today." |
| Giving information | Provides facts the patient needs | "The test takes about ten minutes." |
| Offering self | Shows willingness to be with the patient | "I'll sit with you for a while." |
| Summarising | Pulls together the main points | "So, you've told me three things…" |
| Therapeutic touch | Holding a hand, when culturally acceptable | |

## 3.7 Barriers / non-therapeutic communication

| Block | Example | Why it harms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| False reassurance | "Don't worry, everything will be fine." | Brushes feelings aside; may be untrue |
| Giving advice | "If I were you, I would…" | Takes away the patient's decision |
| Approval / disapproval | "That's bad, you shouldn't think that." | Judges; patient stops sharing |
| Asking "why" | "Why did you stop your tablets?" | Feels like an accusation |
| Changing the subject | Talking about the weather when the patient mentions death | Shows the nurse is uncomfortable |
| Stereotyped comments | "Everyone feels that way." | Feels like a cliché |
| Defensive reply | "The doctors here are very good." | Protects the staff, not the patient |
| Too many / closed questions | "Did you sleep? Eat? Pass urine?" | Turns talk into an interrogation |
| Jargon, talking too fast | "Your Hb is low; you need a PRBC transfusion." | Not understood |

Other barriers: noise, pain, language difference, hearing loss, anxiety, hurry, lack of privacy, cultural taboos.

## 3.8 Professional communication

Communication with doctors, colleagues and other staff should be **clear, concise, factual, timely and respectful**, and kept confidential. A common structured method for passing urgent information is **SBAR** (or ISBAR):

| Step | Content |
| --- | --- |
| (I) Identify | Yourself, the unit and the patient |
| S — Situation | What is happening now |
| B — Background | Diagnosis, relevant history, treatment |
| A — Assessment | What you found (vital signs, observations) and what you think |
| R — Recommendation | What you need or suggest; confirm the plan |

Repeat back any verbal or telephone order, write it down, and get it signed as early as possible according to hospital policy.

## 3.9 The helping relationship (nurse–patient relationship)

A **helping (therapeutic) relationship** is a planned, goal-directed, professional relationship in which the nurse helps the patient meet health needs. It is centred on the patient, has a time limit, and ends when goals are met.

**Purposes**: to build trust, help the patient express feelings, understand the illness, solve problems, cope, and become independent.

**Phases** (based on Hildegard Peplau's work)

| Phase | What happens | Nurse's tasks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-interaction | Before meeting the patient | Read the records, examine own feelings, plan the first meeting |
| Orientation (introductory) | First meetings; getting to know each other | Introduce self, explain role, agree on goals and time, begin to build trust, maintain confidentiality |
| Working (identification and exploitation) | Main work of the relationship | Help the patient explore feelings, give care and information, encourage self-care, solve problems |
| Termination (resolution) | Ending | Prepare the patient for discharge or transfer in advance, review progress, deal with feelings about separation |

Qualities that help: **trust, empathy** (understanding the patient's feelings), **genuineness**, **respect**, **warmth**, **active listening** and **confidentiality**. Sympathy (feeling sorry for someone) is different from empathy and is less helpful.

## 3.10 Communicating with patients, families and the team

- **Patients**: introduce yourself, call them by preferred name, explain before you touch, check understanding (ask them to repeat — the "teach-back" method), allow questions.
- **Families**: keep them informed within confidentiality rules, involve them in care, listen to their worries.
- **Team**: accurate handover, use SBAR, respect roles, resolve disagreements privately.

## 3.11 Communicating with vulnerable groups

| Group | Helpful approach |
| --- | --- |
| Children | Speak at their eye level, simple words, play and pictures, involve parents, be honest about pain |
| Women | Privacy, a female attendant during examinations, sensitivity in reproductive or abuse issues |
| Hearing impaired | Face the person, good light, speak slowly (do not shout), use writing or sign language, hearing aid on |
| Visually impaired | Announce yourself, describe surroundings, guide by offering your arm |
| Speech impaired | Yes/no questions, picture boards, allow time |
| Mentally challenged | Short sentences, patience, repetition, involve carers |
| Elderly | Allow more time, check hearing and vision, speak clearly, show respect, do not talk down |
| Different language | Use a trained interpreter, not a child of the family |

## 3.12 Documentation: purposes of records and reports

A **record** is a permanent written (or electronic) account of the patient's care. A **report** is an oral or written exchange of information about patients among health workers.

**Purposes of records**

1. **Communication** among team members.
2. **Planning and continuity of care** across shifts.
3. **Legal document** — evidence of what care was given; may be used in court.
4. **Quality review and audit**.
5. **Education** of students.
6. **Research and statistics**.
7. **Billing, insurance and reimbursement**.
8. **Health statistics** for planning services.

## 3.13 Confidentiality

Information in records belongs to the patient and must be shared only with people directly involved in care, or as required by law. The nurse must not leave charts open, discuss patients in public places, share passwords or post anything about patients on social media. Medical records are handed over only through the medical records department with proper authorisation. Breaking confidentiality is a breach of ethics and may lead to legal action.

## 3.14 Types of client records / common forms

Admission sheet (identification data), history and physical examination record, doctor's order sheet, **nurses' notes** (progress notes), **graphic (TPR) chart**, intake–output chart, medication administration record (MAR), Kardex (a quick-reference card of current care), care plan, investigation reports, consent forms, operation notes, anaesthesia record, transfer form, discharge summary, incident report (kept separately).

## 3.15 Methods / systems of documentation

| System | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Source-oriented record | Each discipline writes in its own section (doctor's notes, nurses' notes, lab) — traditional, but information is scattered |
| Problem-oriented medical record (POMR) | Organised around the patient's problems: database, problem list, plan, progress notes |
| SOAP / SOAPIE / SOAPIER | Subjective data, Objective data, Assessment, Plan (+ Intervention, Evaluation, Revision) |
| PIE | Problem, Intervention, Evaluation |
| Focus charting (DAR) | Data, Action, Response for each focus (a concern or event) |
| Narrative | Story-like notes in time order — easy to write, hard to search |
| Charting by exception (CBE) | Only abnormal findings written; normal ones checked against set standards |
| Computerised / electronic health records | Fast, legible, accessible; need password protection |
| Flow sheets and checklists | Repeated observations (vital signs, intake–output) in tables |

## 3.16 Guidelines for documentation

Good records are **accurate, complete, concise, current, factual, legible, organised and confidential**.

**Do's**

- Write in blue or black ink (as per hospital policy) or enter electronically.
- Write the date and time of each entry, and sign with name and designation.
- Record facts: what you saw, heard, measured and did, and the patient's response. Quote the patient's own words when useful.
- Record as soon as possible after care; never before it is done.
- Use only approved abbreviations and correct spelling.
- If you make a mistake, draw a single line through it, write "mistaken entry" or "error", initial and date it, and write the correct entry.
- Write a late entry clearly labelled as "late entry" with the actual time.
- Record refusals of treatment and informing the doctor.

**Don'ts**

- Do not erase, use correction fluid, overwrite or tear pages.
- Do not leave blank lines (draw a line through empty space).
- Do not write opinions or labels ("patient is rude", "drunk"); describe behaviour instead.
- Do not chart for someone else or let someone chart for you.
- Do not write "error" without correcting it, or blame colleagues in the notes.
- Do not mention an incident report in the patient's record (record only the facts of what happened and the care given).

**Legal guidelines**: the record is a legal document; what is not recorded may be treated as not done. Keep records safely for the period the hospital and law require.

## 3.17 Reporting

| Report | Purpose and content |
| --- | --- |
| Change-of-shift (handover) report | Given by the outgoing nurse to the incoming nurse, oral (at the bedside or station), written or recorded. Includes identity, diagnosis, condition and changes, vital signs, treatments due, investigations, pending tasks, patient concerns. Bedside handover allows the patient to take part |
| Transfer report | When a patient moves to another unit or hospital: identity, diagnosis, condition, vital signs, medicines last given, allergies, special equipment, family informed, belongings, records sent |
| Incident (occurrence / variance) report | Written report of any unexpected event that harmed or could have harmed a patient, visitor or staff member — fall, medication error, needle-stick injury, equipment failure. Written promptly and factually; used to improve safety, not to punish. Kept separately from the patient's record |
| Telephone report / order | Read back, record time, name of caller, and get signed |

## Remember

- Elements: referent, sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback, context.
- Levels: intrapersonal, interpersonal, transpersonal, small group, public.
- Therapeutic techniques: listening, open questions, silence, clarifying, reflecting, summarising.
- Non-therapeutic: false reassurance, advice, "why" questions, changing the subject, judging.
- Peplau phases: (pre-interaction), orientation, working, termination.
- SBAR = Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation.
- Records are legal documents: date, time, sign; single line through errors; no erasing; no blank spaces.
- Documentation systems: source-oriented, POMR, SOAP(IE), PIE, focus (DAR), narrative, charting by exception, electronic.
- Incident report: factual, prompt, kept out of the patient's chart.
