# Unit 4: Vital signs

INC Unit VI (15 hours theory + 20 hours skill lab): body temperature, pulse, respiration, oxygen saturation and blood pressure — their physiology, how to measure them, their alterations, hot and cold applications, and how to record them. This is the largest unit of the course and the most practical one; it is also a favourite for OSCE.

## 4.1 Vital signs: meaning and guidelines

**Vital signs** (cardinal signs) are measurements that show how well the body's essential functions are working: **temperature, pulse, respiration and blood pressure**. **Oxygen saturation (SpO2)** and **pain** are often called the fifth and sixth vital signs.

**When to take them**: on admission (baseline), as ordered or by ward routine, before and after surgery or an invasive procedure, before and after giving some medicines that affect the heart or breathing, when the patient's condition changes or they say they feel unwell, before and after nursing interventions such as ambulation, and during blood transfusion.

**General guidelines**

1. Know the patient's usual (baseline) values and medical history.
2. Use equipment that is clean, working and of the right size.
3. Control or note factors that change the readings: exercise, eating, smoking, crying, pain, room temperature.
4. Take them in a calm setting with the patient resting and comfortable.
5. Explain what you will do; ask permission.
6. Use a systematic method and measure accurately; repeat if a value seems wrong.
7. Interpret the values against normal ranges and the patient's baseline.
8. Record promptly and report abnormal values or sudden changes at once.

## 4.2 Body temperature: physiology and regulation

**Body temperature** is the balance between heat produced and heat lost. **Core temperature** (deep tissues: abdomen, chest, brain) stays fairly constant; **surface temperature** (skin) varies with the surroundings.

**Heat production**: basal metabolism, muscle activity and shivering, thyroxine and adrenaline (raise metabolism), digestion, fever.

**Heat loss**

| Method | Meaning | Nursing use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radiation | Heat moves from the body surface to cooler objects without contact | Removing heavy blankets in fever |
| Conduction | Heat moves to a cooler object in direct contact | Ice pack, cooling blanket |
| Convection | Heat carried away by moving air | Fan in fever |
| Evaporation | Water (sweat) turns to vapour and takes heat | Tepid sponging |

**Regulation**: the **hypothalamus** acts as the thermostat. Receptors in the skin and core send information to it. When the body is hot, the **anterior hypothalamus** starts heat loss (sweating, vasodilation of skin vessels). When cold, the **posterior hypothalamus** conserves and produces heat (vasoconstriction, shivering, goose flesh, more metabolism).

**Normal values**

| Site | Normal range (adult, approximate) |
| --- | --- |
| Oral | 36.5–37.5 °C (average 37 °C = 98.6 °F) |
| Axillary | About 0.5 °C lower than oral |
| Rectal | About 0.5 °C higher than oral (closest to core) |
| Tympanic | Close to core temperature |

Conversion: °F = (°C × 9/5) + 32; °C = (°F − 32) × 5/9.

**Factors affecting body temperature**: age (newborns regulate poorly; the elderly run lower and may not show fever), **diurnal variation** (lowest early in the morning, highest late afternoon and evening), exercise, hormones (temperature rises slightly after ovulation and in pregnancy), stress and emotions, environment, food and fluids, smoking, illness and medicines.

## 4.3 Assessment of body temperature: sites, equipment and technique

| Site | Advantages | Avoid in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral (under the tongue, in the posterior sublingual pocket) | Easy, comfortable | Infants and young children, unconscious, confused or restless patients, mouth breathers, after oral surgery, seizures, breathlessness; wait 15–30 minutes after hot or cold food, drinks or smoking |
| Axillary | Safe, non-invasive; good for children and unconscious patients | Gives lower readings; needs a dry axilla and correct placement |
| Tympanic (ear canal) | Quick, close to core | Ear infection, ear wax, ear surgery |
| Rectal | Most accurate of these sites | Diarrhoea, rectal surgery or disease, heart patients (vagal stimulation), newborns (risk of perforation); used only when other sites are unsuitable |
| Temporal artery (forehead scanner) | Quick, non-invasive | Sweaty forehead, head covering |

**Equipment**: digital (electronic) thermometer, tympanic thermometer, temporal artery thermometer, disposable chemical-dot thermometer. Glass mercury thermometers are being phased out because mercury is toxic; if one breaks, follow the hospital's mercury spill procedure.

**Procedure: oral temperature with a digital thermometer**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Check the order/routine; wash hands; gather equipment | Prevents infection; saves time |
| 2. Identify the patient; explain the procedure | Right patient; gains co-operation |
| 3. Ask whether the patient has had hot/cold food or drink or smoked in the last 15–30 minutes | These change mouth temperature and give a false reading |
| 4. Put on a clean probe cover; switch on the thermometer | Prevents cross-infection |
| 5. Place the tip under the tongue in the posterior sublingual pocket, to one side | This pocket lies near large blood vessels and reflects core temperature |
| 6. Ask the patient to close the lips (not the teeth) and breathe through the nose | Keeps the probe in place; prevents cooling by air; teeth may break the probe |
| 7. Leave in place until the device signals | Gives an accurate reading |
| 8. Remove, read, discard the probe cover in the correct bin | Prevents spread of micro-organisms |
| 9. Clean the thermometer as per policy; wash hands | Infection control |
| 10. Record on the graphic chart and report abnormal values | Communication and continuity of care |

**Axillary temperature**: dry the axilla (moisture cools the skin), place the tip in the centre of the axilla, lower the arm and fold it across the chest to keep the probe in contact, and wait for the signal. A glass thermometer needs longer in the axilla than in the mouth; follow your procedure manual.

**Tympanic temperature**: gently pull the pinna **up and back** in adults (**down and back** in children under three) to straighten the ear canal; fit the probe snugly so it points at the eardrum; read.

## 4.4 Alterations in body temperature

| Alteration | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Fever (pyrexia)** | Body temperature above normal because pyrogens (from infection, tissue injury, inflammation) **raise the hypothalamic set point** |
| **Hyperthermia** | Body temperature above normal while the set point is unchanged — the body cannot lose heat fast enough (heat stroke, some drug reactions) |
| Hyperpyrexia | Very high fever, above about 41 °C (105.8 °F); dangerous to the brain |
| **Heat cramps** | Painful muscle spasms after heavy sweating and salt loss in heat |
| **Heat exhaustion** | Weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, heavy sweating, cool moist skin, fast weak pulse; caused by fluid and salt loss; temperature normal or moderately raised |
| **Heat stroke** | Failure of heat regulation: very high temperature (above about 40 °C), hot dry skin (sweating may stop), confusion, seizures, unconsciousness — an emergency |
| **Hypothermia** | Core temperature below 35 °C: shivering (which stops as it worsens), pale cold skin, slow pulse and breathing, confusion. Mild 32–35 °C, moderate 28–32 °C, severe below 28 °C |

## 4.5 Fever: definition, causes, stages and types

**Grades of fever** (texts differ slightly): low grade about 37.5–38 °C; moderate 38–39 °C; high 39–41 °C; hyperpyrexia above 41 °C.

**Causes**: infections (bacterial, viral, malarial), tissue injury and surgery, inflammation, dehydration, some cancers, blood transfusion reactions, drug reactions, brain injury affecting the hypothalamus.

**Stages of fever**

| Stage | What happens | Signs | Nursing care |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Onset (invasion, chill phase) | Set point rises; body produces and conserves heat to reach it | Chills, shivering, cold pale skin, goose flesh, fast pulse | Warm blanket, warm drinks, record temperature |
| 2. Course (fastigium, plateau) | Temperature stays at the new high level | Hot flushed skin, thirst, headache, malaise, dry mouth, loss of appetite, restlessness; delirium if high | Remove extra covers, cooling measures, fluids, mouth care, rest |
| 3. Termination (defervescence) | Set point returns to normal; body loses heat | Sweating, flushed skin | Dry and change clothes and linen, fluids, rest |

Defervescence may be by **crisis** (a sudden fall within hours) or by **lysis** (a gradual fall over days).

**Types of fever**

| Type | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Continuous (sustained) | Stays above normal; daily variation less than 1 °C | Typhoid, lobar pneumonia |
| Remittent | Stays above normal but varies by more than 1 °C during the day | Many bacterial infections |
| Intermittent | Present for some hours each day, then returns to normal | Malaria, septicaemia |
| Quotidian, tertian, quartan | Intermittent fever recurring every day, every third day (48 h), every fourth day (72 h) | Types of malaria |
| Relapsing | Days of fever alternate with days of normal temperature | Relapsing fever (Borrelia), Hodgkin's (Pel–Ebstein) |
| Septic (hectic) | Wide swings between very high and normal, with chills and sweats | Abscess, septicaemia |

**Nursing management of a patient with fever**

1. Monitor temperature (every 4 hours, or more often if high), pulse and respiration; record on the graphic chart.
2. Rest in a cool, well-ventilated room; light clothing and bed covers.
3. Plenty of oral fluids unless restricted; record intake and output (fluid is lost in sweat and breathing).
4. Light, nourishing, easily digested diet in small amounts.
5. Cooling measures: tepid sponging, cold compress on the forehead, ice cap (see 4.6).
6. Mouth care (dry mouth, coated tongue), skin care, change of damp linen.
7. Give antipyretics and other medicines as prescribed and check the response.
8. Safety: side rails if delirious; watch children for febrile convulsions.
9. Observe for chills, sweating, dehydration, delirium; report hyperpyrexia at once.

## 4.6 Hot and cold applications

**Effects**

| Heat | Cold |
| --- | --- |
| Vasodilation — more blood flow | Vasoconstriction — less blood flow |
| Relaxes muscles, relieves stiffness and spasm | Numbs nerve endings, reduces pain |
| Speeds healing and suppuration (localises infection) | Reduces swelling and bleeding |
| Increases metabolism and capillary permeability | Slows metabolism and inflammation |
| | Lowers body temperature (tepid sponge) |

**Types**

| | Dry | Moist |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat | Hot water bag, electric heating pad, aquathermia pad, infrared lamp | Hot compress, fomentation, sitz bath, soaks, steam inhalation |
| Cold | Ice cap / ice bag, ice collar, chemical cold pack | Cold compress, tepid sponge, cold soaks |

Moist heat penetrates deeper but burns more easily; dry heat lasts longer but dries the skin.

**Contraindications**

| For heat | For cold |
| --- | --- |
| First hours after an injury (increases swelling and bleeding) | Impaired circulation (Raynaud's disease, peripheral vascular disease) |
| Acute inflammation such as appendicitis (may cause rupture) | Open wounds (delays healing) |
| Active bleeding | Shivering, very young or very old |
| Non-inflammatory oedema | Cold allergy (hypersensitivity) |
| Impaired sensation or confusion; unconscious patients | Impaired sensation |
| Over a malignancy, testes, abdomen in pregnancy | |

**Rebound phenomenon**: the greatest effect is reached in about 20–30 minutes; if heat or cold is left on much longer, the body reacts in the opposite way (for example vessels constrict again under prolonged heat). So applications are usually limited to about 20 minutes and repeated after a rest, as ordered.

**Safety**: check the temperature of water with a bath thermometer (do not rely on touch); for a hot water bag many texts give about 46–52 °C for an adult and lower for children, older adults and unconscious patients — follow your hospital procedure; always cover the bag; check the skin often; never place a bag under a patient; ask the patient to report burning or numbness.

**Procedure: tepid sponging** (to reduce a high temperature by evaporation and conduction)

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Check order; take and record the temperature, pulse and respiration | Baseline for evaluation |
| 2. Explain; give privacy; close windows to avoid draught | Co-operation; dignity; prevents chilling |
| 3. Prepare a basin of lukewarm water a few degrees below body temperature (texts give about 27–34 °C), checked with a bath thermometer, sponge cloths, towels, mackintosh, ice cap | Water slightly cooler than the body removes heat without causing shivering |
| 4. Remove the patient's clothes, cover with a sheet; place mackintosh and towel under the part | Exposes skin for cooling while keeping dignity; protects the bed |
| 5. Put a cold compress or ice cap on the forehead | Cools the head and prevents headache |
| 6. Place wet cloths in the axillae and groins | Large blood vessels lie close to the skin there, so heat loss is faster |
| 7. Sponge face, arms, chest, abdomen, legs and back with long, slow strokes towards the heart, changing cloths often; leave the skin moist | Slow strokes and moisture allow evaporation, which cools; strokes towards the heart aid venous return |
| 8. Continue for about 20–30 minutes; stop if the patient shivers, looks pale or blue, or the pulse becomes weak | Shivering produces heat and defeats the purpose; signs of chilling must be acted on |
| 9. Pat dry, dress in light clothes, make comfortable | Comfort; prevents chilling |
| 10. Recheck the temperature about 30 minutes later; record and report | Evaluates the effect |

**Ice cap**: fill about two-thirds with small pieces of ice (large lumps leave gaps and do not fit the part), expel the air, close, check for leaks, dry, cover with a cloth; apply for about 20 minutes; check the skin; refill as needed.

**Cold compress**: wring out a cloth soaked in cold water, apply to the part (forehead, a sprain), change every few minutes as it warms.

## 4.7 Pulse

**Definition**: the throb of an artery felt over a bony surface, caused by the wave of blood forced into the aorta when the left ventricle contracts.

**Physiology and regulation**: the heartbeat starts in the **sinoatrial (SA) node** (the pacemaker). Each beat sends a stroke volume into the arteries; cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate. The **sympathetic** nerves and adrenaline speed the heart; the **vagus nerve** (parasympathetic) slows it.

**Characteristics of the pulse**

| Characteristic | Normal and abnormal |
| --- | --- |
| Rate (beats per minute) | Adult 60–100. Above 100 = **tachycardia**; below 60 = **bradycardia** (athletes may normally be slower) |
| Rhythm | Regular intervals. Irregular = **dysrhythmia/arrhythmia**. Sinus arrhythmia (rate rises with inspiration) is normal in children |
| Volume (strength) | Graded 0 absent, 1+ weak/thready, 2+ normal, 3+ full/bounding |
| Tension | Resistance of the vessel wall; hard in arteriosclerosis |
| Equality | Pulses on both sides should be equal |

**Normal pulse rates by age (approximate)**: newborn 120–160; infant 100–160; toddler 90–150; school-age child 70–110; adolescent and adult 60–100.

**Factors affecting pulse**: age, sex (women slightly faster), exercise, fever (the rate rises as the temperature rises), emotions and pain, haemorrhage and shock (fast, weak), position (faster on standing), medicines, hypoxia, raised intracranial pressure (slow).

**Pulse sites**

```text
Temporal (in front of the ear)
Carotid (side of neck — never press both at once)
Apical (5th intercostal space, left midclavicular line)
Brachial (inner elbow — infants, BP)
Radial (thumb side of wrist — most common)
Femoral (groin)
Popliteal (back of knee)
Posterior tibial (behind the inner ankle)
Dorsalis pedis (top of the foot)
```

**Procedure: radial pulse**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Wash hands; explain; let the patient rest 5–10 minutes | Activity raises the rate |
| 2. Patient sitting or lying, forearm resting across the chest or at the side, wrist slightly flexed | Relaxes the tendons over the artery so it is easier to feel |
| 3. Place the tips of the first two or three fingers on the radial artery | Fingertips are sensitive; **the thumb is not used** because it has its own pulse |
| 4. Press gently until the pulse is felt | Too much pressure blocks the artery |
| 5. Count for 30 seconds and double it if regular; count a **full minute** if irregular, first assessment, or the patient is very ill | Accuracy |
| 6. Note rhythm, volume and tension | Complete assessment |
| 7. Record and report abnormalities | Communication |

**Apical pulse**: listen with a stethoscope at the 5th intercostal space in the left midclavicular line for a full minute. Used for infants and young children, irregular pulses, heart disease, and before giving some heart medicines.

**Apical–radial pulse and pulse deficit**: two nurses count at the same time for one full minute using one watch — one at the apex, one at the radial artery. **Pulse deficit = apical rate − radial rate.** A deficit means some beats are too weak to reach the wrist (for example in atrial fibrillation).

**Other alterations**: thready (weak, fast, in shock), bounding (strong, in fever or anxiety), pulsus paradoxus (weaker during inspiration), pulsus alternans (alternate strong and weak beats, in heart failure), water-hammer or collapsing pulse (aortic regurgitation).

## 4.8 Respiration

**Definition**: the exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide. It has three parts: **ventilation** (moving air in and out of the lungs), **diffusion** (gas exchange between alveoli and blood) and **perfusion** (blood flow through the lungs).

**Regulation**: respiratory centres in the **medulla oblongata and pons**. The main stimulus is a rise in carbon dioxide (detected by central chemoreceptors); low oxygen stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies.

**Mechanics of breathing**: in **inspiration** (active) the diaphragm contracts and moves down, the external intercostals lift the ribs, the chest enlarges, pressure falls and air flows in. **Expiration** is normally **passive**: the muscles relax and the elastic lungs recoil.

**Characteristics**: rate, depth, rhythm, effort and sounds.

**Normal rates (approximate)**: newborn 30–60; infant 30–50; young child 20–30; adolescent and adult 12–20 breaths per minute.

**Factors affecting respiration**: age, exercise, fever, pain, anxiety, altitude, smoking, anaemia, position, medicines (opioids slow breathing), disease of the lungs and brain.

**Procedure: counting respiration**

1. Count immediately after the pulse **while keeping your fingers on the wrist**, so the patient does not know breathing is being watched (people change their breathing when they know).
2. One breath = one rise and fall of the chest.
3. Count for 30 seconds and double if regular; count a full minute if irregular, in infants, or if abnormal.
4. Note depth, rhythm, effort, use of accessory muscles, colour of skin and lips, and any noise.
5. Record and report.

**Alterations in respiration**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Eupnoea | Normal, quiet breathing |
| Tachypnoea | Rapid breathing (fever, anxiety, pneumonia) |
| Bradypnoea | Slow breathing (opioids, raised intracranial pressure) |
| Apnoea | No breathing |
| Hyperventilation | Fast, deep breathing; blows off CO2 |
| Hypoventilation | Slow, shallow breathing; CO2 builds up |
| Dyspnoea | Difficult, laboured breathing |
| Orthopnoea | Can breathe comfortably only when sitting up (heart failure) |
| Cheyne–Stokes | Cycles of breathing that grow deeper then shallower, followed by apnoea (heart failure, brain damage, near death) |
| Kussmaul | Deep, fast, regular breathing (metabolic acidosis, diabetic ketoacidosis) |
| Biot's | Irregular breaths of varying depth with sudden apnoea (brain injury, meningitis) |
| Stridor | Harsh, high-pitched sound on inspiration (upper airway blockage) |
| Wheeze | Whistling sound, mainly on expiration (asthma) |
| Stertor | Snoring sound (unconscious patient, secretions) |

## 4.9 Arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2)

**SpO2** is the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen, measured without a needle by a **pulse oximeter**, which shines red and infrared light through a finger, toe or earlobe. Normal is about **95–100%**; values below about 90% show significant hypoxaemia and must be reported at once (follow the patient's prescribed target).

**Procedure**: choose a site with good circulation; remove nail polish or artificial nails; warm cold hands; attach the probe so the light source and sensor face each other; keep the hand still; wait for a steady reading and a good pulse wave; compare the displayed pulse with the patient's pulse; record. For continuous monitoring, move a finger probe every few hours to prevent pressure injury.

**Sources of error**: movement, cold or poorly perfused fingers, nail polish, bright external light, severe anaemia, and carbon monoxide poisoning (gives a falsely normal reading).

## 4.10 Blood pressure

**Definitions**

- **Blood pressure**: the force exerted by blood against the walls of the arteries.
- **Systolic pressure**: the highest pressure, during ventricular contraction.
- **Diastolic pressure**: the lowest pressure, when the ventricles relax.
- **Pulse pressure** = systolic − diastolic (normally about 40 mmHg).
- **Mean arterial pressure** ≈ diastolic + one-third of the pulse pressure.

**Physiology and regulation**: BP = cardiac output × peripheral resistance. It also depends on blood volume, blood viscosity and the elasticity of the arteries. It is controlled by **baroreceptors** in the carotid sinus and aortic arch (quick nervous control), the **renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system** and antidiuretic hormone (long-term control through salt and water), and the kidneys.

**Normal values (adult)**: ideally below 120/80 mmHg. **Hypertension** is generally diagnosed when readings are 140/90 mmHg or more on repeated measurements; Indian and international guidelines grade the levels slightly differently. **Hypotension** is usually below 90/60 mmHg. **Orthostatic (postural) hypotension**: a fall of 20 mmHg or more systolic (or 10 mmHg or more diastolic) within a few minutes of standing, with dizziness.

**Factors affecting BP**: age (rises with age), sex, time of day (lowest early morning), exercise, stress, pain, obesity, race and heredity, posture, salt intake, smoking, alcohol, medicines, disease (kidney, heart), blood loss.

**Sites**: brachial artery (usual), radial (forearm), popliteal (thigh — cuff over the thigh, when arms cannot be used), posterior tibial or dorsalis pedis (lower leg).

**Equipment**: sphygmomanometer (aneroid dial, digital/automatic; mercury instruments are being phased out), cuff of correct size, stethoscope. The **bladder of the cuff should be about 40% of the arm's circumference wide and about 80% of it long**.

**Korotkoff sounds**: the sounds heard as the cuff is deflated. The **first clear tapping sound (phase I) = systolic**; the **point where sounds disappear (phase V) = diastolic** in adults. A silent period between them in some hypertensive patients is the **auscultatory gap**; missing it gives a falsely low systolic reading.

**Procedure: brachial blood pressure (two-step method)**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Patient rested for about 5 minutes; no caffeine, smoking or exercise in the previous 30 minutes; bladder empty | These raise BP temporarily |
| 2. Seated with back supported, feet flat, legs uncrossed, arm bared and supported at heart level, palm up | Arm below heart level gives a falsely high reading; above gives falsely low |
| 3. Avoid an arm with an IV line, AV fistula, injury, or on the side of a mastectomy | Prevents harm and false readings |
| 4. Choose the correct cuff; wrap it snugly 2–3 cm above the elbow crease with the bladder centre over the brachial artery | Too small a cuff gives a falsely high reading; too large gives falsely low; correct position compresses the artery evenly |
| 5. Palpate the radial artery, inflate until the pulse disappears, and note this reading; deflate | Estimates systolic pressure and avoids missing an auscultatory gap |
| 6. Wait about 30 seconds | Lets blood flow return to normal |
| 7. Place the stethoscope lightly over the brachial artery (not under the cuff) | Pressing hard distorts the sounds |
| 8. Inflate to about 30 mmHg above the palpated reading | Ensures the artery is fully closed |
| 9. Release the valve so the needle falls about 2–3 mmHg per second | Too fast deflation gives an inaccurate (often low systolic) reading |
| 10. Note the first sound (systolic) and the point where sounds disappear (diastolic) | Korotkoff phases I and V |
| 11. Deflate fully; remove the cuff; wait 1–2 minutes before repeating | Venous congestion gives false readings |
| 12. Record (for example 118/76 mmHg, right arm, sitting); report abnormal values | Communication; site and position affect the value |

**Common errors in BP assessment**

| Error | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Cuff too narrow or too loose | Falsely high |
| Cuff too wide | Falsely low |
| Arm below heart level | Falsely high |
| Arm above heart level | Falsely low |
| Deflating too quickly | Systolic falsely low, diastolic falsely high |
| Deflating too slowly | Venous congestion; diastolic falsely high |
| Repeating too soon | Falsely high systolic |
| Missing the auscultatory gap | Systolic falsely low |
| Eye not level with the dial or mercury | Reading error |
| Patient talking, anxious or after exercise | Falsely high |

**Alterations**: hypertension (headache, nosebleed, but often no symptoms — "silent"), hypotension (dizziness, fainting, pale cold skin in shock), orthostatic hypotension (after bed rest, in the elderly, with some medicines — help such patients to sit, then stand slowly).

## 4.11 Documenting vital signs and care of equipment

- Record on the **graphic (TPR) chart** at the correct time column; plot temperature and pulse as dots joined by lines in the colours your hospital uses (commonly blue for temperature and red for pulse); write respiration, BP and SpO2 in the spaces provided.
- Note the site and method (oral, axillary; right arm, lying) when relevant.
- Write abnormal findings and the action taken in the nurses' notes, and report them.
- Use the hospital's early-warning score if one is in use, and call for help as the score requires.

**Care of equipment**: clean digital thermometers with an alcohol swab and use probe covers; clean the stethoscope earpieces and diaphragm with alcohol between patients; keep BP cuffs clean and fully deflated, store the tubing without kinks and send instruments for regular checking (calibration); wipe pulse oximeter probes between patients.

## Remember

- Normal adult: temperature 36.5–37.5 °C (oral), pulse 60–100/min, respiration 12–20/min, BP below 120/80 mmHg, SpO2 95–100%.
- Fever = raised set point (pyrogens); hyperthermia = normal set point but heat loss fails.
- Stages of fever: onset (chill), course (fastigium), defervescence (crisis or lysis).
- Continuous (<1 °C variation, typhoid), remittent (>1 °C, never normal), intermittent (returns to normal, malaria).
- Tepid sponge: lukewarm water, axillae and groins, long strokes, stop if shivering, recheck after about 30 minutes.
- Heat dilates and relaxes; cold constricts, numbs and reduces swelling; about 20 minutes (rebound).
- Never take a pulse with your thumb. Apical pulse: 5th intercostal space, left midclavicular line, full minute.
- Pulse deficit = apical − radial (two nurses, one watch).
- Count respiration with fingers still on the wrist.
- Cuff bladder 40% × 80% of arm circumference; narrow cuff → falsely high; deflate 2–3 mmHg per second.
- Korotkoff I = systolic, V = diastolic; the auscultatory gap is why we palpate first.
