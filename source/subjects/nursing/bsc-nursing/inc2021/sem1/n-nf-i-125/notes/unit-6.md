# Unit 6: Comfort, rest and sleep, and pain

INC Unit IX (15 hours theory + 15 hours skill lab): comfort (factors, beds and bed making, therapeutic positions, comfort devices), sleep and rest, and pain (physiology, causes, types, assessment, and drug and non-drug relief, including complementary therapies). Bed making and positions are common OSCE stations.

## 6.1 Comfort

**Comfort** is a state of ease and well-being, physical and mental, in which a person is free of pain and distress. Kolcaba described three forms — **relief** (a specific discomfort is removed), **ease** (calm and contentment) and **transcendence** (rising above problems that cannot be removed) — in four contexts: physical, psychospiritual, social and environmental.

**Factors influencing comfort**

| Group | Factors |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | Pain, nausea, itching, breathlessness, fever, wet or wrinkled linen, uncomfortable position, hunger, a full bladder |
| Psychological | Anxiety, fear, loneliness, embarrassment, lack of information |
| Environmental | Noise, light, temperature, odour, ventilation, cleanliness, crowding |
| Social and cultural | Family support, privacy, respect for customs, food habits |
| Spiritual | Faith, meaning, chance to pray |

Nursing measures: relieve pain, give a clean and well-made bed, correct position and support, hygiene, a calm environment, explanation, privacy, emotional support, and respect for beliefs.

## 6.2 Hospital beds

**Types of beds**

| Bed | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Ordinary (standard) hospital bed | General patients; height adjustable in many models |
| Fowler's bed / backrest bed | Head end can be raised |
| Electric / motorised bed | Height, head and knee sections adjusted by remote — less strain on staff and patient |
| ICU / critical care bed | Multiple positions, side rails, weighing scale, X-ray friendly, CPR release lever |
| Low bed (floor bed) | Patients at high risk of falling |
| Air-fluidised bed, low-air-loss mattress, alternating-pressure air mattress | Prevention and treatment of pressure ulcers, burns |
| Water bed | Spreads pressure evenly |
| Turning frames (e.g. Stryker frame) | Turning patients with spinal injury without bending the spine |
| Bariatric bed | Very heavy patients |
| Paediatric cot / crib | Infants and children, with high sides |

**Purposes of bed making**: comfort and rest; a clean, safe surface; prevent pressure ulcers (smooth, dry, wrinkle-free linen); economise time and linen; keep the ward neat; prepare for a specific patient need (post-operative, fracture, cardiac).

**Principles of bed making**

1. Wash hands; collect all linen in order of use before starting — saves time and energy.
2. Use good body mechanics: raise the bed to working height, wide base, work on one side at a time.
3. Never shake linen, and do not let it touch your uniform or the floor — prevents spread of micro-organisms.
4. Put soiled linen directly into the soiled-linen bag, never on the floor or another bed.
5. Make linen tight and wrinkle-free with **mitred corners** — wrinkles cause pressure and discomfort.
6. Keep the patient covered and warm; give privacy.
7. Finish by lowering the bed, raising side rails if needed, and placing the call bell in reach.

**Mitred (square) corner**: tuck the bottom of the sheet under the mattress at the foot; lift the side edge to form a triangle on the top of the mattress; tuck the hanging lower part under; bring the triangle down and tuck it in. It keeps the sheet firm and neat.

**Types of bed making**

| Type | When used | Key points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Closed bed** | Unoccupied bed waiting for a new admission, or after terminal cleaning | Top sheet and blanket pulled up to the head and covered by the bedspread; keeps the bed clean |
| **Open bed** | Unoccupied bed of a patient who is out of bed (ambulant) or newly admitted and ready to get in | Top covers fan-folded to the foot so the patient can get in easily |
| **Occupied bed** | Patient cannot get out of bed | Patient turned to one side; old linen rolled to the middle, new linen placed on the free half; patient rolled over the "hump" to the clean side; other half completed. Patient covered with a bath blanket throughout |
| **Post-operative (anaesthetic / recovery) bed** | Receiving a patient from the operation theatre | Top covers fan-folded to the far side (or the foot) for an easy transfer; mackintosh and draw sheet at the head end to protect from vomit; pillows removed or placed upright at the head to protect from injury; equipment at hand — kidney tray, tongue depressor, mouth gag, suction, oxygen, BP apparatus, airway, warm blankets |
| **Cardiac bed** | Heart failure, breathlessness | Backrest and pillows so the patient sits up (Fowler's or orthopnoeic position); cardiac (over-bed) table with a pillow to lean on; knee support and footrest to prevent sliding |
| **Fracture bed** | Fractures of the spine, pelvis, lower limbs | **Fracture boards** placed under the mattress so it does not sag; bed cradle to keep the weight of covers off the limb; traction equipment if ordered |
| **Amputation bed** | After amputation of a lower limb | Divided top covers so the stump can be watched for bleeding; bed cradle; sandbags; tourniquet kept at hand as per protocol |
| **Burns bed** | Burns | Sterile linen, bed cradle to keep covers off the burnt area, warm room |

**Procedure: occupied bed (main steps with rationale)**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Check the patient's condition and any restrictions (drains, traction, position) | Prevents harm |
| 2. Explain; screen; close windows | Co-operation, privacy, prevents chill |
| 3. Arrange clean linen on a chair in order of use | Saves time |
| 4. Loosen linen all round; replace the top sheet with a bath blanket | Keeps the patient covered and warm |
| 5. Turn the patient to the far side, with the side rail up on that side | Safety |
| 6. Roll soiled linen towards the patient's back; clean and dry the mattress; place clean sheet and draw sheet on the free half and roll the rest towards the patient | Works one half at a time |
| 7. Turn the patient over the roll to the clean side; remove the soiled linen into the linen bag; pull the clean linen through and tuck it tightly | Wrinkle-free bottom sheet prevents pressure areas |
| 8. Change the pillow cover; place the top sheet and blanket; remove the bath blanket from beneath; make a toe pleat | Toe pleat leaves room for the feet and prevents foot drop |
| 9. Make the patient comfortable; bed low, side rails up as needed, call bell in reach | Safety and comfort |
| 10. Remove linen bag; wash hands; record | Infection control; communication |

## 6.3 Therapeutic positions

| Position | Description | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Supine (dorsal)** | Lying flat on the back, head on a pillow | Rest, examination of the front of the body, after spinal anaesthesia (as ordered) |
| **Dorsal recumbent** | On the back with knees bent and feet flat on the bed | Perineal care, vaginal examination, catheterisation of women, inserting a rectal tube |
| **Fowler's** | Head of bed raised: low 15–30°, semi 30–45°, high 60–90° | Breathlessness, heart failure, after thyroid and abdominal surgery, feeding, drainage of the abdomen |
| **Orthopnoeic** | Sitting up, leaning forward over an over-bed table with a pillow | Severe breathlessness (asthma, heart failure) — lets the chest expand fully |
| **Prone** | Lying on the abdomen, head turned to one side | Examination of the back, after some spine surgery, drainage from the mouth, some respiratory distress protocols |
| **Lateral (side-lying)** | On the side with upper hip and knee flexed | Rest, relieving pressure on the back and sacrum, giving an enema (left lateral) |
| **Sims' (semi-prone)** | Left side with the right knee drawn up towards the chest, left arm behind the back | Enemas, rectal examination, vaginal examination, unconscious patient (drainage of secretions) |
| **Trendelenburg** | Bed tilted so the head is lower than the feet | Some pelvic surgery; formerly for shock (now raise legs only, as ordered) |
| **Reverse Trendelenburg** | Feet lower than the head | Reduces reflux, some neck and head surgery |
| **Lithotomy** | On the back with hips and knees flexed and legs in stirrups | Childbirth, vaginal and perineal procedures, cystoscopy |
| **Knee–chest (genupectoral)** | Kneeling with chest on the bed, hips raised | Rectal examination, sigmoidoscopy, to relieve pressure of a prolapsed cord (as ordered) |

**Guidelines**: change position at least every 2 hours for a bed-bound patient; support joints in good alignment; pad bony prominences; check skin and comfort; explain and give privacy; record the position and time.

## 6.4 Comfort devices

| Device | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Pillows | Support, alignment, elevation of a limb, comfort |
| Backrest | Supports the patient in a sitting or Fowler's position |
| Cardiac (over-bed) table | Support for leaning forward (orthopnoea), meals, writing |
| Bed cradle | Keeps the weight of bed covers off the legs or a burn or fracture site |
| Footboard / foot rest | Keeps feet at a right angle, prevents foot drop and sliding down the bed |
| Sandbags | Support a limb and prevent rotation; keep a part still |
| Trochanter roll | Rolled towel along the thigh to prevent external rotation of the hip |
| Hand roll | Keeps fingers in a functional position |
| Air cushion / ring cushion | Relieves pressure (ring cushions are now discouraged for pressure areas as they can reduce blood flow) |
| Special mattresses | Air, water, foam — spread weight to prevent pressure ulcers |
| Knee rest / bolster | Supports the knees, prevents sliding |
| Side rails | Safety |

## 6.5 Sleep and rest

**Rest** is a state of mental and physical calmness and relaxation. **Sleep** is a natural, recurring state of reduced awareness and response, from which a person can be woken.

**Physiology of sleep**: sleep is controlled by the **reticular activating system** in the brainstem (keeps us awake) and the **bulbar synchronising region** (promotes sleep). The **circadian rhythm** (about 24-hour body clock), set by daylight through the hypothalamus and the hormone **melatonin** from the pineal gland, decides when we feel sleepy.

**Stages of sleep**: sleep has two kinds, **non-REM** and **REM** (rapid eye movement).

| Stage | Features |
| --- | --- |
| NREM stage 1 (N1) | Very light sleep, a few minutes; easily woken; drifting feeling |
| NREM stage 2 (N2) | Light sleep; body relaxes further; heart rate and temperature fall |
| NREM stage 3 (N3, deep or slow-wave sleep) | Deepest sleep; hard to wake; body repair, growth hormone release; sleep-walking and bed-wetting happen here |
| REM sleep | Vivid dreams, rapid eye movements, muscles very relaxed, irregular pulse and breathing; important for memory and mood |

```text
Awake → N1 → N2 → N3 → N2 → REM → N2 → N3 … (one cycle ≈ 90 minutes; 4–6 cycles a night)
REM periods get longer towards morning.
```

**Sleep needs by age (approximate)**: newborns sleep most of the day (about 16 hours), children about 10–12 hours, adolescents about 8–10, adults about 7–8, older adults similar but with lighter, more broken sleep.

**Factors affecting sleep**: illness (pain, breathlessness, frequent urination), hospital environment (noise, lights, procedures at night, strange bed), anxiety and depression, lifestyle (shift work, late meals, caffeine, alcohol, smoking), medicines (diuretics, steroids, sedatives — sedatives can reduce REM sleep), exercise and fatigue, diet, age.

**Promoting rest and sleep**

1. Assess the patient's usual sleep pattern and bedtime routine.
2. Keep the environment quiet, dark and at a comfortable temperature; dim lights at night; group night-time care so the patient is disturbed less.
3. Encourage a bedtime routine: warm bath, light snack or warm milk, prayer, reading.
4. Relieve pain and discomfort before sleep; comfortable position, clean dry bed, empty bladder.
5. Avoid caffeine and heavy meals in the evening; limit daytime naps.
6. Relaxation techniques: deep breathing, back rub (massage), soft music.
7. Reduce anxiety by explaining and listening.
8. Give sleep medicines only as prescribed and observe their effect.

**Sleep disorders**

| Disorder | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Insomnia | Difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early; the most common |
| Hypersomnia | Excessive sleep or daytime sleepiness |
| Narcolepsy | Sudden, uncontrollable attacks of sleep during the day, sometimes with sudden muscle weakness (cataplexy) |
| Sleep apnoea | Repeated pauses in breathing during sleep; loud snoring, daytime sleepiness; obstructive type is common in obese people |
| Sleep deprivation | Not enough sleep — irritability, poor concentration, confusion |
| Parasomnias | Unusual behaviour during sleep: sleep-walking (somnambulism), night terrors, nightmares, bed-wetting (enuresis), teeth grinding (bruxism), sleep talking |

## 6.6 Pain

**Pain** is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage. Margo McCaffery's nursing definition: pain is **whatever the person says it is, existing whenever they say it does**. The nurse therefore **believes the patient's report**.

**Physiology of pain (nociception)**

| Process | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Transduction | Injury releases chemicals (prostaglandins, bradykinin, histamine, substance P) that stimulate pain receptors (nociceptors) |
| Transmission | The impulse travels along A-delta fibres (fast, sharp, well-localised pain) and C fibres (slow, dull, aching pain) to the spinal cord and up to the thalamus and brain |
| Perception | The brain (cortex) becomes aware of pain and its meaning |
| Modulation | The body changes the signal — endorphins and descending pathways reduce it |

**Gate control theory** (Melzack and Wall, 1965): a "gate" in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord controls how many pain signals reach the brain. Large-fibre stimulation (rubbing, massage, TENS, heat and cold) and positive thoughts close the gate; anxiety and fear open it. This explains why non-drug measures work.

**Common causes of pain**: tissue injury (trauma, surgery, burns), inflammation, infection, ischaemia (angina), obstruction or stretching of hollow organs (renal stone, distended bladder), muscle spasm, nerve damage, tumours, and psychological factors.

**Types of pain**

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Duration | **Acute** (sudden, short, a known cause, protective; fast pulse, sweating) and **chronic** (lasts beyond about three months or beyond normal healing; may cause depression, fatigue) |
| Origin | **Cutaneous** (skin, sharp, burning), **deep somatic** (bones, muscles, diffuse), **visceral** (organs, poorly localised, cramping) |
| Spread | **Radiating** (spreads from the source), **referred** (felt away from the source — heart pain in the left arm, gallbladder pain in the right shoulder) |
| Cause | **Nociceptive** (tissue damage), **neuropathic** (nerve damage — burning, shooting), **phantom** (felt in an amputated part), **psychogenic** (no physical cause found, but the pain is real), **intractable** (resists treatment) |

## 6.7 Pain assessment

Ask about pain at every assessment (the "fifth vital sign"). A common memory aid is **PQRST**:

| Letter | Question |
| --- | --- |
| P — Provoking / palliating | What brings it on, makes it worse or better? |
| Q — Quality | What does it feel like — sharp, dull, burning, throbbing? |
| R — Region / radiation | Where is it? Does it spread? |
| S — Severity | How bad is it on a scale of 0 to 10? |
| T — Timing | When did it start, how long does it last, is it constant or on and off? |

Also note its effect on sleep, appetite, mood and activity, what the patient has tried, and **non-verbal signs**: facial grimace, guarding, crying, restlessness, rapid pulse, raised BP, sweating.

**Pain scales**

| Scale | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Numeric rating scale (NRS) | 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible); adults who can count |
| Visual analogue scale (VAS) | A 10 cm line; patient marks the point matching their pain |
| Verbal descriptor scale | None, mild, moderate, severe, very severe |
| Wong–Baker FACES scale | Faces from smiling to crying; children (about 3 years and older), people with language barriers |
| FLACC scale | Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability — for young children and non-verbal patients |
| Behavioural scales | For confused, sedated or unconscious patients |

**Sedation ("narcotic") scales**: patients receiving opioid (narcotic) pain medicines are also checked for sedation, because excess sedation comes before breathing slows. A commonly used tool is the **Pasero Opioid-induced Sedation Scale**: S = normal sleep, easy to wake; 1 = awake and alert; 2 = slightly drowsy, easily roused; 3 = frequently drowsy, drifts off during conversation; 4 = very sleepy, minimal or no response. Levels 3 and 4 are unacceptable — stop the opioid, stay with the patient, check breathing and SpO2, and inform the doctor at once. Respiratory rate and depth are recorded with the sedation level.

## 6.8 Pain-relieving measures

**A. Pharmacological (drug) measures** — given only as prescribed. The **WHO analgesic ladder** guides cancer pain: step 1 non-opioid (paracetamol, NSAIDs) ± adjuvant; step 2 weak opioid ± non-opioid ± adjuvant; step 3 strong opioid ± non-opioid ± adjuvant. Adjuvants include some antidepressants and anticonvulsants for nerve pain. Giving analgesics **by the clock** (regularly) controls continuous pain better than waiting until pain returns.

**Use of narcotics (opioids)**: they are effective for severe pain but can cause sedation, respiratory depression, constipation, nausea and itching, and they are controlled drugs.

- Check the prescription, the patient's pain and sedation level and respiratory rate before each dose.
- Store them under double lock; keep a register; count the stock at each shift; a second nurse witnesses giving and discarding of unused drug, as per hospital and legal rules (the NDPS Act controls narcotic drugs in India).
- Prevent constipation with fluids, fibre and prescribed laxatives.
- Fear of addiction should not stop proper pain relief; addiction is uncommon when opioids are used correctly for pain.

**Principles of safe medicine administration (the "rights")**: every pain medicine is given with the rights checked — **right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, right documentation**, and also the right reason, right response (evaluate the effect) and the patient's right to know and to refuse. Doses are always as prescribed; nurses never decide them.

**Routes used for pain relief**: oral (first choice when the patient can swallow), sublingual or buccal, rectal, transdermal (patch), subcutaneous, intramuscular, intravenous, epidural and intrathecal (spinal), and local or regional (nerve blocks, local anaesthetic infiltration). After a dose, reassess pain (commonly about 30 minutes after an injection and about an hour after an oral dose, or as per protocol) and record the response.

**Patient-controlled analgesia (PCA)**: a programmed pump that lets the patient press a button to receive a small preset dose of an analgesic, usually intravenously. A **lockout interval** prevents another dose too soon, and a maximum limit is set. Benefits: quick relief, patient control, less anxiety. Only the patient should press the button (not relatives — risk of overdose). Monitor pain, sedation, breathing and the pump record.

**TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation)**: a small battery device sends mild electrical currents through skin electrodes placed near the painful area. It works by the gate control mechanism and possibly endorphin release. Used for back pain, post-operative pain and labour pain. Not used over the front of the neck, eyes, broken skin, or in people with a pacemaker.

**Invasive techniques of pain management**: epidural analgesia (catheter in the epidural space), intrathecal (spinal) analgesia, nerve blocks with local anaesthetic, local infiltration of wounds, and, rarely for intractable pain, surgical procedures that interrupt nerve pathways. Nurses monitor the insertion site, sensation and movement of the legs, BP, breathing and sedation.

**Newer measures**: wound catheters that infuse local anaesthetic, long-acting local anaesthetic preparations, nerve stimulators, virtual reality distraction, and structured pain management services.

**B. Non-pharmacological measures**

| Measure | How it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Positioning, support, a comfortable bed | Removes pressure and strain |
| Heat and cold applications | Relax muscles / numb and reduce swelling |
| Massage (back rub) | Relaxation; closes the "gate" |
| Distraction (music, TV, conversation, games) | Shifts attention from pain |
| Relaxation and deep breathing | Reduces muscle tension and anxiety |
| Guided imagery | Patient imagines a pleasant scene |
| Splinting the wound when coughing | Reduces pull on the incision |
| Information and reassurance | Reduces fear, which magnifies pain |
| Presence and touch | Comfort and trust |

**C. Complementary and alternative therapies (CAM)**: therapies used alongside (complementary) or instead of (alternative) standard treatment — **yoga, meditation, acupressure, acupuncture, aromatherapy, music therapy, therapeutic touch, reflexology, hypnosis**, and **AYUSH** systems (Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Homoeopathy). The nurse asks about their use, supports safe practices the patient values, and tells the doctor about any herbal products (some interact with medicines).

## Remember

- Comfort: relief, ease, transcendence.
- Closed bed = waiting for admission; open bed = ambulant patient; occupied bed = patient in bed; post-operative bed = covers fan-folded, vomit protection, emergency equipment ready; fracture bed = fracture boards and bed cradle; cardiac bed = sitting up with backrest and cardiac table.
- Mitred corner keeps sheets tight; never shake linen.
- Fowler's: low 15–30°, semi 30–45°, high 60–90°. Sims' = left side, right knee up (enema). Lithotomy = legs in stirrups. Dorsal recumbent = knees bent, feet flat.
- Sleep: NREM N1–N3 + REM; cycle about 90 minutes; melatonin and circadian rhythm.
- Pain is what the patient says it is; assess with PQRST and a scale (NRS, FACES, FLACC).
- Gate control theory (Melzack and Wall, 1965): massage, TENS, heat and cold close the gate.
- WHO ladder: non-opioid → weak opioid → strong opioid, ± adjuvants.
- Opioids: check pain, sedation and breathing; double-lock storage; register.
- PCA: lockout interval; only the patient presses the button.
- Medication rights: patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation (+ reason, response, right to refuse).
