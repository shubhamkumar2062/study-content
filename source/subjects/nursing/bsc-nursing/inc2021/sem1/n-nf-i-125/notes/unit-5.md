# Unit 5: Equipment and linen, infection control and a safe environment

INC Unit VII (3 hours): equipment and linen. INC Unit VIII (10 hours theory + 3 skill lab): infection control in the clinical setting and biomedical waste management. INC Unit X (5 hours theory + 3 skill lab): promoting safety in the health care environment. They are joined because all three are about keeping the patient's surroundings clean, safe and well supplied.

## 5.1 Hospital equipment and linen: types

Equipment is either **disposable** (single use, then discarded — syringes, gloves, catheters, probe covers) or **reusable** (cleaned, disinfected or sterilised and used again — instruments, bedpans, BP cuffs).

| Type | Examples | Care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Linen | Sheets, draw sheets, pillow covers, blankets, towels, gowns, patient clothes, screens | Count and send soiled linen in the correct bag; infected linen in a separate labelled bag; never shake linen (spreads dust and micro-organisms); store clean linen in a closed, dry cupboard |
| Rubber goods | Mackintosh, hot water bags, ice caps, air cushions, tubing, gloves | Wash in cold or lukewarm water (heat damages rubber), dry both sides, powder lightly, store flat or hanging without folds, away from heat, oil and sharp objects; hot water bags stored with a little air inside so the sides do not stick |
| Glassware | Measuring jars, specimen bottles, glass syringes (now rare) | Rinse in cold water first (hot water sets protein), wash, dry, store safely; broken glass into the blue box |
| Metal articles | Instruments, kidney trays, bowls, bedpans | Rinse in cold water, clean with detergent and brush, dry well to prevent rusting; lubricate hinges; sterilise as required; never mix sharp instruments with heavy articles |
| Enamel ware | Bowls, trays | Avoid knocking (chips cause rust) |
| Plastics | Bowls, jugs, bedpans, tubing | Wash with warm (not boiling) water and detergent; many cannot be autoclaved |
| Furniture | Beds, lockers, over-bed tables, chairs, stretchers, wheelchairs | Damp dust daily, check wheels and brakes, report repairs |

## 5.2 Indent, inventory and maintenance

- **Indent**: a written request for supplies from the store or pharmacy, usually on a fixed day; it lists the item, quantity in stock and quantity needed, and is signed by the in-charge.
- **Inventory**: a record of all equipment and linen in the ward, checked regularly (stock book); missing or broken items are reported; old items are written off (condemned) by the proper procedure.
- **Maintenance**: **preventive** (regular cleaning, checking and servicing to avoid breakdown) and **corrective** (repair after breakdown). Faulty equipment is labelled "not for use" and sent for repair.
- Rules: use equipment only for its purpose, return borrowed items, keep a fixed place for each article, teach staff to handle items correctly.

## 5.3 Nature of infection

**Infection** is the entry and multiplication of micro-organisms in the body that causes harm. **Colonisation** is the presence of micro-organisms without harm. A **pathogen** causes disease; **virulence** is its power to do so.

## 5.4 Chain of infection

Infection spreads only when all six links are present. Breaking **any one link** stops it.

```text
   1. Infectious agent ──► 2. Reservoir ──► 3. Portal of exit
          ▲                                          │
          │                                          ▼
   6. Susceptible host ◄── 5. Portal of entry ◄── 4. Mode of transmission
```

| Link | Examples | How the nurse breaks it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infectious agent | Bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites | Prompt diagnosis and treatment, disinfection, sterilisation |
| Reservoir | People, animals, food, water, soil, equipment, wet surfaces | Hygiene, clean environment, dry equipment, change dressings |
| Portal of exit | Respiratory secretions, blood, urine, faeces, wound drainage | Cover mouth when coughing, careful handling of body fluids, cover wounds |
| Mode of transmission | Contact (direct, indirect), droplet, airborne, vehicle (food, water, blood), vector (mosquito) | **Hand hygiene**, PPE, isolation, safe food and water |
| Portal of entry | Broken skin, mucous membranes, respiratory, urinary, GI tracts, catheters | Aseptic technique, catheter care, skin care |
| Susceptible host | Very young, elderly, malnourished, immunosuppressed, diabetic, after surgery | Nutrition, immunisation, rest, treat underlying disease |

## 5.5 Types and stages of infection

**Types**: local (in one area, a boil) and systemic (spreads through the body, septicaemia); acute and chronic; primary and secondary (a new infection on top of the first); exogenous (from outside) and endogenous (from the patient's own flora); communicable; **iatrogenic** (caused by a treatment or procedure); **opportunistic** (caused by organisms that are normally harmless, in a weak host).

**Stages of infection**

| Stage | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Incubation period | From entry of the organism to the first symptoms; the organism multiplies |
| Prodromal stage | Vague, general symptoms (tiredness, mild fever); the person is often infectious |
| Illness (full stage) | Specific signs and symptoms of the disease |
| Convalescence | Symptoms subside and the person recovers |

## 5.6 Factors increasing susceptibility to infection

Extremes of age, poor nutrition, stress and fatigue, chronic diseases (diabetes, cancer, kidney failure), immunosuppression (steroids, chemotherapy, HIV), broken skin (wounds, burns), invasive devices (catheters, IV lines, ventilators), long hospital stay, inadequate immunisation, smoking.

## 5.7 Body defences against infection

1. **First line (non-specific barriers)**: intact skin, mucous membranes, cilia, tears, saliva and lysozyme, stomach acid, normal flora, flushing by urine, coughing and sneezing.
2. **Second line — the inflammatory response**: when tissue is injured, blood vessels dilate and become leaky; white cells (neutrophils, macrophages) move in and engulf organisms (phagocytosis). Five signs: **redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function**. Stages: vascular and cellular response → inflammatory exudate (serous, purulent, sanguineous) → tissue repair. Fever is also part of the defence.
3. **Third line — the immune response**: specific defence by lymphocytes. **Humoral immunity** (B cells make antibodies) and **cell-mediated immunity** (T cells). Immunity may be natural or acquired, active (infection, vaccine) or passive (maternal antibodies, ready-made antibodies).

## 5.8 Health care associated (nosocomial) infection

A **health care associated infection (HAI)** is an infection a patient gets while receiving care in a health facility that was not present or incubating at admission — usually appearing **48 hours or more after admission**, or within a set time after discharge or surgery.

**Common HAIs**: catheter-associated urinary tract infection, surgical site infection, ventilator-associated pneumonia, central-line bloodstream infection, and diarrhoea.

**Causes**: poor hand hygiene, invasive devices, contaminated equipment and environment, overuse of antibiotics (resistant organisms), overcrowding, weak patients.

**Prevention**: hand hygiene, standard precautions, aseptic technique, care bundles for catheters and lines, early removal of devices, environmental cleaning, sterilisation of instruments, safe injection practice, staff immunisation, surveillance by the hospital infection control committee and infection control nurse.

## 5.9 Asepsis: medical and surgical

**Asepsis** means freedom from disease-causing micro-organisms.

| | Medical asepsis (clean technique) | Surgical asepsis (sterile technique) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aim | Reduce the number and spread of micro-organisms | Keep an area or object free of **all** micro-organisms, including spores |
| Used for | Daily care, oral medicines, feeding, bed making | Surgery, catheterisation, injections, dressings of surgical wounds |
| Methods | Hand hygiene, clean gloves, cleaning, disinfection, isolation | Sterile gloves, gowns, instruments, sterile field |

**Definitions**: **cleaning** removes dirt and organic matter; **disinfection** destroys most pathogens but not all spores (for example with alcohol, chlorine, boiling); **sterilisation** destroys all micro-organisms including spores (autoclave — steam under pressure, commonly 121 °C at 15 lb/in² for at least 15 minutes; dry heat; ethylene oxide; plasma). An **antiseptic** is used on living tissue; a **disinfectant** on objects.

**Principles of surgical asepsis**: a sterile object stays sterile only when touched by another sterile object; only sterile items go on a sterile field; a sterile item out of sight or below waist level is considered contaminated; prolonged exposure to air contaminates; moisture passing through a sterile wrapper contaminates by capillary action (strike-through); the 2.5 cm (1 inch) edge of a sterile field is considered unsterile; if in doubt, consider it unsterile.

## 5.10 Hand hygiene

The single most important way to prevent the spread of infection.

**WHO's five moments for hand hygiene**: (1) before touching a patient, (2) before a clean or aseptic procedure, (3) after body fluid exposure risk, (4) after touching a patient, (5) after touching the patient's surroundings.

**Hand rub** (alcohol-based, about 20–30 seconds) is used when hands are not visibly dirty. **Hand washing** with soap and water (about 40–60 seconds) is used when hands are visibly soiled, after using the toilet, and after caring for patients with diarrhoea (for example *Clostridioides difficile*), because alcohol does not kill spores.

**Procedure: hand washing**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Remove rings and watch; nails short; sleeves above the elbows | Micro-organisms collect under jewellery and long nails |
| 2. Wet hands with running water; apply enough soap to cover all surfaces | Running water carries organisms away |
| 3. Rub palm to palm | Friction removes organisms |
| 4. Right palm over the back of the left hand with fingers interlaced, and vice versa | Cleans the backs of the hands |
| 5. Palm to palm with fingers interlaced | Cleans between the fingers |
| 6. Backs of fingers to opposing palms with fingers interlocked | Cleans the knuckles |
| 7. Rotational rubbing of each thumb clasped in the other palm | Thumbs are often missed |
| 8. Rotational rubbing of the fingertips in the other palm, backwards and forwards | Cleans fingertips and nails |
| 9. Rinse with hands held so water runs from fingertips to wrists or as per policy; dry with a single-use towel | Removes soap and loosened organisms; wet hands spread organisms more easily |
| 10. Turn off the tap with the towel (or elbow) | Prevents re-contaminating clean hands |

## 5.11 Personal protective equipment (PPE)

PPE includes gloves, gowns or aprons, masks (surgical mask, N95 respirator), eye protection (goggles, face shield), caps and shoe covers. It is chosen by the **expected exposure**, not the diagnosis.

**Order of putting on (donning)**: hand hygiene → gown → mask or respirator → goggles or face shield → gloves (pulled over the gown cuffs).

**Order of taking off (doffing)**: gloves → goggles or face shield → gown → mask or respirator (by the ties, without touching the front) → hand hygiene. (Some protocols remove the gown and gloves together.) The outside of PPE is contaminated; remove it at the doorway and discard it in the correct bin.

## 5.12 Standard precautions

**Standard precautions** are applied to **all patients, at all times**, because any blood or body fluid may be infectious. They include:

1. Hand hygiene.
2. PPE according to the expected exposure.
3. Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette.
4. Safe injection practices; never recap needles by hand; discard sharps immediately into a puncture-proof container.
5. Safe handling of patient care equipment and linen.
6. Environmental cleaning and spill management.
7. Correct patient placement (single room if the patient may contaminate the surroundings).
8. Waste management.
9. Management of needle-stick and splash injuries (wash with soap and water, do not squeeze or suck, report at once for assessment and post-exposure care).

**Transmission-based precautions** are added for known or suspected infections: **contact** (gloves and gown — MRSA, scabies), **droplet** (surgical mask within about 1 metre — influenza, diphtheria) and **airborne** (N95 respirator, negative-pressure or well-ventilated single room — tuberculosis, measles, chickenpox).

## 5.13 Biomedical waste management

**Biomedical waste** is any waste produced during diagnosis, treatment or immunisation of people or animals, or in research. In India it is managed under the **Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016** (with later amendments). Most hospital waste (about 75–90%) is general, non-hazardous waste; the rest is hazardous.

**Types of hospital waste**: general (paper, food, packaging), infectious (soiled dressings, cultures), anatomical (body parts, tissues), sharps (needles, blades), pharmaceutical (expired medicines), chemical (disinfectants, lab chemicals), radioactive, and pressurised containers.

**Colour-coded segregation under the 2016 Rules (summary)**

| Colour | What goes in | Typical treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Yellow** (bag) | Human anatomical waste, animal waste, soiled waste (dressings, cotton, plaster casts, bags contaminated with blood or body fluids), expired or discarded medicines, chemical waste, discarded linen and mattresses, microbiology and lab waste | Incineration, plasma pyrolysis or deep burial (as permitted) |
| **Red** (bag) | Contaminated recyclable waste: tubing, bottles, IV sets, catheters, urine bags, syringes without needles, gloves | Autoclaving or microwaving/chemical treatment, then shredding and recycling |
| **White (translucent)** puncture-proof, leak-proof, tamper-proof container | Sharps including metal: needles, syringes with fixed needles, scalpel blades | Autoclaving or dry-heat sterilisation, then shredding or encapsulation |
| **Blue** (puncture-proof box) | Glassware: broken or discarded glass, medicine vials and ampoules (except those contaminated with cytotoxic waste); metallic body implants | Disinfection, then recycling |

General waste goes in a separate (usually black) bin and is handled by the municipal system.

**Rules**: segregate at the **point of generation** (the bedside), fill bags only up to about three-quarters, label with the biohazard symbol, never mix categories, store in a secure place, and send for treatment within the time allowed (untreated waste is not to be stored beyond 48 hours). Staff are trained and immunised against hepatitis B.

**Hazards of poor waste management**: needle-stick injuries and infections (hepatitis B and C, HIV), spread of HAIs, injury to rag-pickers, re-use of discarded syringes, pollution of air, soil and water, and spread of resistant organisms.

**Nurse's role**: segregate correctly, use the right container, never recap needles, report injuries, keep records, and teach junior staff.

## 5.14 Promoting safety: the physical environment

| Factor | What the nurse ensures |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | Comfortable room temperature; extra warmth for newborns, the elderly and the very ill |
| Humidity | Moderate; very dry air dries mucous membranes, very damp air encourages fungi |
| Ventilation | Fresh air without draughts; windows, exhaust fans — dilutes airborne organisms and odours |
| Light | Good light for work and safety, natural light by day; dim night light for sleep and to prevent falls |
| Noise | Low noise, especially at night; soft voices, oiled trolley wheels, alarms answered quickly |
| Odour | Prompt removal of soiled items, emptying bedpans, ventilation, clean dressings |
| Pest control | Screens, covered food and waste bins, regular pest control (rats, cockroaches, mosquitoes, flies spread disease) |
| Cleanliness | Damp dusting, mopping, clean linen, tidy unit |

## 5.15 Reduction of physical hazards

**Fire**: causes include electrical faults, oxygen use near flames, smoking, and flammable liquids. Prevention: no smoking, checked wiring, "no smoking — oxygen in use" signs, keep exits clear, know where extinguishers are, fire drills.

In a fire follow **RACE**: **R**escue patients in immediate danger, **A**larm (raise the alarm), **C**ontain (close doors and windows, turn off oxygen if safe), **E**xtinguish or Evacuate. To use an extinguisher, remember **PASS**: **P**ull the pin, **A**im at the base of the fire, **S**queeze the handle, **S**weep from side to side. Water is used only for paper, wood and cloth fires, never on electrical fires or burning oil; a carbon dioxide or dry-powder extinguisher is used for electrical fires and flammable liquids. Evacuate horizontally first (to a safe area on the same floor), then down by the stairs, never by lift.

**Other accidents**: electrical shock (check cords, use earthed plugs, dry hands), burns from hot applications, scalds from hot drinks, cuts from sharps, wrong-patient errors (two identifiers, wristbands), medication errors, choking, and patients wandering.

## 5.16 Fall risk assessment

Falls are among the commonest accidents in hospitals. **Risk factors**: age over 65, history of falls, confusion, sedative or blood-pressure medicines, postural hypotension, weakness after illness, poor vision, urinary frequency or urgency, use of walking aids, IV poles, unfamiliar surroundings, wet floors, poor lighting, ill-fitting footwear.

**Assessment tools**: the **Morse Fall Scale** is commonly used. It scores history of falling, a secondary diagnosis, use of an ambulatory aid, an IV line or heparin lock, gait, and mental status; a total of 45 or more indicates high risk (cut-offs may be adjusted by each hospital). Assess on admission, after any change in condition, after a fall, and at regular intervals.

**Prevention**: bed at the lowest level with brakes on, side rails as per policy, call bell and personal items within reach, night light, non-skid slippers, clear walkways, dry floors, grab bars in toilets, help with toileting at regular times, orient the patient to the room, review medicines with the doctor, "fall risk" sign or band, and help to sit up slowly before standing.

**After a fall (post-fall assessment)**: do not lift the patient at once; check responsiveness, airway, breathing, injuries (head, hip); take vital signs; inform the doctor; move safely with help; observe closely for the next 24 hours (especially for head injury); record and complete an incident report; review the care plan.

## 5.17 Safety devices

**Restraints** are physical, chemical (medicines) or environmental methods used to limit a person's movement. They should be the **last resort** after other measures fail.

| Type | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Jacket / vest restraint | Keeps a confused patient in bed or chair |
| Belt restraint | Secures a patient on a stretcher or wheelchair |
| Limb (wrist/ankle) restraint | Prevents pulling out tubes or lines |
| Mitten restraint | Prevents scratching or pulling at dressings |
| Elbow restraint | Stops a child bending the elbow to touch the face (after cleft lip repair) |
| Mummy restraint | Short-term wrap of an infant for a procedure |

**Purposes / indications**: protect patients from injuring themselves or others, prevent removal of tubes and dressings, keep a child still for a procedure — only when less restrictive measures have failed.

**Legal implications and consent**: restraints restrict a person's freedom; wrongly used they can be **false imprisonment** or **assault and battery**. They need a **written order** that states the type, reason and duration, renewed as per policy; **informed consent** from the patient or relative; clear documentation; and the least restrictive method.

**Practice guidelines for applying restraints**

1. Try alternatives first: frequent observation, a family member at the bedside, diversion, treating the cause of confusion.
2. Explain to the patient and family.
3. Use the correct size; pad bony prominences.
4. Tie to the **bed frame**, not the side rail (a rail moves and can injure), with a **quick-release knot** so it can be undone at once in an emergency.
5. Allow **two fingers' space** under the restraint to protect circulation and skin.
6. Check circulation, skin, breathing and position frequently (commonly every 15–30 minutes), and **remove each restraint at regular intervals (about every 2 hours)** for exercise, skin care, toileting, food and fluids.
7. Never restrain a patient flat on the back if they may vomit (risk of aspiration).
8. Document the reason, type, time applied and removed, observations and the patient's response.

**Other safety devices**: side rails (prevent rolling out of bed, but climbing over them can cause worse falls), grab bars in toilets and corridors, call bells and emergency (ambu) alarms, non-skid slippers and mats, bed and wheelchair brakes, bed-exit alarms, wristbands, and low beds.

**Role of the nurse in a safe and clean environment**: assess risks on admission and regularly, keep the unit tidy and well lit, follow infection control and waste rules, check equipment, prevent falls and fires, use restraints correctly, educate patients and families, and report hazards and incidents.

## Remember

- Rubber: cold water, dry, powder, store flat. Glass: rinse in cold water first. Metal: dry to prevent rust.
- Chain of infection: agent → reservoir → portal of exit → transmission → portal of entry → host; break any link.
- Stages of infection: incubation, prodromal, illness, convalescence.
- Inflammation: redness, heat, swelling, pain, loss of function.
- HAI: appears 48 hours or more after admission.
- Medical asepsis = clean technique; surgical asepsis = sterile technique (no organisms, including spores).
- WHO five moments; hand rub 20–30 s; hand wash 40–60 s; soap and water for spores and visible soil.
- Donning: gown → mask → goggles → gloves. Doffing: gloves → goggles → gown → mask → hand hygiene.
- BMW Rules 2016: yellow (anatomical, soiled, chemical), red (contaminated plastics), white translucent (sharps), blue (glass).
- Fire: RACE and PASS. Morse Fall Scale: 45 or more = high risk.
- Restraints: last resort, written order, consent, tie to bed frame, quick-release knot, two fingers, release about every 2 hours.
