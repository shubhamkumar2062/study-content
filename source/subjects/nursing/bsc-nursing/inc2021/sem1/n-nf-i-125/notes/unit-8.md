# Unit 8: First aid (including basic CPR)

INC Unit XIV (20 hours theory + 20 hours skill lab) — the mandatory First Aid module (40 hours including basic CPR), taught with the NDMA / Indian Red Cross Society first aid module. It covers the principles of first aid and the first aid management of wounds, bleeding and shock, musculoskeletal injuries, transport of the injured, breathing emergencies and CPR, unconsciousness, foreign bodies, burns, poisoning, bites and stings, cold and heat injuries, and community emergencies.

> First aid methods are updated every few years by resuscitation councils and the Red Cross. Learn the method your college teaches from the current IRCS/NDMA module, and recheck CPR figures against the latest guidelines.

## 8.1 First aid: definition, aims, scope and rules

**First aid** is the immediate care given to an injured or suddenly ill person, using the resources available, until professional medical help takes over.

**Aims ("the 3 Ps")**: **P**reserve life; **P**revent the condition from getting worse; **P**romote recovery. Also relieve pain and protect the unconscious.

**Scope**: first aid is given at home, school, work, on roads and in disasters, by anyone trained. It does not replace medical care; the first aider does not diagnose in detail or give prescription medicines (except helping a person use their own medicine such as an asthma inhaler or adrenaline auto-injector).

**Basic principles and golden rules**

1. **Ensure safety** — of yourself, bystanders and the casualty (traffic, fire, electricity, gas). Do not become a second casualty.
2. **Call for help early** — national emergency number **112**, ambulance **108** in most states.
3. **Assess quickly** using a primary survey: **DRSABCD** — **D**anger, **R**esponse, **S**end for help, **A**irway, **B**reathing, **C**irculation/CPR, **D**efibrillation.
4. **Act on priorities**: breathing and severe bleeding first, then shock, then fractures and other injuries.
5. **Reassure** the casualty; be calm and confident.
6. **Prevent infection** — gloves, clean hands, clean dressings.
7. **Do no further harm** — do not move a casualty unnecessarily (suspect spinal injury in falls and road accidents); do not give food or drink to a seriously injured or unconscious person.
8. **Keep the casualty warm and comfortable.**
9. **Hand over** to medical help with a clear report (what happened, what you found, what you did, any changes).
10. **The "golden hour"**: survival after serious injury is best when definitive treatment starts within about the first hour — so get help and transport quickly.

**Qualities of a first aider**: observant, quick-thinking, tactful, calm, resourceful, sympathetic, able to lead bystanders.

**First aid kit**: sterile dressings and gauze, cotton, roller and triangular bandages, adhesive plasters and tape, safety pins, scissors, forceps (tweezers), disposable gloves, antiseptic solution, cold pack, thermometer, CPR face shield, torch, notebook and pen, ORS packets, and a list of emergency numbers.

## 8.2 Wounds

A **wound** is a break in the continuity of the skin or tissues.

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Abrasion (graze) | Top layer scraped; dirt may be embedded |
| Incised | Clean cut by a sharp object; may bleed heavily |
| Laceration | Torn, irregular edges; less bleeding, more tissue damage |
| Puncture | Small opening, deep track (nail, needle) — risk of tetanus and deep infection |
| Avulsion | Tissue torn away as a flap |
| Contusion (bruise) | Bleeding under unbroken skin |
| Gunshot / stab | Entry and maybe exit wounds; deep internal damage |
| Amputation | Part of a limb cut off |

**First aid for wounds**

1. Wear gloves; control bleeding first (8.3).
2. For minor wounds, wash with clean running water; remove loose dirt; cover with a sterile, non-stick dressing and bandage.
3. **Do not remove an embedded object** — it may be plugging a bleeding vessel. Pad around it and bandage so there is no pressure on the object.
4. For an **amputated part**: wrap it in a clean, damp cloth, put it in a plastic bag, and place the bag on ice (never put the part directly on ice or in water); send it with the casualty.
5. Advise medical care for deep, dirty, puncture or bite wounds and for tetanus protection.
6. Watch for signs of infection later: redness, swelling, heat, pus, fever.

## 8.3 Haemorrhage (bleeding)

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Arterial | Bright red, spurting with each heartbeat; heavy and dangerous |
| Venous | Dark red, steady flow |
| Capillary | Slow oozing from a graze |

Bleeding may be **external** or **internal** (hidden — suspect after a heavy blow, fracture of the thigh or pelvis; signs of shock, bruising, swelling, blood from body openings, vomiting or coughing blood).

**First aid for severe external bleeding**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Protect yourself (gloves) | Blood-borne infection |
| 2. Expose the wound; apply **firm direct pressure** with a sterile pad or clean cloth (or the casualty's own hand) | Pressure squeezes vessels and allows clotting |
| 3. Keep pressure for at least about 10 minutes without lifting to check | Lifting breaks the forming clot |
| 4. Raise the injured part (if no fracture) and support it | Reduces blood flow to the area |
| 5. Apply a firm pressure bandage; if blood soaks through, add another pad on top — do not remove the first | Removing disturbs clots |
| 6. For **life-threatening bleeding from a limb** that direct pressure cannot control, a trained person applies a **tourniquet** high on the limb, notes the time, and does not loosen it; the casualty goes to hospital immediately | A tourniquet saves life when nothing else works |
| 7. Lay the casualty down, treat for shock, call 112/108 | Prevents fainting and worsening shock |

**Nosebleed (epistaxis)**: sit the casualty up, **lean forward**, pinch the soft part of the nose for about 10 minutes, breathe through the mouth, spit out blood; do not tilt the head back (blood is swallowed and may cause vomiting or choking). Get help if it does not stop in about 20–30 minutes.

**Internal bleeding**: lay down, raise the legs if no injury, keep warm, nothing by mouth, call an ambulance, monitor.

## 8.4 Shock

**Shock** is a life-threatening failure of circulation, in which the tissues do not get enough oxygenated blood.

| Type | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Hypovolaemic | Loss of blood or fluid — bleeding, burns, severe diarrhoea and vomiting |
| Cardiogenic | Pump failure — heart attack |
| Distributive | Vessels widen: **septic** (severe infection), **anaphylactic** (severe allergy), **neurogenic** (spinal cord injury) |
| Obstructive | Blockage of blood flow — pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax |

(Simple fainting is sometimes called "psychogenic shock", but it is a brief loss of consciousness, not true shock.)

**Signs**: pale, cold, clammy skin; rapid, weak pulse; rapid, shallow breathing; thirst; nausea; restlessness, anxiety, then drowsiness and unconsciousness; falling BP; blue lips.

**First aid**

1. Call for help; treat the cause (stop bleeding).
2. Lay the casualty flat; **raise the legs** (if there is no leg, pelvic or spinal injury) — helps blood return to vital organs.
3. Loosen tight clothing; keep warm with a blanket (do not overheat).
4. Give nothing by mouth (may need surgery; risk of vomiting); moisten the lips if thirsty.
5. Reassure; do not leave the casualty alone.
6. Monitor breathing, pulse and response every few minutes; if unconscious and breathing, place in the recovery position; if breathing stops, start CPR.
7. In **anaphylaxis** (swelling of face and throat, wheeze, rash, collapse after a sting, food or medicine), call an ambulance at once and help the casualty use their own prescribed adrenaline auto-injector if they have one; let a breathless casualty sit up.

## 8.5 Musculoskeletal injuries

**Fracture**: a break in the continuity of a bone.

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Closed (simple) | Skin intact |
| Open (compound) | Bone end breaks the skin or there is a wound over the fracture — risk of infection |
| Complicated | Damage to nearby nerves, vessels or organs |
| Comminuted | Bone broken into several pieces |
| Greenstick | Incomplete break on one side — in children's soft bones |
| Impacted | Broken ends driven into each other |
| Stress / pathological | Repeated strain / weak bone (osteoporosis, tumour) |

**Signs**: pain and tenderness, swelling, bruising, deformity, shortening or abnormal position of a limb, loss of function, abnormal movement, grating (crepitus — do not test for it), signs of shock.

**General first aid for fractures**

1. Do not move the casualty unless there is danger; deal with breathing, bleeding and shock first.
2. **Support the injured part** in the position found; steady it with your hands.
3. **Immobilise**: use a splint (padded board, rolled newspaper, or the uninjured leg/the body) to keep **the joint above and the joint below** the fracture still; pad between the splint and the limb; tie bandages above and below the fracture, not over it.
4. For an **open fracture**, cover the wound with a sterile dressing and control bleeding by pressure around (not on) the bone; do not push the bone back.
5. Check circulation beyond the injury (colour, warmth, pulse, sensation) before and after bandaging; loosen if it is too tight.
6. Give nothing by mouth; treat for shock; arrange transport.

**Specific examples**: collar bone or upper arm — support the arm in a **sling**; forearm and wrist — padded splint and sling; thigh — immobilise both legs together with padding between them (major bleeding risk); **suspected spinal injury** — do not move; keep the head, neck and body in line; support the head with your hands; if the casualty must be turned, use a **log roll** with several helpers.

**Dislocation**: displacement of a bone end from a joint (shoulder, finger, jaw). Signs: severe pain, deformity, inability to move the joint. First aid: support in the most comfortable position (sling), apply a cold pack, **do not try to put it back**, get medical help.

**Sprain** (stretched or torn ligament) and **strain** (overstretched muscle): pain, swelling, bruising. First aid: **RICE** — **R**est, **I**ce (cold pack wrapped in cloth, about 20 minutes at a time), **C**ompression (firm, not tight, bandage), **E**levation. Seek medical care if unable to bear weight or move the part (a fracture may be present).

**Muscle cramp**: gently stretch and massage the muscle; fluids if due to heat and sweating.

## 8.6 Transportation of injured persons

**Principles**: move only if necessary for safety or to reach help; first stabilise (airway, bleeding, splinting); move as gently and as little as possible; support injured parts; keep the head, neck and spine in line if spinal injury is possible; use correct body mechanics; explain to the casualty; watch the casualty throughout.

| Method | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| **One-rescuer** | |
| Human crutch | Casualty who can walk with support (one leg injured) |
| Cradle carry | Children and light adults |
| Pick-a-back | Conscious casualty who can hold on |
| Drag (clothes, blanket or shoulder drag) | Moving quickly out of danger (fire, smoke), keeping the body in line |
| **Two-rescuer** | |
| Four-handed seat | Conscious casualty who can use both arms to hold the rescuers' shoulders |
| Two-handed seat | Casualty who cannot use the arms |
| Fore-and-aft carry | Moving through narrow spaces |
| Chair carry | Stairs and narrow corridors |
| **Stretcher** (standard, scoop, spine board, or improvised with blankets and poles or shirts and poles) | Serious injuries, unconscious casualties, fractures of the spine, pelvis or legs |

Loading a stretcher: test it first; use the blanket lift or log roll; lift together on a command; secure the casualty; keep the head end at a level the leader can watch.

## 8.7 Respiratory emergencies and basic CPR

**Common breathing emergencies**: choking (airway obstruction), drowning, suffocation and strangulation, smoke and gas inhalation, severe asthma attack, hyperventilation.

**Choking (foreign body airway obstruction)**

| Casualty | First aid |
| --- | --- |
| Adult or child, **mild** (can cough, speak, breathe) | Encourage strong coughing; stay with them |
| Adult or child (over 1 year), **severe** (cannot speak, cough or breathe; clutches the throat) | Lean them forward and give up to **5 sharp back blows** between the shoulder blades; if not cleared, give up to **5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich manoeuvre)** — stand behind, fist above the navel and below the breastbone, sharp inward and upward thrusts. Alternate until the object comes out. Use chest thrusts for pregnant or very obese people. Abdominal thrusts can cause injury, so the casualty should be checked by a doctor afterwards |
| **Infant** (under 1 year) | Lay face down along your forearm, head lower than the chest; up to 5 back blows; then turn face up and give up to 5 **chest thrusts** with two fingers on the lower breastbone. **No abdominal thrusts** in infants |
| Becomes unconscious | Lower gently to the ground, call for help, start CPR (look in the mouth for the object before giving breaths; do not do blind finger sweeps) |

**Drowning**: reach or throw a rope or float rather than entering the water if untrained ("reach, throw, don't go"). Once out, check breathing; if not breathing normally, start CPR — rescue breaths are especially important because the problem is lack of oxygen. Keep warm; all near-drowning casualties need hospital checks.

**Asthma attack**: sit upright leaning slightly forward, help use their own reliever inhaler, stay calm, call an ambulance if not improving.

**Smoke or gas**: do not enter a smoke- or gas-filled space unless safe; move the casualty to fresh air; give CPR if needed.

**Basic life support / CPR (adult)** — follows current international (AHA/ERC) guidance; check the latest update.

```text
Danger? → Responsive? (tap shoulders, shout) → Shout for help, call 112/108, get an AED
        → Open airway (head tilt–chin lift; jaw thrust if spinal injury suspected)
        → Look for normal breathing (no more than 10 seconds; gasping is NOT normal)
        → Not breathing normally → START CPR (compressions first: C–A–B)
```

| Element | Adult standard |
| --- | --- |
| Hand position | Heel of one hand on the centre of the chest (lower half of the breastbone), other hand on top, fingers interlocked, arms straight, shoulders over the hands |
| Rate | **100–120 compressions per minute** |
| Depth | **At least 5 cm but not more than 6 cm** |
| Recoil | Let the chest come up fully after each compression |
| Ratio | **30 compressions : 2 breaths** |
| Breaths | Each breath over about 1 second, just enough to make the chest rise; use a face shield or pocket mask |
| Interruptions | Keep pauses under 10 seconds |
| Hands-only CPR | An untrained or unwilling bystander can give continuous compressions without breaths |
| AED | Switch on as soon as it arrives and follow the voice prompts; keep clear during shock; resume compressions at once |
| When to stop | Casualty starts breathing normally, help takes over, you are exhausted, or the scene becomes unsafe |
| Changing rescuers | Swap compressors about every 2 minutes to keep quality |

**Children and infants (summary)**: compression depth about one-third of the chest depth (about 5 cm in a child, about 4 cm in an infant); child — one or two hands; infant — two fingers or two thumbs on the lower breastbone; one rescuer 30:2; two trained rescuers 15:2. If alone with a child and no phone, give about 2 minutes of CPR before leaving to call for help.

**Recovery position**: for an unconscious casualty who **is breathing normally** and has no suspected spinal injury. Turn them on to the side with the upper leg bent at the hip and knee to stop them rolling, head tilted back slightly so the tongue does not block the airway and vomit can drain; keep checking breathing.

## 8.8 Unconsciousness

**Unconsciousness** is an interruption of the brain's normal activity in which the person cannot respond to the surroundings.

**Causes** (memory aid **FISH SHAPED**): **F**ainting, **I**nfection, **S**hock, **H**ead injury, **S**troke, **H**eart attack, **A**sphyxia, **P**oisoning (including alcohol and drugs), **E**pilepsy, **D**iabetes (low or high blood sugar).

**Assessing level of response**: **AVPU** — **A**lert, responds to **V**oice, responds to **P**ain, **U**nresponsive. The **Glasgow Coma Scale** (used by health workers) scores eye opening (1–4), verbal response (1–5) and motor response (1–6): total 3 to 15; 8 or less means severe impairment.

**General first aid**

1. Check danger, response, airway and breathing.
2. Not breathing normally → CPR.
3. Breathing → **recovery position** (unless spinal injury is suspected; then keep the head in line and support the airway with a jaw thrust).
4. Call 112/108; look for a cause (injury, medical bracelet, medicines, smell of alcohol or chemicals).
5. Loosen tight clothing; keep warm; **nothing by mouth**.
6. Monitor breathing, pulse and response every few minutes and record changes; hand over.

**Specific conditions**

| Condition | First aid |
| --- | --- |
| **Fainting** | Lay down and raise the legs; fresh air; loosen clothing; recovery usually within a minute or two; if not, treat as unconscious |
| **Seizure (fit)** | Protect from injury (move hard objects, cushion the head); **do not restrain, do not put anything in the mouth**; note the time; after jerking stops, recovery position; call an ambulance if it lasts more than 5 minutes, repeats, is the first fit, or the person is injured or pregnant |
| **Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)** in a known diabetic | If conscious and able to swallow: give a sugary drink, glucose or sugar, then a snack; if unconscious, nothing by mouth — recovery position and ambulance |
| **Stroke** — use **FAST** | **F**ace drooping, **A**rm weakness, **S**peech difficulty, **T**ime to call an ambulance; note the time symptoms began; nothing by mouth |
| **Head injury** | Suspect neck injury; control bleeding; monitor; any loss of consciousness, vomiting, drowsiness or fluid from ears/nose needs hospital |

## 8.9 Foreign bodies

| Site | First aid | Do not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Skin** (splinter, thorn) | Clean the area; remove a superficial splinter with clean tweezers, pulling in the line of entry; wash and cover. Leave deeply embedded objects and fish hooks for medical removal | Dig deeply; squeeze |
| **Eye** (dust, insect) | Do not rub; blink; pull the upper lid over the lower lid; flush with clean water; remove a loose particle on the white of the eye with a moist cotton tip. If it is **embedded or on the coloured part (cornea)**, cover the eye with a pad and seek help. **Chemical in the eye**: flush with gentle running water for at least 15–20 minutes, keeping the eye open | Rub; try to remove embedded objects |
| **Ear** | **Live insect**: tilt the head with the affected ear up and gently flood the ear with lukewarm water so it floats out. Other objects: seek medical help | Probe with pins, sticks or cotton buds |
| **Nose** | Breathe through the mouth; close the other nostril and blow gently; if it stays, seek help | Probe; sniff it further in |
| **Throat** | Treat as choking (8.7); a fish bone stuck — seek medical help | Blind finger sweeps |
| **Stomach (swallowed object)** | Small, smooth objects (coins) often pass; seek medical advice. **Button batteries, magnets and sharp objects need urgent hospital care** | Induce vomiting |

## 8.10 Burns and scalds

A **burn** is injury from dry heat (flame, hot metal), electricity, chemicals, radiation (sun) or friction. A **scald** is a burn from moist heat (hot water, steam, oil).

**Depth**

| Depth | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Superficial (first degree) | Outer skin only; red, painful, no blisters (sunburn) |
| Partial thickness (second degree) | Blisters, very painful, red and moist |
| Full thickness (third degree) | All skin layers; white, waxy, brown or charred; may be painless because nerves are destroyed |

**Extent — Rule of Nines (adult)**

```text
Head and neck ............. 9%
Each arm .................. 9%   (× 2 = 18%)
Front of trunk ............ 18%
Back of trunk ............. 18%
Each leg .................. 18%  (× 2 = 36%)
Perineum / genitals ....... 1%
                     Total 100%
```

For small or scattered burns, the casualty's own **palm with fingers ≈ 1%** of the body surface. Children have a larger head and smaller legs, so special charts are used.

**Seek hospital care for**: burns larger than the casualty's palm, all full-thickness burns, burns of the face, hands, feet, genitals or over joints, burns going all round a limb, chemical and electrical burns, burns with breathing difficulty or smoke inhalation (soot around the nose and mouth, hoarse voice), and burns in children and the elderly.

**First aid for burns and scalds**

| Step | Rationale |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Stop the burning: remove from the source; if clothes are on fire — **stop, drop and roll**, or smother with a blanket | Ends the injury |
| 2. **Cool the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running water for about 20 minutes** | Stops the burning process, reduces pain and depth |
| 3. Remove rings, watches, belts and tight clothing from the area before it swells; **do not pull off clothing stuck to the burn** | Swelling makes removal impossible later; pulling tears the skin |
| 4. Cover loosely with cling film (laid on, not wrapped tightly) or a clean, non-fluffy cloth or sterile dressing | Protects from infection, reduces pain |
| 5. Keep the casualty warm (cool the burn, not the person) | Large burns cause heat loss and shock |
| 6. Treat for shock; call 112/108 for serious burns; monitor breathing | Fluid loss causes shock |
| 7. **Do not** apply ice, butter, oil, toothpaste, ink or home remedies; **do not burst blisters**; do not use adhesive dressings | They damage tissue and cause infection |

**Chemical burns**: protect yourself; brush off dry powder first; flush with plenty of running water for at least 20 minutes; remove contaminated clothing.

**Electrical burns**: **switch off the power** at the mains before touching the casualty (or move them away with dry, non-conducting material such as wood if switching off is impossible); check breathing and start CPR if needed; look for entry and exit burns; always send to hospital (internal damage and heart rhythm problems).

## 8.11 Poisoning, bites and stings

**Poisoning** can be by mouth (swallowed), by breathing (inhaled), through the skin (absorbed) or by injection (including bites and stings).

**General first aid for poisoning**

1. Ensure your own safety (gas, chemicals — gloves).
2. Check airway, breathing and response; CPR if needed (use a face shield; avoid mouth-to-mouth if poison is on the lips).
3. Find out **what** was taken, **how much**, **when** and **how**; keep the container, tablet strips or vomit to send with the casualty.
4. Call an ambulance or a poison information centre.
5. **Do not induce vomiting** — corrosives burn the gullet again on the way up, and kerosene or petrol can be breathed into the lungs.
6. Do not give anything by mouth unless advised by a medical professional; do not try to "neutralise" a corrosive.
7. Unconscious and breathing → recovery position.

**Specific poisons common in India**

| Poison | First aid points |
| --- | --- |
| Pesticides (organophosphates) | Wear gloves; remove contaminated clothes; wash skin with soap and water; keep airway clear (secretions); rush to hospital with the container |
| Kerosene / petrol | Do not induce vomiting (risk of aspiration pneumonia); keep upright or in recovery position; hospital |
| Corrosives (acids, alkalis, toilet cleaners) | Do not induce vomiting; if conscious and able, the mouth may be rinsed; hospital at once |
| Medicines overdose | Keep the strips; recovery position if drowsy; hospital |
| Carbon monoxide, smoke, gas | Fresh air if safe; turn off the source; no flames or switches near gas leaks; CPR if needed |
| Food poisoning | Fluids and oral rehydration solution (ORS); medical help if severe, bloody diarrhoea, or in infants and the elderly |

**Snake bite**: India's medically important venomous snakes include the **"Big Four"** — **Indian cobra, common krait, Russell's viper and saw-scaled viper**. Most bites are from non-venomous snakes, but every bite is treated as dangerous.

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Reassure — fear speeds the pulse | Panic or run |
| Keep the casualty still; immobilise the bitten limb like a fracture, kept at about heart level | Cut, suck or burn the wound |
| Remove rings, bangles, watches and tight clothing from the limb (it will swell) | Apply a tight tourniquet |
| Transport to hospital quickly (anti-snake venom is given only in hospital) | Use traditional remedies or delay for a "healer" |
| Note the time of the bite and any symptoms; describe the snake if seen safely | Try to catch or kill the snake |

**Dog, cat and monkey bites (risk of rabies)**: **wash the wound immediately with soap and plenty of running water for about 15 minutes**, apply an antiseptic, do not cover tightly or stitch at once, and send the person to a health facility the same day for anti-rabies vaccination as per national guidelines. Note the animal and observe it if possible.

**Scorpion sting**: reassure, keep still, wash the site, apply a cold pack for pain, and go to hospital quickly — some Indian scorpions (the red scorpion) can cause dangerous heart and lung effects, especially in children.

**Bee and wasp stings**: scrape the sting out sideways with a fingernail or card (do not squeeze it — more venom enters); wash; apply a cold pack; watch for anaphylaxis (swelling of face and throat, wheeze, collapse) and call an ambulance if it appears; stings inside the mouth need urgent care.

**Insect bites and leeches**: wash; cold pack; for leeches, slide a fingernail under the sucker to detach (salt also works); do not pull hard.

## 8.12 Frostbite and effects of heat

**Frostbite** (high altitudes and cold regions): freezing of tissues, usually fingers, toes, ears and nose. Signs: pins and needles, then numbness; skin white, waxy, hard; later blisters and blackening.

First aid: move to a warm, sheltered place; remove wet clothing and rings; **rewarm gently** in warm (not hot) water or by body warmth (hands in the armpits) **only if there is no risk of refreezing**; **do not rub** the part (damages tissue) and do not use direct heat (fire, heater, hot water bottle) — the numb skin burns easily; pad between fingers and toes; raise the part; get medical help. Also treat **hypothermia** (move out of the cold, dry clothes, cover the head, warm drinks if fully conscious, warm the body core gradually).

**Effects of heat** (common in Indian summers and heat waves)

| Condition | Signs | First aid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunburn | Red, painful skin | Shade, cool water, soothing lotion; avoid more sun |
| Heat cramps | Painful muscle spasms after sweating | Rest in a cool place; drink ORS or a salted, sugared drink; gently stretch the muscle |
| Heat exhaustion | Heavy sweating, pale moist skin, headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, rapid weak pulse | Cool, shaded place; lie down and raise the legs; loosen clothing; sips of ORS or water; sponge with cool water; seek help if not improving within about 30 minutes |
| **Heat stroke** (emergency) | Very high temperature, hot and often dry skin, confusion, seizures, collapse | **Call an ambulance; cool rapidly** — move to shade, remove outer clothing, wet the skin with cool water and fan it, cold packs to the neck, armpits and groin; recovery position if unconscious; nothing by mouth if not fully alert; CPR if needed |

**Prevention**: drink water often, avoid outdoor work at midday, light loose cotton clothing, head covering, and check on the elderly and children during heat waves.

## 8.13 Community emergencies

A **community emergency or disaster** is a sudden event that causes many casualties or great damage, beyond the ability of the local community to cope.

**Types**: **natural** — earthquakes, floods, cyclones, landslides, tsunamis, heat and cold waves, droughts; **man-made** — fires, building collapse, road, rail and air accidents, stampedes, industrial and chemical accidents (the Bhopal gas tragedy of 1984 is the best-known Indian example), bomb blasts, and epidemics.

**Disaster management in India**: under the **Disaster Management Act, 2005**, the **National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA)** (headed by the Prime Minister) makes policies; state and district authorities plan locally; the **National Disaster Response Force (NDRF)** carries out specialised rescue. The Indian Red Cross Society and many volunteers help.

**Disaster management cycle**

```text
   Prevention / Mitigation → Preparedness → [DISASTER] → Response → Recovery / Rehabilitation
          ↑                                                                   │
          └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Triage**: sorting casualties so that the greatest number survive. A common simple method (START) uses colour tags:

| Tag | Priority | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Red** | Immediate — life-threatening but treatable | Breathing fast or with difficulty, no radial pulse or poor capillary refill, cannot follow commands |
| **Yellow** | Delayed — serious but can wait a little | Fractures without shock |
| **Green** | Minor — "walking wounded" | Small cuts and bruises; can walk |
| **Black** | Dead or not expected to survive with the resources available | Not breathing even after the airway is opened |

**Role of the first aider and nurse in community emergencies**

1. Personal and scene safety; follow the incident commander's instructions.
2. Raise the alarm (112/108, fire 101) and give clear information.
3. Triage and give life-saving first aid (airway, CPR, bleeding control, shock).
4. Move casualties safely; record and tag them.
5. **Psychological first aid**: calm, listen, help people meet basic needs and contact family.
6. Prevent outbreaks after disasters: safe drinking water, sanitation, food hygiene, vector control, vaccination drives.
7. Before disasters: take part in drills, community training and preparedness planning.

## Remember

- First aid aims: preserve life, prevent worsening, promote recovery. Safety first; call 112 / 108.
- Primary survey: DRSABCD.
- Bleeding: direct pressure about 10 minutes, elevate, add pads (don't remove); tourniquet only for life-threatening limb bleeding — note the time.
- Nosebleed: sit, lean forward, pinch soft part of the nose.
- Shock: lay flat, raise legs, keep warm, nothing by mouth.
- Fracture: immobilise joint above and below; don't push bone back. Sprain: RICE. Dislocation: don't reduce.
- Choking adult: 5 back blows + 5 abdominal thrusts; infant: back blows + chest thrusts, no abdominal thrusts.
- Adult CPR: 100–120/min, 5–6 cm, full recoil, 30:2, AED early; infant 4 cm, child 5 cm, two-rescuer 15:2.
- Recovery position for unconscious but breathing casualties. FAST for stroke. Never put anything in the mouth during a fit.
- Burns: cool running water about 20 minutes; no ice, butter or toothpaste; don't burst blisters. Rule of nines; palm ≈ 1%.
- Poisoning: don't induce vomiting; keep the container. Snake bite: immobilise, no cutting, sucking or tight tourniquet; hospital.
- Animal bite: soap and running water for about 15 minutes; anti-rabies vaccination.
- Frostbite: warm gently, never rub. Heat stroke: cool rapidly, ambulance.
- Triage colours: red immediate, yellow delayed, green minor, black dead/expectant.
