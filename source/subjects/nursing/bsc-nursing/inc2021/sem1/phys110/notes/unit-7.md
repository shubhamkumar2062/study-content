# Unit 7: The renal and reproductive systems

This file joins INC Unit IX, Renal system (4 hours), and Unit X, The reproductive system (4 hours). Part A explains how the kidneys make urine and keep the internal environment constant, and how the bladder empties. Part B explains the menstrual cycle, oogenesis, fertilisation, implantation, the breast, spermatogenesis and semen.

# Part A — The renal system

## 7.1 Functions of the kidney in maintaining homeostasis

1. **Excretion of waste products** — urea (from protein), creatinine (from muscle), uric acid (from nucleic acids), bilirubin products, drugs and toxins.
2. **Water balance** — urine volume and concentration vary to keep body water constant (under ADH).
3. **Electrolyte balance** — Na+, K+, Cl−, Ca2+, phosphate, Mg2+ are kept at normal levels.
4. **Acid–base balance** — kidneys excrete H+ and reabsorb and generate bicarbonate, keeping blood pH 7.35–7.45.
5. **Regulation of blood pressure** — by controlling blood volume (salt and water) and through **renin** (renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system).
6. **Endocrine functions** — **erythropoietin** (red cell production), **calcitriol** (active vitamin D — the second hydroxylation happens in the kidney), renin, prostaglandins.
7. **Metabolic functions** — gluconeogenesis during prolonged fasting; breakdown of some peptide hormones such as insulin.

In kidney failure every one of these fails: urea and creatinine rise, fluid overload and oedema, high potassium, acidosis, hypertension, anaemia and bone disease follow.

## 7.2 The nephron and renal blood flow

Each kidney has about **one million nephrons**. A nephron = **renal corpuscle** (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule) + **renal tubule** (proximal convoluted tubule → loop of Henle → distal convoluted tubule) → collecting duct.

- **Cortical nephrons** (about 85%) have short loops; **juxtamedullary nephrons** have long loops reaching deep into the medulla and help concentrate urine.
- **Juxtaglomerular apparatus** — where the distal tubule touches its own afferent arteriole: **juxtaglomerular cells** (secrete renin) and **macula densa** (senses NaCl in tubular fluid).
- **Renal blood flow** is about **1200–1300 mL/min — about 20–25% of cardiac output** — far more than the kidneys need for their own metabolism, because it is needed for filtration.
- Blood passes through **two capillary beds in series**: high-pressure glomerular capillaries (filtration) and low-pressure peritubular capillaries (reabsorption).

## 7.3 Formation of urine

Urine is formed in three steps:

```text
Blood → 1. GLOMERULAR FILTRATION (in Bowman's capsule)
            filtrate = plasma minus proteins and cells
      → 2. TUBULAR REABSORPTION (useful substances back into blood)
      → 3. TUBULAR SECRETION (more wastes added from blood into tubule)
      → URINE  (amount excreted = filtered − reabsorbed + secreted)
```

### 1. Glomerular filtration and GFR

The **glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** is the volume of filtrate formed by both kidneys per minute: about **125 mL/min**, that is about **180 litres a day**. As normal urine output is only about **1–1.5 litres a day**, about **99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed**. The **filtration fraction** (GFR ÷ renal plasma flow) is about 20%.

The **filtration membrane** (capillary endothelium with pores, basement membrane, podocytes with filtration slits) lets water and small solutes through but holds back cells and almost all proteins (the negative charge repels albumin). **Proteinuria** means this barrier is damaged.

**Net filtration pressure**

| Force | Approximate value | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure | about 60 mmHg | Favours filtration |
| Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure | about 18 mmHg | Opposes |
| Plasma colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure | about 32 mmHg | Opposes |
| **Net filtration pressure** | 60 − (18 + 32) = **about 10 mmHg** | Favours filtration |

### Factors affecting GFR

| Factor | Effect on GFR |
| --- | --- |
| Glomerular capillary pressure (systemic BP, afferent and efferent arteriole tone) | Afferent constriction ↓ GFR; afferent dilation ↑; moderate efferent constriction ↑ |
| Severe fall in BP (shock, haemorrhage) | ↓ — urine output falls (oliguria) |
| Sympathetic stimulation | ↓ (afferent constriction) |
| Obstruction of urine outflow (stone, enlarged prostate) | ↑ capsular pressure → ↓ GFR |
| Plasma protein concentration | Low protein → ↑ GFR; high (dehydration) → ↓ |
| Surface area / number of nephrons | Kidney disease → ↓ |
| Age | ↓ with ageing |

**Autoregulation** keeps renal blood flow and GFR nearly constant when mean BP varies between about **80 and 180 mmHg**, by two mechanisms:

- **Myogenic mechanism** — afferent arteriole constricts when stretched by high pressure.
- **Tubuloglomerular feedback** — when GFR rises, more NaCl reaches the **macula densa**, which signals the afferent arteriole to constrict.

**Measuring GFR**: the **clearance** of a substance that is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted equals GFR. **Inulin** is the reference substance; in practice **creatinine clearance** (from a 24-hour urine and a blood sample) or an estimated GFR calculated from serum creatinine is used. Normal serum creatinine is about 0.6–1.2 mg/dL; blood urea about 15–40 mg/dL (lab ranges vary).

### 2. Tubular reabsorption

| Segment | What is reabsorbed |
| --- | --- |
| **Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)** — does the bulk | About **65%** of filtered Na+ and water; **all glucose and amino acids** (normally); most bicarbonate, K+, phosphate; about half the urea |
| **Loop of Henle** | Descending limb — water (permeable to water); thick ascending limb — Na+, K+, Cl− but **not water** → dilute tubular fluid and a salty medulla (**countercurrent multiplier**), which lets the body make concentrated urine |
| **Distal convoluted tubule** | Na+ and Cl− (with water if ADH present); Ca2+ under PTH |
| **Collecting duct** | Na+ under **aldosterone** (K+ and H+ secreted in exchange); **water under ADH** (aquaporins) — fine adjustment of urine volume |

**Renal threshold for glucose** — glucose carriers can reabsorb only a limited amount; when blood glucose exceeds about **180 mg/dL**, glucose appears in urine (**glycosuria**), as in uncontrolled diabetes, carrying water with it (polyuria).

### 3. Tubular secretion

Substances moved from peritubular blood into the tubule: **H+** (acid–base balance), **K+** (in exchange for Na+), creatinine (a little), ammonia, many drugs (penicillin, some diuretics) and toxins. This clears substances faster than filtration alone.

### Concentration and dilution of urine

- Plenty of water → ADH low → collecting ducts impermeable → **large volume of dilute urine** (as dilute as about 50 mOsm/L).
- Dehydration → ADH high → water reabsorbed into the salty medulla → **small volume of concentrated urine** (up to about 1200 mOsm/L).
- The **countercurrent system** of the loop of Henle and its blood vessels (vasa recta) keeps the medulla salty so concentration is possible.

## 7.4 Regulation of renal function

| Regulator | Trigger | Action on kidney |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ADH** | ↑ plasma osmolality; ↓ blood volume | Water reabsorption in collecting ducts → concentrated urine |
| **Aldosterone** | Angiotensin II; ↑ plasma K+ | Na+ reabsorption, K+ and H+ secretion (distal tubule, collecting duct) |
| **Renin–angiotensin system** | ↓ renal perfusion, ↓ NaCl at macula densa, sympathetic stimulation | Angiotensin II: vasoconstriction (efferent arteriole keeps GFR up), aldosterone and ADH release, thirst |
| **Atrial natriuretic peptide** | Atrial stretch (high blood volume) | ↑ Na+ and water excretion; ↑ GFR; inhibits renin and aldosterone |
| **Parathyroid hormone** | ↓ plasma calcium | ↑ Ca2+ reabsorption, ↓ phosphate reabsorption, activates vitamin D |
| **Sympathetic nerves** | Exercise, stress, haemorrhage | Constrict afferent arterioles → ↓ GFR; release renin |

### Normal urine

| Feature | Normal |
| --- | --- |
| Volume | about 1–1.5 L/day (varies with intake); less than about 400 mL/day = oliguria; above about 2.5–3 L/day = polyuria; near none = anuria |
| Colour | Pale yellow (urochrome) — darker when concentrated |
| Appearance | Clear |
| Reaction (pH) | About 6 (range about 4.5–8); depends on diet |
| Specific gravity | About 1.010–1.025 |
| Odour | Faintly aromatic; ammoniacal on standing |
| Abnormal constituents | Normally no glucose, protein, ketones, blood or bile |

## 7.5 Functions of the ureters, bladder and urethra

- **Ureters** carry urine from the renal pelvis to the bladder by **peristaltic waves** (several per minute), not by gravity alone, so urine flows even when lying down. They pass obliquely through the bladder wall, so filling of the bladder compresses them — a **valve-like** action that prevents **reflux** of urine to the kidney.
- **Urinary bladder** — a muscular reservoir. Its wall (the **detrusor** smooth muscle) stretches to hold urine with little rise in pressure until it is fairly full. The **internal urethral sphincter** (smooth muscle, involuntary) sits at the bladder neck. Capacity in adults is roughly 600–800 mL; the urge to void is usually felt at about 300–400 mL.
- **Urethra** — carries urine out; the **external urethral sphincter** (skeletal muscle, voluntary, pudendal nerve) allows voluntary control. In men the urethra also carries semen. The female urethra is short (about 4 cm), which is one reason urinary infection is more common in women.

## 7.6 Micturition

**Micturition** is the emptying of the urinary bladder.

```text
Bladder fills → stretch receptors in the wall (urge felt at about 300–400 mL)
   → afferent impulses in PELVIC nerves → sacral spinal cord (S2–S4) MICTURITION CENTRE
   → parasympathetic (pelvic nerve) efferents:
        DETRUSOR CONTRACTS + internal sphincter relaxes
   → urine enters the urethra → more reflex contraction (positive feedback)
   → if convenient, brain allows the EXTERNAL sphincter (pudendal nerve) to relax → urine passes
```

- **Higher control**: centres in the **pons** coordinate detrusor contraction with sphincter relaxation; the **cerebral cortex** can inhibit or start the reflex voluntarily. Abdominal muscles and the diaphragm can help empty the bladder by raising abdominal pressure.
- **Infants** void by spinal reflex alone; voluntary control develops at about 2–3 years as the nervous system matures (toilet training).
- **Sympathetic** nerves (hypogastric) relax the detrusor and contract the internal sphincter, helping storage.

**Abnormalities**

| Condition | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| **Retention of urine** | Bladder cannot empty: enlarged prostate, urethral stricture, after anaesthesia or surgery, some drugs, spinal shock |
| **Incontinence** | Loss of voluntary control: unconsciousness, spinal cord injury, weak pelvic floor (stress incontinence), dementia |
| **Automatic (reflex) bladder** | Spinal cord cut above the sacral segments: after the spinal shock period, the bladder empties by reflex without control or sensation |
| **Atonic bladder** | Damage to sacral sensory nerves: bladder overfills, overflow dribbling |
| **Enuresis** | Involuntary voiding at night beyond the age of control |

## 7.7 Application and implication in nursing (renal)

- **Intake and output chart** and **urine output monitoring** — in an adult an hourly output of at least about 0.5 mL/kg is a common sign of adequate kidney perfusion; falling output in shock or after surgery is reported early.
- **Catheter care** — closed drainage system, bag below bladder level (gravity and no backflow), asepsis, adequate fluid intake; record colour and volume.
- **Retention** — palpate or scan for a full bladder; privacy, running water sound, warm bedpan and normal posture help a patient pass urine before catheterisation is considered.
- **Bladder training** — regular timed voiding for incontinence; pelvic floor exercises.
- **Specimens** — midstream clean-catch sample for culture; 24-hour collection for creatinine clearance (discard first sample, include last).
- **Fluid and diet** in kidney disease — restriction of fluid, sodium, potassium and protein as ordered; daily weight; watch for oedema, breathlessness, high potassium.
- **Drugs excreted by the kidney** may accumulate in renal failure — report reduced urine output when a patient is on such drugs.

# Part B — The reproductive system

## 7.8 Female reproductive system — hormones of the ovary

The ovary produces **ova** and the hormones **oestrogens**, **progesterone**, **inhibin** and **relaxin** under control of the hypothalamus (GnRH) and anterior pituitary (FSH, LH).

| Hormone | Source | Main functions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oestrogen** | Granulosa cells of growing follicles; placenta in pregnancy | Development of female secondary sex characters (breasts, fat distribution, pubertal growth); proliferation of endometrium; thin watery cervical mucus at ovulation (allows sperm through); growth of uterus and vagina; helps closure of epiphyses; protects bone; raises HDL |
| **Progesterone** | **Corpus luteum**; placenta | Makes the endometrium **secretory** (ready for implantation); thick cervical mucus; relaxes uterine muscle (maintains pregnancy); develops breast alveoli; raises body temperature slightly after ovulation (about 0.3–0.5 °C) |
| **Inhibin** | Granulosa cells, corpus luteum | Inhibits FSH |
| **Relaxin** | Corpus luteum, placenta | Softens cervix and relaxes pelvic ligaments in late pregnancy |

**Functions of the ovary**: produce ova (oogenesis and ovulation) and secrete hormones.

## 7.9 The menstrual cycle

The **menstrual cycle** is the series of monthly changes in the ovary and uterus that prepare for pregnancy. Average length is **28 days** (normally about 21–35 days). Day 1 is the first day of bleeding. It starts at **menarche** (about 11–14 years in Indian girls) and ends at **menopause** (about 45–55 years).

| Phase (uterus) | Days (28-day cycle) | Ovary | Hormones | Changes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Menstrual phase** | 1–5 | Corpus luteum of last cycle has regressed | Oestrogen and progesterone low | Functional layer of endometrium sheds with bleeding (about 30–80 mL); FSH begins to rise |
| **Proliferative (follicular) phase** | 6–14 | **Follicles grow** under FSH; one becomes dominant (Graafian) | **Oestrogen rises** | Endometrium regrows and thickens; glands and vessels proliferate |
| **Ovulation** | about day 14 | Graafian follicle ruptures and releases the secondary oocyte | High oestrogen triggers the **LH surge** (positive feedback) | Thin stretchy cervical mucus; slight rise of basal body temperature after ovulation |
| **Secretory (luteal) phase** | 15–28 | Ruptured follicle becomes the **corpus luteum** (under LH) | **Progesterone** (and oestrogen) high | Endometrium becomes thick, glands secrete glycogen and mucus, spiral arteries coil — ready for implantation |
| **If no pregnancy** | Day 28 | Corpus luteum degenerates (corpus albicans) after about 10–12 days | Progesterone and oestrogen fall | Spiral arteries constrict → endometrium dies → menstruation |

**The luteal phase is fairly constant at about 14 days**; variations in cycle length are mostly in the follicular phase. So ovulation is roughly 14 days *before* the next period — the basis of calculating the fertile period.

## 7.10 Oogenesis

**Oogenesis** is the formation of ova in the ovary.

1. **Before birth** — oogonia multiply by mitosis and become **primary oocytes**, which begin the first meiotic division and **stop in prophase I**. Each is surrounded by cells as a **primordial follicle**. All the oocytes a woman will ever have are formed before birth (a few hundred thousand remain at puberty; about 400–500 are ovulated in a lifetime; the rest degenerate).
2. **At puberty, each cycle** — under FSH a few follicles develop; just before ovulation the dominant primary oocyte **completes meiosis I**, forming a large **secondary oocyte** (haploid, 23 chromosomes) and a small **first polar body**.
3. The secondary oocyte starts meiosis II and **stops at metaphase II**; it is released at ovulation in this state.
4. **Meiosis II is completed only if a sperm enters** — giving the **ovum** and a **second polar body**. Without fertilisation the secondary oocyte degenerates within about 24 hours.

So one primary oocyte gives **one ovum** (and polar bodies), unlike spermatogenesis, where one primary spermatocyte gives four sperm.

## 7.11 Fertilisation

**Fertilisation** is the union of a sperm and a secondary oocyte to form a **zygote**.

- **Site**: usually the **ampulla of the uterine (fallopian) tube**.
- **Timing**: the oocyte is fertilisable for about 12–24 hours after ovulation; sperm can survive in the female tract for about 3–5 days — hence the fertile window of a few days around ovulation.
- **Steps**:
  1. Sperm travel through cervix, uterus and tube (helped by uterine and tubal contractions).
  2. **Capacitation** — in the female tract sperm become able to fertilise (their membrane changes over several hours).
  3. **Acrosome reaction** — enzymes released from the acrosome digest a path through the corona radiata and zona pellucida.
  4. One sperm fuses with the oocyte membrane; its nucleus enters.
  5. **Zona (cortical) reaction** — the zona pellucida hardens to block other sperm (**prevents polyspermy**).
  6. The oocyte completes meiosis II; male and female pronuclei fuse → **zygote** with 46 chromosomes.
- **Results**: restores the diploid number; determines **sex** (X-bearing sperm → female XX; Y-bearing sperm → male XY — the father's sperm decides); brings genetic variation; starts cleavage.

## 7.12 Implantation

- The zygote divides repeatedly (**cleavage**) while moving down the tube → **morula** (about day 3–4) → reaches the uterus and forms a **blastocyst** (inner cell mass, which becomes the embryo, and outer **trophoblast**, which becomes the placenta).
- **Implantation** begins about **6–7 days after fertilisation**: the blastocyst attaches to the secretory endometrium (usually the upper posterior wall) and the trophoblast invades it. The endometrium is now called the **decidua**.
- The trophoblast secretes **human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)**, which keeps the corpus luteum alive so progesterone continues and menstruation does not occur. hCG in urine or blood is the basis of **pregnancy tests**. The placenta takes over hormone production later in the first trimester.
- Implantation outside the uterine cavity (most often in the tube) is an **ectopic pregnancy**.

## 7.13 Functions of the breast

The breast (mammary gland) produces and secretes milk to nourish the baby and passes on protective antibodies.

| Stage | Hormones | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Puberty | Oestrogen (ducts), progesterone (lobules), GH | Breast development |
| Pregnancy (**mammogenesis**) | Oestrogen, progesterone, prolactin, human placental lactogen | Ducts and alveoli grow; milk production held back by high oestrogen and progesterone |
| After delivery (**lactogenesis**) | Fall in placental hormones lets **prolactin** act | Milk secretion begins; **colostrum** in the first few days (yellow, rich in protein and **IgA**, low in fat) |
| **Milk ejection (let-down)** | **Oxytocin** | Suckling stimulates nerve endings in the nipple → hypothalamus → oxytocin from posterior pituitary → myoepithelial cells contract and squeeze milk out; can be triggered by the baby's cry and blocked by anxiety or pain |
| Maintenance (**galactopoiesis**) | Prolactin released with each suckling | Frequent suckling and emptying keep milk production going |

High prolactin during frequent breastfeeding suppresses GnRH, so ovulation is often delayed (lactational amenorrhoea) — not a reliable contraceptive on its own.

## 7.14 Male reproductive system — spermatogenesis

**Spermatogenesis** is the formation of sperm in the **seminiferous tubules** of the testes. It begins at puberty and continues throughout life; one cycle takes about **64–74 days**.

```text
Spermatogonium (46, by mitosis keeps the supply)
   → PRIMARY SPERMATOCYTE (46) — MEIOSIS I →
   → 2 SECONDARY SPERMATOCYTES (23 each) — MEIOSIS II →
   → 4 SPERMATIDS (23 each) — SPERMIOGENESIS (no division: head, acrosome, tail formed) →
   → 4 SPERMATOZOA → mature and gain motility in the EPIDIDYMIS
```

- **Sertoli cells** support and nourish developing sperm, form the **blood–testis barrier**, secrete **inhibin** and androgen-binding protein; they respond to **FSH**.
- **Leydig (interstitial) cells** between the tubules secrete **testosterone** in response to **LH**.
- Spermatogenesis needs a temperature about **2–3 °C below body temperature** — the reason the testes lie in the scrotum. Undescended testes (cryptorchidism), fever, tight clothing and heat exposure reduce sperm production.
- **Structure of a sperm**: head (nucleus with 23 chromosomes; acrosome cap with enzymes), middle piece (mitochondria for energy), tail (movement).

## 7.15 Male hormones and their functions

| Hormone | Source | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Testosterone** | Leydig cells (LH) | Before birth: development of male genital organs and descent of testes. At puberty: growth of penis, testes, scrotum; male secondary sex characters (beard, body hair, deep voice from larynx enlargement, broad shoulders, muscle growth); pubertal growth spurt then closure of epiphyses; libido; with FSH maintains spermatogenesis; stimulates red cell production; protein anabolism |
| **Inhibin** | Sertoli cells | Inhibits FSH (negative feedback) |
| **FSH and LH** | Anterior pituitary | FSH → spermatogenesis via Sertoli cells; LH → testosterone |

## 7.16 Semen

**Semen** is the fluid ejaculated at orgasm: sperm plus secretions of the accessory glands.

| Source | Contribution | Contents and role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Testes and epididymis | Small (sperm) | Spermatozoa |
| **Seminal vesicles** | About 60% | **Fructose** (energy for sperm), prostaglandins (help sperm movement in the female tract), fibrinogen-like proteins (semen clots briefly) |
| **Prostate** | About 30% | Thin milky alkaline fluid with citrate, calcium, enzymes (including prostate-specific antigen, which liquefies the clot) |
| **Bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands** | Small | Mucus that lubricates and neutralises acid in the urethra before ejaculation |

- **Volume** per ejaculate is commonly about 2–5 mL; **pH** slightly alkaline (about 7.2–8), which protects sperm from the acid vagina.
- **Sperm count**: many millions per mL; WHO semen analysis manuals give lower reference limits (for example a concentration of about 15 million per mL in the 2010 edition) — confirm the current reference values used by your laboratory. A large proportion should be motile and of normal shape.
- Low counts or poor motility contribute to infertility.

## 7.17 Application and implication in providing nursing care (reproductive)

- **Menstrual history** — date of last menstrual period, cycle length and flow are part of every female patient's assessment; the expected date of delivery is calculated from the LMP.
- **Menstrual hygiene** — teach adolescent girls about the cycle, safe sanitary products, changing them regularly and nutrition (iron for blood loss).
- **Family planning counselling** — understanding the fertile window, the role of hormones and the time sperm and ova survive explains natural and hormonal methods.
- **Antenatal care** — early pregnancy testing (hCG), folic acid before and in early pregnancy as advised, recognising signs of ectopic pregnancy (missed period, abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding).
- **Breastfeeding support** — early initiation after delivery, giving colostrum, correct positioning and attachment, frequent feeds, a calm environment for let-down, and reassurance.
- **Male reproductive health** — avoid prolonged heat to the scrotum; examine for undescended testis in newborn boys; testicular self-examination.
- **Privacy, dignity and cultural sensitivity** are essential in all reproductive care.

## Remember

- Kidney: excretion, water, electrolyte and acid–base balance, BP (renin), erythropoietin, calcitriol.
- Renal blood flow about 1200–1300 mL/min (20–25% of CO); GFR 125 mL/min (180 L/day); about 99% reabsorbed; urine 1–1.5 L/day.
- Net filtration pressure = 60 − (18 + 32) ≈ 10 mmHg; autoregulation between about 80–180 mmHg mean BP.
- PCT reabsorbs about 65% of Na+ and water and all glucose; renal threshold for glucose ≈ 180 mg/dL.
- ADH → water reabsorption; aldosterone → Na+ in, K+ out; ANP → Na+ and water out.
- Micturition reflex centre S2–S4; parasympathetic contracts detrusor; external sphincter voluntary (pudendal); pons and cortex control.
- Menstrual cycle: menstrual (1–5), proliferative (oestrogen, 6–14), ovulation (LH surge, about day 14), secretory (progesterone, 15–28); luteal phase ≈ 14 days.
- Primary oocytes arrest in prophase I before birth; secondary oocyte arrests in metaphase II and completes it only at fertilisation.
- Fertilisation in the ampulla; acrosome reaction; zona reaction blocks polyspermy; father's sperm decides sex.
- Implantation about day 6–7 after fertilisation; hCG keeps the corpus luteum going (pregnancy test).
- Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin ejects it; colostrum is rich in IgA.
- Spermatogenesis in seminiferous tubules (about 64–74 days), needs 2–3 °C lower temperature; FSH → Sertoli cells; LH → Leydig cells → testosterone.
- Semen: seminal vesicles 60% (fructose), prostate 30% (alkaline fluid).
