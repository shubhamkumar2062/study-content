# Unit 6: The musculoskeletal system

This unit covers INC Unit VIII (6 hours). It explains what bones do, how they heal, how joints move and fail, and how skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle contract. The INC lists "structured essay" for this unit, so the mechanism of muscle contraction and bone healing deserve full preparation.

## 6.1 Functions of bones

1. **Support** — framework that bears body weight and gives shape.
2. **Protection** — skull protects the brain, vertebral column the spinal cord, rib cage the heart and lungs, pelvis the pelvic organs.
3. **Movement** — bones act as **levers** and joints as pivots, moved by muscles attached through tendons.
4. **Mineral storage** — about 99% of body calcium and most phosphate; released into blood when needed (under PTH).
5. **Blood cell formation** — red marrow makes red cells, white cells and platelets.
6. **Fat storage** — yellow marrow in the shafts of long bones in adults.
7. **Acid–base help** — bone salts buffer acid in long-standing acidosis.

### Bone cells and remodelling

| Cell | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **Osteoblasts** | Build bone: lay down collagen and promote mineral (calcium phosphate) deposition |
| **Osteocytes** | Mature bone cells trapped in lacunae; maintain bone and sense mechanical stress |
| **Osteoclasts** | Large multinucleated cells that **resorb** (break down) bone, releasing calcium |

Bone is living tissue that is constantly **remodelled**: osteoclasts remove old bone and osteoblasts lay down new bone. In young adults the two are balanced. Weight-bearing exercise strengthens bone; immobility, bed rest and weightlessness weaken it.

**Factors affecting bone growth and strength**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Calcium, phosphate, protein | Raw materials |
| **Vitamin D** | Calcium absorption from gut; lack → rickets (children), osteomalacia (adults) |
| Vitamin C | Collagen formation |
| Vitamin A | Bone growth and remodelling |
| **Growth hormone**, thyroxine | Growth at epiphyseal plates in childhood |
| **Sex hormones** (oestrogen, testosterone) | Growth spurt at puberty, then closure of epiphyses; oestrogen protects bone — after menopause bone loss speeds up (osteoporosis) |
| **PTH** | Raises blood calcium by resorbing bone |
| **Calcitonin** | Inhibits resorption |
| Cortisol (excess or long-term steroids) | Weakens bone |
| Mechanical stress (exercise) | Strengthens bone |

## 6.2 Movements of bones of the axial and appendicular skeleton

### Terms for movement

| Movement | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flexion | Bending; reduces the angle between bones | Bending the elbow |
| Extension | Straightening; increases the angle | Straightening the knee |
| Hyperextension | Extension beyond the anatomical position | Bending the head backwards |
| Abduction | Movement away from the midline | Raising the arm sideways |
| Adduction | Movement towards the midline | Bringing the arm back to the side |
| Rotation | Turning around a long axis | Shaking the head "no" |
| Circumduction | Circular movement combining flexion, abduction, extension, adduction | Swinging the arm in a circle |
| Pronation | Turning the palm to face backwards/down | Palm down |
| Supination | Turning the palm to face forwards/up | Palm up (holding soup) |
| Inversion / Eversion | Sole turned inward / outward | Ankle sprain usually in inversion |
| Dorsiflexion / Plantar flexion | Toes pulled up / pointed down | Walking on heels / tiptoe |
| Elevation / Depression | Raising / lowering | Shrugging the shoulders; opening the jaw |
| Protraction / Retraction | Moving forward / backward | Jutting the jaw out |
| Opposition | Thumb touches the other fingertips | Gripping a pen |

### Axial skeleton

- **Skull** — only the mandible moves freely (at the temporomandibular joint): elevation and depression (opening and closing the mouth), protraction, retraction and side-to-side grinding in chewing.
- **Head on neck** — nodding (flexion–extension) mainly at the atlanto-occipital joint; turning ("no") at the atlanto-axial pivot joint.
- **Vertebral column** — flexion, extension, lateral flexion and rotation; each joint moves a little but together the range is large. The cervical and lumbar regions move most; the thoracic region is held by the ribs. The curves and intervertebral discs absorb shock.
- **Ribs and sternum** — move upward and outward in inspiration (pump-handle and bucket-handle movements).

### Appendicular skeleton

| Region | Main movements |
| --- | --- |
| Shoulder girdle (scapula, clavicle) | Elevation, depression, protraction, retraction, rotation of the scapula — enlarges arm movement |
| Shoulder joint | Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, medial and lateral rotation, circumduction — the most mobile joint, and the most easily dislocated |
| Elbow | Flexion and extension |
| Radioulnar joints | Pronation and supination |
| Wrist | Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, circumduction |
| Thumb (saddle joint) | Opposition, flexion, extension, abduction, adduction |
| Hip | Flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, circumduction — stable because of a deep socket |
| Knee | Flexion and extension (slight rotation when bent) |
| Ankle | Dorsiflexion and plantar flexion |
| Foot (intertarsal joints) | Inversion and eversion |

## 6.3 Bone healing

A **fracture** is a break in the continuity of bone. Healing passes through overlapping stages:

| Stage | Time (approximate, adult long bone) | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Haematoma formation** | First hours | Torn vessels bleed; a clot forms between the bone ends; bone cells at the edges die |
| 2. **Inflammation and cellular proliferation** | First days | Inflammatory cells clear debris; new capillaries grow in; fibroblasts and osteogenic cells multiply |
| 3. **Soft callus (fibrocartilaginous callus)** | About 1–3 weeks | Collagen and cartilage bridge the gap; the fracture is "sticky" but not strong |
| 4. **Hard (bony) callus** | About 3–12 weeks | Osteoblasts turn the soft callus into woven bone; the fracture becomes clinically united |
| 5. **Remodelling** | Months to years | Osteoclasts remove excess callus; woven bone replaced by compact bone shaped to the lines of stress |

**Factors that help**: good blood supply, close alignment and immobilisation of fragments, adequate nutrition (protein, calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C), young age.

**Factors that delay**: infection, poor blood supply (for example neck of femur, scaphoid), movement at the fracture site, gap between ends, soft tissue trapped between ends, old age, diabetes, smoking, steroids, malnutrition. Delay may cause **delayed union**, **non-union** or **malunion** (healing in a wrong position).

Children's bones heal faster and remodel well; elderly bones heal slowly and fracture more easily (osteoporosis).

## 6.4 Joints and joint movements

A **joint (articulation)** is a place where two or more bones meet.

| Type | Movement | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fibrous** (synarthrosis) | Immovable | Sutures of the skull; teeth in sockets |
| **Cartilaginous** (amphiarthrosis) | Slightly movable | Intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis, first rib–sternum |
| **Synovial** (diarthrosis) | Freely movable | Shoulder, elbow, hip, knee |

### Synovial joint — features and physiology

- Bone ends covered by smooth **articular (hyaline) cartilage**.
- **Joint cavity** enclosed by a **fibrous capsule** lined by **synovial membrane**.
- **Synovial fluid** — clear, viscous (contains hyaluronic acid): **lubricates** the joint, **nourishes** the articular cartilage (which has no blood vessels), absorbs shock and removes debris (phagocytes). Movement and weight-bearing pump fluid in and out of the cartilage, which is why regular movement keeps cartilage healthy.
- **Ligaments** stabilise; some joints have **menisci** (knee) or **bursae** (sacs reducing friction).

### Types of synovial joints

| Type | Movement | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hinge | One plane: flexion–extension | Elbow, knee, interphalangeal |
| Pivot | Rotation | Atlanto-axial; proximal radioulnar |
| Ball and socket | All directions | Shoulder, hip |
| Condyloid (ellipsoid) | Two planes, no rotation | Wrist, metacarpophalangeal |
| Saddle | Two planes plus opposition | Carpometacarpal joint of the thumb |
| Plane (gliding) | Sliding | Intercarpal, intertarsal |

**Factors that limit or stabilise movement**: shape of articular surfaces, ligaments, muscle tone, contact of soft parts, and the capsule.

## 6.5 Alteration of joint disease

| Condition | What happens | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Osteoarthritis** | Degeneration ("wear and tear") of articular cartilage; bone spurs (osteophytes) | Pain worse with use and at the end of the day, short morning stiffness, creaking (crepitus), mainly knees, hips, spine; common in elderly and obese |
| **Rheumatoid arthritis** | Autoimmune inflammation of synovial membrane; pannus erodes cartilage and bone | Symmetrical swelling of small joints of hands and feet, long morning stiffness, deformities, tiredness, systemic involvement |
| **Gout** | Uric acid crystals deposit in joints | Sudden severe pain, redness and swelling, often of the big toe; tophi |
| **Septic arthritis** | Bacterial infection of a joint | Hot, swollen, very painful joint with fever — an emergency |
| **Ankylosing spondylitis** | Inflammation and fusion of spinal joints | Back stiffness in young adults, "bamboo spine" |
| **Sprain** | Stretch or tear of ligaments | Pain, swelling, bruising after twisting |
| **Dislocation** | Bone ends displaced from each other | Deformity, loss of movement, severe pain (shoulder, hip) |
| **Ankylosis / contracture** | Fusion or shortening due to disease or immobility | Fixed joint, loss of range |

## 6.6 Properties and functions of skeletal muscle

**Functions**: movement and locomotion; maintenance of **posture**; **heat production** (a major source of body heat, especially during exercise and shivering); stabilising joints; protection of abdominal organs; help in venous return (muscle pump), breathing, swallowing and voluntary control of sphincters.

### Properties

| Property | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Excitability** | Responds to a stimulus (nerve impulse) with an action potential |
| **Contractility** | Shortens and develops tension |
| **Extensibility** | Can be stretched |
| **Elasticity** | Returns to its resting length after stretch |
| **Conductivity** | Action potential spreads along the fibre |

Further physiological features:

- **All-or-none law** — a single muscle fibre contracts fully or not at all; the **whole muscle** grades its force by recruiting more motor units and by firing them faster.
- **Motor unit** — one motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it supplies. Small units (eye, fingers: few fibres per neuron) give fine control; large units (thigh, back: hundreds of fibres) give power.
- **Simple twitch** — a single contraction with latent, contraction and relaxation periods.
- **Summation and tetanus** — repeated stimuli before relaxation is complete add up; at high frequency the contractions fuse into a smooth sustained contraction (**tetanus**) — the normal way voluntary movements are made.
- **Muscle tone** — slight continuous contraction at rest, maintained by stretch reflexes; keeps posture and readiness. Lost in lower motor neuron lesions (flaccid), increased in upper motor neuron lesions (spastic).
- **Fatigue** — reduced response after prolonged activity (ATP and glycogen depletion, lactic acid, failure at the neuromuscular junction).
- **Isotonic contraction** — muscle shortens and moves a load (lifting a cup). **Isometric contraction** — tension rises but length stays the same (pushing a wall, holding posture).
- **Length–tension relationship** — a muscle contracts most strongly from about its normal resting length.

### Neuromuscular junction

The motor nerve ending releases **acetylcholine (ACh)**, which binds to nicotinic receptors on the **motor end plate**, opening ion channels → **end-plate potential** → muscle action potential. ACh is quickly destroyed by **acetylcholinesterase**, so each nerve impulse gives one muscle impulse. **Myasthenia gravis** (antibodies against ACh receptors) causes weakness that worsens with use.

## 6.7 Mechanism of muscle contraction (sliding filament theory)

Each muscle fibre contains **myofibrils** made of repeating **sarcomeres** (Z line to Z line). Thick filaments are **myosin** (with heads that bind actin and split ATP); thin filaments are **actin**, with the regulatory proteins **tropomyosin** (covers binding sites at rest) and **troponin** (binds calcium).

```text
       Z line                               Z line
         |  thin (actin)  ═══════            |
         |        ▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬▬ thick (myosin)
         |  ═══════                          |
  Relaxed: little overlap.  Contracted: actin slides towards the centre,
  Z lines move closer, sarcomere and I band shorten; A band length stays the same.
```

### Steps

1. **Nerve impulse** reaches the neuromuscular junction → **ACh** released → muscle **action potential**.
2. The action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down the **T tubules** into the fibre.
3. This triggers release of **calcium ions** from the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** (excitation–contraction coupling).
4. Ca2+ binds to **troponin C** → troponin–tropomyosin complex shifts → **active sites on actin are uncovered**.
5. **Myosin heads**, already energised by splitting ATP (ADP + Pi attached), bind to actin forming **cross-bridges**.
6. **Power stroke** — the heads tilt, pulling actin filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere; ADP and Pi are released.
7. A new **ATP** binds to myosin → the head **detaches** from actin; ATP is split and the head is re-cocked.
8. The cycle repeats as long as Ca2+ and ATP are present → filaments **slide** past each other and the muscle shortens. The filaments themselves do not shorten.

### Relaxation

When nerve impulses stop, Ca2+ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by a **Ca2+-ATPase** (uses ATP); troponin–tropomyosin covers actin again; the muscle relaxes.

**ATP is needed for both contraction (power) and relaxation (detachment and calcium pumping).** After death ATP runs out, cross-bridges cannot detach, and muscles become stiff — **rigor mortis**.

### Energy for contraction

| Source | Speed | Lasts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stored ATP | Immediate | A few seconds |
| **Creatine phosphate** → ATP | Very fast | About 10–15 seconds (sprint) |
| Anaerobic glycolysis (glucose → lactic acid) | Fast | Short bursts, minutes; produces lactic acid and fatigue |
| Aerobic respiration (glucose, fatty acids in mitochondria) | Slower | Long endurance activity |

After heavy exercise extra O2 is used to restore ATP, creatine phosphate and glycogen and to clear lactic acid — the **oxygen debt**.

## 6.8 Cardiac and smooth muscle

### Cardiac muscle — structure and properties

- **Striated**, **branched** fibres with usually **one central nucleus**.
- Joined end to end by **intercalated discs** containing **gap junctions** (impulses pass straight from cell to cell) and desmosomes (hold cells together during contraction). So the atria and ventricles each behave as a **functional syncytium**.
- Many mitochondria — depends on aerobic metabolism; very sensitive to lack of O2.
- **Autorhythmicity** (pacemaker cells), **long refractory period** (cannot be tetanised), all-or-none for the whole chamber, **Frank–Starling** response to stretch.
- Calcium for contraction comes partly from the ECF, so plasma calcium affects the force of the heartbeat.
- Involuntary; rate modified by autonomic nerves and hormones.

### Smooth muscle — structure and properties

- **Spindle-shaped**, non-striated fibres with one central nucleus; actin and myosin are not arranged in sarcomeres.
- Contraction is triggered by calcium binding to **calmodulin**, which activates **myosin light-chain kinase** (no troponin).
- **Slow**, **sustained** contraction using little energy — ideal for keeping vessel tone and sphincters closed.
- **Single-unit (visceral) smooth muscle** — fibres linked by gap junctions, contract together, show spontaneous rhythmic activity and contract when stretched (gut, uterus, ureter, small vessels).
- **Multi-unit smooth muscle** — each fibre separately innervated, precise control (iris, ciliary muscle, large arteries, arrector pili).
- **Plasticity** — can be stretched a lot and adjust its tension (bladder, uterus).
- Involuntary; controlled by autonomic nerves, hormones (oxytocin, adrenaline) and local factors (stretch, O2, CO2).

### Comparison of the three types

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Long cylindrical | Branched | Spindle |
| Striations | Present | Present | Absent |
| Nuclei | Many, peripheral | One (or two), central | One, central |
| Intercalated discs | Absent | Present | Absent (gap junctions in single-unit) |
| Control | Voluntary (somatic nerves) | Involuntary (autonomic) | Involuntary (autonomic, hormones) |
| Calcium binds to | Troponin | Troponin | Calmodulin |
| Speed of contraction | Fast | Intermediate | Slow, sustained |
| Tetanus possible | Yes | No | Sustained tone instead |
| Fatigue | Fatigues | Does not fatigue normally | Resists fatigue |
| Regeneration | Limited | Very little (scar) | Good |

## 6.9 Application and implication in nursing

- **Positioning and turning** — keep joints in functional positions, support with pillows, use foot boards or splints to prevent **foot drop** and contractures in bedridden patients.
- **Range-of-motion exercises** — active or passive, several times a day, maintain joint mobility, muscle strength, cartilage nourishment and circulation.
- **Immobility effects** — bone loss (calcium leaves bone and can form kidney stones), muscle wasting, contractures, pressure ulcers, venous stasis; early ambulation counters these.
- **Fracture care** — check the **neurovascular status** of the limb beyond a cast or splint: pain, pallor, pulse, paraesthesia (tingling), paralysis, plus swelling and temperature. Severe pain on passive stretch may mean compartment syndrome — report at once.
- **Body mechanics** — nurses lift with a wide base, bent knees and a straight back, holding the load close; this protects the vertebral column.
- **Osteoporosis prevention** — calcium and vitamin D-rich diet, sunlight, weight-bearing exercise, fall prevention in the elderly.
- **Muscle weakness and tetany** — low potassium causes weakness; low calcium causes tetany; report these signs.
- **Joint disease** — warm applications for stiffness, rest during acute inflammation, maintain mobility, weight control.

## Remember

- Bones: support, protection, movement, mineral store (99% of calcium), haemopoiesis, fat store.
- Osteoblasts build, osteoclasts resorb, osteocytes maintain; vitamin D, PTH, calcitonin, GH and sex hormones control bone.
- Bone healing: haematoma → inflammation and proliferation → soft callus → hard callus → remodelling.
- Joints: fibrous (immovable), cartilaginous (slightly movable), synovial (freely movable); synovial fluid lubricates and nourishes cartilage.
- Osteoarthritis = cartilage wear; rheumatoid arthritis = autoimmune synovitis; gout = uric acid crystals.
- Skeletal muscle properties: excitability, contractility, extensibility, elasticity; motor unit; tetanus; tone.
- Contraction: ACh → action potential → T tubules → Ca2+ from sarcoplasmic reticulum → troponin → cross-bridge cycling with ATP → sliding filaments.
- ATP is needed for detachment and relaxation; no ATP → rigor mortis.
- Cardiac muscle: branched, intercalated discs, syncytium, cannot be tetanised; smooth muscle: spindle, calmodulin, slow sustained tone.
