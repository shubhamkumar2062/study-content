# Unit 3: The digestive system

This unit covers INC Unit III (8 hours) — one of the heaviest units and a common source of essay questions. It covers the secretions of the mouth, stomach, pancreas, liver and intestines, the movements of the gut, and how carbohydrates, proteins and fats are digested and absorbed.

## 3.1 Functions of the organs of the digestive tract

The digestive system does six jobs: **ingestion** (taking food in), **propulsion** (moving it along), **mechanical digestion** (chewing, churning, segmentation), **chemical digestion** (enzymes break large molecules into small ones), **absorption** (into blood and lymph) and **elimination** (defaecation).

| Organ | Main functions |
| --- | --- |
| Mouth | Chewing (mastication), mixing with saliva, taste, start of starch digestion, swallowing begins |
| Pharynx and oesophagus | Swallowing; carry the bolus to the stomach by peristalsis; lower oesophageal sphincter prevents reflux |
| Stomach | Stores food; churns it into chyme; secretes HCl, pepsinogen, intrinsic factor, mucus, gastrin; kills microbes; controls emptying into duodenum; absorbs a little water and alcohol |
| Small intestine | Main site of digestion and absorption; receives bile and pancreatic juice; villi and microvilli give a huge absorptive area |
| Large intestine | Absorbs water and electrolytes; forms and stores faeces; bacteria make vitamin K and some B vitamins; secretes mucus |
| Rectum and anal canal | Store faeces until defaecation |
| Liver, gall bladder, pancreas | Accessory organs: bile, digestive enzymes, metabolism (see 3.5) |

## 3.2 Saliva

**Daily volume**: about 1–1.5 litres. **pH**: about 6.0–7.4 (near neutral).

### Composition

- **Water** — about 99.5%.
- **Organic**: **salivary amylase (ptyalin)** — starts starch digestion; **lingual lipase** — small amount of fat digestion; **mucin** — lubricates; **lysozyme** — kills bacteria; **immunoglobulin A**; blood group substances in some people.
- **Inorganic**: Na+, K+, Cl−, HCO3−, calcium, phosphate, thiocyanate.

Glands: **parotid** (watery, serous, rich in amylase), **submandibular** (mixed; gives the largest share, about 70% at rest), **sublingual** (mostly mucous).

### Regulation of salivary secretion

Salivary secretion is controlled **entirely by nerves** (no hormone controls it).

- **Parasympathetic** supply is the main stimulus and gives large amounts of watery saliva: via the **facial nerve** (chorda tympani) to the submandibular and sublingual glands, and via the **glossopharyngeal nerve** (otic ganglion) to the parotid.
- **Sympathetic** stimulation gives a small amount of thick, mucus-rich saliva (a dry mouth in fear).
- **Unconditioned reflex** — food touching the mouth (taste and touch receptors) → salivary nuclei in the brainstem → secretion. Present from birth.
- **Conditioned reflex** — learned; the sight, smell, thought or sound linked with food causes salivation (Pavlov's experiments).

### Functions of saliva

1. Moistens and lubricates food, forming a bolus for easy swallowing.
2. Starts digestion of starch (amylase) and of fat (lingual lipase).
3. Dissolves food so taste buds can detect it.
4. Keeps the mouth clean and moist; helps speech.
5. Protects teeth and mucosa: buffers acid (bicarbonate), antibacterial lysozyme and IgA.
6. Helps water balance — dry mouth signals thirst.
7. Excretes some substances (heavy metals such as lead and mercury, some drugs, some viruses such as rabies).

## 3.3 Gastric juice

**Daily volume**: about 1.5–2 litres. **pH**: very acidic, about 1.0–3.5.

### Composition and source

| Constituent | Cell | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrochloric acid | Parietal (oxyntic) cells | Kills microbes; activates pepsinogen to pepsin and gives the acid pH pepsin needs; softens fibrous food; helps absorption of iron and calcium |
| Intrinsic factor | Parietal cells | Needed for absorption of vitamin B12 in the terminal ileum; lack causes pernicious anaemia |
| Pepsinogen | Chief (peptic) cells | Converted to **pepsin**, which starts protein digestion |
| Gastric lipase | Chief cells | Small amount of fat digestion |
| Mucus and bicarbonate | Mucous neck cells and surface cells | Protective gel layer — shields the stomach wall from acid and pepsin |
| Gastrin (hormone, into blood) | G cells of the pyloric antrum | Stimulates acid secretion and gastric motility |

### Mechanism of HCl secretion by parietal cells

```text
Inside the parietal cell:
  CO2 + H2O ──(carbonic anhydrase)──► H2CO3 ──► H+ + HCO3−
  H+   is pumped into the gland lumen by the H+–K+ ATPase (proton pump), in exchange for K+
  Cl−  follows H+ into the lumen through chloride channels  → H+ + Cl− = HCl
  HCO3− passes into blood in exchange for Cl−
        → blood leaving the stomach after a meal is alkaline ("alkaline tide")
```

Drugs that block the proton pump or histamine H2 receptors reduce acid; this is why histamine, acetylcholine and gastrin are the three main stimulants of the parietal cell.

### Regulation — three phases of gastric secretion

| Phase | Trigger | Pathway | Share |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cephalic** | Sight, smell, taste, thought of food | Vagus nerve → acid and pepsinogen directly, and gastrin release | about 30% |
| **Gastric** | Food in stomach: stretch of the wall, peptides and amino acids | Local and vagal reflexes; **gastrin** from G cells; histamine | about 60% |
| **Intestinal** | Chyme in duodenum | Small early stimulation; then mostly **inhibition** | about 10% |

**Inhibition of gastric secretion and emptying**: acid in the antrum (pH below about 2) stops gastrin release (negative feedback); acid, fat, hypertonic chyme and distension in the duodenum trigger the **enterogastric reflex** and release of **secretin, cholecystokinin (CCK), gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP)** and **somatostatin**. This lets the duodenum receive chyme only as fast as it can handle it.

**Functions of the stomach** (summary): storage, mixing, protein digestion begins, defence against microbes, intrinsic factor, control of emptying, absorption of alcohol and a few drugs.

## 3.4 Pancreatic juice

**Daily volume**: about 1.2–1.5 litres. **pH**: alkaline, about 8.0–8.3 (rich in bicarbonate).

### Composition

- **Bicarbonate** (from duct cells) — neutralises acid chyme so pancreatic and intestinal enzymes can work.
- **Proteolytic enzymes** (secreted as inactive forms so they do not digest the pancreas): **trypsinogen**, **chymotrypsinogen**, **procarboxypeptidase**, proelastase. **Enterokinase (enteropeptidase)** from the duodenal mucosa converts trypsinogen to **trypsin**, which then activates the others (and more trypsinogen). A **trypsin inhibitor** inside the pancreas prevents early activation; failure of this protection leads to acute pancreatitis.
- **Pancreatic amylase** — starch → maltose, maltotriose, dextrins.
- **Pancreatic lipase** with **colipase** — triglycerides → monoglycerides + fatty acids; **phospholipase**, **cholesterol esterase**.
- **Nucleases** — ribonuclease and deoxyribonuclease.

### Regulation of pancreatic secretion

| Phase | Mechanism | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cephalic and gastric | Vagus nerve | Enzyme-rich juice (small amount) |
| Intestinal (main) | **Secretin** released from duodenal S cells by **acid** chyme | Large volume of **watery, bicarbonate-rich** juice |
| Intestinal (main) | **CCK** released from I cells by **fats and proteins** in duodenum | **Enzyme-rich** juice; also gall bladder contraction |

## 3.5 Functions of the liver, gall bladder and pancreas

### Liver — the body's chemical factory

1. **Carbohydrate metabolism** — stores glucose as glycogen (glycogenesis), releases it (glycogenolysis), makes new glucose (gluconeogenesis); keeps blood glucose steady.
2. **Fat metabolism** — makes and breaks down fatty acids, makes cholesterol and lipoproteins, forms ketone bodies in starvation.
3. **Protein metabolism** — deamination of amino acids; forms **urea** from ammonia; makes **plasma proteins** (albumin, globulins except immunoglobulins, fibrinogen, prothrombin and most clotting factors).
4. **Bile formation** — about 500–1000 mL a day.
5. **Storage** — glycogen, vitamins A, D, B12, iron (as ferritin), copper.
6. **Detoxification and drug metabolism** — alcohol, many drugs, hormones (insulin, steroids) are broken down and excreted.
7. **Excretion** — bilirubin, cholesterol, drugs into bile.
8. **Defence** — Kupffer cells (macrophages) remove bacteria and old red cells.
9. **Heat production** — high metabolic activity.
10. **Haemopoiesis** in the fetus; activation of vitamin D (first hydroxylation).

### Gall bladder

- Stores bile between meals and **concentrates** it 5–10 times by absorbing water and salts.
- Contracts and empties bile into the duodenum when **CCK** is released (fatty meal); the sphincter of Oddi relaxes at the same time.
- Mixes bile with mucus.

### Pancreas

- **Exocrine** (about 99% of the gland, acini and ducts): pancreatic juice — digestion of all three major food types and neutralisation of acid.
- **Endocrine** (islets of Langerhans): insulin, glucagon, somatostatin — control of blood glucose (detailed in Unit 5).

## 3.6 Bile — composition and functions

| Constituent | Note |
| --- | --- |
| Water | about 97% of hepatic bile |
| **Bile salts** (sodium and potassium salts of glycocholic and taurocholic acids) | Made from cholesterol; the active part for digestion |
| **Bile pigments** — bilirubin (mainly) and biliverdin | Breakdown products of haemoglobin; give bile its golden-yellow colour and faeces its brown colour (as stercobilin) |
| Cholesterol, lecithin (phospholipid) | Kept in solution by bile salts; if balance fails, gallstones form |
| Electrolytes, bicarbonate | Help neutralise acid |

**Functions of bile**

1. **Emulsification of fat** — bile salts lower surface tension and break large fat globules into tiny droplets, increasing the area for lipase.
2. **Micelle formation** — bile salts carry fatty acids, monoglycerides, cholesterol and **fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K** to the intestinal cells for absorption.
3. **Excretion** of bilirubin, cholesterol, drugs and heavy metals.
4. Neutralises acid chyme (bicarbonate).
5. Mild laxative action; inhibits putrefaction.
6. **Enterohepatic circulation** — about 90–95% of bile salts are reabsorbed in the terminal ileum, return to the liver in portal blood and are secreted again, so the same pool is reused several times a day.

If bile does not reach the gut (obstruction), fat is not absorbed → pale, bulky, fatty stools (steatorrhoea), deficiency of vitamin K (bleeding tendency) and **obstructive jaundice** with dark urine.

## 3.7 Secretions of the small and large intestine

### Small intestine (succus entericus)

- **Volume** about 1.8 litres a day; slightly alkaline (pH about 7.5–8).
- **Brunner's glands** (duodenum) secrete thick alkaline mucus that protects the duodenum from acid.
- **Crypts of Lieberkühn** secrete water and electrolytes.
- Enzymes of the **brush border** (on microvilli) finish digestion: **disaccharidases** (maltase, sucrase, lactase, isomaltase), **peptidases** (aminopeptidases, dipeptidases), intestinal lipase, **enterokinase**.
- Hormones from the mucosa: secretin, CCK, GIP, motilin.
- Regulation: mainly local reflexes from distension and irritation; also the hormones above.

### Large intestine

- Secretes **mucus** rich in bicarbonate and K+; **no digestive enzymes**.
- Absorbs most of the remaining **water** (about 1.5 L reaches the colon daily; only about 100–200 mL leaves in faeces) and sodium.
- **Bacterial flora** ferment fibre, produce gases, and synthesise **vitamin K**, biotin and some other B vitamins.

## 3.8 Movements of the alimentary tract

| Movement | Where | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mastication** | Mouth | Breaks food, mixes with saliva |
| **Deglutition (swallowing)** | Mouth → pharynx → oesophagus | Moves bolus to stomach (see below) |
| **Peristalsis** | Whole tract | Wave of contraction behind and relaxation in front of the bolus, pushing it forward |
| **Receptive relaxation and mixing waves** | Stomach | Stomach relaxes to receive food; churning mixes food into chyme |
| **Gastric emptying** | Pylorus | Chyme released in small amounts; liquids empty quickly, fats slowest (a mixed meal takes about 3–4 hours) |
| **Segmentation** | Small intestine | Ring-like contractions chop and mix chyme with juices and bring it into contact with the mucosa |
| **Migrating motor complex** | Stomach and small intestine between meals | Sweeps leftover material onward ("housekeeper") |
| **Haustral churning** | Colon | Slow mixing that helps water absorption |
| **Mass movements** | Colon | Strong waves 2–3 times a day, often after meals (**gastrocolic reflex**), pushing faeces into the rectum |
| **Defaecation** | Rectum and anus | Elimination of faeces |

### Stages of swallowing

1. **Oral (voluntary)** — tongue pushes the bolus back into the pharynx.
2. **Pharyngeal (involuntary reflex)** — soft palate rises to close the nasal passage, larynx rises and the epiglottis covers it, breathing stops for a moment (deglutition apnoea), upper oesophageal sphincter opens. The swallowing centre is in the medulla and pons.
3. **Oesophageal (involuntary)** — peristalsis carries the bolus to the stomach in a few seconds; the lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes to let it in.

### Defaecation reflex

Faeces entering the rectum stretch its wall → impulses to the sacral spinal cord (S2–S4) → parasympathetic signals contract the rectum and relax the internal anal sphincter. The external anal sphincter is under voluntary control (pudendal nerve), so defaecation can be postponed. Straining (Valsalva) — closing the glottis and contracting abdominal muscles — raises abdominal pressure to help expel faeces.

## 3.9 Digestion and absorption

### Digestion in different parts

| Site | Carbohydrates | Proteins | Fats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mouth** | Salivary amylase: starch → maltose, dextrins (short time) | — | Lingual lipase (small) |
| **Stomach** | Salivary amylase acts inside the bolus until acid stops it | Pepsin: proteins → proteoses, peptones, polypeptides | Gastric lipase (small); in infants rennin curdles milk |
| **Small intestine** | Pancreatic amylase → disaccharides; brush-border enzymes: maltose → glucose + glucose; sucrose → glucose + fructose; lactose → glucose + galactose | Trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase → small peptides; brush-border peptidases → amino acids, di- and tripeptides | Bile emulsifies; pancreatic lipase → monoglycerides + fatty acids |
| **Large intestine** | Bacterial fermentation of fibre | Bacterial putrefaction | — |

### Absorption

The small intestine is built for absorption: **circular folds**, **villi** and **microvilli** (brush border) increase the surface area hundreds of times; each villus has a capillary network and a central lymph vessel (**lacteal**).

| Nutrient | How it is absorbed | Route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glucose and galactose | Secondary active transport with Na+ (SGLT1) | Blood → portal vein |
| Fructose | Facilitated diffusion (GLUT5) | Blood |
| Amino acids, di- and tripeptides | Na+-coupled and H+-coupled transport | Blood |
| Fatty acids and monoglycerides | Enter cells from micelles by diffusion; re-made into triglycerides and packed as **chylomicrons** | **Lymph (lacteals)** → thoracic duct → blood |
| Short- and medium-chain fatty acids | Diffuse directly | Blood |
| Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) | With fats in micelles | Lymph |
| Water | Osmosis, following absorbed solutes | Blood |
| Iron, calcium | Active, mainly duodenum | Blood |
| Vitamin B12 | Bound to intrinsic factor, terminal ileum | Blood |
| Bile salts | Active, terminal ileum | Blood (enterohepatic) |

About 8–9 litres of fluid enter the gut each day (diet plus secretions); most is absorbed in the small intestine and most of the rest in the colon.

## 3.10 Application and implications in nursing

- **Nil by mouth before surgery** — food in the stomach can be vomited and inhaled under anaesthesia; the fasting time follows the hospital's anaesthesia protocol.
- **Vomiting** loses acid (H+ and Cl−) and K+ → metabolic alkalosis, dehydration and hypokalaemia; **diarrhoea** loses bicarbonate-rich fluid and K+ → metabolic acidosis and dehydration. Oral rehydration solution works because glucose and sodium are absorbed together (SGLT1), pulling water with them.
- **Nasogastric tube care** — check tube position as per protocol before every feed or drug; gastric aspirate is normally acidic.
- **Feeding position** — sitting up for meals and feeds, and for about 30 minutes after, reduces reflux and aspiration; check the swallow and gag reflex in stroke patients before oral feeding.
- **Jaundice** — note yellow sclera, colour of urine and stools; clay-coloured stools and dark urine suggest blocked bile flow; check for bleeding (vitamin K).
- **Constipation** — encourage fluids, fibre, activity and a regular time after meals (gastrocolic reflex); avoid ignoring the urge.
- **Bowel sounds** are checked after abdominal surgery; return of peristalsis guides when oral feeding can restart.
- **Mouth care** for patients who cannot eat — saliva flow falls and infection (parotitis, thrush) can follow.
- **Patients after gastrectomy** may lack intrinsic factor and need lifelong vitamin B12 as prescribed; they also tolerate small frequent meals better (dumping).

## Remember

- Saliva 1–1.5 L/day, pH near neutral; ptyalin starts starch digestion; control is purely nervous (facial and glossopharyngeal nerves).
- Gastric juice pH 1–3.5: parietal cells → HCl + intrinsic factor; chief cells → pepsinogen; G cells → gastrin.
- HCl via carbonic anhydrase and the H+–K+ pump; alkaline tide after meals.
- Phases of gastric secretion: cephalic 30%, gastric 60%, intestinal 10%.
- Pancreatic juice pH about 8; secretin → bicarbonate-rich juice; CCK → enzyme-rich juice and gall bladder contraction; enterokinase activates trypsinogen.
- Bile salts emulsify fat and form micelles; 90–95% are reabsorbed in the ileum (enterohepatic circulation).
- Segmentation mixes; peristalsis propels; mass movements fill the rectum (gastrocolic reflex).
- Sugars and amino acids → portal blood; fats → chylomicrons → lacteals.
- B12 needs intrinsic factor and is absorbed in the terminal ileum.
