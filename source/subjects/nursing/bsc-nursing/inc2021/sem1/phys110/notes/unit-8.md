# Unit 8: The nervous system

This unit covers INC Unit XI (8 hours) — the largest single unit and the one the INC tests with "brief structured essays". It covers neurons and nerve impulses, functions of the brain parts, sensory and motor systems, peripheral and autonomic nerves, limbic system and higher functions, the vestibular apparatus, cranial nerves, pain, reflexes, and CSF with the blood–brain barrier.

## 8.1 Overview of the nervous system

The nervous system **receives** information (sensory input), **integrates** it (decides what to do) and **responds** (motor output) — quickly, through electrical impulses.

```text
Nervous system
├── Central nervous system (CNS): brain + spinal cord
└── Peripheral nervous system (PNS): 12 pairs cranial nerves + 31 pairs spinal nerves
      ├── Afferent (sensory) division — carries impulses TO the CNS
      └── Efferent (motor) division — carries impulses FROM the CNS
            ├── Somatic nervous system → skeletal muscles (voluntary)
            └── Autonomic nervous system → heart, smooth muscle, glands (involuntary)
                   ├── Sympathetic
                   └── Parasympathetic
```

## 8.2 Neurons — types, structure and functions (review)

The **neuron** is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Mature neurons do not divide (so damage to the CNS is largely permanent).

**Parts**

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Cell body (soma) | Contains nucleus and Nissl granules (protein synthesis); keeps the neuron alive |
| **Dendrites** | Short branched processes that **receive** impulses and carry them **towards** the cell body |
| **Axon** | Single long process carrying impulses **away** from the cell body; begins at the axon hillock (where impulses start) and ends in axon terminals (synaptic knobs) |
| **Myelin sheath** | Fatty insulating layer formed by **Schwann cells** (PNS) or **oligodendrocytes** (CNS); speeds conduction |
| **Nodes of Ranvier** | Gaps in the myelin where the impulse "jumps" |
| Neurilemma | Outer Schwann cell layer in PNS; helps regeneration of cut peripheral nerves |

**Types**

- By structure: **multipolar** (many dendrites, one axon — motor neurons, most CNS neurons), **bipolar** (retina, olfactory epithelium, inner ear), **pseudounipolar** (sensory neurons in dorsal root ganglia).
- By function: **sensory (afferent)**, **motor (efferent)**, **interneurons (association neurons)** — interneurons, entirely within the CNS, are the most numerous.

**Neuroglia** support neurons: astrocytes (blood–brain barrier, nutrition, repair), oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin), microglia (phagocytes), ependymal cells (line ventricles, help form CSF); Schwann cells and satellite cells in the PNS.

**Properties of nerve fibres**: excitability, conductivity, all-or-none response, refractory period, summation, adaptation, and practical **indefatigability** (nerve fibres themselves hardly fatigue; synapses do).

**Classification of fibres by speed**: **A** fibres (myelinated, large; fastest, up to about 120 m/s — motor to skeletal muscle, touch, proprioception; Aδ for fast pain), **B** fibres (myelinated, preganglionic autonomic), **C** fibres (unmyelinated, thin, slowest, about 0.5–2 m/s — slow pain, postganglionic autonomic).

**Regeneration**: a cut peripheral nerve can regrow slowly (about 1–3 mm a day) along its Schwann cell tube if the cell body survives; CNS axons largely fail to regenerate.

## 8.3 The nerve impulse

### Resting membrane potential

At rest the inside of a neuron is **negative** compared with the outside — about **−70 mV**. This is due to:

1. The **Na+–K+ pump** (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in).
2. The membrane being far more permeable to K+ than to Na+ at rest; K+ leaks out, leaving negative proteins inside.
3. Large negatively charged proteins and phosphates that cannot leave the cell.

### Action potential

```text
 mV
 +30 ┤        /\            ← overshoot
   0 ┤───────/──\──────────
     │      /    \           depolarisation: Na+ rushes IN
 −55 ┤ ····/······\········· threshold
 −70 ┤──/          \    ___  resting potential
     │              \__/     hyperpolarisation: K+ still leaving
                    repolarisation: K+ moves OUT
```

1. **Stimulus** depolarises the membrane to **threshold** (about −55 mV).
2. **Depolarisation** — voltage-gated **Na+ channels open**; Na+ rushes in; the inside becomes positive (about +30 mV).
3. **Repolarisation** — Na+ channels close (inactivate) and voltage-gated **K+ channels open**; K+ flows out; inside becomes negative again.
4. **Hyperpolarisation** — K+ channels close slowly, so the potential briefly dips below −70 mV.
5. The **Na+–K+ pump** restores the original ion distribution over time.

- **All-or-none law** — once threshold is reached, the action potential is full size; a stronger stimulus produces more impulses per second, not bigger ones.
- **Refractory period** — during the **absolute refractory period** no second impulse can be started; during the **relative refractory period** only a stronger stimulus can. This limits the firing rate and keeps impulses moving in one direction.

### Propagation

- In **unmyelinated** fibres the impulse spreads continuously along the membrane (slow).
- In **myelinated** fibres it jumps from node to node — **saltatory conduction** — which is much faster and uses less energy. Diseases destroying myelin (for example multiple sclerosis, Guillain–Barré syndrome) slow or block conduction.
- Speed increases with fibre diameter and myelination.

### Synapse

A **synapse** is the junction where an impulse passes from one neuron to the next (or to a muscle or gland).

1. Impulse reaches the axon terminal → voltage-gated Ca2+ channels open → Ca2+ enters.
2. Vesicles release a **neurotransmitter** into the synaptic cleft (exocytosis).
3. Transmitter binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane → excitatory (depolarising) or inhibitory (hyperpolarising) potential.
4. Transmitter is removed by enzymes (acetylcholinesterase), reuptake or diffusion.

**Properties of synapses**: one-way conduction; **synaptic delay** (about 0.5 ms); summation (spatial and temporal); fatigue; sensitivity to hypoxia and drugs. **Common neurotransmitters**: acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin, glutamate (main excitatory in CNS), GABA (main inhibitory in brain), glycine (inhibitory in spinal cord), endorphins.

## 8.4 Functions of the brain

### Medulla oblongata

- Contains **vital centres**: **cardiac centre** (heart rate), **vasomotor centre** (blood vessel tone, BP), **respiratory centres** (rhythm of breathing).
- **Reflex centres**: swallowing, coughing, sneezing, vomiting, salivation.
- Pathway for all ascending and descending tracts; **decussation (crossing) of the pyramids** — most motor fibres cross here, so each side of the brain controls the opposite side of the body.
- Nuclei of cranial nerves IX, X, XI, XII.
- Damage to the medulla (for example by compression when intracranial pressure is high — coning) is rapidly fatal.

### Pons

- **Pneumotaxic and apneustic centres** regulate breathing rate and depth.
- Bridge (relay) between the cerebrum, cerebellum and medulla.
- Nuclei of cranial nerves V, VI, VII, VIII; part of the micturition control.

### Midbrain

- Centres for **visual reflexes** (superior colliculi — turning eyes and head towards a sudden sight) and **auditory reflexes** (inferior colliculi — startle to sound).
- Pupillary light reflex pathway; nuclei of cranial nerves III and IV.
- Substantia nigra (dopamine) — loss causes Parkinson's disease.

### Cerebellum

- Coordinates voluntary movements so they are smooth and accurate (compares intended and actual movement).
- Maintains **balance and posture** (with the vestibular system) and **muscle tone**.
- Involved in learning motor skills.
- **Signs of cerebellar damage** (on the same side as the lesion): **ataxia** (unsteady, wide-based, staggering gait), **intention tremor** (tremor when reaching for an object), **dysdiadochokinesia** (cannot do rapid alternating movements), **dysmetria** (past-pointing), **nystagmus**, slurred (scanning) speech, **hypotonia**. Alcohol temporarily affects the cerebellum — the basis of the drunken gait.

### Cerebrum

The largest part; the **cerebral cortex** (grey matter) is divided into lobes:

| Lobe | Main areas and functions |
| --- | --- |
| **Frontal** | **Primary motor area** (precentral gyrus, area 4) — voluntary movement of the opposite side, body represented upside down ("motor homunculus"); premotor area (planned skilled movements); **Broca's motor speech area** (area 44, usually left side) — damage causes expressive aphasia (knows what to say but cannot say it); prefrontal area — personality, judgement, planning, social behaviour |
| **Parietal** | **Primary somatosensory area** (postcentral gyrus, areas 3, 1, 2) — touch, pressure, pain, temperature, position from the opposite side; sensory association — recognising objects by touch; taste area near the lateral sulcus |
| **Temporal** | **Primary auditory area** (areas 41, 42); **Wernicke's area** (understanding speech — damage causes receptive aphasia with fluent but meaningless speech); smell; memory (with hippocampus) |
| **Occipital** | **Primary visual area** (area 17) and visual association areas — seeing and interpreting what is seen |

The **dominant hemisphere** (left in most right-handed and many left-handed people) controls language. **Basal ganglia** (deep grey matter: caudate, putamen, globus pallidus) help start and smooth movements and control posture and tone — their disease gives tremor, rigidity or involuntary movements.

## 8.5 Sensory and motor nervous system

### Sensory system

**Receptors** convert stimuli into nerve impulses. Classified by stimulus: mechanoreceptors (touch, pressure, stretch), thermoreceptors, nociceptors (pain), chemoreceptors, photoreceptors. Receptors show **adaptation** (touch adapts fast, pain hardly at all).

**Ascending (sensory) tracts**

| Tract | Sensations | Crossing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dorsal (posterior) column – medial lemniscus** | Fine touch, two-point discrimination, vibration, **proprioception** (position sense) | Crosses in the medulla |
| **Lateral spinothalamic** | **Pain and temperature** | Crosses in the spinal cord within a segment or two of entry |
| **Anterior spinothalamic** | Crude touch and pressure | Crosses in the spinal cord |
| **Spinocerebellar** | Unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum | Mostly uncrossed |

General pathway: receptor → first-order neuron (dorsal root ganglion) → second-order neuron (spinal cord or medulla; crosses) → **thalamus** → third-order neuron → **somatosensory cortex** (parietal lobe).

### Motor system

- **Pyramidal (corticospinal) tract** — from the motor cortex through the internal capsule and brainstem; about 80–90% of fibres cross at the medullary pyramids (lateral corticospinal tract), the rest cross lower down. Controls **skilled voluntary movements**, especially of the hands and fingers.
- **Extrapyramidal tracts** (from basal ganglia, brainstem nuclei — rubrospinal, vestibulospinal, reticulospinal, tectospinal) — control **posture, muscle tone, balance** and automatic movements.
- **Upper motor neuron (UMN)** — from the cortex to the brainstem or spinal cord. **Lower motor neuron (LMN)** — from the anterior horn cell (or cranial nerve nucleus) to the muscle ("final common pathway").

| Feature | UMN lesion (e.g. stroke) | LMN lesion (e.g. polio, peripheral nerve injury) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paralysis | Spastic; whole limb or half body | Flaccid; individual muscles |
| Muscle tone | Increased (spasticity) | Decreased (hypotonia) |
| Tendon reflexes | Exaggerated | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar response | **Extensor (Babinski sign positive)** | Flexor or absent |
| Muscle wasting | Little (disuse only) | Marked, early |
| Fasciculations | Absent | May be present |

**Clonus** (rhythmic contractions on sudden stretch) also indicates a UMN lesion.

## 8.6 Peripheral nervous system

### Spinal nerves

- **31 pairs**: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal.
- Each is formed by a **dorsal (posterior) root — sensory**, with its dorsal root ganglion, and a **ventral (anterior) root — motor** (Bell–Magendie law); so every spinal nerve is **mixed**.
- Except in the thoracic region, the front branches form **plexuses**: cervical (phrenic nerve to the diaphragm, C3–C5), brachial (nerves of the arm: radial, median, ulnar), lumbar (femoral), sacral (**sciatic** — avoid in IM injections).
- **Dermatome** — the area of skin supplied by one spinal nerve; useful to locate spinal cord or root lesions (for example T10 at the umbilicus).

### Cranial nerves and their functions

| No. | Name | Type | Main function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | Olfactory | Sensory | Smell |
| II | Optic | Sensory | Vision |
| III | Oculomotor | Motor (with parasympathetic) | Most eye movements, raises the eyelid, constricts pupil, accommodation |
| IV | Trochlear | Motor | Superior oblique — eye down and out |
| V | Trigeminal | Mixed | Sensation of face, cornea, teeth; muscles of chewing |
| VI | Abducens | Motor | Lateral rectus — eye outward |
| VII | Facial | Mixed | Muscles of facial expression; taste anterior 2/3 of tongue; tears, submandibular and sublingual saliva |
| VIII | Vestibulocochlear | Sensory | Hearing (cochlear) and balance (vestibular) |
| IX | Glossopharyngeal | Mixed | Taste posterior 1/3; pharynx sensation; gag reflex (afferent); parotid saliva; carotid sinus and body |
| X | Vagus | Mixed | Parasympathetic to heart, lungs, gut (as far as proximal colon); pharynx and larynx muscles (swallowing, voice); aortic receptors |
| XI | Accessory | Motor | Sternocleidomastoid and trapezius (turning head, shrugging) |
| XII | Hypoglossal | Motor | Movements of the tongue |

A memory aid for the type (S = sensory, M = motor, B = both): "Some Say Marry Money, But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More" (I–XII).

## 8.7 Autonomic nervous system and autonomic functions

The **autonomic nervous system (ANS)** controls the internal organs automatically: heart, smooth muscle of vessels and viscera, and glands. Its higher control centre is the **hypothalamus**, with centres in the brainstem and spinal cord. Each pathway has **two neurons**: preganglionic (from CNS to a ganglion) and postganglionic (ganglion to organ).

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin (outflow) | **Thoracolumbar** — lateral horn T1–L2 | **Craniosacral** — cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X and sacral S2–S4 |
| Ganglia | Close to the spinal cord (sympathetic chain) | Near or in the organ |
| Preganglionic fibres | Short | Long |
| Neurotransmitter at ganglion | Acetylcholine | Acetylcholine |
| Postganglionic transmitter | **Noradrenaline** (except sweat glands — acetylcholine) | **Acetylcholine** |
| Overall role | "**Fight or flight**" — prepares the body for emergency | "**Rest and digest**" — conserves energy |

### Effects on organs

| Organ | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pupil | Dilates | Constricts |
| Salivary glands | Scanty thick saliva | Plenty of watery saliva |
| Heart | ↑ rate and force | ↓ rate |
| Bronchioles | Dilate | Constrict, ↑ secretions |
| Blood vessels (skin, gut) | Constrict | Little effect |
| Blood vessels (skeletal muscle) | Dilate | — |
| Gut movement and secretion | ↓; sphincters contract | ↑; sphincters relax |
| Liver | Glycogenolysis → ↑ blood glucose | Glycogen formation |
| Urinary bladder | Detrusor relaxes, internal sphincter contracts (storage) | Detrusor contracts (voiding) |
| Sweat glands | ↑ sweating | — |
| Adrenal medulla | Secretes adrenaline | — |
| Male sex organs | Ejaculation | Erection |

**Autonomic functions** in summary: control of heart rate, BP, breathing (with the respiratory centre), digestion, micturition, defaecation, sweating, body temperature, pupil size and sexual function — all largely without conscious effort.

## 8.8 Limbic system and higher mental functions

### Limbic system

A ring of structures around the brainstem — the "emotional brain": hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate gyrus, parts of the hypothalamus and thalamus, olfactory areas.

| Structure | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **Hippocampus** | Converts short-term memory into long-term memory (learning); spatial memory. Damaged early in Alzheimer's disease |
| **Amygdala** | Emotions, especially **fear** and aggression; emotional memory |
| **Hypothalamus** | See below |
| **Thalamus** | See below |
| Cingulate gyrus | Links emotion with behaviour |

### Hypothalamus — functions

1. **Temperature regulation** (thermostat).
2. **Hunger and satiety** (feeding and satiety centres).
3. **Thirst** and water balance — makes **ADH** (and oxytocin).
4. Control of the **anterior pituitary** by releasing and inhibiting hormones.
5. Highest centre of the **autonomic nervous system** (heart rate, BP, gut).
6. **Emotional** and behavioural responses (rage, pleasure, sexual behaviour).
7. **Circadian rhythms** and sleep–wake cycle (suprachiasmatic nucleus).

### Thalamus — functions

- Major **relay station** for all sensations except smell, on their way to the cortex.
- **Crude awareness** of pain, temperature and touch.
- Relay for motor signals from cerebellum and basal ganglia to the motor cortex.
- Part of the system keeping us awake and alert (with the reticular activating system).

### Higher mental functions

- **Consciousness and alertness** — maintained by the **reticular activating system** of the brainstem projecting to the cortex.
- **Learning and memory** — short-term (seconds to minutes) and long-term memory; the hippocampus is needed to form new long-term memories.
- **Speech and language** — Broca's (expression) and Wernicke's (comprehension) areas in the dominant hemisphere.
- **Thinking, judgement, personality** — prefrontal cortex.
- **Sleep** — alternating **NREM** sleep (deep, restorative, slow-wave stages) and **REM** sleep (rapid eye movements, dreaming, muscle relaxation), cycling about every 90 minutes.

## 8.9 Vestibular apparatus

Part of the inner ear concerned with **balance (equilibrium)**; it signals head position and movement through the vestibular part of cranial nerve VIII.

| Part | Receptor | Detects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Semicircular canals** (three, at right angles) | **Crista ampullaris** in the ampulla, with hair cells in a jelly cupula | **Rotational (angular) acceleration** — turning the head; endolymph lags behind and bends the cupula |
| **Utricle and saccule** | **Macula** with hair cells under a jelly layer containing **otoliths** (calcium carbonate crystals) | **Static head position** relative to gravity and **linear acceleration** (moving forward, in a lift) |

Impulses go to the vestibular nuclei, cerebellum, eye-movement nuclei (keeping the eyes steady while the head moves — vestibulo-ocular reflex), spinal cord (postural muscles) and cortex (awareness of position).

Disturbances: **vertigo** (false sense of spinning), **nystagmus**, **motion sickness** (conflict between visual and vestibular signals → nausea, vomiting), Ménière's disease, benign positional vertigo.

## 8.10 Physiology of pain

**Pain** is an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage. It is protective — it warns of injury.

- **Receptors**: **nociceptors** — free nerve endings stimulated by mechanical, thermal and chemical damage. Chemicals released by injured tissue (bradykinin, prostaglandins, histamine, K+, H+, serotonin) stimulate or sensitise them. Nociceptors adapt very little.
- **Fibres and types of pain**:

| | Fast pain | Slow pain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fibres | Aδ (myelinated) | C (unmyelinated) |
| Felt | Within about 0.1 s | After 1 s or more, increasing |
| Quality | Sharp, pricking, well localised | Burning, aching, throbbing, poorly localised |
| Example | Needle prick | Ache after an injury |

- **Pathway**: nociceptor → dorsal horn of spinal cord (substance P, glutamate) → crosses → **lateral spinothalamic tract** → thalamus → somatosensory cortex (localisation) and limbic system (emotional suffering).
- **Gate control theory (Melzack and Wall)** — impulses in large touch fibres activate inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal horn that "close the gate" to pain signals. This explains why rubbing a hurt area, massage, warmth and electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) reduce pain. Descending signals from the brain (influenced by attention, anxiety, mood) can also open or close the gate.
- **Endogenous pain control** — the body makes its own opioids (**endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins**) acting in the brainstem and spinal cord; released in stress, exercise and by placebo effects.

### Somatic, visceral and referred pain

| Type | Source | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Somatic — superficial** | Skin, mucous membranes | Sharp, well localised |
| **Somatic — deep** | Muscles, bones, joints, tendons | Dull, aching, less localised |
| **Visceral** | Internal organs | Poorly localised; caused by **stretch/distension, ischaemia, spasm, inflammation and chemical irritation** — not by cutting or burning; often with nausea, sweating, BP change |
| **Referred** | Pain from a viscus felt at a distant body surface | Felt in skin areas supplied by the same spinal segment |

**Referred pain** occurs because visceral and skin afferents from the same spinal segment **converge** on the same neurons in the spinal cord; the brain, used to signals from the skin, wrongly places the pain there.

| Organ | Pain referred to |
| --- | --- |
| Heart (angina, infarction) | Centre of chest, left shoulder and **inner side of the left arm**, neck, jaw |
| Diaphragm (irritation, e.g. by blood or pus) | **Tip of the shoulder** (C3–C5) |
| Appendix (early) | Around the **umbilicus** |
| Gall bladder | Right upper abdomen, right shoulder blade |
| Kidney and ureter | Loin to groin, testis or labium |
| Stomach, duodenum | Epigastrium |

## 8.11 Reflexes

A **reflex** is an automatic, rapid, involuntary response to a stimulus, not needing conscious thought.

### Reflex arc

```text
Stimulus → RECEPTOR → AFFERENT (sensory) neuron → CENTRE (spinal cord/brainstem;
           synapse, ± interneuron) → EFFERENT (motor) neuron → EFFECTOR (muscle/gland) → Response
```

### Types

- **Monosynaptic (stretch) reflex** — one synapse between sensory and motor neuron, e.g. **knee jerk**: tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps → muscle spindle → sensory neuron → spinal cord (L2–L4) → motor neuron → quadriceps contracts → leg kicks.
- **Polysynaptic reflex** — one or more interneurons, e.g. **withdrawal (flexor) reflex**: touching a hot object → limb pulled away; with the **crossed extensor reflex** the opposite leg straightens to bear weight.
- **Superficial reflexes** — corneal (V and VII), abdominal, plantar (normal: toes curl down; in UMN lesions and infants under about 1 year the big toe goes up — Babinski sign), cremasteric.
- **Deep tendon reflexes** — biceps (C5–C6), triceps (C6–C7), supinator (C5–C6), knee (L2–L4), ankle (S1–S2).
- **Visceral (autonomic) reflexes** — pupillary light reflex, baroreceptor reflex, micturition, defaecation, vomiting, coughing, swallowing.
- **Conditioned reflexes** — learned (salivation at the sound linked with food).

**Clinical value**: reflexes test the integrity of the sensory nerve, spinal segment and motor nerve; exaggerated reflexes suggest UMN lesions, absent reflexes suggest LMN or sensory lesions; a lost corneal and pupillary reflex is serious in the unconscious patient. Reflexes may be graded 0 (absent) to 4+ (very brisk with clonus).

## 8.12 Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)

**Formation**: mainly by the **choroid plexuses** of the ventricles (especially the lateral ventricles), partly by ependyma. About **500 mL is formed per day**, while the total volume at any time is about **150 mL** — so it is renewed several times a day.

**Composition (normal adult)**

| Feature | Normal |
| --- | --- |
| Appearance | Clear and colourless |
| Pressure (lying down, lumbar) | about 70–180 mm of CSF (water) |
| Protein | about 15–45 mg/dL (much less than plasma) |
| Glucose | about 50–80 mg/dL — roughly 60% of blood glucose |
| Chloride | slightly higher than plasma |
| Cells | 0–5 lymphocytes per µL; no red cells |

In bacterial meningitis CSF becomes turbid with many neutrophils, high protein and low glucose.

**Circulation**

```text
Choroid plexus of LATERAL VENTRICLES
   → interventricular foramina (of Monro)
   → THIRD VENTRICLE (+ more from its choroid plexus)
   → cerebral aqueduct (of Sylvius)
   → FOURTH VENTRICLE
   → median aperture (Magendie) and two lateral apertures (Luschka)
   → SUBARACHNOID SPACE around brain and spinal cord (small amount in central canal)
   → absorbed through ARACHNOID VILLI (granulations) into the DURAL VENOUS SINUSES
```

**Functions of CSF**

1. **Protection** — a fluid cushion that absorbs shock.
2. **Buoyancy** — the brain floats, so its effective weight is greatly reduced and it does not crush its own lower parts and vessels.
3. **Chemical stability** — keeps the environment of neurons constant; changes in its H+ drive breathing (central chemoreceptors).
4. **Nutrition and waste removal** for nervous tissue.
5. Helps adjust **intracranial pressure** — when brain volume increases, CSF is displaced.

**Hydrocephalus** — excess CSF due to blocked flow or poor absorption; in infants the head enlarges and fontanelles bulge. CSF is sampled by **lumbar puncture** below the end of the spinal cord, usually between the L3–L4 or L4–L5 vertebrae (the cord ends at about L1–L2 in adults).

## 8.13 Blood–brain barrier and blood–CSF barrier

### Blood–brain barrier (BBB)

- Formed by **tight junctions** between the endothelial cells of brain capillaries, a continuous basement membrane and the foot processes of **astrocytes**.
- Lets through: water, O2, CO2, glucose (by carriers), lipid-soluble substances (alcohol, anaesthetic gases, many sedatives).
- Keeps out: most proteins, many water-soluble drugs (including many antibiotics), bile pigments in adults, toxins and microbes.
- **Absent** in a few regions (circumventricular organs) such as the **area postrema** (vomiting centre's trigger zone — can sense toxins in blood), posterior pituitary and parts of the hypothalamus that monitor blood.
- **Immature in newborns** — so high unconjugated bilirubin can enter the brain (kernicterus).
- Inflammation (meningitis) makes it leakier, allowing some drugs in.

### Blood–CSF barrier

- At the **choroid plexus**: the barrier is the **epithelial cells** of the choroid plexus joined by tight junctions (the capillaries there are leaky).
- Controls what enters the CSF from blood; active transport keeps CSF composition different from plasma.

**Importance**: protects neurons from harmful substances and fluctuations in blood chemistry; explains why some drugs do not reach the brain and why others (such as some antibiotics) are chosen for brain infections.

## 8.14 Application and implication in nursing

- **Neurological observations** — level of consciousness using the **Glasgow Coma Scale** (eye opening 1–4, verbal response 1–5, best motor response 1–6; total 3–15), pupil size, equality and reaction to light, limb power, vital signs. A falling GCS, a dilating pupil, rising BP with a slow pulse and irregular breathing suggest rising intracranial pressure — report at once.
- **Unconscious patient** — airway first; side-lying position; eye care (no blink); turning, mouth care, bladder and bowel care; talk to the patient (hearing may persist).
- **Pain** — pain is what the patient says it is; assess site, intensity (numerical or faces scale), character, duration and effect; use non-drug methods (positioning, massage, warmth or cold, distraction) as well as prescribed analgesics; remember that chest pain may be felt in the arm or jaw and shoulder pain may come from the diaphragm.
- **Paralysed patients** — UMN lesions bring spasticity and contractures; LMN lesions bring flaccidity and wasting; both need positioning, range-of-motion exercises and skin care. Patients with lost sensation need protection from burns and pressure.
- **Lumbar puncture** — explain, position correctly (curled lateral or sitting bent forward to open the spaces), maintain asepsis, label samples, observe for headache, leakage and change in consciousness afterwards.
- **Vertigo and falls** — help patients with balance problems to move slowly, use support and avoid sudden head movements.
- **Autonomic effects** — fear raises pulse, BP and blood sugar and dries the mouth; reassurance and a calm environment matter. After spinal injury, watch bladder and bowel function and sudden BP changes.
- **IM injections** — knowledge of the sciatic nerve course guides safe site selection.

## Remember

- Resting membrane potential about −70 mV; action potential: Na+ in (depolarisation), K+ out (repolarisation); all-or-none; saltatory conduction in myelinated fibres.
- Synapse: Ca2+ entry → transmitter release; one-way; synaptic delay ≈ 0.5 ms.
- Medulla: cardiac, vasomotor, respiratory centres; pons: pneumotaxic and apneustic; cerebellum: coordination, balance, tone (ataxia, intention tremor if damaged).
- Frontal: motor (area 4), Broca's (44); parietal: sensory (3,1,2); temporal: hearing (41,42), Wernicke's; occipital: vision (17).
- Dorsal column: fine touch, vibration, proprioception; lateral spinothalamic: pain and temperature.
- UMN lesion: spastic, brisk reflexes, Babinski positive; LMN: flaccid, absent reflexes, wasting.
- 31 pairs spinal nerves (8, 12, 5, 5, 1); 12 cranial nerves — know each function.
- Sympathetic: thoracolumbar, noradrenaline, fight or flight; parasympathetic: craniosacral (III, VII, IX, X, S2–S4), acetylcholine, rest and digest.
- Hypothalamus: temperature, hunger, thirst, ADH, pituitary, ANS, emotion, circadian rhythm; thalamus: sensory relay.
- Semicircular canals: rotation; utricle and saccule: head position and linear acceleration.
- Fast pain Aδ, slow pain C; gate control theory; referred pain by convergence (heart → left arm).
- Knee jerk L2–L4 (monosynaptic); ankle S1–S2.
- CSF: choroid plexus, about 500 mL/day, volume about 150 mL; lateral → third → aqueduct → fourth → subarachnoid space → arachnoid villi.
- BBB: tight junctions and astrocytes; absent at area postrema; immature in newborns (kernicterus).
