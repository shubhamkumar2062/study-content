# Unit 4: The circulatory system and blood

This file joins INC Unit IV, Circulatory and lymphatic system (6 hours), and Unit V, Blood (5 hours), because the heart, vessels and blood work as one transport system. Part A covers the heart and circulation; Part B covers blood, haemostasis, blood groups and immunity. Essay questions are common from both parts.

# Part A — Heart and circulation

## 4.1 Functions of the heart and properties of cardiac muscle

The heart is a double pump: the **right side** pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation); the **left side** pumps oxygenated blood to the whole body (systemic circulation). It also secretes **atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)**, which increases sodium and water loss in urine when the atria are stretched.

**Properties of cardiac muscle**

| Property | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Rhythmicity (autorhythmicity)** | Produces its own impulses without nerve supply (from the SA node) |
| **Excitability** | Responds to a stimulus with an action potential |
| **Conductivity** | Impulse spreads from cell to cell through gap junctions in intercalated discs |
| **Contractility** | Contracts as a whole; follows the **all-or-none law** for the whole chamber |
| **Long refractory period** | About as long as the contraction itself, so the heart **cannot be tetanised** — it must relax and fill between beats |

## 4.2 Conduction system of the heart

```text
SA node (right atrium, near opening of SVC) — pacemaker, about 70–80/min at rest
      │  impulse spreads through both atria → atrial contraction
      ▼
AV node (lower part of interatrial septum) — DELAY of about 0.1 s
      │  lets atria finish emptying before ventricles contract
      ▼
Bundle of His → right and left bundle branches (interventricular septum)
      ▼
Purkinje fibres → ventricular muscle (from apex upward) → ventricular contraction
```

If the SA node fails, lower tissues take over at slower intrinsic rates: AV node about 40–60/min; Purkinje/ventricular tissue about 15–40/min. This is why complete heart block gives a very slow pulse.

**ECG (electrocardiogram)** — the record of the heart's electrical activity from the skin:

| Wave | Represents |
| --- | --- |
| P wave | Atrial depolarisation |
| PR interval (about 0.12–0.20 s) | Time from start of atrial depolarisation to start of ventricular depolarisation (includes AV delay) |
| QRS complex (under about 0.12 s) | Ventricular depolarisation (atrial repolarisation is hidden in it) |
| T wave | Ventricular repolarisation |

## 4.3 The cardiac cycle

The **cardiac cycle** is the sequence of events in one heartbeat — one systole and one diastole of atria and ventricles. At a heart rate of 75/min, one cycle lasts **0.8 s**.

| Event | Duration at 75/min |
| --- | --- |
| Atrial systole | about 0.1 s |
| Atrial diastole | about 0.7 s |
| Ventricular systole | about 0.3 s |
| Ventricular diastole | about 0.5 s |

When the heart rate rises, **diastole shortens much more than systole**. Because the ventricles fill and the coronary arteries (left side) flow mainly in diastole, very fast rates reduce filling and coronary flow.

### Phases (left ventricle)

1. **Atrial systole** — atria contract and top up the ventricles (adds about 20–30% of filling; most filling is passive).
2. **Isovolumetric (isometric) contraction** — ventricles start contracting; the AV valves close (**first heart sound**); semilunar valves are still closed; pressure rises sharply with no change in volume.
3. **Ejection** — ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure (about 80 mmHg), the aortic valve opens; rapid then slow ejection.
4. **Isovolumetric relaxation** — ventricles relax; the semilunar valves close (**second heart sound**); all valves closed; pressure falls with no volume change.
5. **Rapid filling** — when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure the AV valves open and blood rushes in (third heart sound may be heard in children and young adults).
6. **Slow filling (diastasis)** — then the next atrial systole.

### Heart sounds

| Sound | Cause | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 "lub" | Closure of mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of ventricular systole | Longer, lower-pitched; best at the apex |
| S2 "dub" | Closure of aortic and pulmonary valves at the start of ventricular diastole | Shorter, sharper; best at the base |
| S3 | Rapid ventricular filling | Normal in children; may mean heart failure in adults |
| S4 | Atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle | Usually abnormal |

A **murmur** is the sound of turbulent flow through a narrowed (stenosed) or leaking (regurgitant) valve.

## 4.4 Stroke volume and cardiac output

| Term | Definition | Normal adult value (at rest) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| End-diastolic volume (EDV) | Blood in each ventricle at the end of filling | about 120–130 mL |
| End-systolic volume (ESV) | Blood left after contraction | about 50–60 mL |
| **Stroke volume (SV)** | EDV − ESV: blood pumped by one ventricle per beat | about 70 mL |
| **Ejection fraction** | SV ÷ EDV × 100 | about 55–70% |
| **Cardiac output (CO)** | SV × heart rate: blood pumped by one ventricle per minute | about **5 L/min** (70 mL × 72) |
| Cardiac index | CO per square metre of body surface | about 3.2 L/min/m² |

The **cardiac reserve** is the ability to raise output above the resting level: in exercise output can rise four to six times or more.

### Factors affecting cardiac output

1. **Preload (venous return / end-diastolic volume)** — **Frank–Starling law**: within limits, the more the ventricular muscle is stretched by filling, the more forcefully it contracts. So the heart automatically pumps out what it receives. Venous return rises with muscle pump, respiratory pump, increased blood volume and venoconstriction; it falls with haemorrhage, dehydration and standing still.
2. **Contractility** — increased by sympathetic stimulation, adrenaline, thyroxine, calcium; decreased by hypoxia, acidosis, heart muscle damage, some drugs.
3. **Afterload** — resistance the ventricle must pump against (aortic pressure, peripheral resistance). High blood pressure and aortic stenosis raise afterload and reduce stroke volume.
4. **Heart rate** — output rises with rate up to a point; at very high rates the filling time is too short and output falls.

## 4.5 Blood pressure

**Arterial blood pressure** is the lateral pressure exerted by blood on the walls of arteries.

| Term | Meaning | Typical adult value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Systolic pressure | Highest pressure, during ventricular systole | about 120 mmHg (range roughly 100–130) |
| Diastolic pressure | Lowest pressure, during diastole | about 80 mmHg (roughly 60–85) |
| Pulse pressure | Systolic − diastolic | about 40 mmHg |
| Mean arterial pressure (MAP) | Diastolic + 1/3 pulse pressure | about 93 mmHg |

**Hypertension** in adults is usually diagnosed when repeated readings are **140/90 mmHg or above**; **hypotension** usually means systolic below about 90 mmHg or a reading too low to keep organs perfused. Follow the current guideline used in your hospital for exact grades.

**Basic relation**: Blood pressure = Cardiac output × Total peripheral resistance.

### Factors influencing blood pressure

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiac output (heart rate, stroke volume) | ↑ CO → ↑ mainly systolic pressure |
| Peripheral resistance (diameter of arterioles) | Vasoconstriction → ↑ mainly diastolic pressure |
| Blood volume | Haemorrhage, dehydration → ↓ BP; fluid overload → ↑ BP |
| Viscosity of blood | Polycythaemia → ↑; anaemia → ↓ |
| Elasticity of large arteries | Stiff arteries in old age → ↑ systolic, wide pulse pressure |
| Age | BP rises with age |
| Sex | Slightly lower in women before menopause |
| Posture | Lying → lower; standing → brief fall, corrected by reflex |
| Exercise, emotion, pain, anxiety | ↑ |
| Sleep | ↓ |
| Meals | Slight ↑ |
| Body build | Higher in obese people |

### Regulation of blood pressure

**Short-term (seconds) — nervous**

- **Baroreceptor reflex**: stretch receptors in the **carotid sinus** (nerve: glossopharyngeal) and **aortic arch** (nerve: vagus) sense pressure. A **rise** in BP → more impulses to the medulla → vagal activity ↑, sympathetic activity ↓ → heart rate and force fall, arterioles and veins dilate → BP falls back. A **fall** in BP does the opposite.
- **Chemoreceptor reflex**: low O2, high CO2 or acidosis stimulate carotid and aortic bodies → vasoconstriction → BP rises (important in severe hypotension).
- **CNS ischaemic response**: when the vasomotor centre itself is starved of blood, intense sympathetic discharge raises BP (e.g. raised intracranial pressure — **Cushing's reflex**: rising BP with a slow pulse).

**Intermediate and long-term (minutes to days) — hormonal and renal**

- **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system**: low renal blood flow → kidney releases **renin** → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → (ACE in lungs) → **angiotensin II**, a strong vasoconstrictor that also releases **aldosterone** (salt and water retention) and ADH and stimulates thirst.
- **ADH (vasopressin)**: water retention and, at high levels, vasoconstriction.
- **ANP** from stretched atria: sodium and water excretion, vasodilation → lowers BP.
- **Adrenaline and noradrenaline** from the adrenal medulla.
- **Kidney (pressure natriuresis)**: a raised pressure increases salt and water loss in urine, lowering blood volume — the main long-term controller.
- **Capillary fluid shift**: low pressure draws tissue fluid into capillaries.

## 4.6 Pulse

**Pulse** is the wave of expansion felt in an artery as blood is ejected into the aorta with each ventricular systole. It travels faster than the blood itself.

**Sites**: temporal, carotid, apical (5th left intercostal space, midclavicular line — counted with a stethoscope), brachial, radial (most common), femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis.

**What is assessed**: rate, rhythm, volume (strength), character, condition of the vessel wall, and equality on both sides.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Normal adult rate | about **60–100/min** at rest |
| Tachycardia | above 100/min |
| Bradycardia | below 60/min (normal in trained athletes and in sleep) |
| Pulse deficit | Apical rate minus radial rate; seen in atrial fibrillation (some beats too weak to reach the wrist) |
| Thready pulse | Weak, rapid — shock, haemorrhage |
| Bounding pulse | Strong — fever, anaemia, aortic regurgitation |
| Pulsus alternans | Strong and weak beats alternate — left ventricular failure |

Normal resting heart rate by age (approximate): newborn 120–160/min, infant 100–160, child 80–120, adult 60–100.

## 4.7 Principles of circulation

- Blood flows from **high to low pressure**; flow = pressure difference ÷ resistance.
- **Resistance** depends chiefly on the **radius** of the vessel (halving the radius raises resistance about sixteen times — Poiseuille's law), on vessel length and on blood viscosity. So small changes in arteriolar diameter control blood flow and pressure.

| Vessel | Functional name | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aorta and large arteries | Elastic (pressure reservoir) vessels | Stretch in systole, recoil in diastole → keep flow continuous (windkessel effect) |
| Arterioles | **Resistance vessels** | Main site of peripheral resistance; control BP and flow to each organ |
| Capillaries | **Exchange vessels** | Very thin; slowest flow (allows exchange); total cross-section largest |
| Venules and veins | **Capacitance vessels** | Hold about 60–65% of blood volume; can be tightened to return blood to the heart |

**Venous return** is helped by: the pressure gradient left from the heart, **skeletal muscle pump** with **venous valves**, **respiratory pump** (falling chest pressure on inspiration), venoconstriction, and gravity above heart level.

**Capillary exchange (Starling forces)** — at the arterial end hydrostatic pressure (about 30–35 mmHg) exceeds plasma oncotic pressure (about 25 mmHg) and fluid moves out; at the venous end hydrostatic pressure falls (about 15 mmHg) and fluid moves back in. The small excess left in the tissues returns via **lymphatics**.

## 4.8 Coronary, pulmonary and systemic circulation

### Coronary circulation

- The **right and left coronary arteries** arise from the aortic sinuses just above the aortic valve. The left divides into the anterior descending and circumflex branches.
- Venous blood drains mainly through the **coronary sinus** into the right atrium.
- Resting coronary flow is about **225–250 mL/min**, about **4–5% of cardiac output**, though the heart is only about 0.5% of body weight.
- The heart extracts about **70% of the O2** from coronary blood even at rest, so more O2 can come only from **more flow**.
- Left ventricular flow occurs mainly in **diastole**, because the contracting muscle squeezes the vessels in systole. Tachycardia shortens diastole and reduces flow.
- Flow is controlled mainly by **local metabolites** (adenosine, low O2, CO2, H+) that dilate coronary vessels when the heart works harder.
- Narrowing of coronary arteries causes **angina** (pain with exertion); complete blockage causes **myocardial infarction**.

### Pulmonary vs systemic circulation

| Feature | Pulmonary | Systemic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pump | Right ventricle | Left ventricle |
| Path | RV → pulmonary trunk → lungs → pulmonary veins → LA | LV → aorta → body tissues → venae cavae → RA |
| Pressure | Low (about 25/8 mmHg) | High (about 120/80 mmHg) |
| Resistance | Low | High (about 7 times more) |
| Wall of right vs left ventricle | Thin | Thick |
| Arteries carry | Deoxygenated blood | Oxygenated blood |
| Response to hypoxia | Vasoconstriction | Vasodilation |
| Purpose | Gas exchange | Supply of O2 and nutrients to tissues |

## 4.9 Heart rate — regulation, normal values and variations

**Nervous control** from the **cardiovascular centre in the medulla**:

- **Parasympathetic (vagus nerves)** to the SA and AV nodes → **slows** the heart (acetylcholine). At rest the vagus is always slightly active — **vagal tone** — which is why the resting rate (about 72) is lower than the SA node's own rate (about 90–100).
- **Sympathetic (cardiac nerves from T1–T4)** → **speeds** the heart and increases force (noradrenaline on β1 receptors).

**Reflexes**

- **Baroreceptor reflex** — a rise in BP slows the heart (Marey's law: heart rate varies inversely with blood pressure).
- **Bainbridge reflex** — stretch of the right atrium by increased venous return increases heart rate.
- **Chemoreceptor reflex** — hypoxia and hypercapnia increase heart rate (indirectly through breathing and sympathetic activity).
- **Respiratory sinus arrhythmia** — heart rate rises in inspiration and falls in expiration; normal, especially in children.
- Pressure on the eyeball or carotid sinus can slow the heart (vagal effect).

**Other factors**: hormones (adrenaline, thyroxine ↑), body temperature (rate rises about 10 beats per °C of fever), age (faster in infants), sex (slightly faster in women), exercise, emotion, pain, sleep (↓), athletic training (↓), blood loss (↑), electrolytes (high potassium slows and can stop the heart; calcium affects contraction).

## 4.10 Cardiovascular homeostasis in exercise and posture

### Exercise

- **Heart rate and stroke volume rise** (sympathetic activity, adrenaline, more venous return from the muscle pump), so cardiac output can reach about 20–25 L/min in a fit adult.
- **Redistribution of blood**: arterioles in active muscles dilate (local metabolites — CO2, H+, K+, adenosine, heat), while vessels of the gut, kidneys and resting muscle constrict. Skin flow rises later to lose heat.
- **Systolic pressure rises**; diastolic changes little or falls slightly (muscle vasodilation lowers resistance).
- Venous return increases (muscle and respiratory pumps, venoconstriction).
- After exercise, heart rate returns towards normal; how fast it recovers is a sign of fitness.

### Posture

- On standing up from lying, about 500–700 mL of blood pools in the veins of the legs → venous return, stroke volume and BP fall briefly.
- **Baroreceptors** detect the fall → sympathetic activity ↑ → heart rate rises and arterioles and veins constrict → BP is restored within seconds.
- If the reflex is weak (elderly, prolonged bed rest, dehydration, some drugs, autonomic disease), the person becomes dizzy or faints — **orthostatic (postural) hypotension**, usually defined as a fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing.
- Standing still for long (soldiers on parade) lets blood pool and can cause fainting; contracting leg muscles helps.

# Part B — Blood

## 4.11 Functions and physical characteristics of blood

**Functions**

1. **Transport** — O2, CO2, nutrients, waste products (urea, creatinine), hormones, heat.
2. **Regulation** — body temperature, pH (buffers), water and electrolyte balance, osmotic pressure (plasma proteins).
3. **Protection** — white cells and antibodies fight infection; clotting prevents blood loss.

**Physical characteristics**

| Feature | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Volume in adults | about 5–6 litres (about 7–8% of body weight; about 70–80 mL/kg) |
| Colour | Bright red in arteries, dark red in veins |
| pH | 7.35–7.45 (slightly alkaline) |
| Specific gravity | whole blood about 1.050–1.060 |
| Viscosity | about 3–5 times that of water |
| Temperature | about 38 °C (slightly above body surface temperature) |
| Composition | Plasma about 55%; formed elements about 45% (haematocrit / PCV) |

**Plasma** is about 91–92% water; the rest is proteins, nutrients, electrolytes, gases, hormones and wastes. **Plasma proteins** (total about 6.4–8.3 g/dL):

| Protein | Amount | Main role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Albumin | about 3.5–5 g/dL | Main source of plasma **oncotic pressure** (keeps fluid in vessels); carries drugs, bilirubin, hormones |
| Globulins | about 2–3.5 g/dL | Antibodies (gamma globulins), carriers of iron, copper, hormones |
| Fibrinogen | about 0.2–0.4 g/dL | Clotting (becomes fibrin) |

**Serum** = plasma without fibrinogen and the other used-up clotting factors (the fluid left after blood clots).

## 4.12 Formation of blood cells (haemopoiesis)

- **Sites**: yolk sac in early embryo → liver and spleen in mid-fetal life → **red bone marrow** from late fetal life onwards. In adults, active marrow is mainly in **flat bones** (sternum, ribs, skull, pelvis), **vertebrae** and the upper ends of the femur and humerus. That is why bone marrow is sampled from the iliac crest or sternum.
- All blood cells come from a **pluripotent haemopoietic stem cell**, which gives a **myeloid** line (red cells, granulocytes, monocytes, platelets) and a **lymphoid** line (lymphocytes).
- Growth factors (cytokines) control each line: **erythropoietin** (red cells), **thrombopoietin** (platelets), colony-stimulating factors and interleukins (white cells).

## 4.13 Erythropoiesis and red blood cells

### Stages

```text
Stem cell → proerythroblast → early normoblast → intermediate normoblast
         → late normoblast (nucleus thrown out) → reticulocyte → mature RBC
         (about 7 days in the marrow; reticulocytes mature in 1–2 days in blood)
```

During maturation the cell becomes smaller, fills with haemoglobin and loses its nucleus.

### Factors needed for erythropoiesis

| Factor | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Erythropoietin** | Hormone made mainly by the **kidneys** when tissues are hypoxic (anaemia, altitude, lung disease); stimulates red cell production. Low in chronic kidney disease → anaemia |
| **Iron** | Part of haem; lack → small pale cells (microcytic hypochromic anaemia) |
| **Vitamin B12 and folic acid** | Needed for DNA synthesis and cell division; lack → large immature cells (megaloblastic anaemia) |
| **Intrinsic factor** | Needed for B12 absorption (pernicious anaemia when absent) |
| **Proteins** | For globin |
| Vitamin C | Helps iron absorption |
| Copper, cobalt, vitamin B6 | For haemoglobin synthesis |
| Thyroxine, androgens, growth hormone | Stimulate production |

### Red blood cells

- Biconcave, non-nucleated discs, about 7.5 µm across; the shape gives a large surface for gas exchange and lets them bend through narrow capillaries.
- **Count**: about 4.5–5.5 million/µL (men), 4.0–5.0 million/µL (women).
- **Haemoglobin**: about 13–17 g/dL (men), 12–15 g/dL (women), higher in newborns; laboratory ranges vary.
- **Functions**: transport of O2 (oxyhaemoglobin) and CO2; buffering (haemoglobin is a good buffer); carbonic anhydrase for CO2 carriage; carry blood group antigens.

### Life cycle and destruction

- Life span about **120 days**. Old cells become fragile and are trapped and destroyed by macrophages mainly in the **spleen** (the "graveyard of red cells"), liver and marrow.
- Haemoglobin → **globin** (broken into amino acids, reused) + **haem**. Haem → **iron** (stored as ferritin, reused) + **biliverdin** → **bilirubin** (unconjugated, carried by albumin to the liver) → conjugated in the liver → excreted in bile → changed by gut bacteria to urobilinogen → stercobilin (faeces) and urobilin (urine).
- Too much breakdown (haemolysis) or failure to excrete bilirubin gives **jaundice**.

## 4.14 White blood cells — types and functions

**Total count**: about **4,000–11,000/µL**. Above 11,000 = **leucocytosis** (infection, inflammation, leukaemia); below 4,000 = **leucopenia** (some viral infections, marrow suppression).

| Type | Share (differential count) | Main function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Neutrophils** | 50–70% | First defenders against bacteria; phagocytosis; increase in acute bacterial infection; form pus |
| **Eosinophils** | 1–4% | Fight parasites (worms); control allergic reactions; increase in allergy and worm infestation |
| **Basophils** | 0–1% | Release histamine and heparin; allergic reactions |
| **Lymphocytes** | 20–40% | Specific immunity: B cells (antibodies) and T cells (cell-mediated immunity); increase in viral infections and tuberculosis |
| **Monocytes** | 2–8% | Become **macrophages** in tissues; phagocytosis; present antigens to lymphocytes; increase in chronic infections |

Neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils are **granulocytes** (granules in cytoplasm); lymphocytes and monocytes are **agranulocytes**. Neutrophils move out of vessels (diapedesis), travel towards chemicals released by microbes (chemotaxis) and swallow them (phagocytosis).

## 4.15 Platelets

- Small, non-nucleated fragments of large marrow cells called **megakaryocytes**; production is stimulated by **thrombopoietin** (mainly from the liver).
- **Count**: about **1.5–4 lakh/µL** (150,000–400,000/µL). Life span about 7–10 days; old platelets are removed by the spleen.
- A count below about 1 lakh/µL is **thrombocytopenia** (seen for example in dengue); very low counts cause spontaneous bleeding into skin (petechiae, purpura) and mucosa.

**Functions**: form the **platelet plug**; release substances that cause vessel spasm (serotonin, thromboxane A2) and help clotting (platelet factor 3 — phospholipid surface); **clot retraction** (contractile proteins pull the clot tight); repair of vessel walls (platelet-derived growth factor); phagocytosis of small particles.

## 4.16 Haemostasis and the clotting mechanism

**Haemostasis** means stopping bleeding from an injured vessel. It has three main steps, followed by clot retraction and dissolution.

### Step 1 — Vascular constriction

The injured vessel contracts at once (local muscle response, nerve reflex, and serotonin and thromboxane A2 from platelets). This reduces blood loss for a short time.

### Step 2 — Platelet plug formation

1. **Adhesion** — platelets stick to exposed collagen under the damaged endothelium, with the help of **von Willebrand factor**.
2. **Activation** — platelets swell, put out projections and release ADP, thromboxane A2 and serotonin.
3. **Aggregation** — ADP and thromboxane A2 attract more platelets, which stick together and form a loose **platelet plug**. This alone can seal small vessel injuries (it is a positive-feedback process).

Aspirin reduces thromboxane A2 formation, which is why it reduces platelet plug formation and increases bleeding time.

### Step 3 — Coagulation (blood clotting)

Clotting factors are mostly plasma proteins made in the liver, numbered I–XIII (there is no factor VI).

| Factor | Name |
| --- | --- |
| I | Fibrinogen |
| II | Prothrombin |
| III | Tissue factor (thromboplastin) |
| IV | Calcium ions |
| V | Labile factor (proaccelerin) |
| VII | Stable factor (proconvertin) |
| VIII | Antihaemophilic factor |
| IX | Christmas factor |
| X | Stuart–Prower factor |
| XI | Plasma thromboplastin antecedent |
| XII | Hageman factor |
| XIII | Fibrin-stabilising factor |

```text
INTRINSIC PATHWAY (all factors in blood;        EXTRINSIC PATHWAY (tissue injury;
 started by contact with collagen/glass)          fast, seconds)
 XII → XIIa                                       Tissue factor (III) + VII
 XIa                                                      │
 IXa (+ VIIIa, Ca2+, platelet phospholipid)               │
            └──────────────┬──────────────────────────────┘
                           ▼
                COMMON PATHWAY: X → Xa
         Xa + Va + Ca2+ + phospholipid = PROTHROMBIN ACTIVATOR
                           ▼
              Prothrombin (II) ──► THROMBIN (IIa)
                           ▼
              Fibrinogen (I) ──► FIBRIN threads (loose)
                           ▼   (XIIIa + Ca2+)
              Stable cross-linked FIBRIN MESH traps red cells = CLOT
```

Thrombin also activates factors V, VIII, XI and XIII and platelets — another positive feedback that speeds clotting.

- **Vitamin K** is needed by the liver to make factors **II, VII, IX and X** (and proteins C and S). Its lack (newborns, obstructive jaundice, long antibiotic use) causes bleeding. Warfarin-type drugs work by blocking vitamin K action.
- **Calcium** is needed at almost every stage. Citrate, oxalate and EDTA prevent clotting of blood samples outside the body by removing calcium; heparin works by helping antithrombin.
- **Haemophilia A** = lack of factor VIII; **haemophilia B** = lack of factor IX (X-linked, mainly in males).

### Clot retraction and fibrinolysis

- Within about 30–60 minutes the clot shrinks and squeezes out serum (**clot retraction**, by platelet contractile proteins), pulling the wound edges together.
- When the vessel has healed, the clot is dissolved by **plasmin**, formed from plasminogen by **tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)** — **fibrinolysis**.
- Clotting inside intact vessels is prevented by a smooth endothelium, the glycocalyx, thrombomodulin, antithrombin, protein C and the continuous flow of blood. A clot formed inside a vessel is a **thrombus**; a piece that travels is an **embolus**.

### Tests of haemostasis

| Test | What it tests | Typical normal (varies with method and lab) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bleeding time (BT)** | Platelet function and vascular response | about 1–5 minutes (Duke method); prolonged in low platelets, aspirin use |
| **Clotting time (CT)** | Overall clotting (mainly intrinsic) | about 4–10 minutes (capillary or tube methods) |
| **Prothrombin time (PT)** | Extrinsic and common pathway (factors VII, X, V, II, I) | about 11–16 seconds; reported with **INR**; used to monitor warfarin-type therapy and liver function |
| **Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT/PTT)** | Intrinsic and common pathway | about 25–40 seconds; used to monitor heparin; prolonged in haemophilia |
| Platelet count | Number of platelets | 1.5–4 lakh/µL |

Always compare results with the laboratory's own control and reference range.

## 4.17 Blood groups

### ABO system (Landsteiner)

Blood groups depend on **antigens (agglutinogens)** on red cells; plasma contains naturally occurring **antibodies (agglutinins)** against the antigens a person does *not* have.

| Group | Antigen on RBC | Antibody in plasma | Can receive red cells from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O |
| AB | A and B | None | A, B, AB, O (universal recipient) |
| O | None | Anti-A and anti-B | O only (universal donor of red cells) |

In India group **B and O** are the most common and AB the least, though the pattern varies by region.

### Rh system

- **Rh positive** = red cells carry the **D antigen** (most Indians); **Rh negative** = no D antigen.
- Anti-D antibodies are **not** naturally present; they form only after an Rh-negative person is exposed to Rh-positive cells (transfusion or pregnancy).
- **Haemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis)**: an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive baby can be sensitised at delivery; in a later Rh-positive pregnancy her anti-D (IgG) crosses the placenta and destroys the baby's red cells → anaemia, jaundice, hydrops. It is prevented by giving **anti-D immunoglobulin** to the mother as per obstetric protocol.

### Transfusion

- Blood is always **grouped and cross-matched** (donor cells mixed with recipient serum) before transfusion.
- A mismatched transfusion causes **agglutination and haemolysis** → fever, chills, back pain, chest tightness, low BP, dark urine, kidney failure; it can be fatal.
- **Uses of blood grouping**: transfusion, preventing haemolytic disease of the newborn, organ transplants, and (as supporting evidence) in disputed parentage and forensic work.

## 4.18 Reticuloendothelial system and immunity

### Reticuloendothelial (mononuclear phagocyte) system

A widespread system of phagocytic cells derived from monocytes:

| Location | Cell |
| --- | --- |
| Liver | Kupffer cells |
| Lungs | Alveolar macrophages (dust cells) |
| Spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow | Fixed macrophages lining sinuses |
| Brain | Microglia |
| Connective tissue | Histiocytes |
| Bone | Osteoclasts (same origin) |
| Skin | Langerhans cells (antigen-presenting) |

**Functions**: phagocytosis of bacteria, debris and old red cells; formation of **bilirubin** from haemoglobin; storage of iron; **antigen presentation** to lymphocytes; production of cytokines that start immune responses.

### Immunity

**Immunity** is the body's ability to resist infection and harmful substances.

| Innate (non-specific) immunity | Acquired (specific) immunity |
| --- | --- |
| Present from birth; same response every time; fast | Develops after exposure to an antigen; specific; has **memory** — faster, stronger second response |
| Skin and mucous membranes, acid in stomach, lysozyme, cilia, phagocytes, natural killer cells, complement, inflammation, fever, interferons | Lymphocytes and antibodies |

**Acquired immunity** has two arms:

- **Humoral (antibody-mediated) immunity** — **B lymphocytes** become **plasma cells** that secrete **antibodies (immunoglobulins)**. Classes: **IgG** (most abundant, crosses the placenta), **IgA** (in secretions: saliva, tears, breast milk), **IgM** (first produced, largest), **IgE** (allergy, worms), **IgD** (on B cells). Antibodies neutralise toxins, clump microbes, activate complement and coat microbes for phagocytosis.
- **Cell-mediated immunity** — **T lymphocytes** (matured in the **thymus**): helper T cells (CD4) direct the response (destroyed in HIV infection); cytotoxic T cells (CD8) kill virus-infected and cancer cells; regulatory T cells control the response. Important against viruses, intracellular bacteria such as tuberculosis, fungi, transplants and tumours.

| Type of acquired immunity | Natural | Artificial |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Active** (own body makes antibodies; slow onset; long-lasting) | Recovering from an infection | Vaccination |
| **Passive** (ready-made antibodies; immediate; short-lived) | Maternal IgG across the placenta; IgA in breast milk | Injection of immunoglobulin or antiserum (e.g. anti-rabies immunoglobulin, anti-tetanus immunoglobulin) |

## 4.19 Application and implication in nursing

- **Vital signs** — count the radial pulse for a full minute when it is irregular; check apical and radial together (two nurses) for pulse deficit; measure BP with the correct cuff size, arm supported at heart level, after about 5 minutes' rest, and note the arm and position used.
- **Postural hypotension** — make patients sit, then dangle legs before standing, especially the elderly, after bed rest or surgery; record lying and standing BP when ordered.
- **Venous return** — leg exercises, early ambulation and graduated compression stockings (as prescribed) prevent venous stasis and deep vein thrombosis.
- **Chest pain** — any chest pain is reported at once; rest reduces cardiac O2 demand; monitor rate, rhythm and BP.
- **Blood transfusion** — confirm identity, group, cross-match and the bag details at the bedside with a second nurse; observe the patient closely, especially in the first 15 minutes; stop the transfusion and inform the doctor at the first sign of reaction.
- **Bleeding risk** (low platelets, anticoagulants, liver disease) — use soft toothbrush, avoid IM injections where possible, apply longer pressure after venepuncture, watch for petechiae, gum bleeding, black stools and blood in urine.
- **Anaemia** — fatigue, breathlessness and pallor; encourage iron- and folate-rich foods; ask about menstrual loss and worm infestation.
- **Infection control** — neutropenic patients need strict hand hygiene and protective isolation; know the meaning of a raised neutrophil count (bacterial) versus lymphocyte count (viral).
- **Immunisation** — the basis of vaccination schedules is active artificial immunity; maternal antibodies protect infants for a few months; breastfeeding gives passive protection through IgA.
- **Rh-negative mothers** — ensure group and Rh typing in antenatal care so anti-D prophylaxis can be given as per protocol.

## Remember

- SA node is the pacemaker (70–80/min); AV node delays 0.1 s; the heart cannot be tetanised (long refractory period).
- Cardiac cycle 0.8 s at 75/min: ventricular systole 0.3 s, diastole 0.5 s; S1 = AV valve closure, S2 = semilunar closure.
- SV ≈ 70 mL; CO = SV × HR ≈ 5 L/min; ejection fraction 55–70%; Frank–Starling law.
- BP = CO × peripheral resistance; normal about 120/80 mmHg; pulse pressure 40; MAP ≈ diastolic + 1/3 pulse pressure.
- Baroreceptors (carotid sinus, aortic arch) give short-term control; renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and kidneys give long-term control.
- Coronary flow ≈ 4–5% of CO, mainly in diastole; O2 extraction ≈ 70%.
- Blood volume ≈ 5–6 L; pH 7.35–7.45; plasma 55%; albumin gives oncotic pressure.
- RBC life 120 days; erythropoietin from the kidney; iron, B12, folic acid needed.
- WBC 4,000–11,000/µL: neutrophils 50–70%, lymphocytes 20–40%; platelets 1.5–4 lakh/µL.
- Haemostasis: vascular spasm → platelet plug → coagulation (intrinsic/extrinsic → Xa → thrombin → fibrin); vitamin K factors II, VII, IX, X.
- ABO: O = universal donor, AB = universal recipient; Rh-negative mother + Rh-positive baby → haemolytic disease of the newborn.
- IgG crosses the placenta; IgA is in milk; IgM appears first; T cells mature in the thymus.
