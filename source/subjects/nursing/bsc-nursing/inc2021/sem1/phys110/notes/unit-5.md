# Unit 5: The endocrine system and the sensory organs

This file joins INC Unit VI, The endocrine system (5 hours), and Unit VII, The sensory organs (4 hours). Both are about how the body senses and responds: hormones act slowly and widely through the blood, while sense organs feed the nervous system with information about the outside world.

# Part A — The endocrine system

## 5.1 Hormones — basic ideas

A **hormone** is a chemical messenger secreted by an endocrine (ductless) gland into the blood, which acts on specific **target cells** that carry receptors for it.

| Chemical type | Examples | How they act |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proteins and peptides | Insulin, glucagon, GH, ADH, oxytocin, PTH | Bind receptors on the cell membrane → **second messengers** (for example cyclic AMP) inside the cell |
| Amines (from amino acids) | Adrenaline, noradrenaline (membrane receptors); thyroxine (enters the cell) | — |
| Steroids (from cholesterol) | Cortisol, aldosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone | Lipid-soluble; enter the cell, bind receptors inside, change gene activity (slow but lasting effects) |

**Control of secretion**

- **Negative feedback** — the commonest: e.g. thyroxine in blood inhibits TSH and TRH release.
- **Nervous control** — sympathetic nerves to the adrenal medulla; hypothalamus to the posterior pituitary.
- **Level of a substance in blood** — glucose controls insulin; calcium controls PTH and calcitonin.
- **Hypothalamus–pituitary axis** — releasing and inhibiting hormones from the hypothalamus reach the anterior pituitary through the **hypophyseal portal veins**.

```text
Hypothalamus ── releasing hormone (TRH) ──► Anterior pituitary ── TSH ──► Thyroid ── T3, T4 ──► tissues
     ▲                                             ▲                                      │
     └────────────── negative feedback ────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## 5.2 Pineal gland

- Small gland on the roof of the third ventricle.
- Secretes **melatonin**, mainly in **darkness** (light falling on the retina inhibits it through a pathway via the hypothalamus).
- **Functions**: sets the **circadian (day–night) rhythm** and sleep–wake cycle; may influence the timing of puberty; antioxidant actions.
- Disturbed by night shifts and jet lag — relevant to nurses' own health and to patients in brightly lit wards at night.

## 5.3 Pituitary gland (hypophysis)

Called the "master gland" because it controls several other glands, though it is itself controlled by the hypothalamus.

### Anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis)

| Hormone | Target | Main actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Growth hormone (GH, somatotropin)** | Most tissues; liver (makes IGF-1) | Growth of bone and muscle; protein synthesis; fat breakdown; raises blood glucose (anti-insulin) |
| **Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)** | Thyroid | Growth of the thyroid and release of T3, T4 |
| **Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH)** | Adrenal cortex | Secretion of cortisol |
| **Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)** | Ovary / testis | Growth of follicles and oestrogen secretion; spermatogenesis |
| **Luteinising hormone (LH)** | Ovary / testis | Ovulation and corpus luteum (progesterone); testosterone from Leydig cells |
| **Prolactin** | Breast | Milk production after delivery |

**Disorders of GH**: excess before epiphyses close → **gigantism**; after → **acromegaly** (large hands, feet, jaw, coarse face); deficiency in childhood → **pituitary dwarfism** (short but well proportioned, normal intelligence).

### Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis)

These two hormones are **made in the hypothalamus** (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei), carried down nerve fibres and stored here.

| Hormone | Actions |
| --- | --- |
| **ADH (antidiuretic hormone, vasopressin)** | Increases water reabsorption in kidney collecting ducts (inserts aquaporins) → concentrated urine; constricts arterioles at high levels. Released when plasma osmolality rises or blood volume falls |
| **Oxytocin** | Contraction of the pregnant uterus in labour (positive feedback); **milk ejection** (let-down) during suckling |

- Lack of ADH → **diabetes insipidus**: very large volumes of dilute urine and great thirst.
- Excess ADH (SIADH) → water retention and low plasma sodium.

## 5.4 Thyroid gland

Secretes **thyroxine (T4)** and **tri-iodothyronine (T3)** from the follicles, and **calcitonin** from the parafollicular (C) cells.

**Synthesis of thyroid hormones (outline)**: iodide is **trapped** from the blood → **oxidised** to iodine → attached to tyrosine on **thyroglobulin** (forming MIT and DIT) → **coupled** to form T3 and T4 → **stored** in the colloid → taken back into the cell and released into blood under TSH. T4 is the main product; T3 is more active, and much T4 is converted to T3 in tissues. Iodine comes from the diet (iodised salt, seafood).

**Actions of T3/T4**

1. Raise **basal metabolic rate** and O2 consumption; heat production.
2. Essential for **growth and development**, especially of the **brain** and skeleton in fetal life and infancy.
3. Increase carbohydrate absorption and use; increase fat breakdown, lower cholesterol.
4. Increase heart rate and cardiac output (more β receptors).
5. Increase alertness and reflexes; affect mood.
6. Needed for normal menstrual cycles and fertility.

**Calcitonin** lowers blood calcium by inhibiting osteoclasts (bone resorption) — its role in adults is minor.

| Condition | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| **Hypothyroidism in infants — congenital hypothyroidism (cretinism)** | Mental retardation, short stature, coarse features, large tongue, constipation, prolonged jaundice — preventable if detected early (newborn screening) |
| **Hypothyroidism in adults (myxoedema)** | Tiredness, weight gain, cold intolerance, dry skin, hair loss, slow pulse, constipation, puffy face, slow speech and thought, heavy periods |
| **Hyperthyroidism (Graves' disease)** | Weight loss despite good appetite, heat intolerance, sweating, tremor, rapid pulse, anxiety, protruding eyes (exophthalmos), goitre |
| **Goitre** | Enlarged thyroid; common cause in India is **iodine deficiency** |

India runs a national programme for iodine deficiency disorders based on universal use of iodised salt (the National Iodine Deficiency Disorders Control Programme); check the current details on the National Health Mission website.

## 5.5 Parathyroid glands

Four small glands on the back of the thyroid; they secrete **parathyroid hormone (PTH)** when blood calcium falls.

**Actions of PTH** (all raise blood calcium):

1. **Bone** — stimulates osteoclast activity → releases calcium and phosphate.
2. **Kidney** — increases calcium reabsorption and **increases phosphate excretion**.
3. **Kidney** — activates vitamin D (calcitriol), which increases **calcium absorption from the gut**.

Normal total serum calcium is about 9–10.5 mg/dL (about 2.2–2.6 mmol/L).

- **Hypoparathyroidism** (for example after accidental removal during thyroid surgery) → low calcium → increased nerve excitability → **tetany**: tingling around mouth and fingers, muscle cramps, carpopedal spasm, laryngeal spasm, convulsions. **Trousseau's sign** (carpal spasm when a BP cuff is inflated on the arm) and **Chvostek's sign** (twitching of facial muscles on tapping the facial nerve in front of the ear).
- **Hyperparathyroidism** → high calcium → weak bones, kidney stones, abdominal pain, depression ("bones, stones, groans and moans").

## 5.6 Thymus

- Lies in the upper mediastinum; large in children, shrinks after puberty (replaced by fat).
- Hormones: **thymosin**, thymopoietin, thymulin.
- **Function**: maturation of **T lymphocytes** and development of cell-mediated immunity; it teaches T cells to ignore the body's own tissues.

## 5.7 Pancreas — the islets of Langerhans

| Cell | Hormone | Main action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alpha (α) | **Glucagon** | Raises blood glucose |
| Beta (β) — the majority | **Insulin** | Lowers blood glucose |
| Delta (δ) | Somatostatin | Inhibits insulin and glucagon release, slows digestion |
| PP (F) cells | Pancreatic polypeptide | Inhibits pancreatic exocrine secretion |

### Insulin

Released when blood glucose rises (after meals). It is the only hormone that **lowers blood glucose**.

- **Carbohydrate**: increases glucose uptake into muscle and fat cells (more GLUT4 carriers); increases glycogen formation in liver and muscle; reduces glucose release and gluconeogenesis by the liver.
- **Fat**: increases fat storage; prevents fat breakdown and ketone formation.
- **Protein**: increases amino acid uptake and protein synthesis (anabolic).
- Moves **potassium** into cells.

### Glucagon

Released when blood glucose falls (fasting, exercise). Raises blood glucose by **glycogenolysis** and **gluconeogenesis** in the liver; increases fat breakdown and ketone formation.

**Other hormones that raise blood glucose**: adrenaline, cortisol, growth hormone, thyroxine — so stress, illness and steroid therapy raise blood sugar.

### Blood glucose and disorders

- Normal fasting plasma glucose is below 100 mg/dL (about 70–100); diabetes is diagnosed by laboratory criteria such as fasting plasma glucose **126 mg/dL or above** on repeat testing (or other accepted tests).
- **Diabetes mellitus** — lack of insulin (type 1) or resistance to it (type 2) → high blood glucose → **polyuria** (glucose above the renal threshold drags water into urine), **polydipsia**, **polyphagia**, weight loss, tiredness, slow wound healing, infections; in severe insulin lack, fat breakdown gives **ketoacidosis** with deep sighing (Kussmaul) breathing and fruity breath.
- **Hypoglycaemia** — sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, confusion, abnormal behaviour, fits, unconsciousness; the brain depends on glucose, so it is an emergency.

## 5.8 Adrenal glands

Each gland has an outer **cortex** (essential for life) and inner **medulla**.

### Adrenal cortex

| Zone | Hormone group | Main hormone | Main actions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zona glomerulosa (outer) | **Mineralocorticoids** | Aldosterone | Na+ and water reabsorption and K+ (and H+) excretion in the kidney → maintains blood volume and BP. Controlled by angiotensin II and high plasma K+ |
| Zona fasciculata (middle) | **Glucocorticoids** | Cortisol | Raises blood glucose (gluconeogenesis); breaks down protein and mobilises fat; anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive; helps body cope with **stress**; needed for normal BP response to adrenaline. Controlled by ACTH; peaks in the early morning |
| Zona reticularis (inner) | **Sex hormones** | Androgens (DHEA) | Pubic and axillary hair; libido in women |

| Disorder | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Cushing's syndrome** (excess cortisol, often from steroid therapy) | Moon face, buffalo hump, central obesity, thin skin, purple striae, high blood sugar, high BP, weak bones, infections |
| **Addison's disease** (lack of cortical hormones) | Weakness, weight loss, low BP, low sodium, high potassium, low sugar, skin pigmentation |
| **Conn's syndrome** (excess aldosterone) | High BP, low potassium (muscle weakness) |

### Adrenal medulla

- Secretes **adrenaline (epinephrine)** (about 80%) and **noradrenaline** under **sympathetic** nerve stimulation — part of the **"fight or flight"** response.
- **Actions**: faster, stronger heartbeat; raised BP; dilated bronchioles; dilated pupils; blood diverted to muscles; raised blood glucose (glycogenolysis); increased metabolism and alertness; relaxed gut.
- A tumour (**phaeochromocytoma**) causes attacks of high BP, headache, sweating and palpitations.

## 5.9 Other hormones

| Source | Hormone | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypothalamus | Releasing and inhibiting hormones (TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH, somatostatin, dopamine) | Control anterior pituitary |
| Kidney | Erythropoietin; renin; calcitriol (active vitamin D) | Red cell production; BP control; calcium absorption |
| Heart (atria) | Atrial natriuretic peptide | Sodium and water loss; lowers BP |
| Stomach and intestine | Gastrin, secretin, CCK, GIP | Control of digestion |
| Adipose tissue | Leptin | Signals satiety (fullness) to the hypothalamus |
| Placenta | hCG, oestrogen, progesterone, human placental lactogen | Maintain pregnancy; hCG is the basis of pregnancy tests |
| Ovary | Oestrogen, progesterone, inhibin, relaxin | Reproduction (Unit 7) |
| Testis | Testosterone, inhibin | Reproduction (Unit 7) |
| Many tissues | Prostaglandins (local hormones) | Inflammation, pain, fever, uterine contraction, platelet aggregation |

## 5.10 Alterations in disease — summary

- **Too much hormone**: tumour of the gland, autoimmune stimulation (Graves'), overtreatment with hormones.
- **Too little hormone**: gland destroyed (autoimmune, infection, surgery, radiation), lack of raw material (iodine), lack of the controlling pituitary hormone.
- **Hormone present but not working**: receptor resistance (type 2 diabetes).

## 5.11 Application and implication in nursing (endocrine)

- **Diabetes** — capillary blood glucose monitoring, recognising hypo- and hyperglycaemia, foot care, diet and exercise teaching, insulin storage and injection technique and timing with meals as prescribed.
- **After thyroid surgery** — watch the airway (bleeding, swelling, laryngeal nerve injury causing hoarseness), and watch for tetany from low calcium; keep calcium gluconate available as per protocol.
- **Steroid therapy** — patients must not stop long-term steroids suddenly (the suppressed adrenal cannot respond to stress); watch for raised sugar, BP, infection and mood change.
- **Diabetes insipidus or head injury** — strict intake–output and specific gravity of urine.
- **Newborn screening** for congenital hypothyroidism and teaching mothers the value of iodised salt.
- **Stress response** — pain, fear and surgery raise cortisol and adrenaline, which raise blood sugar and BP; reassurance and pain relief matter physiologically.

# Part B — The sensory organs

## 5.12 Functions of the skin

1. **Protection** — barrier against microbes, chemicals, water loss and mechanical injury; melanin absorbs ultraviolet light; acid mantle (pH about 5.5) and sebum discourage bacteria.
2. **Regulation of body temperature** — see below.
3. **Sensation** — receptors for touch, pressure, vibration, heat, cold and pain.
4. **Synthesis of vitamin D** — ultraviolet light converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in the skin to vitamin D3.
5. **Excretion** — small amounts of water, salt, urea in sweat.
6. **Absorption** — of lipid-soluble substances (some drugs as patches and ointments).
7. **Storage** — fat in the subcutaneous layer (energy and insulation).
8. **Immune defence** — Langerhans cells present antigens.
9. **Communication** — facial expression, blushing, pallor.

### Temperature regulation

Normal oral temperature is about **36.5–37.5 °C** (axillary slightly lower, rectal slightly higher). The **hypothalamus** acts as the thermostat.

| When the body is too hot | When the body is too cold |
| --- | --- |
| Skin arterioles **dilate** → more heat lost by radiation, conduction and convection | Skin arterioles **constrict** → heat kept in the core |
| **Sweating** — evaporation of sweat removes heat | Shivering — muscle activity produces heat |
| Less heat production; lethargy | Piloerection ("goose flesh"); raised metabolism (thyroxine, adrenaline) |
| Behaviour: lighter clothes, shade | Behaviour: warm clothes, curling up |

**Fever** is a rise in the hypothalamic set point (by pyrogens such as interleukins and prostaglandins) — the person shivers and feels cold while the temperature rises, then sweats as it falls.

### Skin receptors

| Receptor | Sensation |
| --- | --- |
| Free nerve endings | Pain, temperature, itch |
| Meissner's corpuscles | Light (fine) touch — fingertips, lips |
| Merkel's discs | Sustained touch and pressure |
| Pacinian corpuscles | Deep pressure and vibration |
| Ruffini endings | Stretch of skin, sustained pressure |
| Hair follicle receptors | Movement of hairs |

## 5.13 Vision

### Path of light and formation of the image

Light passes through the **cornea** (most refraction) → aqueous humour → **pupil** → **lens** (fine focusing) → vitreous humour → **retina**. An inverted, smaller image forms on the retina; the brain interprets it correctly.

### Accommodation for near vision

When looking at a near object:

1. **Ciliary muscle contracts** → suspensory ligaments slacken → lens becomes **more convex** (more refraction).
2. **Pupil constricts** → reduces blurring and spherical aberration.
3. **Eyes converge** → image falls on corresponding points of both retinas.

These are controlled by the oculomotor nerve (parasympathetic for ciliary muscle and pupil). The **near point** moves farther away with age (presbyopia).

### Pupillary reflexes

- **Light reflex** — light shone into one eye constricts that pupil (**direct**) and the other pupil (**consensual**). Pathway: optic nerve → midbrain → oculomotor nerve (parasympathetic). Checked in every neurological observation.
- **Near (accommodation) reflex** — pupils constrict on looking at a near object.
- Sympathetic stimulation (fear, darkness) **dilates** pupils.

### Photoreceptors

| Rods | Cones |
| --- | --- |
| About 120 million; more at the periphery | About 6 million; concentrated in the **fovea centralis** (macula) |
| Work in **dim light** (night vision); black and white | Work in **bright light**; **colour** vision (three types: red, green, blue) and sharp detail |
| Pigment **rhodopsin** (visual purple), made from **vitamin A** — deficiency → **night blindness** | Pigments: iodopsins |

The **optic disc** (where the optic nerve leaves) has no receptors — the **blind spot**. **Dark adaptation** (rhodopsin rebuilds) takes about 20–30 minutes. **Colour blindness** (commonly red–green, X-linked) is more frequent in males.

### Visual pathway

```text
Retina → optic nerve → OPTIC CHIASMA (fibres from the NASAL half of each retina cross)
      → optic tract → lateral geniculate body (thalamus) → optic radiation
      → visual cortex in the OCCIPITAL lobe (area 17)
```

So each occipital lobe sees the opposite half of the visual field. A pituitary tumour pressing on the chiasma cuts the crossing fibres → loss of both outer (temporal) fields — **bitemporal hemianopia**.

### Errors of refraction

| Error | Problem | Image falls | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Myopia** (short sight) | Eyeball too long or lens too strong; far objects blurred | In front of the retina | **Concave (diverging)** lens |
| **Hypermetropia** (long sight) | Eyeball too short or lens too weak; near objects blurred | Behind the retina | **Convex (converging)** lens |
| **Astigmatism** | Uneven curvature of cornea or lens | Blurred in some directions | **Cylindrical** lens |
| **Presbyopia** | Lens loses elasticity with age (usually after about 40); cannot accommodate for near work | Behind the retina for near objects | **Convex** lens for reading |

### Ageing changes in the eye

- **Presbyopia**; slower adaptation to dark and glare sensitivity; pupils smaller.
- Lens yellows and may become cloudy — **cataract** (common cause of blindness, treatable by surgery).
- Fewer tears — **dry eye**; drooping lids.
- Grey ring round the cornea (**arcus senilis**).
- Higher risk of **glaucoma** (raised intraocular pressure damaging the optic nerve) and **macular degeneration**.

## 5.14 Hearing

### Mechanism of hearing

```text
Sound waves → pinna → external auditory canal → TYMPANIC MEMBRANE vibrates
 → ossicles: malleus → incus → stapes (amplify about 20 times;
   tympanic membrane area much larger than oval window)
 → stapes footplate pushes OVAL WINDOW → waves in perilymph and endolymph of the cochlea
 → BASILAR MEMBRANE vibrates → hair cells of the ORGAN OF CORTI bend against
   the tectorial membrane → nerve impulses in the COCHLEAR nerve (VIII)
 → brainstem nuclei → medial geniculate body (thalamus)
 → AUDITORY CORTEX in the TEMPORAL lobe (areas 41, 42)
 Round window bulges out to release pressure.
```

- **Pitch**: high-frequency sounds stimulate the base of the cochlea (near the oval window), low frequencies the apex. Humans hear about **20–20,000 Hz**; speech is mostly 250–4,000 Hz.
- **Loudness**: larger vibration → more hair cells and higher firing rate. Measured in decibels; prolonged noise above about 85 dB can damage hearing.
- The **Eustachian (auditory) tube** equalises air pressure on both sides of the eardrum (swallowing or yawning opens it).
- **Conductive deafness** — sound cannot reach the inner ear (wax, otitis media, perforated drum, otosclerosis). **Sensorineural deafness** — damage to cochlea or nerve (ageing, noise, some drugs, infection).
- **Presbycusis** — age-related loss, first for high-pitched sounds.

## 5.15 Taste (gustation)

- Receptors are **taste buds**, mostly on the papillae of the tongue, also on palate, pharynx and epiglottis. Food must be **dissolved in saliva** to be tasted.
- Five basic tastes: **sweet, salty, sour, bitter, umami** (savoury). All areas of the tongue can sense all tastes.
- **Nerves**: anterior two-thirds of tongue — **facial nerve** (chorda tympani); posterior one-third — **glossopharyngeal nerve**; epiglottis region — **vagus**. Pathway → medulla → thalamus → taste area in the parietal cortex (near the face area and insula).
- Much of "flavour" is actually smell — food tastes bland with a cold.

## 5.16 Smell (olfaction)

- Receptors are **olfactory cells** (modified neurons) in the olfactory epithelium in the roof of the nasal cavity; odorants must dissolve in the mucus.
- Pathway: olfactory nerve fibres pass through the **cribriform plate** → olfactory bulb → olfactory tract → olfactory cortex in the temporal lobe and **limbic system** (so smells trigger memories and emotions). Smell is the only sense not relayed through the thalamus first.
- Smell **adapts** quickly (we stop noticing a constant odour). Loss of smell (**anosmia**) occurs with colds, head injury (fracture of cribriform plate), ageing and some viral infections.

## 5.17 Ageing changes in the senses — summary

| Sense | Change with age |
| --- | --- |
| Vision | Presbyopia, cataract, poor dark adaptation, glare sensitivity, dry eyes |
| Hearing | Presbycusis (high tones first), wax build-up |
| Taste and smell | Fewer taste buds and olfactory cells → food tastes bland; risk of eating spoiled food or adding too much salt; danger of not smelling gas or smoke |
| Skin and touch | Thin dry skin, fewer sweat glands (heat stroke risk), reduced pain and temperature sense (burn and pressure ulcer risk), poor temperature control |

## 5.18 Application and implications in nursing (sensory organs)

- **Temperature** — record by the right method; tepid sponging and cool environment help heat loss; protect elderly patients and newborns from cold.
- **Pressure ulcer prevention** — reduced sensation in elderly, diabetic and paralysed patients means they do not feel pressure; turn them two-hourly as per protocol and inspect skin.
- **Hot water bottles and heat applications** — check temperature carefully in patients with poor sensation.
- **Eye care** — protect the cornea of unconscious patients (they cannot blink); record pupil size and reaction in neurological patients; teach correct instillation of eye drops; vitamin A to prevent night blindness under national programmes.
- **Hearing loss** — face the patient, speak clearly and slowly at a normal pitch, reduce background noise, check hearing aids; never instil anything into an ear with a possibly perforated drum unless ordered.
- **Taste and smell loss** — make food attractive, check dentures and oral hygiene, ensure smoke and gas alarms in elderly homes.
- **Elderly patients** — good lighting, glasses within reach and clear paths reduce falls.

## Remember

- Peptide hormones act on membrane receptors and second messengers; steroids and thyroid hormones act inside the cell.
- Hypothalamus controls the anterior pituitary through portal veins; ADH and oxytocin are made in the hypothalamus and stored in the posterior pituitary.
- GH excess: gigantism (child), acromegaly (adult); ADH lack: diabetes insipidus.
- T3/T4 raise metabolism and are essential for brain growth (cretinism if lacking in infancy); iodine deficiency → goitre.
- PTH raises calcium (bone, kidney, vitamin D); low calcium → tetany, Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs.
- Insulin (β cells) is the only hormone that lowers blood glucose; glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol, GH raise it.
- Cortex: aldosterone (salt), cortisol (sugar, stress), androgens; medulla: adrenaline (fight or flight).
- Melatonin from the pineal sets the circadian rhythm; thymus matures T cells.
- Accommodation: ciliary muscle contracts, lens bulges, pupil constricts, eyes converge.
- Rods: dim light, rhodopsin, vitamin A; cones: colour, fovea.
- Myopia → concave lens; hypermetropia and presbyopia → convex; astigmatism → cylindrical.
- Hearing: tympanic membrane → ossicles → oval window → cochlea → organ of Corti → VIII nerve → temporal lobe.
- Taste: facial (anterior 2/3), glossopharyngeal (posterior 1/3); smell goes to the limbic system without a thalamic relay.
