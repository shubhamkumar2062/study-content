# Unit 1: General physiology — cell, body fluids, tissues, membranes and glands

This unit covers INC Unit I (4 hours). Anatomy tells you what the cell and tissues look like; physiology tells you how they work. Almost every nursing action that involves fluids, electrolytes, wounds or infusions rests on the ideas in this unit.

## 1.1 Physiology and homeostasis

**Physiology** is the study of the normal functions of the living body and its parts.

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a nearly constant internal environment (the extracellular fluid) in spite of changes outside the body. Body temperature, blood glucose, blood pH, blood pressure, plasma sodium and water content are all kept within narrow limits.

Every homeostatic system has three parts:

| Part | Job | Example: body temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Receptor (sensor) | Detects a change | Thermoreceptors in skin and hypothalamus |
| Control centre | Compares with the set point and decides | Hypothalamus |
| Effector | Carries out the correction | Sweat glands, skin blood vessels, skeletal muscle (shivering) |

### Negative and positive feedback

- **Negative feedback** — the response *opposes* the original change and brings the value back to normal. Most body control works this way. Examples: a rise in blood glucose → insulin → glucose falls; a fall in blood pressure → baroreceptor reflex → heart rate and vessel tone rise.
- **Positive feedback** — the response *increases* the original change until some event ends it. It is used only for short, self-limiting events. Examples: oxytocin and uterine contractions during labour; clotting of blood; the rise of sodium entry during the upstroke of a nerve action potential; the LH surge before ovulation.

```text
Negative feedback loop

  Change (e.g. BP falls)
        │
        ▼
  Receptor (baroreceptors) ──► Control centre (medulla)
                                      │
                                      ▼
                              Effector (heart, arterioles)
                                      │
                                      ▼
                     Response opposes the change (BP rises back)
```

## 1.2 Cell physiology

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of the body. Its main parts and their work:

| Part | Main function |
| --- | --- |
| Cell (plasma) membrane | Boundary; controls what enters and leaves; carries receptors |
| Nucleus | Holds DNA; controls protein synthesis and cell division |
| Mitochondria | Aerobic respiration; make most of the cell's ATP ("power house") |
| Ribosomes | Protein synthesis |
| Rough endoplasmic reticulum | Makes proteins for export and for membranes |
| Smooth endoplasmic reticulum | Lipid and steroid synthesis, detoxification, calcium store in muscle |
| Golgi apparatus | Packs, modifies and dispatches proteins in vesicles |
| Lysosomes | Digestive enzymes; break down worn-out parts and engulfed bacteria |
| Peroxisomes | Break down fatty acids and hydrogen peroxide |
| Cytoskeleton, centrosome | Shape, movement, spindle formation during division |

### The cell membrane

The membrane is a **lipid bilayer** (phospholipids with water-loving heads outward and water-fearing tails inward) with proteins floating in it — the **fluid mosaic model**. Cholesterol keeps it stable; carbohydrate chains on the outer surface act as identity markers (for example blood group antigens).

Membrane proteins act as channels, carriers, pumps, receptors for hormones and enzymes. Because the core is fatty, lipid-soluble substances (O2, CO2, alcohol, steroid hormones) pass easily, while ions and glucose need proteins to cross.

## 1.3 Transport across the cell membrane

```text
Transport across membrane
├── Passive (no ATP; down the concentration gradient)
│     ├── Simple diffusion
│     ├── Facilitated diffusion (carrier or channel)
│     ├── Osmosis (water)
│     └── Filtration (pushed by hydrostatic pressure)
├── Active (uses ATP; can go against the gradient)
│     ├── Primary active transport (Na+–K+ pump)
│     └── Secondary active transport (cotransport, countertransport)
└── Vesicular transport (bulk; uses ATP)
      ├── Endocytosis: phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated
      └── Exocytosis
```

### Passive transport

- **Simple diffusion** — molecules move from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration until evenly spread. Gas exchange in the lungs is the classic example. The rate rises with a larger concentration difference, larger surface area, higher temperature and thinner membrane, and falls with larger molecular size.
- **Facilitated diffusion** — still down the gradient, but a carrier protein helps. Glucose enters most cells this way (GLUT carriers). It shows *saturation*: once all carriers are busy, the rate cannot rise further.
- **Osmosis** — movement of **water** across a selectively permeable membrane from a dilute solution (more water) to a concentrated solution (less water). The pressure needed to stop it is the **osmotic pressure**. Plasma osmolality is about 285–295 mOsm/kg water.
- **Filtration** — water and small solutes are pushed through a membrane by hydrostatic pressure, for example at the glomerulus and at the arterial end of capillaries.

### Tonicity — why it matters at the bedside

| Solution | Effect on red cells | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isotonic | No change in size | 0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline), 5% glucose at first |
| Hypotonic | Water enters; cells swell and may burst (haemolysis) | Plain water, 0.45% saline |
| Hypertonic | Water leaves; cells shrink (crenation) | 3% saline, 10% glucose |

### Active transport

- **Primary active transport** uses ATP directly. The **sodium–potassium pump (Na+–K+ ATPase)** pushes **3 Na+ out** and takes **2 K+ in** for each ATP. It keeps Na+ high outside and K+ high inside, helps maintain the resting membrane potential and controls cell volume. Other pumps: calcium pump, the H+–K+ pump of gastric parietal cells.
- **Secondary active transport** uses the Na+ gradient made by the pump. In **cotransport (symport)** Na+ and another substance move in the same direction (Na+–glucose in the gut and kidney tubules). In **countertransport (antiport)** they move in opposite directions (Na+–H+ exchange in kidney tubules).

### Vesicular (bulk) transport

- **Endocytosis** — the membrane folds around material and pinches off a vesicle. **Phagocytosis** ("cell eating") is taking in large particles such as bacteria by neutrophils and macrophages. **Pinocytosis** ("cell drinking") takes in droplets of fluid. **Receptor-mediated endocytosis** takes up specific substances such as LDL cholesterol.
- **Exocytosis** — vesicles fuse with the membrane and release contents outside: hormones, neurotransmitters, digestive enzymes, mucus.

## 1.4 Body fluid compartments

Water is the largest single component of the body. In a young adult man it is about **60% of body weight**; in women about 50–55% (more fat, which holds little water); in a newborn about 75%; in the elderly it falls to about 45–50%.

```text
Total body water (about 60% of body weight; 42 L in a 70 kg man)
├── Intracellular fluid (ICF) — 2/3 — about 40% of body weight (≈ 28 L)
└── Extracellular fluid (ECF) — 1/3 — about 20% of body weight (≈ 14 L)
      ├── Interstitial fluid (incl. lymph) — about 3/4 of ECF (≈ 10.5 L)
      ├── Plasma — about 1/4 of ECF (≈ 3.5 L)
      └── Transcellular fluid — small: CSF, synovial, pleural, aqueous humour, GI secretions
```

### Major electrolytes

| Ion | ECF (plasma) | ICF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium (Na+) | High: about 135–145 mmol/L — main ECF cation | Low (about 10–14) |
| Potassium (K+) | Low: about 3.5–5.0 mmol/L | High (about 140) — main ICF cation |
| Chloride (Cl−) | High: about 98–106 mmol/L — main ECF anion | Low |
| Bicarbonate (HCO3−) | About 22–28 mmol/L | Low |
| Phosphate, proteins | Low | High — main ICF anions |
| Magnesium (Mg2+) | Low | Relatively high |
| Calcium (Ca2+) | About 9–10.5 mg/dL total in plasma | Very low in cytosol |

Laboratory reference ranges differ slightly; always read the range printed on the report.

**Functions of electrolytes**: control of osmotic pressure and water distribution (Na+ decides ECF volume); nerve and muscle excitability (Na+, K+, Ca2+); acid–base buffering (HCO3−, phosphate, proteins); enzyme activity; blood clotting (Ca2+); bone (Ca2+, phosphate).

### How water and electrolytes are kept in balance

- **Intake** — drinking (thirst centre in hypothalamus), food, and water made by metabolism (about 300 mL a day).
- **Output** — urine (largest and the only one finely controlled), insensible loss through skin and breath (about 700–900 mL a day in an adult at rest), sweat, faeces (about 100–200 mL).
- **Hormonal control** — ADH (water), aldosterone (sodium reabsorption, potassium excretion), atrial natriuretic peptide (sodium loss), the renin–angiotensin system.
- **Between compartments** — water moves freely across cell membranes by osmosis, so a fall in ECF sodium draws water into cells (cell swelling, brain oedema), and a rise draws water out (cell shrinkage). Between plasma and interstitial fluid, the balance of capillary hydrostatic pressure and plasma protein (oncotic) pressure decides fluid movement (Starling forces).

**Oedema** is excess fluid in the interstitial space. Causes: raised capillary pressure (heart failure, venous obstruction), low plasma proteins (malnutrition, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome), leaky capillaries (inflammation, burns), blocked lymphatics (filariasis), sodium retention.

## 1.5 The cell cycle

The cell cycle is the sequence of events by which a cell grows and divides into two daughter cells.

```text
     ┌──────────── Interphase (about 90% of the time) ────────────┐
     G1 (growth, normal work) ──► S (DNA copied) ──► G2 (prepare) ──► M (mitosis + cytokinesis)
      ▲                                                                       │
      └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
     G0 = resting phase: cell has left the cycle (e.g. neurons, most adult cardiac muscle)
```

- **G1 phase** — cell grows, makes proteins and organelles; checkpoint decides whether to divide.
- **S phase** — DNA is replicated, so each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids.
- **G2 phase** — final growth; checks that DNA is copied correctly.
- **M phase (mitosis)** — prophase (chromosomes condense, nuclear membrane breaks down, spindle forms), metaphase (chromosomes line up at the equator), anaphase (chromatids pulled to opposite poles), telophase (nuclear membranes re-form) — followed by **cytokinesis** (cytoplasm divides). Mitosis gives two identical diploid (46-chromosome) cells; it is used for growth and repair.
- **Meiosis** — two divisions that halve the chromosome number to 23 (haploid); it occurs only in the gonads to form ova and sperm and creates genetic variation (crossing over).

**Checkpoints** stop a cell with damaged DNA from dividing. When this control fails, cells divide without restraint — the basis of cancer. Many anticancer treatments and radiotherapy act on rapidly dividing cells, which is why hair follicles, gut lining and bone marrow are affected.

**Cells by ability to divide**: labile cells divide throughout life (skin, gut lining, bone marrow); stable cells divide when needed (liver, kidney tubules); permanent cells cannot divide (neurons, cardiac muscle) — damage heals by scar.

## 1.6 Tissues — formation and repair

**Tissue** = a group of similar cells with the intercellular material that together do one job. The four basic tissues are epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous. All tissues develop from the three germ layers of the embryo (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm).

### Tissue repair

1. **Regeneration** — dead cells are replaced by the same kind of cells, so normal structure and function return. Possible in labile and stable cells when the supporting framework is intact (for example a skin graze, liver after partial damage).
2. **Repair by fibrosis (scar)** — when damage is large or the cells cannot divide, the gap is filled with granulation tissue that becomes fibrous scar. Function of that part is lost (scar after myocardial infarction).

### Phases of wound healing

| Phase | Approximate time | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemostasis | Minutes | Vessel spasm, platelet plug, clot seals the wound |
| Inflammation | First 1–4 days | Vessels dilate and leak; neutrophils then macrophages clear debris and bacteria; redness, warmth, swelling, pain |
| Proliferation | About day 3 to 3 weeks | Fibroblasts lay down collagen; new capillaries form (granulation tissue — red, granular); epithelium grows over; wound contracts |
| Maturation (remodelling) | 3 weeks to a year or more | Collagen reorganised and strengthened; scar pales and flattens |

- **Healing by first intention** — clean, closely joined edges (surgical incision); little granulation tissue, thin scar.
- **Healing by second intention** — gaping or infected wound; fills from the base with much granulation tissue; slow, large scar.

**Factors that delay healing**: poor blood supply, infection, foreign body, diabetes, malnutrition (low protein, vitamin C, zinc), anaemia, old age, corticosteroid therapy, smoking, movement or tension on the wound.

## 1.7 Membranes and glands — functions

### Membranes

| Membrane | Where | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mucous membrane | Lines tracts open to the outside: digestive, respiratory, urinary, reproductive | Secretes mucus; protects, lubricates, traps dust and microbes; absorbs (gut) |
| Serous membrane | Two layers (parietal and visceral) lining closed cavities: pleura, pericardium, peritoneum | Secretes thin serous fluid so organs glide without friction |
| Synovial membrane | Lines cavities of freely movable joints, bursae, tendon sheaths | Secretes synovial fluid that lubricates and nourishes articular cartilage |
| Cutaneous membrane | The skin | Protection, temperature control, sensation |

### Glands

A **gland** is a cell or group of cells that makes and releases a secretion.

- **Exocrine glands** release secretions through **ducts** onto a surface or into a cavity: sweat, salivary, sebaceous, gastric glands, exocrine pancreas, liver (bile).
- **Endocrine glands** are ductless and release **hormones into the blood**: pituitary, thyroid, adrenals, islets of pancreas.
- **Mixed glands** do both: pancreas, testis, ovary.

By the way they release secretion:

| Type | How | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Merocrine (eccrine) | Secretion leaves by exocytosis; cell intact | Salivary, most sweat glands |
| Apocrine | Top part of the cell breaks off with the secretion | Axillary sweat glands, mammary (fat part) |
| Holocrine | Whole cell bursts and becomes the secretion | Sebaceous glands |

## 1.8 Application and implication in nursing

- **Intravenous fluids** — knowing tonicity explains why isotonic saline expands ECF, why too much hypotonic fluid can cause cell (including brain) swelling, and why hypertonic fluid is given slowly under close watch. Fluids, their type and rate are always as prescribed.
- **Intake–output chart** — the nurse measures all intake and output to judge fluid balance; daily weight under the same conditions is the best bedside guide to body water change.
- **Dehydration** — dry mouth, thirst, poor skin turgor, sunken eyes, low urine output, rising pulse; infants and the elderly dehydrate fastest because of their water proportions and weaker thirst.
- **Oedema** — assess pitting, elevate limbs, turn often (oedematous skin breaks down easily), restrict sodium or fluid when ordered.
- **Electrolyte watch** — low potassium causes muscle weakness and heart rhythm problems; vomiting and diarrhoea, diuretics and wound drainage all cause losses.
- **Wound care** — aseptic technique, adequate protein and vitamin C, control of blood sugar, pressure relief and early detection of infection all speed healing.
- **Cancer therapy** — patients on drugs that kill dividing cells need mouth care, infection prevention and monitoring of blood counts, because labile tissues are hit first.

## Remember

- Homeostasis keeps the ECF constant; most control is by negative feedback; positive feedback is rare (labour, clotting, LH surge).
- Passive transport needs no ATP (diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, filtration); the Na+–K+ pump moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per ATP.
- Total body water ≈ 60% of body weight: ICF 40%, ECF 20% (interstitial 15%, plasma 5%).
- Na+ and Cl− are the main ECF ions; K+ and phosphate the main ICF ions.
- Cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA copy) → G2 → M; neurons and cardiac muscle sit in G0 and heal by scar.
- Wound healing: haemostasis → inflammation → proliferation → remodelling.
- Mucous membranes line open tracts, serous membranes line closed cavities, synovial membranes line joints.
- Exocrine glands have ducts; endocrine glands release hormones into blood; sebaceous glands are holocrine.
