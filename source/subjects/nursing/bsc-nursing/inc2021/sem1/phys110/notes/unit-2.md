# Unit 2: The respiratory system

This unit covers INC Unit II (6 hours). It explains how air moves in and out of the lungs, how oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged and carried, how breathing is controlled, and what goes wrong in hypoxia, cyanosis and dyspnoea. Oxygen therapy, positioning and breathing exercises all make sense once these ideas are clear.

## 2.1 Functions of the respiratory organs

| Organ | Physiological function |
| --- | --- |
| Nose and nasal cavity | Warms, moistens and filters air (hairs, mucus); smell; resonance of voice |
| Pharynx | Common passage for air and food; tonsils give local immune defence |
| Larynx | Voice production; protects the airway (epiglottis closes during swallowing; cough reflex) |
| Trachea and bronchi | Conduct air; mucus and cilia form the **mucociliary escalator** that sweeps dust and microbes up to the throat |
| Bronchioles | Smooth muscle controls airway diameter (and so airflow resistance) |
| Alveoli | Site of gas exchange; type II cells make surfactant; alveolar macrophages remove particles |
| Pleura | Pleural fluid lets lungs glide; the pressure in the pleural space keeps lungs expanded |

**Non-respiratory functions of the lungs**: help keep blood pH normal by removing CO2; filter small clots from venous blood; act as a blood reservoir; convert angiotensin I to angiotensin II (by angiotensin-converting enzyme on the capillary walls); heat and water loss; defence against inhaled microbes.

## 2.2 Physiology of respiration

**Respiration** has four stages:

1. **Pulmonary ventilation** — movement of air between the atmosphere and the alveoli (breathing).
2. **External respiration** — exchange of O2 and CO2 between alveoli and pulmonary capillary blood.
3. **Transport of gases** — carriage of O2 and CO2 in blood.
4. **Internal respiration** — exchange of gases between systemic capillary blood and tissue cells (and use of O2 in the mitochondria — cellular respiration).

### Mechanism of breathing

Air flows from high pressure to low pressure. Breathing works by changing the volume of the thoracic cavity, which changes the pressure inside the lungs (Boyle's law: at constant temperature, pressure and volume of a gas vary inversely).

**Inspiration (active)**

- The **diaphragm** contracts and moves down — this alone does about 75% of quiet breathing and increases the vertical size of the chest.
- The **external intercostal muscles** contract, lifting the ribs and sternum upward and outward (bucket-handle and pump-handle movements), increasing the side-to-side and front-to-back size.
- Thoracic volume rises → intrapleural pressure becomes more negative → lungs expand → pressure in the alveoli falls slightly below atmospheric (about −1 mmHg) → air flows in.
- In deep or laboured breathing **accessory muscles** help: sternocleidomastoid, scalene muscles, pectoralis minor, serratus anterior.

**Expiration (passive in quiet breathing)**

- Diaphragm and external intercostals relax; elastic recoil of lungs and chest wall reduces volume → alveolar pressure rises above atmospheric (about +1 mmHg) → air flows out.
- **Forced expiration** (coughing, exercise, asthma) is active: internal intercostals and abdominal muscles (rectus abdominis, obliques, transversus abdominis) contract.

### Pressures

| Pressure | Value (relative to atmosphere) |
| --- | --- |
| Intra-alveolar (intrapulmonary) | 0 at rest; about −1 mmHg in inspiration, +1 mmHg in expiration |
| Intrapleural | Always negative in quiet breathing: about −2.5 mmHg at end of expiration to about −6 mmHg at end of inspiration |

The negative intrapleural pressure keeps the lungs stuck to the chest wall. If air enters the pleural space (**pneumothorax**), the negative pressure is lost and the lung collapses.

### Compliance and surfactant

- **Compliance** = change in lung volume per unit change in pressure — how easily the lungs stretch. It falls in pulmonary fibrosis, oedema and surfactant lack; it rises in emphysema (but recoil is lost).
- **Surfactant**, a lipoprotein (mainly dipalmitoyl lecithin) made by type II alveolar cells, lowers surface tension of the fluid lining the alveoli. It keeps small alveoli from collapsing, makes the lungs easier to inflate and helps keep alveoli dry. It is produced in good amount only late in fetal life, so premature babies may develop **respiratory distress syndrome**.
- **Airway resistance** rises when bronchioles narrow (asthma, mucus); parasympathetic stimulation constricts and sympathetic stimulation (adrenaline on β2 receptors) dilates them.

## 2.3 Pulmonary circulation — functional features

The right ventricle pumps the whole cardiac output through the lungs, but the pulmonary circuit is a **low-pressure, low-resistance** system.

| Feature | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure | Pulmonary artery about 25/8 mmHg (mean about 15 mmHg) versus aorta about 120/80 |
| Vessels | Thin-walled, short, wide, very distensible |
| Response to low O2 | **Hypoxic vasoconstriction** — vessels in poorly ventilated areas narrow, diverting blood to well-ventilated alveoli (opposite to systemic vessels, which dilate) |
| Capillary pressure | Low (about 7–10 mmHg), so fluid normally does not leak into alveoli; if left atrial pressure rises (left heart failure) → pulmonary oedema |
| Reservoir | Holds about 450–500 mL of blood that can shift into systemic circulation |
| Filter | Traps small clots and air bubbles from venous blood |
| Regional flow | When standing, gravity gives the bases more blood flow than the apices |

## 2.4 Pulmonary ventilation — lung volumes and capacities

Measured with a **spirometer**. Average values for a young adult man (women about 20–25% less):

| Volume / capacity | Meaning | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tidal volume (TV) | Air breathed in or out in one quiet breath | about 500 mL |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | Extra air that can be breathed in after a normal inspiration | about 3000 mL |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | Extra air that can be breathed out after a normal expiration | about 1100 mL |
| Residual volume (RV) | Air left in lungs after maximum expiration (cannot be measured by simple spirometry) | about 1200 mL |
| Inspiratory capacity (IC) | TV + IRV | about 3500 mL |
| Functional residual capacity (FRC) | ERV + RV — air left after quiet expiration | about 2300 mL |
| Vital capacity (VC) | IRV + TV + ERV — maximum air breathed out after maximum inspiration | about 4600 mL |
| Total lung capacity (TLC) | VC + RV | about 5800 mL |

```text
 Volume
   │ ┌──────── IRV 3000 ────────┐  ┐
   │ │                          │  │ IC 3500    ┐
   │ ├──────── TV 500 ──────────┤  ┘            │ VC 4600   ┐
   │ │──────── ERV 1100 ────────┤  ┐            ┘           │ TLC 5800
   │ │──────── RV 1200 ─────────┘  ┘ FRC 2300               ┘
```

- **Minute (pulmonary) ventilation** = tidal volume × respiratory rate = 500 mL × 12/min = **about 6 L/min**.
- **Anatomical dead space** — air in the conducting airways that takes no part in exchange: about **150 mL**.
- **Alveolar ventilation** = (TV − dead space) × rate = (500 − 150) × 12 = **about 4.2 L/min**. This is the figure that actually matters for gas exchange: rapid shallow breathing ventilates mostly dead space, while slower deeper breathing is more efficient.
- **FEV1** — volume breathed out in the first second of a forced expiration; normally about 80% of the forced vital capacity. It falls more than FVC in obstructive disease (asthma, COPD).
- Normal adult respiratory rate: about **12–20 breaths per minute** at rest; newborn about 30–60.

## 2.5 Exchange of gases in the lungs

Gases move by **diffusion** from higher to lower **partial pressure**. The partial pressure of a gas is the share of total pressure that gas contributes (Dalton's law). Atmospheric pressure at sea level is 760 mmHg; O2 is about 21% of dry air.

| Site | PO2 (mmHg) | PCO2 (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atmospheric air | about 159 | about 0.3 |
| Alveolar air | about 104 | about 40 |
| Mixed venous blood (pulmonary artery) | about 40 | about 45 |
| Arterial blood | about 95–100 | about 40 |
| Tissue cells | about 40 or less | about 45 or more |

So O2 moves from alveoli (104) into blood (40), and CO2 from blood (45) into alveoli (40). Although the CO2 gradient is small, CO2 is about **20 times more soluble** than O2 and diffuses easily.

**The respiratory membrane** (alveolar epithelium, fused basement membranes, capillary endothelium) is only about 0.5 µm thick and has a huge area (about 70 m² in total).

**Factors affecting diffusion**: partial pressure difference; surface area (reduced in emphysema, lung removal); thickness of the membrane (increased in pulmonary oedema, fibrosis, pneumonia); solubility of the gas; the match between ventilation and blood flow (ventilation–perfusion ratio, normally about 0.8).

## 2.6 Carriage of oxygen

- About **97–98%** of O2 is carried bound to **haemoglobin** as oxyhaemoglobin; only **about 2–3%** is dissolved in plasma.
- Each gram of haemoglobin can bind about **1.34 mL of O2**; with 15 g/dL of Hb, fully saturated blood carries about 20 mL O2 per 100 mL.
- Arterial blood is about 97–98% saturated; normal **SpO2** by pulse oximetry is about **95–100%**. Venous blood at rest is about 75% saturated, so tissues take up only about a quarter of the O2 — a reserve that is used in exercise.

### Oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve

```text
 % saturation
 100 ┤                       ___________
     │                 ____/
  75 ┤ ............../  ◄ venous blood (PO2 40)
     │             /
  50 ┤ ......... /  ◄ P50 ≈ 26–27 mmHg
     │         /
     │      _/
   0 ┼────┴────┴────┴────┴────┴────
     0   20   40   60   80   100  PO2 (mmHg)
```

The curve is **S-shaped (sigmoid)**: the flat upper part means blood stays well saturated even when alveolar PO2 falls somewhat; the steep middle part means a small fall in tissue PO2 releases a lot of O2.

**Shift to the right** (Hb gives up O2 more easily — useful in active tissues): rise in PCO2, rise in H+ (fall in pH), rise in temperature, rise in 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG). The effect of CO2 and H+ is called the **Bohr effect**.

**Shift to the left** (Hb holds O2 more tightly): opposite changes, **fetal haemoglobin**, and carbon monoxide. **Carbon monoxide** binds Hb about 200 times more strongly than O2, so it blocks O2 carriage even though the arterial PO2 may be normal.

## 2.7 Carriage of carbon dioxide

| Form | Share of CO2 carried |
| --- | --- |
| As **bicarbonate** ions in plasma | about 70% |
| As **carbamino compounds** (bound to Hb and plasma proteins) | about 20–23% |
| **Dissolved** in plasma | about 7% |

```text
In red cells at the tissues:
CO2 + H2O  ⇌  H2CO3  ⇌  H+  +  HCO3−
       (carbonic anhydrase)
H+ is buffered by haemoglobin; HCO3− leaves the cell for plasma,
and Cl− enters in exchange  → CHLORIDE SHIFT (Hamburger phenomenon).
In the lungs every step runs backwards and CO2 is breathed out.
```

**Haldane effect** — when Hb gives up O2 in the tissues it can carry more CO2 and H+; when it takes up O2 in the lungs it releases CO2. This helps both loading and unloading.

## 2.8 Exchange of gases in the tissues

Arterial blood arriving at tissues has PO2 about 95–100 and PCO2 about 40 mmHg. Tissue PO2 is about 40 (lower in active muscle) and PCO2 about 45 or more. So O2 diffuses out of capillaries into cells and CO2 diffuses from cells into blood. More O2 is released where tissues are active, warm, acidic and rich in CO2 (right shift).

## 2.9 Regulation of respiration

Breathing is controlled automatically by nerve centres in the brainstem, adjusted by chemical and reflex signals, and can be overridden voluntarily for a short time.

### Respiratory centres

| Centre | Site | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dorsal respiratory group | Medulla | Sets basic rhythm; mainly inspiratory; sends impulses via phrenic and intercostal nerves |
| Ventral respiratory group | Medulla | Active in forced breathing (both inspiratory and expiratory neurons) |
| Pneumotaxic centre | Upper pons | Switches off inspiration; controls rate and depth |
| Apneustic centre | Lower pons | Promotes prolonged inspiration (held in check by the pneumotaxic centre) |

### Chemical regulation

- **Central chemoreceptors** (medulla) respond to **H+ in the CSF**. CO2 crosses the blood–brain barrier easily and forms H+, so a rise in arterial PCO2 is the **strongest normal stimulus to breathing**.
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors** — **carotid bodies** (at the carotid bifurcation; nerve: glossopharyngeal) and **aortic bodies** (nerve: vagus) — respond mainly to a **fall in arterial PO2** (strongly below about 60 mmHg), and also to raised PCO2 and H+.
- In some people with long-standing CO2 retention (for example advanced COPD), the central receptors become less sensitive and the drive from low O2 becomes more important — a reason oxygen therapy in such patients is given and watched exactly as prescribed.

### Reflex and other control

- **Hering–Breuer (inflation) reflex** — stretch receptors in the bronchial walls send impulses through the vagus when the lungs are inflated, stopping further inspiration; prevents over-inflation (important in infants, weak at normal adult tidal volumes).
- Irritant receptors cause **cough** and **sneeze**; juxtacapillary (J) receptors are stimulated in pulmonary congestion.
- Proprioceptors in joints and muscles increase breathing at the start of exercise.
- Higher centres: voluntary control (speaking, singing, breath-holding), emotions, pain, body temperature (fever increases rate).

## 2.10 Hypoxia, cyanosis, dyspnoea and periodic breathing

### Hypoxia

**Hypoxia** is lack of O2 at the tissue level.

| Type | Cause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hypoxic (hypoxaemic) | Low arterial PO2 | High altitude, airway obstruction, pneumonia, respiratory depression |
| Anaemic | Low O2-carrying capacity; arterial PO2 normal | Anaemia, haemorrhage, carbon monoxide poisoning |
| Stagnant (ischaemic) | Slow blood flow | Heart failure, shock, arterial blockage |
| Histotoxic | Cells cannot use O2 | Cyanide poisoning |

Signs: restlessness, confusion, headache, rapid pulse and breathing, poor judgement; later drowsiness, cyanosis (not in all types), arrhythmias, unconsciousness.

### Cyanosis

**Cyanosis** is a bluish colour of skin and mucous membranes that appears when the amount of **deoxygenated (reduced) haemoglobin in capillary blood exceeds about 5 g/dL**.

- **Central cyanosis** — seen on lips, tongue and mucous membranes; due to poor oxygenation of arterial blood (lung disease, right-to-left heart shunts).
- **Peripheral cyanosis** — seen on fingertips, nail beds and ear lobes with warm tongue; due to slow flow and high O2 extraction (cold exposure, shock, heart failure).
- It may be **absent in severe anaemia** (not enough Hb to reach 5 g/dL reduced) and in **carbon monoxide poisoning** (cherry-red colour), and is more obvious in **polycythaemia**.

### Dyspnoea

**Dyspnoea** is difficult or laboured breathing that the person is aware of ("air hunger"). Causes: lung disease (asthma, pneumonia), heart failure, anaemia, anxiety, exertion. **Orthopnoea** = breathlessness on lying flat, relieved by sitting up (heart failure). **Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea** = sudden breathlessness that wakes the patient at night.

Related terms: **eupnoea** (normal quiet breathing), **tachypnoea** (rapid breathing), **bradypnoea** (slow breathing), **apnoea** (temporary stop in breathing), **hyperpnoea** (increased depth), **hyperventilation** (breathing more than needed, washing out CO2 → light-headedness, tingling).

### Periodic breathing

Abnormal rhythm in which breathing waxes and wanes or stops in cycles.

- **Cheyne–Stokes breathing** — breaths gradually increase in depth, then gradually decrease, followed by a period of apnoea; then the cycle repeats. Seen in severe heart failure, brain damage, at high altitude, in some healthy people during sleep, and near death.
- **Biot's breathing** — groups of quick, equal-depth breaths separated by irregular periods of apnoea; seen with damage to the medulla (meningitis, raised intracranial pressure).
- **Kussmaul breathing** (not periodic but worth knowing) — deep, rapid, sighing breaths of metabolic acidosis such as diabetic ketoacidosis.

## 2.11 Respiratory changes during exercise

- **Ventilation rises** — it jumps up at the start (nerve signals from the motor cortex and from proprioceptors in moving joints), then rises steadily as CO2 production, H+ and temperature increase. Minute ventilation may rise from about 6 L/min to 100 L/min or more in hard exercise.
- Tidal volume and rate both increase.
- **O2 consumption** rises many times; more O2 is extracted in muscles because of the right shift of the dissociation curve (more CO2, H+, heat).
- Pulmonary blood flow and diffusing capacity increase as more capillaries open, especially at the lung apices.
- After heavy exercise breathing stays raised for a while to repay the **oxygen debt** (to remove lactic acid and restore creatine phosphate and ATP).
- Training improves efficiency: lower resting rate, greater vital capacity in some, better O2 extraction.

## 2.12 Application and implication in nursing

- **Counting respiration** — count for a full minute without the patient knowing (people change their breathing when watched); note rate, depth, rhythm, effort, use of accessory muscles, and colour.
- **Positioning** — Fowler's or high-sitting position lowers the abdominal organs, lets the diaphragm move freely and eases orthopnoea; the orthopnoeic position (leaning forward on a table) helps severe breathlessness.
- **Deep breathing, coughing and incentive spirometry** after surgery keep alveoli open and prevent atelectasis and pneumonia; early ambulation helps.
- **Oxygen therapy** — oxygen is a prescribed drug; the nurse checks the prescription, humidifies when needed, monitors SpO2 and the patient's colour, level of consciousness and breathing, keeps away open flames and oil, and is especially careful with patients known to retain CO2.
- **Pulse oximetry** reads falsely in carbon monoxide poisoning, cold or poorly perfused fingers and with nail polish; always relate the reading to how the patient looks.
- **Airway care** — suction, chest physiotherapy and adequate fluids keep secretions thin; the mucociliary escalator is damaged by smoking and dry gases.
- **Chest drains** — understanding negative intrapleural pressure explains why an underwater seal and an airtight system are needed after pneumothorax.
- **Premature babies** — watch for grunting, chest retractions and cyanosis that suggest surfactant deficiency.

## Remember

- Respiration = ventilation → external respiration → transport → internal respiration.
- Diaphragm does about 75% of quiet inspiration; quiet expiration is passive recoil.
- Intrapleural pressure is negative (about −2.5 to −6 mmHg); surfactant from type II cells lowers surface tension.
- TV 500 mL, VC about 4600 mL, RV about 1200 mL, TLC about 5800 mL; dead space 150 mL; minute ventilation about 6 L/min.
- Alveolar PO2 104, PCO2 40; venous PO2 40, PCO2 45.
- O2: 97% on Hb (1.34 mL O2/g Hb); CO2: 70% bicarbonate, 23% carbamino, 7% dissolved; chloride shift.
- Right shift of the dissociation curve: ↑CO2, ↑H+, ↑temperature, ↑2,3-BPG (Bohr effect).
- CO2 (via H+ in CSF) is the strongest drive to breathe; carotid and aortic bodies sense low O2.
- Cyanosis needs > 5 g/dL reduced Hb; may be absent in severe anaemia and CO poisoning.
- Cheyne–Stokes = crescendo–decrescendo with apnoea; Biot's = groups of breaths with apnoea.
