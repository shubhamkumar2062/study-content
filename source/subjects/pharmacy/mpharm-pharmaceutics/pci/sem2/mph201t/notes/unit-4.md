# Unit 4: Pulmonary and intranasal drug delivery systems

The lung and the nose are the two mucosal routes that combine a very large or very permeable surface with easy, non-invasive access. The syllabus asks for aerosols, propellants, containers, types, preparation and evaluation for the pulmonary route, and the same four headings for the intranasal route.

## 4.1 Why the lung

| Feature | Value / note |
| --- | --- |
| Alveolar surface area | Roughly 70–100 m² in an adult |
| Alveolar epithelium | Type I pneumocytes, about 0.1–0.5 µm thick |
| Blood supply | The entire cardiac output passes through the pulmonary circulation |
| Metabolism | Low first-pass metabolism; fewer drug-metabolising enzymes than the liver or gut |
| Onset | Very rapid for both local and systemic action |

**Advantages**: small dose for a local effect in asthma and COPD, so fewer systemic side effects; rapid onset; avoidance of hepatic first pass and of the harsh gastric environment; useful for peptides and proteins; needle-free.
**Limitations**: efficiency depends heavily on the patient's inspiratory effort and technique; much of the dose is lost in the device, mouth and throat; mucociliary clearance and alveolar macrophages remove the drug; local irritation and cough; the lung's defence mechanisms limit the dose; dose reproducibility is harder than for an oral tablet.

### Mechanisms of deposition and the aerodynamic diameter

Deposition is governed by the **mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD)**, defined as the diameter of a unit-density sphere with the same settling velocity as the particle: d_ae = d_geo × √(ρ/χ), where ρ is particle density and χ the dynamic shape factor. A porous, low-density particle can therefore be large geometrically (which stops aggregation and macrophage uptake) while still being small aerodynamically.

| Mechanism | Dominant particle size | Region |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inertial impaction | Above about 5 µm | Oropharynx and large conducting airways (fast, turbulent flow) |
| Sedimentation (gravity) | About 1–5 µm | Smaller bronchi, bronchioles, alveoli — helped by breath-holding |
| Brownian diffusion | Below about 0.5 µm | Alveoli, but much is exhaled again |

**The respirable fraction is therefore about 1–5 µm**; above this the drug lands in the throat and is swallowed, below about 0.5 µm it is largely breathed out. Other factors: inspiratory flow rate (slow and deep for a pressurised inhaler, fast and forceful for a dry powder inhaler), breath-holding for about 10 seconds, airway geometry and disease, hygroscopic growth of the droplet, and particle charge.

**Clearance**: the mucociliary escalator removes material deposited on ciliated airways within hours; alveolar macrophages phagocytose particles of about 1–3 µm in the alveoli; absorption into the blood, and metabolism, remove the rest.

## 4.2 Pharmaceutical aerosols

A **pharmaceutical aerosol** is a pressurised dosage form in which the therapeutically active ingredient is dissolved, suspended or emulsified in a propellant or a mixture of propellant and solvent, and is expelled as a fine dispersion of liquid or solid particles when the valve is opened.

### a. Components

```text
   ┌─────────────┐  actuator (mouthpiece, spray orifice)
   │   ╔═══╗     │
   │   ║   ║     │  metering valve + stem, gasket, spring
   │ ──╨───╨──   │  valve ferrule crimped to the can
   │   ░░░░░░    │  vapour phase of propellant
   │   ▓▓▓▓▓▓    │  liquid: drug + propellant (+ cosolvent, surfactant)
   │   │dip│     │  dip tube reaching the bottom
   └───┴───┴─────┘  container: aluminium / stainless steel / coated glass
```

| Part | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| **Propellant** | Provides the energy; may also be the solvent or vehicle |
| **Container** | Must withstand pressures up to about 140–180 psig; aluminium (seamless, often internally lacquered with epoxy or fluoropolymer to stop drug adsorption and corrosion), stainless steel, or plastic-coated glass for low-pressure products |
| **Valve** | *Continuous* valve for topical products; **metering valve** for inhalers, delivering a reproducible 25–100 µL per actuation. Parts: ferrule, mounting cup, stem, gasket (nitrile, neoprene — a source of extractables), spring, dip tube, metering chamber |
| **Actuator** | Decides the spray pattern and droplet size through the orifice diameter and expansion chamber; types are spray, foam, solid-stream, and the special oral and nasal adaptors |
| **Product concentrate** | Drug, cosolvent (ethanol), surfactant/suspending agent (oleic acid, lecithin, sorbitan trioleate), antioxidant, flavour |

### b. Propellants

A propellant is a liquefied gas or compressed gas that supplies the pressure to expel the contents and to atomise them.

| Class | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)** | Propellant 11 (trichlorofluoromethane), 12 (dichlorodifluoromethane), 114 (dichlorotetrafluoroethane) | Historic standard; blended to adjust vapour pressure; **phased out under the Montreal Protocol** because they deplete stratospheric ozone |
| **Hydrofluoroalkanes (HFA)** | HFA-134a (tetrafluoroethane), HFA-227ea (heptafluoropropane) | Present-day inhaler propellants; no chlorine, so no ozone depletion. They are poor solvents for the old surfactants, so formulations had to be redeveloped with ethanol as cosolvent and new valve elastomers; they do have a high global-warming potential, which is driving a further change to low-GWP propellants |
| **Hydrocarbons** | Propane, n-butane, isobutane | Cheap, low toxicity, no ozone effect, but **flammable**; used in topical and non-inhalation products |
| **Compressed gases** | Nitrogen, carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide | Inert; pressure falls as the product is used; give a wet spray or foam rather than a fine mist |
| **Dimethyl ether** | | Good solvent, but flammable |

Vapour pressure is adjusted by **blending** propellants and is predicted by Raoult's law (P = P_A x_A + P_B x_B).

### c. Types of aerosol systems

| System | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Two-phase (solution)** | Liquid (drug dissolved in propellant ± cosolvent) + vapour. Simplest; fine mist; needs the drug to be soluble |
| **Three-phase (suspension)** | Drug micronised and suspended in propellant, with a surfactant to prevent caking; must be shaken before use. Most older inhalers are of this type |
| **Three-phase (emulsion)** | Aqueous phase + propellant + vapour; gives a **foam** (stable, quick-breaking or thermal) when the propellant is in the internal phase |
| **Barrier (separate-compartment) systems** | A piston or collapsible bag separates product from propellant (compressed gas); the product leaves without propellant contact — used for sterile and viscous products |

### d. Devices for pulmonary delivery

**1. Pressurised metered-dose inhaler (pMDI)**
Drug in solution or suspension in HFA propellant with a metering valve. Each actuation gives a fixed dose. Cheap, portable, sealed and dose-reproducible, but requires the patient to coordinate actuation with a slow deep breath, and much of the dose impacts in the oropharynx because the plume leaves at high speed. **Spacers / valved holding chambers** solve both problems by slowing the plume and allowing the large particles to settle out. Breath-actuated pMDIs remove the need for coordination. Formulation issues: suspension flocculation and caking, valve clogging, moisture ingress, loss of prime, and "tail-off" of dose at the end of the canister — hence dose counters.

**2. Dry powder inhaler (DPI)**
Micronised drug (1–5 µm) is usually blended with a coarse carrier, most often **α-lactose monohydrate (about 50–100 µm)**, to improve flow and dose uniformity; the fine drug must detach from the carrier during inhalation. Energy comes from the patient's own inspiratory flow, so no coordination is needed and no propellant is used, but performance depends on inspiratory effort and the device's internal resistance, and powders are sensitive to humidity. Types: single-dose capsule-based, multi-dose reservoir, and multi-unit blister devices. Advances include engineered particles (spray-dried, large porous, co-spray-dried with leucine) and force-control agents such as magnesium stearate.

**3. Nebulisers**
Convert a solution or suspension into a mist breathed over several minutes during tidal breathing — useful for infants, the elderly, the very breathless and for large doses.
- *Jet (air-jet) nebulisers*: compressed gas through a Venturi draws liquid up and shatters it; baffles return large droplets. Cheap, but slow and some drug is wasted during exhalation (solved by breath-enhanced and breath-actuated designs).
- *Ultrasonic nebulisers*: a piezoelectric crystal vibrating at 1–3 MHz raises a fountain of droplets. Fast, quiet, but heats the fluid (bad for proteins) and handles suspensions poorly.
- *Vibrating-mesh nebulisers*: fluid is pushed through thousands of laser-drilled holes; efficient, low residual volume, gentle on proteins, portable.

**4. Soft-mist inhaler**: mechanical energy (a spring) forces solution through a fine nozzle system to give a slow-moving, long-lasting mist with a high fine-particle fraction, with no propellant.

**Dosage forms for the lung**: solutions, suspensions, dry powders, liposomal dispersions (liposomal amikacin and amphotericin for lung infections), nanoparticles and microparticles for sustained lung release, and vaccines.

### e. Preparation of aerosols

1. **Manufacture of the concentrate** — micronisation of the drug (jet milling or spray drying) to 1–5 µm; mixing with cosolvent, surfactant and other excipients in a closed, moisture-controlled area; or preparation of the solution.
2. **Filling**
   - **Cold filling**: the concentrate and the chilled propellant (about −40 °C) are metered into the chilled can and the valve is crimped on at once. Simple, but unsuitable for aqueous and heat-labile products and there is a moisture-condensation risk.
   - **Pressure filling**: the concentrate is added at room temperature, the valve is crimped, air is evacuated, and propellant is forced in through the valve under pressure. Preferred: less propellant loss, less moisture, suitable for most products — the method used for inhalers.
   - **Compressed-gas filling** for products using nitrogen or carbon dioxide.
3. **Crimping of the valve** to a controlled depth and collet diameter.
4. **Leak testing** in a water bath at about 50–55 °C, weight-loss testing over time, and spray testing of samples.
5. **Capping, labelling and packing**; the label must carry the flammability and pressure warnings.

### f. Evaluation of pulmonary products

| Test | Purpose and method |
| --- | --- |
| **Aerodynamic particle size distribution** | The key test: **cascade impactor** (Andersen eight-stage, or the Next Generation Impactor) or multistage liquid impinger. Each stage collects a size band; the drug on each stage is assayed; MMAD, geometric standard deviation and the **fine particle fraction / fine particle dose** (usually the mass below 5 µm) are calculated |
| **Delivered (emitted) dose and dose uniformity** | Dose unit sampling apparatus; tested at the beginning, middle and end of the canister or device |
| Vapour pressure and leakage | Pressure gauge, water-bath leak test, weight loss |
| Spray pattern and plume geometry | Impaction on a treated plate or laser imaging |
| Net content, moisture, foreign particulates | Weight of filled and emptied container; Karl Fischer |
| Valve delivery (shot weight) | Mass of two actuations after priming |
| Flammability and combustibility | Flame extension and closed-drum tests for hydrocarbon propellants |
| Dissolution / in-vitro release and permeation | For sustained-release inhaled particles |
| Microbiological quality, preservative efficacy | For aqueous nebuliser solutions |
| Biological tests | Deposition imaging (gamma scintigraphy), pharmacokinetics, local tolerability |

## 4.3 Intranasal drug delivery

### a. The nasal route

The nasal cavity has a total surface of about **150 cm²** and three regions:

| Region | Features | Role in delivery |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nasal vestibule | Anterior, squamous epithelium, hairs | Filters particles; poor absorption |
| Respiratory region (turbinates) | Largest area; pseudostratified columnar ciliated epithelium with microvilli; very rich blood supply | **Main site of systemic absorption** |
| Olfactory region | About 3–10 cm² at the roof; olfactory neurons pass through the cribriform plate | **Nose-to-brain transport** |

**Advantages**: rapid absorption and onset, comparable with intravenous for some small lipophilic drugs; avoids hepatic first-pass metabolism and gastrointestinal degradation; large, highly vascular surface; easy, needle-free self-administration, so good patient acceptance; possible direct nose-to-brain delivery; useful for vaccines (mucosal IgA response).
**Limitations**: volume limited to about 25–200 µL per nostril; **mucociliary clearance** removes the formulation in about 15–20 min; enzymes in the mucosa (peptidases, proteases, cytochrome P450) degrade peptides; large hydrophilic molecules absorb poorly; absorption changes in the common cold, rhinitis and allergy; possible irritation, ciliotoxicity and long-term mucosal damage; the dose that can be given is small.

**Mechanisms of absorption**: transcellular passive diffusion of lipophilic drugs; the aqueous paracellular route through tight junctions for small hydrophilic molecules; carrier-mediated transport; and transcytosis for larger molecules. Nose-to-brain transport proceeds by the **olfactory pathway** (intraneuronal axonal transport, slow; and extraneuronal perineural transport along the olfactory nerve into the cerebrospinal fluid, fast) and the **trigeminal pathway**.

**Factors affecting nasal absorption**: molecular weight (absorption falls sharply above about 1 kDa without an enhancer), lipophilicity and degree of ionisation (the nasal surface pH is about 5.5–6.5), solubility and dissolution rate of the particles, pH, osmolarity and viscosity of the formulation, volume and concentration, deposition site (anterior deposition is cleared more slowly than posterior), and the device's spray angle and droplet size (droplets of about 10–50 µm stay in the nose; below 10 µm they may reach the lung).

### b. Types of nasal dosage forms

| Form | Comment |
| --- | --- |
| **Solutions and suspensions (drops)** | Simple and cheap, but the dose is not reproducible and the drops drain quickly to the throat |
| **Metered sprays (mechanical pump)** | The standard form: reproducible dose, better deposition in the turbinates, less drainage |
| **Aerosols (pMDI with a nasal adaptor)** | Reproducible dose, protection from contamination; propellant may irritate |
| **Gels and in-situ gelling systems** | Thermosensitive (poloxamer 407), pH-sensitive (carbopol), ion-sensitive (gellan gum, deacetylated gellan) — liquid in the bottle, gel in the nose, so residence time rises |
| **Powders** | Better stability, no preservative, longer residence; may irritate |
| **Microspheres and nanoparticles** | Chitosan, starch, alginate: mucoadhesive, protect peptides, prolong contact, open tight junctions |
| **Liposomes, niosomes, nanoemulsions, microemulsions** | Protect the drug and improve permeation; used in nose-to-brain research |
| **Inserts and bioadhesive films** | Prolonged local contact |

**Excipients**: buffers (pH about 4.5–6.5), tonicity adjusters (sodium chloride, dextrose; isotonic or slightly hypertonic is preferred), **permeation enhancers** (chitosan, bile salts, fatty acids, cyclodextrins, phospholipids, surfactants — ciliotoxicity must be checked), **mucoadhesives** (chitosan, carbopol, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, sodium hyaluronate), viscosity modifiers, enzyme inhibitors (bestatin, aprotinin), preservatives (benzalkonium chloride, phenylethyl alcohol — ciliotoxicity is a concern, hence preservative-free multidose devices) and antioxidants.

### c. Preparation

Nasal solutions and suspensions are prepared like other sterile or low-bioburden aqueous products: the drug and excipients are dissolved in water for injection, the pH, tonicity and viscosity are adjusted, the solution is filtered (0.22 µm) and filled into the spray container under controlled conditions; suspensions need micronised drug and a wetting agent. Gels are prepared by hydrating the polymer at low temperature. Microspheres and nanoparticles are prepared by the methods of Units 2 and 3 (spray drying, ionic gelation, emulsion solvent evaporation) and then dispersed in the vehicle or filled as a powder. Containers: amber glass or plastic bottles with a metering spray pump and actuator, unit-dose or bi-dose devices for emergency and nose-to-brain products, and preservative-free multidose systems with a filter or a one-way valve.

### d. Evaluation of nasal products

| Test | Method / purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Appearance, pH, osmolality, viscosity, surface tension | Routine physical quality |
| Drug content, content uniformity, impurities | HPLC |
| **Spray pattern and plume geometry** | Impaction and imaging at set distances; shows how the dose spreads in the cavity |
| **Droplet size distribution** | Laser diffraction; droplets of about 10–50 µm stay in the nose; the fraction below 10 µm must be low to avoid lung deposition |
| Shot weight and dose per actuation; uniformity through the container | Gravimetric and assay, at beginning, middle and end |
| Gelation temperature / gelling capacity | For in-situ gels |
| **Mucoadhesive strength and nasal residence** | Texture analyser, wash-off test; gamma scintigraphy or fluorescence in vivo |
| **Ex-vivo permeation** | Sheep, goat or bovine nasal mucosa in a Franz diffusion cell; flux and permeability coefficient |
| **Ciliotoxicity / ciliary beat frequency** | Frog palate or excised mucosa; essential when an enhancer is used |
| **Histopathology of the mucosa** | After repeated dosing |
| Microbial limits, sterility (if claimed), preservative efficacy | Pharmacopoeial |
| In-vivo | Pharmacokinetics; for brain targeting the drug targeting efficiency and direct transport percentage are calculated from the brain and plasma AUC after nasal and intravenous dosing |

**Marketed and studied uses**: decongestants and topical corticosteroids for rhinitis; antihistamines; sumatriptan and other migraine treatments; desmopressin and calcitonin (peptides); hormone and vitamin B12 products; emergency products for opioid overdose and for seizure clusters; live attenuated influenza vaccine; and intranasal insulin and neuropeptides as nose-to-brain research areas.

## Remember

- Respirable (fine particle) size is **1–5 µm**; above 5 µm impaction in the throat, below 0.5 µm is exhaled. MMAD = d_geo√(ρ/χ), which is why large porous particles work.
- Aerosol components: propellant, container, valve (metering valve for inhalers, 25–100 µL), actuator, product concentrate. Systems: two-phase solution, three-phase suspension or emulsion (foam), barrier.
- Propellants: CFCs (11, 12, 114) withdrawn under the Montreal Protocol → **HFA-134a and HFA-227ea** for inhalers; hydrocarbons and compressed gases for topical products.
- Devices: pMDI (needs coordination; use a spacer), DPI (lactose carrier, patient's own inspiratory effort), nebulisers (jet, ultrasonic, vibrating mesh), soft-mist inhaler.
- Filling: cold filling or, preferably, **pressure filling**; then crimping, leak testing in a 50–55 °C water bath, and spray testing.
- Key inhaler test = **cascade impactor** (Andersen or NGI) giving MMAD, GSD and fine particle fraction; plus delivered dose uniformity.
- Nasal cavity about 150 cm²: vestibule (filter), respiratory region (systemic absorption), olfactory region 3–10 cm² (nose-to-brain). Volume about 25–200 µL, mucociliary clearance about 15–20 min, surface pH about 5.5–6.5.
- Nasal strategies: mucoadhesives (chitosan, carbopol), in-situ gels (poloxamer 407, gellan gum), permeation enhancers, enzyme inhibitors, micro- and nanoparticles.
- Nasal evaluation extras: spray pattern, droplet size (keep the fraction below 10 µm low), gelation temperature, ex-vivo permeation through excised mucosa, and ciliotoxicity.
