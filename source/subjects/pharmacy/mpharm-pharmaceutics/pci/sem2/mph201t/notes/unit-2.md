# Unit 2: Targeting methods, nanoparticles and liposomes — types, preparation and evaluation

Unit 1 explained *why* a carrier goes where it goes. This unit is about the two carriers the syllabus names — **nanoparticles** and **liposomes** — and about the **methods** by which any carrier is aimed at a target. Expect the formulation, the method of preparation and the evaluation table to carry most of the marks.

## 2.1 Targeting methods — an organised list

| Method | Principle | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive | Natural fate of the carrier plus pathology of the tissue (MPS uptake, EPR, capillary blockade) | Liposomal amphotericin B to liver and spleen; PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin to tumours |
| Active, ligand-mediated | Ligand–receptor recognition on the target cell | Folate, transferrin, galactose, mannose, RGD, hyaluronic acid, aptamers |
| Active, antibody-mediated | Antigen–antibody binding | Immunoliposomes, antibody-coated nanoparticles, antibody–drug conjugates |
| Physical / stimulus-responsive | External or local trigger | Magnetic, thermosensitive, pH-sensitive, redox-sensitive, ultrasound- and light-triggered |
| Inverse | Suppress or saturate the MPS first | Pre-dose of blank carrier or dextran sulfate |
| Dual / double / combination | Carrier adds its own effect; or space + time control; or two ligands | Antiviral carrier with antiviral drug; transferrin + cell-penetrating peptide |
| Cell-mediated | Living cells as carriers | Resealed erythrocytes, macrophages, stem cells |
| Prodrug-based | Site-specific activation | ADEPT, GDEPT, colon-targeted azo and polysaccharide prodrugs |

### Ligand coupling chemistry (postgraduate detail)

A ligand can be attached to a carrier in four ways:

1. **Physical adsorption** — simple but reversible and poorly controlled.
2. **Direct covalent coupling to the carrier surface** — carbodiimide (EDC/NHS) chemistry between surface –COOH and ligand –NH2; glutaraldehyde; maleimide–thiol coupling to cysteine residues.
3. **Coupling to the distal end of a PEG chain** (the preferred method) — the ligand stands above the steric layer and remains accessible. Functional PEG derivatives used: maleimide-PEG-DSPE, NHS-PEG-DSPE, and PEG with carboxyl, amine, thiol or azide ends.
4. **Non-covalent affinity coupling** — biotin–avidin/streptavidin bridges; also "click" chemistry (azide–alkyne) and post-insertion of ligand-PEG-lipid micelles into preformed liposomes.

Points to add in an answer: the reaction must not denature the ligand; for an antibody, coupling through the Fc region or through carbohydrate residues keeps the antigen-binding sites free; ligand density matters — too few ligands give weak binding, too many increase immune clearance and can reduce tumour penetration.

## 2.2 Nanoparticles

**Nanoparticles** are solid colloidal carriers of roughly 10–1000 nm (most injectable systems are below about 300 nm) in which the drug is dissolved, entrapped, encapsulated, adsorbed or covalently attached.

### a. Types

| Type | Structure and note |
| --- | --- |
| Nanospheres | Matrix type: drug dispersed throughout the polymer |
| Nanocapsules | Reservoir type: liquid (oily or aqueous) core inside a polymer shell |
| Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN) | Solid lipid core (glyceryl behenate, stearic acid, tripalmitin) stabilised by surfactant; aqueous dispersion, no organic solvent needed |
| Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLC) | Blend of solid and liquid lipid, giving an imperfect crystal lattice, higher loading and less drug expulsion on storage than SLN |
| Polymeric micelles | Self-assembled core–shell structure from amphiphilic block copolymers (PEG-PLA, PEG-PCL, Pluronics), about 10–100 nm, for poorly soluble drugs |
| Dendrimers | Monodisperse, regularly branched polymers built generation by generation around a core (PAMAM, PPI, polylysine); drug solubilised in internal cavities or conjugated to surface groups; high surface functionality but cationic generations are haemolytic and cytotoxic, which is reduced by PEGylation or surface acetylation |
| Nanocrystals | Pure drug nanocrystals stabilised by surfactant; raise saturation solubility and dissolution rate (Ostwald–Freundlich and Noyes–Whitney) |
| Inorganic | Gold, silica (including mesoporous silica), iron oxide (magnetic, also an MRI contrast agent), quantum dots, carbon nanotubes |
| Protein and polysaccharide based | Albumin, gelatin, chitosan, alginate nanoparticles |

### b. Polymers and excipients

- **Synthetic biodegradable**: poly(lactic acid) (PLA), poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA — degradation rate set by the lactide:glycolide ratio and molecular weight), polycaprolactone (slow), poly(alkyl cyanoacrylates) (fast, surface erosion), polyanhydrides, polyorthoesters.
- **Natural**: chitosan (cationic, mucoadhesive, permeation enhancing), alginate, gelatin, albumin, dextran, hyaluronic acid.
- **Stabilisers and surfactants**: poloxamers, polysorbates, polyvinyl alcohol, lecithin, sodium cholate, TPGS.
- **Cryoprotectants** for freeze-drying: mannitol, trehalose, sucrose, lactose.

### c. Methods of preparation

**From preformed polymers**

1. **Emulsification–solvent evaporation.** Polymer and drug are dissolved in a volatile water-immiscible solvent (dichloromethane, ethyl acetate), emulsified into an aqueous surfactant solution by high-speed homogenisation or sonication, and the solvent is evaporated under reduced pressure so that the polymer hardens into nanospheres. For water-soluble drugs a **double emulsion (w/o/w)** is used. Size is controlled by stirring/sonication energy, surfactant type and concentration, polymer concentration and phase-volume ratio.
2. **Nanoprecipitation (solvent displacement, Fessi method).** Polymer and drug in a water-miscible solvent (acetone, ethanol) are added dropwise to an aqueous phase containing a stabiliser. Rapid diffusion of the solvent lowers the polymer's solubility and it precipitates as nanoparticles (Marangoni effect). Simple, reproducible, gives small particles; suited to lipophilic drugs.
3. **Emulsification–solvent diffusion.** A partially water-miscible solvent (benzyl alcohol, propylene carbonate) saturated with water is used; dilution with a large volume of water makes the solvent diffuse out and the particles form.
4. **Salting-out.** Acetone solution of polymer is emulsified into an aqueous solution saturated with an electrolyte (magnesium acetate) or sucrose, which prevents mixing; dilution with water then drives acetone out. Avoids chlorinated solvents.
5. **Supercritical fluid methods** (RESS, SAS) — supercritical CO2 as solvent or antisolvent; solvent-free and attractive for heat- and solvent-sensitive drugs.
6. **Spray drying** and **nano spray drying**.

**By polymerisation of monomers**

7. **Emulsion polymerisation** of alkyl cyanoacrylates in an acidic aqueous medium (anionic polymerisation initiated by OH⁻); fast and done in water.
8. **Interfacial polymerisation** for nanocapsules.

**For lipid and polysaccharide systems**

9. **High-pressure homogenisation** for SLN/NLC — *hot* homogenisation (lipid melt emulsified above its melting point, then cooled) or *cold* homogenisation (solidified lipid milled and homogenised in cold surfactant solution; better for heat-labile and hydrophilic drugs).
10. **Microemulsion dilution**, **solvent emulsification–evaporation**, **double emulsion** and **membrane contactor** methods for SLN.
11. **Ionic gelation / polyelectrolyte complexation** — chitosan cross-linked with sodium tripolyphosphate, or chitosan–alginate complexes; mild and aqueous, ideal for proteins and nucleic acids.
12. **Desolvation / coacervation** for albumin and gelatin nanoparticles, followed by cross-linking (glutaraldehyde or glutamine transaminase).
13. **Self-assembly** of block copolymers (direct dissolution, dialysis, film hydration) for micelles.
14. **Media milling and high-pressure homogenisation** for nanocrystals.

### d. Evaluation of nanoparticles

| Parameter | Method | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) | Dynamic light scattering / photon correlation spectroscopy; laser diffraction for larger systems | PDI below about 0.3 means a narrow distribution |
| Zeta potential | Laser Doppler electrophoresis | Beyond about ±30 mV usually means good electrostatic stability |
| Morphology | SEM, TEM, cryo-TEM, atomic force microscopy | Confirms shape, surface, nanocapsule wall |
| Drug loading and entrapment efficiency | Separate free drug by ultracentrifugation, ultrafiltration, dialysis or gel filtration, then assay | Loading % = drug in particles / total particle weight; EE % = entrapped drug / total drug added |
| Physical state of drug and lipid | DSC, powder X-ray diffraction | Shows amorphous drug, polymorphic transitions of SLN |
| Surface chemistry | FTIR, XPS, NMR | Confirms conjugation of ligand or PEG |
| In-vitro release | Dialysis bag, reverse dialysis, sample-and-separate, Franz cell; fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas models | Burst release is common |
| Residual solvent and monomer | Gas chromatography | ICH Q3C limits |
| Stability | Size, PDI, zeta, drug content and release on storage; freeze-drying with cryoprotectant | Aggregation and drug expulsion are the usual failures |
| Sterility, endotoxin, osmolarity, pH | Pharmacopoeial tests | Parenteral products |
| Biological | Cytotoxicity (MTT), haemolysis, cell uptake (flow cytometry, confocal microscopy), protein corona, pharmacokinetics and biodistribution | |

### e. Advantages, limitations and applications

**Advantages**: pass through the smallest capillaries and are taken up by cells; raise the solubility, dissolution rate and oral bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs; protect labile drugs; give sustained or triggered release; can carry a targeting ligand; usable by injection, by mouth, by the ocular, nasal and pulmonary routes.
**Limitations**: small size and large surface give aggregation and burst release; drug loading is often low; some materials are toxic or poorly cleared; dose dumping and residual solvents; costly aseptic manufacture; difficult scale-up and regulatory characterisation.
**Applications**: cancer chemotherapy; delivery to the brain; antimicrobial and antitubercular therapy; vaccines and adjuvants; gene and siRNA delivery (lipid nanoparticles); ocular and pulmonary delivery; diagnostics and theranostics.

## 2.3 Liposomes

**Liposomes** are vesicles in which one or more concentric phospholipid **bilayers** enclose aqueous compartments. They were described by Alec Bangham in the 1960s. Water-soluble drug sits in the aqueous core, lipid-soluble drug within the bilayer, and amphiphilic drug at the interface.

```text
                 Unilamellar vesicle
            o o o o o o o o o o o o
        o                             o        o   = polar head group
      o    ─────────────────────────    o      ─   = hydrocarbon chains
     o    (  aqueous core: hydrophilic   )  o
     o    (  drug                        )  o      lipophilic drug lies
      o    ─────────────────────────    o          inside the bilayer
        o                             o
            o o o o o o o o o o o o
```

### a. Components

| Component | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phospholipid | Forms the bilayer | Phosphatidylcholine (egg or soy lecithin), hydrogenated soy PC, DPPC, DSPC, DOPE, phosphatidylglycerol, sphingomyelin |
| Cholesterol (up to about 30–50 mol%) | Fills gaps between chains, raises packing order, reduces permeability and serum-induced leakage, abolishes the sharp phase transition | Cholesterol |
| Charge inducer | Prevents aggregation; cationic lipids bind nucleic acids | Stearylamine, DOTAP, DOTMA (+); dicetyl phosphate, phosphatidylserine (−) |
| Steric stabiliser | Long circulation | PEG2000-DSPE |
| Antioxidant / chelator | Protects unsaturated lipids | α-tocopherol, EDTA |

Two physical properties govern behaviour: the **phase transition temperature (Tc)** — below Tc the bilayer is a rigid gel, above it is liquid-crystalline and leaky (DPPC about 41 °C, DSPC about 55 °C, egg PC below 0 °C) — and the **packing parameter** of the lipid, which decides whether bilayers or micelles form.

### b. Classification

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Size and lamellarity | Small unilamellar vesicles (SUV, about 20–100 nm), large unilamellar vesicles (LUV, above about 100 nm), giant unilamellar vesicles (above 1 µm), multilamellar vesicles (MLV, about 0.5–5 µm), oligolamellar and multivesicular vesicles |
| Composition / function | Conventional; long-circulating (stealth, PEGylated); immunoliposomes (antibody or Fab on the surface); cationic (gene delivery); pH-sensitive (DOPE + cholesteryl hemisuccinate or oleic acid); thermosensitive (DPPC-rich, release near 41–43 °C); magnetic; archaeosomes (archaebacterial lipids); virosomes (viral envelope proteins); transfersomes and ethosomes (ultradeformable, for skin); bubble liposomes for ultrasound |
| Preparation method | MLV by hand-shaking, REV, FATMLV, DRV, proliposomes |

### c. Methods of preparation

**1. Passive loading — mechanical dispersion**

- **Thin-film hydration (Bangham method)**: lipids and lipophilic drug are dissolved in chloroform–methanol in a round-bottom flask; the solvent is removed on a rotary evaporator under reduced pressure to leave a thin dry film; traces of solvent are removed under vacuum; the film is hydrated above Tc with the aqueous drug solution, with gentle shaking or rotation, giving heterogeneous MLVs. Annealing above Tc improves uniformity.
- **Sonication** (bath or probe) of MLVs gives SUVs; probe sonication risks metal shedding, lipid degradation and local heating.
- **Extrusion** through stacked polycarbonate membranes of defined pore size (400 → 200 → 100 nm) above Tc gives uniform LUVs with low PDI — the standard laboratory and pilot-scale sizing method.
- **French pressure cell**: MLVs forced through a narrow orifice at high pressure; gentle, good for labile material.
- **Microfluidisation** and **high-pressure homogenisation**: scalable, continuous.
- **Freeze–thaw cycling** and **dehydration–rehydration (DRV)**: raise aqueous entrapment considerably.

**2. Solvent dispersion**

- **Ethanol injection**: lipid–ethanol solution injected rapidly through a fine needle into the aqueous phase gives SUVs; simple, but dilute and ethanol must be removed.
- **Ether injection**: lipids in ether injected slowly into warm aqueous phase above the ether boiling point; ether evaporates and LUVs form; higher entrapment, slow.
- **Reverse-phase evaporation (REV)**: aqueous drug solution is emulsified (w/o) into lipid dissolved in diethyl ether or isopropyl ether; the organic solvent is removed under reduced pressure, the system passes through a viscous gel, and the gel collapses into large unilamellar vesicles. Gives high aqueous entrapment (often quoted as about 50%), so it suits expensive, water-soluble actives; drug is exposed to organic solvent and sonication.
- **Double emulsion** and **microfluidic** methods for precise control.

**3. Detergent removal**: lipids are solubilised as mixed micelles with a detergent (sodium cholate, octyl glucoside, Triton X-100); the detergent is removed by dialysis, gel filtration (Sephadex), adsorption on polystyrene beads or dilution, and bilayers close into homogeneous vesicles. Gentle; slow; residual detergent must be tested for.

**4. Active (remote) loading — the industrially important method.** A transmembrane gradient is created, and an amphipathic weak base or acid is added to preformed empty liposomes; the neutral form crosses the bilayer and is trapped inside in its ionised or precipitated form. Methods: **pH gradient** (citrate buffer pH 4 inside, pH 7.4 outside), **ammonium sulfate gradient** (used for doxorubicin, which precipitates inside as a gel-like sulfate salt), **calcium acetate gradient** for weak acids, and ionophore-based (A23187) metal-ion gradients. Entrapment efficiencies above 90% and good retention are achieved — this is how liposomal anthracyclines are made.

**5. Proliposomes**: a dry, free-flowing powder of lipid and drug coated on a soluble carrier (sorbitol, sodium chloride), which forms liposomes on adding water; solves the stability problem of aqueous dispersions.

**Processing after preparation**: separation of free drug (gel filtration, dialysis, ultracentrifugation, ion-exchange), sizing (extrusion, homogenisation), sterilisation (filtration through 0.22 µm is preferred; autoclaving and irradiation damage lipids), and freeze-drying with a cryoprotectant such as trehalose or sucrose.

### d. Evaluation of liposomes

| Parameter | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Vesicle size, distribution | DLS, laser diffraction, electron microscopy, field-flow fractionation |
| Lamellarity | ³¹P-NMR with a shift reagent, freeze-fracture electron microscopy, small-angle X-ray scattering |
| Shape and surface | TEM, cryo-TEM, SEM, AFM |
| Surface charge | Zeta potential |
| Phospholipid and cholesterol content | Stewart assay / phosphate assay (Bartlett), HPLC, enzymatic cholesterol assay |
| Entrapment efficiency and drug content | Separate free drug (minicolumn centrifugation, dialysis, ultrafiltration), lyse vesicles with Triton X-100 or methanol, assay by HPLC or UV |
| Phase transition temperature and fluidity | DSC; fluorescence anisotropy with DPH |
| In-vitro release and leakage | Dialysis in buffer and in serum; calcein or carboxyfluorescein dequenching |
| Chemical stability | Lipid hydrolysis (lyso-PC by HPLC/TLC) and oxidation (conjugated dienes, malondialdehyde, peroxide value) |
| Physical stability | Size, zeta, drug retention and appearance on storage; aggregation and fusion |
| Sterility, pyrogens, osmolarity, pH | Pharmacopoeial tests |
| In-vivo | Pharmacokinetics, biodistribution (radiolabel or fluorescent label), efficacy and toxicity |

### e. Advantages, limitations and applications

**Advantages**: materials are biocompatible, biodegradable and non-immunogenic; both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs can be carried; drug is protected; toxicity to healthy tissue is lowered; surface can be modified for long circulation or active targeting; several products are approved, so the regulatory route is established.
**Limitations**: phospholipids hydrolyse and oxidise, giving short shelf life; drug leakage and vesicle fusion on storage; low entrapment of some water-soluble drugs by passive methods; rapid MPS clearance of conventional vesicles; high cost of pure lipids; difficult sterilisation and scale-up; batch-to-batch variability.

**Applications**: anticancer therapy (PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin for Kaposi sarcoma, ovarian and breast cancer; liposomal daunorubicin; liposomal irinotecan; liposomal cytarabine); systemic fungal infection (liposomal amphotericin B, far less nephrotoxic than the deoxycholate form); vaccines and adjuvants, including virosomes and lipid-nanoparticle mRNA vaccines; gene and siRNA delivery with cationic and ionisable lipids; topical, ocular and pulmonary delivery; and diagnostic imaging.

## Remember

- Ligand coupling: EDC/NHS to –COOH, maleimide to –SH, biotin–avidin, click chemistry, post-insertion of ligand-PEG-DSPE; couple at the distal end of PEG so the ligand is accessible.
- Nanoparticle methods: solvent evaporation, nanoprecipitation, solvent diffusion, salting-out, emulsion polymerisation, ionic gelation, hot/cold high-pressure homogenisation (SLN, NLC), supercritical fluids.
- Nanoparticle evaluation: size and PDI by DLS, charge by zeta potential, shape by TEM/AFM, entrapment after separating free drug, physical state by DSC and XRD, release in a dialysis bag with Higuchi and Korsmeyer–Peppas fitting.
- Liposome structure: phospholipid + cholesterol bilayer; SUV 20–100 nm, LUV above 100 nm, MLV 0.5–5 µm; Tc of DPPC about 41 °C.
- Liposome preparation: thin-film hydration → sonication/extrusion; ethanol and ether injection; reverse-phase evaporation (high aqueous entrapment); detergent removal; **active loading** by pH or ammonium sulfate gradient (how liposomal doxorubicin is made); proliposomes for stability.
- Liposome evaluation extras over nanoparticles: lamellarity by ³¹P-NMR, phospholipid assay (Stewart/Bartlett), lyso-PC as a marker of hydrolysis, calcein dequenching for leakage, sterilise by 0.22 µm filtration.
