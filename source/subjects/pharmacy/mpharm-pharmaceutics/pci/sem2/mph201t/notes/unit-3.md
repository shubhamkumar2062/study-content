# Unit 3: Microcapsules and microspheres, monoclonal antibodies, niosomes, aquasomes, phytosomes and electrosomes

This unit collects the micrometre-sized carriers, the most specific homing device in medicine (the monoclonal antibody), and four "-some" systems built on surfactants, ceramic cores, phospholipid complexes and electrically treated vesicles. Questions are usually "types, preparation and evaluation" or "compare X with liposomes".

## 3.1 Microcapsules and microspheres

**Microencapsulation** is the process of enclosing solids, liquids or gases in a thin polymeric coat to give particles of roughly 1–1000 µm.

| Term | Structure |
| --- | --- |
| **Microcapsule** | Reservoir type — a distinct core surrounded by a continuous wall |
| **Microsphere** | Matrix type — drug dispersed molecularly or as crystals through the polymer |
| Nanocapsule / nanosphere | The same two structures below 1 µm |

**Reasons for microencapsulation**: sustained or controlled release; taste and odour masking (chloramphenicol palmitate, paracetamol); protection from moisture, light and oxygen (vitamin A, ferrous sulfate); separation of incompatible materials in one dosage form; converting liquids and oils into free-flowing powders; reducing gastric irritation (aspirin, potassium chloride, indomethacin); improving flow and compressibility; safe handling of irritant or hygroscopic solids; and targeting.

### a. Types of microspheres

| Type | Material and purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Bio-degradable polymeric | PLGA, PLA, polycaprolactone — depot injections |
| Natural polymeric | Albumin, gelatin, chitosan, starch, alginate |
| Synthetic non-biodegradable | Polymethyl methacrylate, ethyl cellulose (oral) |
| **Magnetic** | Magnetite (Fe3O4) in albumin, chitosan or PLGA; held at the site by an external magnet |
| **Floating (hydrodynamically balanced)** | Low density, remain buoyant in gastric fluid, prolong gastric residence |
| **Radioactive** | Yttrium-90 or other isotope in resin or glass spheres, 20–30 µm, lodged in tumour capillaries for internal radiotherapy |
| **Bioadhesive / mucoadhesive** | Chitosan, carbopol, sodium CMC — adhere to nasal, buccal, ocular or GI mucosa |
| Polymeric drug–antibody (immuno-microspheres) | Antibody on the surface for active targeting |

### b. Methods of preparation

**1. Physico-chemical methods**

- **Coacervation–phase separation**, in three steps: (i) formation of three immiscible phases — vehicle, core material and coating polymer; (ii) deposition of the coating around the core; (iii) rigidisation of the coat by heat, cross-linking or desolvation.
  - *Simple coacervation*: one polymer, desolvated by a non-solvent (sodium sulfate or alcohol added to gelatin).
  - *Complex coacervation*: two oppositely charged polymers — classically gelatin (positive below its isoelectric point, about pH 4) and acacia (negative) — which attract and separate as a coacervate; cross-linked with glutaraldehyde or formaldehyde.
  - *Organic phase separation*: ethyl cellulose in cyclohexane with temperature change, or polymer–polymer incompatibility (adding polyisobutylene).
- **Solvent evaporation / extraction from emulsion**: o/w for lipophilic drugs; w/o/w double emulsion for peptides and proteins; o/o (non-aqueous) for very water-soluble drugs.
- **Ionotropic gelation**: sodium alginate droplets dropped into calcium chloride solution form calcium alginate beads; mild, entirely aqueous, excellent for cells, proteins and probiotics.
- **Emulsion cross-linking**: aqueous gelatin, albumin or chitosan emulsified in oil, then hardened by heat (thermal denaturation) or a chemical cross-linker.
- **Polymerisation** (suspension, emulsion, interfacial) and **solvent-change / salting-out** methods.

**2. Mechanical / physical methods**

- **Spray drying**: drug and polymer solution or dispersion is atomised into hot air; the solvent flashes off and microspheres are collected in a cyclone. One step, continuous, scalable; the usual industrial choice. Yield losses and heat exposure are the drawbacks.
- **Spray congealing**: molten wax or lipid carrying the drug is atomised into cold air and solidifies.
- **Air-suspension (Wurster) coating**: particles are fluidised and coating solution is sprayed from the bottom; gives very uniform films on particles above about 50 µm.
- **Pan coating** for larger particles; **multi-orifice centrifugal** (extrusion through a rotating disc); **electrostatic** methods.
- **Supercritical fluid** and **membrane emulsification** methods for narrow size distribution.

### c. Release mechanisms

Diffusion through the polymer wall or matrix (Higuchi kinetics for a matrix); erosion or degradation of the polymer (PLGA by bulk hydrolysis, typically biphasic with a burst, a lag and then an erosion-controlled phase); osmotic pressure causing cracks in the wall; and swelling-controlled release from hydrogel spheres. Release data are fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi (Q = k√t) and Korsmeyer–Peppas (Mt/M∞ = kt^n) models; for spheres n ≤ 0.43 indicates Fickian diffusion and n = 0.85 case-II (erosion) transport.

### d. Evaluation of microspheres

| Parameter | Method |
| --- | --- |
| Particle size and distribution | Optical microscopy, sieve analysis, laser diffraction, Coulter counter |
| Shape and surface | SEM (surface, pores, wall thickness), TEM |
| Flow properties | Angle of repose, bulk and tapped density, Carr's index, Hausner ratio |
| Drug content, loading and entrapment efficiency | Dissolve or digest the spheres, assay by UV or HPLC |
| Drug–polymer interaction and physical state | FTIR, DSC, powder X-ray diffraction |
| Swelling index and density | Weight gain in buffer; density by pycnometry (floating systems need density below 1) |
| In-vitro release | USP dissolution apparatus, dialysis bag, flow-through cell; model fitting |
| Buoyancy (floating) | Floating lag time and total floating time in 0.1 N HCl |
| Mucoadhesion | In-vitro wash-off test on intestinal mucosa; tensile strength |
| Magnetic systems | Magnetite content, magnetic responsiveness in a field |
| Residual solvent, cross-linker and sterility | GC; tests for free glutaraldehyde; pharmacopoeial sterility |
| Stability | Size, drug content and release after storage at controlled conditions |

**Applications**: long-acting intramuscular and subcutaneous depots of peptides (PLGA microspheres for luteinising-hormone-releasing-hormone analogues and octreotide, releasing over weeks to months), vaccine single-shot delivery, chemoembolisation and radioembolisation of liver tumours, oral sustained release, nasal and pulmonary delivery, and cell encapsulation.

## 3.2 Monoclonal antibodies

**Antibodies (immunoglobulins)** are Y-shaped glycoproteins of two heavy and two light chains, with two antigen-binding fragments (Fab) carrying the hypervariable complementarity-determining regions, and a constant Fc region responsible for effector functions and for the long half-life through FcRn recycling. **Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)** come from a single B-cell clone and recognise one epitope; **polyclonal** antisera contain many clones against many epitopes.

### a. Preparation — hybridoma technology (Köhler and Milstein, 1975)

```text
  Mouse immunised with antigen (several boosters)
        ↓
  Spleen removed → B lymphocytes (make antibody, die in culture)
        +
  Myeloma cells (immortal, HGPRT-deficient, make no antibody)
        ↓  fusion with polyethylene glycol (or electrofusion)
  Mixture of cells
        ↓  grown in HAT medium
  Hypoxanthine–aminopterin–thymidine selection:
    aminopterin blocks the de-novo nucleotide pathway;
    unfused myeloma cells lack HGPRT and cannot use the salvage pathway → die
    unfused B cells die naturally in a few days
    only HYBRIDOMAS (immortality from myeloma + HGPRT from B cell) survive
        ↓
  Screening of supernatants (ELISA, RIA) for the wanted specificity
        ↓
  Cloning by limiting dilution (one cell per well) → monoclonality
        ↓
  Expansion in culture flasks, roller bottles or bioreactors
        ↓
  Purification: ammonium sulfate precipitation → Protein A/G affinity
  chromatography → ion-exchange and size-exclusion polishing →
  viral clearance and sterile filtration
```

Modern alternatives: **recombinant and phage-display libraries** (fully human antibodies selected in vitro), **transgenic mice carrying human immunoglobulin genes**, **single-B-cell cloning**, and expression in CHO or NS0 cell lines.

### b. Types by origin

| Type | Composition | Suffix | Immunogenicity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Murine | 100% mouse | -omab | High (human anti-mouse antibody response) |
| Chimeric | Mouse variable + human constant (about 65% human) | -ximab | Moderate |
| Humanised | Only the CDRs are mouse (about 95% human) | -zumab | Low |
| Fully human | Entirely human sequence | -umab | Lowest |

**Fragments and engineered formats**: Fab, F(ab′)2, single-chain variable fragment (scFv), diabodies, nanobodies (single-domain), and bispecific antibodies that bind a tumour antigen with one arm and a T cell (CD3) with the other.

### c. Applications in targeting and therapy

1. **Naked therapeutic antibodies** — block a receptor or ligand, or recruit antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity and complement: trastuzumab (HER2), rituximab (CD20), cetuximab (EGFR), bevacizumab (VEGF), and anti-TNF-α agents in autoimmune disease.
2. **Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs)** — a highly potent cytotoxic payload joined by a linker, released only after internalisation: trastuzumab emtansine, brentuximab vedotin, gemtuzumab ozogamicin. Design variables are the linker (cleavable hydrazone, disulfide or peptide; non-cleavable thioether), the conjugation site, and the drug-to-antibody ratio (usually about 2–4), which controls both potency and aggregation.
3. **Immunoliposomes and antibody-coated nanoparticles** — the antibody guides the whole carrier.
4. **Radioimmunoconjugates** for imaging and radioimmunotherapy.
5. **Immunotoxins** — antibody linked to a protein toxin.
6. **ADEPT** — antibody-directed enzyme prodrug therapy: the antibody–enzyme conjugate localises first, the prodrug is given later and is converted to the active drug only at the tumour.
7. **Diagnostics** — ELISA, RIA, immunohistochemistry, lateral-flow pregnancy tests, blood grouping, imaging.

**Advantages**: unmatched specificity, long half-life, many possible payloads, and defined mechanisms.
**Limitations**: large size (about 150 kDa) limits penetration into solid tumours and prevents oral use; immunogenicity; antigen heterogeneity and antigen loss cause resistance; cold-chain storage; very high cost; aggregation and other stability problems of protein formulations.

## 3.3 Niosomes

**Niosomes** are bilayer vesicles formed by hydrating **non-ionic surfactants**, usually with cholesterol and a charge inducer. Structurally they resemble liposomes but are chemically more stable and cheaper.

**Components**: sorbitan esters (Span 20, 40, 60, 80), polysorbates (Tween 20, 60, 80), polyoxyethylene alkyl ethers (Brij), alkyl glycerol ethers; **cholesterol** to give rigidity and stop leakage; **dicetyl phosphate or stearylamine** to prevent aggregation.
Surfactants with HLB roughly 4–8 form vesicles best; those of high HLB need more cholesterol. The **critical packing parameter** (CPP = v/a·lc) predicts the shape: CPP between 0.5 and 1 favours bilayer vesicles.

**Preparation**: thin-film hydration; ether injection; **reverse-phase evaporation**; **sonication**; **micro-fluidisation**; the **"bubble" method** (surfactant–cholesterol dispersion bubbled with nitrogen at about 70 °C); **transmembrane pH-gradient (remote) loading**; and the **proniosome** approach — surfactant coated on a water-soluble carrier (sorbitol, maltodextrin) as a dry, free-flowing powder that forms niosomes on adding warm water, which removes the stability problems of an aqueous dispersion.

**Evaluation**: the same list as liposomes — size and PDI, zeta potential, morphology by TEM, entrapment after separating free drug, bilayer rigidity by fluorescence probes, in-vitro release, osmotic shock and storage stability.

| Feature | Liposomes | Niosomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bilayer former | Phospholipids | Non-ionic surfactants |
| Chemical stability | Lower (oxidation, hydrolysis to lyso-PC) | Higher — no ester-linked unsaturated fatty acids to oxidise |
| Raw material purity and cost | Natural phospholipids vary; expensive | Synthetic, uniform, cheap |
| Storage | Often under nitrogen, refrigerated | Easier; proniosomes are dry |
| Status | Several approved products | Mainly cosmetics, topical and research |

**Applications**: topical and transdermal delivery, cosmetics, anticancer and antitubercular delivery under investigation, leishmaniasis (macrophage uptake), ocular delivery, oral peptide delivery and vaccine adjuvants.

## 3.4 Aquasomes

**Aquasomes** are three-layered, self-assembled, water-like particles of roughly 60–300 nm, described as "bodies of water" because a surface water layer preserves the conformation of the adsorbed biomolecule.

```text
   [ 1 ] Ceramic / polymer CORE        — nanocrystalline calcium phosphate
            (hydroxyapatite), ceramic diamond, tin oxide, brushite
                    ↓ coated with
   [ 2 ] Polyhydroxy oligomeric FILM   — cellobiose, trehalose, sucrose,
            pyridoxal-5-phosphate, citrate
                    ↓ adsorption
   [ 3 ] Therapeutic agent (DRUG)      — protein, peptide, enzyme, antigen,
            insulin, haemoglobin
```

**Principle**: the sugar coat replaces the hydration shell of the protein, so the protein keeps its native three-dimensional structure and activity — "molecular shielding". Binding of the drug is non-covalent (ionic, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals), so the drug is released in its active form.

**Preparation (three stages)**

1. **Core formation** — nanocrystalline hydroxyapatite by co-precipitation from calcium chloride and disodium hydrogen phosphate solutions with pH control, or by inverted-micelle, sonication or plasma condensation methods; the core is sonicated, filtered and freeze-dried.
2. **Coating** — the core is dispersed in a solution of the polyhydroxy oligomer, stirred and sonicated so that the sugar adsorbs; unadsorbed sugar is removed by centrifugation and the product is lyophilised.
3. **Drug loading** — the coated cores are incubated in a cold buffered solution of the drug at a suitable pH; the drug adsorbs on the sugar layer and the product is freeze-dried.

**Evaluation**: core size and crystallinity (XRD, SEM, TEM), sugar coating (FTIR, carbohydrate assay such as the phenol–sulfuric acid or anthrone method), drug loading, in-vitro release, retention of biological activity (for example enzyme assay or immunogenicity of an adsorbed antigen), haemocompatibility and stability.

**Advantages**: protects fragile proteins from denaturation; no chemical modification of the drug; good physical stability as a dry powder; large surface for loading; biodegradable and biocompatible core.
**Limitations**: only drugs that adsorb well can be carried; loading is limited; burst release is possible; scale-up and reproducible core preparation are difficult.
**Applications**: delivery of insulin, haemoglobin (as an oxygen carrier), enzymes such as serratiopeptidase, antigens and vaccine adjuvants, and some antibiotics and anti-inflammatory drugs.

## 3.5 Phytosomes

**Phytosomes** (phospholipid complexes of phytoconstituents) are formed when a standardised plant extract or a polar phytoconstituent — usually a flavonoid or a terpenoid — reacts with phosphatidylcholine, commonly in a 1:1 or 1:2 molar ratio. The polar head of the phospholipid binds the phytoconstituent by hydrogen bonding, while the fatty-acid chains face outward, forming a lipophilic envelope.

**Why they are needed**: many useful phytoconstituents (silymarin, curcumin, quercetin, rutin, green-tea catechins, ginkgo flavonoids, glycyrrhizin, boswellic acids) are large, polar or poorly water-soluble molecules, so they are poorly absorbed from the gut. The complex is lipid-soluble enough to cross the enterocyte membrane, which raises bioavailability several-fold.

**Difference from liposomes**: in a liposome the drug is *dissolved in the aqueous core or dispersed in the bilayer*, with hundreds or thousands of phospholipid molecules per vesicle and no chemical bond; in a phytosome the phytoconstituent is **chemically bonded (by hydrogen bonds) to the polar head of the phospholipid**, giving a defined molecular complex — confirmed by shifts in ¹H- and ¹³C-NMR signals of the phospholipid head group.

**Preparation**: the phytoconstituent and phosphatidylcholine are refluxed in an aprotic solvent (dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, dioxane) at a controlled temperature; the solvent is reduced and the complex precipitated with a non-solvent (n-hexane) and filtered, or the solvent is removed by evaporation, lyophilisation or spray drying.

**Evaluation**: confirmation of complex formation by DSC (disappearance or shift of melting endotherms), FTIR, ¹H/¹³C-NMR and powder X-ray diffraction; solubility in water and n-octanol; particle size and zeta potential of the dispersion; entrapment/complexation efficiency; in-vitro dissolution and ex-vivo permeation; and in-vivo bioavailability and pharmacodynamic studies (for example liver-protective activity for silymarin phytosome).

**Advantages**: markedly better absorption and bioavailability, lower effective dose, protection of the constituent from gut and bacterial degradation, improved skin penetration in cosmetics, and good stability.
**Limitations**: the phytoconstituent must have a suitable group to hydrogen-bond with the phospholipid; limited loading; rapid clearance of the free complex; and standardisation of the extract is essential.

## 3.6 Electrosomes

**Electrosomes** are vesicular carriers prepared or loaded with the help of an **electrical field**. Two senses are used in the literature, and both are acceptable in an answer:

1. **Electroformation of vesicles.** Lipid is deposited as a thin film on conducting electrodes (indium tin oxide glass or platinum wires), the chamber is filled with the aqueous phase, and a low-voltage alternating field (typically a few volts at about 10 Hz) is applied. The field causes the hydrated lipid film to swell and bud off, producing large, mostly unilamellar vesicles of very uniform size. The method is gentle, solvent-free and used especially to make giant unilamellar vesicles for research and for encapsulating cells and macromolecules.
2. **Electroporation-assisted loading.** Preformed vesicles (or cells, as in resealed erythrocytes) are exposed to short high-voltage pulses that create transient aqueous pores in the bilayer; drug, protein or nucleic acid in the surrounding medium diffuses in, and the pores reseal when the field is removed. This gives high loading of large, hydrophilic molecules that cannot be loaded passively or by a pH gradient.

**Evaluation** follows the liposome list — size and lamellarity, zeta potential, entrapment efficiency, retention of activity of the loaded macromolecule, membrane integrity after resealing, and release.

**Advantages**: uniform, largely unilamellar vesicles; no organic solvent or detergent; loading of fragile macromolecules under mild conditions.
**Limitations**: low yield and dilute product; specialised apparatus; ionic strength of the medium is restricted in electroformation; pulse conditions must be optimised or the vesicles lyse.

## Remember

- Microcapsule = reservoir (core + wall); microsphere = matrix. Microencapsulation is used for sustained release, taste masking, protection, separating incompatibles and converting liquids to powders.
- Coacervation has three steps: three-phase formation → deposition of coat → rigidisation. Complex coacervation uses gelatin + acacia; simple coacervation uses a desolvating agent.
- Spray drying is the usual industrial method; ionotropic gelation (alginate + calcium chloride) is the mild aqueous method; PLGA solvent evaporation gives peptide depots.
- Hybridoma: spleen B cell + HGPRT-deficient myeloma, fused with PEG, selected in HAT medium, screened by ELISA, cloned by limiting dilution. Suffixes: -omab murine, -ximab chimeric, -zumab humanised, -umab human.
- ADC design: potent payload, cleavable or non-cleavable linker, drug-to-antibody ratio about 2–4.
- Niosomes: non-ionic surfactant (Span, Tween, Brij) + cholesterol + charge inducer; more chemically stable and cheaper than liposomes; proniosomes are the dry form.
- Aquasomes are three-layered: ceramic core (hydroxyapatite) → polyhydroxy oligomer (cellobiose, trehalose) → adsorbed protein; the sugar layer protects protein conformation.
- Phytosome = phytoconstituent **hydrogen-bonded** to the polar head of phosphatidylcholine (a molecular complex, not a vesicle) — used to raise the bioavailability of flavonoids such as silymarin and curcumin.
- Electrosomes: vesicles formed by an alternating electric field on a lipid-coated electrode, or loaded by electroporation pulses — gentle, solvent-free, good for macromolecules.
