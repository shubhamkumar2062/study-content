# How to use this guide

Advanced Biopharmaceutics and Pharmacokinetics (MPH202T) is a numerical, concept-heavy postgraduate paper. Study one unit at a time: read the notes, redo every worked example with a calculator without looking, draw each graph once from memory (hysteresis loop, two-compartment curve, Michaelis–Menten curve, BE crossover scheme), then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short (5-mark) and long (10-mark) questions, rated by how likely that kind of question is.

**How the units map to the PCI syllabus.** The PCI syllabus has five units. Syllabus Unit 3 is very large, so it is split here: Unit 3 covers compartmental and non-compartmental pharmacokinetics, and Unit 4 covers nonlinear pharmacokinetics and drug interactions. Syllabus Units 4 and 5 appear here as Units 5 and 6.

| Unit 2 | Unit 2 | Product design, dissolution testing, IVIVC, f1/f2, stability |

**Worked examples.** All data tables use example numbers for hypothetical drugs, chosen so that the arithmetic is clear. They are not values for any real medicine. The same "Drug X" used in the B.Pharm course (KE 0.1733 h⁻¹, t½ 4 h, Vd 25 L, CL 4.33 L/h, F 0.8, Ka 1.2 h⁻¹) runs through Units 3, 5 and 6, so you can see how compartmental, non-compartmental, bioavailability and extended-release calculations fit together. "Drug Y" shows the two-compartment model and "Drug Z" capacity-limited elimination.

**End-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** The PCI M.Pharm scheme gives MPH202T 100 marks: 25 internal assessment and 75 in the end-semester theory examination of 3 hours. PCI does not set a question pattern for M.Pharm, so the mock paper here uses the common PCI 75-mark split (also used for M.Pharm by some universities, for example Bahra University, Shimla). Confirm the exact pattern with your university.

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — a central topic that appears in most papers; prepare the full answer with equations, a graph and a worked numerical.
- **Medium** — appears every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — less common, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common patterns in pharmacokinetics papers. They are not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Begin every derivation with the model diagram and assumptions, then the differential equation, its solution, and the meaning of each symbol with units.
2. Numericals: write the formula, substitute with units, give the answer with units (mg/L, L/h, h⁻¹, mg·h/L). Method marks are given even if the arithmetic slips; a sense check (for example V1 < Vss < Vβ, GMR inside its CI) earns credit.
3. Draw the right graph: semilog plot with residuals, Michaelis–Menten rate curve, AUC vs dose curves for nonlinearity, effect vs concentration loop, BE confidence-interval diagram with 80 and 125% lines.
4. In BE answers always state the regulatory numbers: 90% CI, 80.00–125.00%, log-transformed AUC and Cmax, washout ≥ 5 half-lives, sampling ≥ 3 half-lives, NTI 90.00–111.11%.
5. For drug interactions, name the mechanism (enzyme or transporter), the direction (inhibition or induction), the time course, and a mechanistic example; add the AUC-ratio equation for extra marks.
6. Link biopharmaceutics to regulation: ICH M9 (BCS biowaivers), ICH M10 (bioanalysis), ICH M12 (drug interactions), ICH Q1A (stability), CDSCO and the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019.
7. Postgraduate examiners reward recent concepts: transport model (1/Peff = 1/PABL + 1/(Pm + Ppara)), TMDD, FcRn recycling, GalNAc conjugates, reference-scaled BE for highly variable drugs.
