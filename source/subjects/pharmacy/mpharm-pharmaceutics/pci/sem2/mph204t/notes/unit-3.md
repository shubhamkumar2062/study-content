# Unit 3: Formulation building blocks, perfumes and controversial ingredients

Most cosmetics are built from a small set of ingredient families combined in different proportions. A cold cream and a vanishing cream use similar oils and waxes; a shampoo and a face wash share surfactants; almost every water-containing product needs the same preservative thinking. This unit treats those families as **building blocks**: what each one does, how it is classified, and how the blocks are assembled into classic products. It ends with perfumes, the fragrance allergens named in EU law, and three ingredient groups that attract public controversy.

## 3.1 The building-block approach

```text
                      FINISHED COSMETIC
   ┌──────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┐
   │ Vehicle /    │ Functional    │ Actives       │ Stabilising & │
   │ base         │ excipients    │ (claims)      │ sensory       │
   ├──────────────┼───────────────┼───────────────┼───────────────┤
   │ water, oils, │ surfactants,  │ moisturisers, │ preservatives,│
   │ alcohols,    │ emulsifiers,  │ UV filters,   │ antioxidants, │
   │ powders      │ thickeners    │ fluoride,     │ chelators, pH │
   │              │               │ anti-dandruff │ adjusters,    │
   │              │               │ agents …      │ colour, scent │
   └──────────────┴───────────────┴───────────────┴───────────────┘
```

Choosing blocks starts from the **target product profile**: skin/hair type, the claim, sensory feel (light, rich, non-greasy), format (cream, gel, stick, bar), pack (jar, tube, pump, aerosol), cost, regulatory lists (BIS IS 4707, permitted colours) and stability (shelf life usually 24–36 months).

## 3.2 Surfactants: classification and application

A surfactant has a **hydrophilic head** and a **lipophilic tail**. Above the **critical micelle concentration (CMC)** molecules associate into micelles that hold oil and dirt. Useful concepts:

| Concept | Meaning | Formulation use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CMC | Concentration at which micelles form; above it surface tension stops falling | Lower CMC = efficient, often milder (fewer free monomers) |
| Krafft point | Temperature below which an ionic surfactant is too insoluble to form micelles | Soaps of long saturated acids need warm water; important for clear liquids |
| HLB (Griffin) | Hydrophilic–lipophilic balance on a 0–20 scale; for polyoxyethylene esters HLB = E/5 (E = % ethylene oxide by weight); for fatty acid esters HLB = 20(1 − S/A) | 3–6 w/o emulsifiers; 7–9 wetting agents; 8–18 o/w emulsifiers; 13–15 detergents; 15–18 solubilisers |
| Required HLB | The HLB an oil phase needs for a stable emulsion | Blend emulsifiers: HLB(mix) = f·HLB(A) + (1 − f)·HLB(B) |
| Critical packing parameter | P = v/(a0·lc): < 1/3 spherical micelles, 1/3–1/2 rods, 1/2–1 bilayers, > 1 reverse micelles | Explains why some surfactants thicken (rod micelles) or form liposome-like structures |

**Classification by charge of the head group**

| Class | Examples | Properties | Applications |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Anionic** | Soaps (sodium/potassium salts of fatty acids), sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS), sodium laureth sulphate (SLES), ammonium lauryl sulphate, alpha-olefin sulphonate, sodium cocoyl isethionate (SCI), sodium lauroyl sarcosinate, sodium cocoyl glutamate, sulphosuccinates | Strong cleansing and foaming; may irritate (SLS > SLES > acyl amino acids); sensitive to hard water (soaps) | Shampoos, body and face washes, toothpaste (SLS as foaming agent), syndet bars |
| **Cationic** | Quaternary ammonium compounds: cetrimonium chloride, behentrimonium chloride/methosulphate, distearyldimonium chloride; esterquats; benzalkonium chloride, cetylpyridinium chloride | Adsorb on negatively charged hair and skin; conditioning, anti-static; antimicrobial; little foam; can irritate eyes | Hair conditioners, antimicrobial mouthwash (CPC), preservative (benzalkonium) |
| **Amphoteric (zwitterionic)** | Cocamidopropyl betaine, coco betaine, sodium lauroamphoacetate, hydroxysultaines | Charge depends on pH; mild; boost foam and viscosity; reduce irritation of anionics | Baby shampoos, mild washes, secondary surfactant |
| **Non-ionic** | Fatty alcohol ethoxylates (ceteareth-20, laureth-4), polysorbates, sorbitan esters, glyceryl stearate, PEG esters, alkyl polyglucosides (decyl/coco glucoside), fatty acid alkanolamides (cocamide MEA), sucrose esters, poloxamers | Not affected by hard water or charge; good emulsifiers and solubilisers; usually mild; may inactivate some preservatives | Creams and lotions (emulsifiers), perfume solubilisers in toners, mild cleansers, foam stabilisers |

**Applications by function**

| Function | Typical surfactants |
| --- | --- |
| Primary cleanser / foamer | SLES, SLS, SCI, alpha-olefin sulphonate |
| Secondary (mildness, foam, viscosity) | Cocamidopropyl betaine, alkyl polyglucosides, cocamide MEA |
| O/W emulsifier | Ceteareth-20, polysorbate 60, glyceryl stearate + PEG-100 stearate, cetearyl glucoside |
| W/O emulsifier | Sorbitan oleate, polyglyceryl-3 diisostearate, PEG-30 dipolyhydroxystearate, beeswax–borax (in situ soap) |
| Solubiliser | Polysorbate 20, PEG-40 hydrogenated castor oil |
| Conditioner | Behentrimonium chloride, polyquaternium-10 (cationic polymer) |
| Wetting agent / dispersant | Low-HLB to mid-HLB non-ionics, sarcosinates |

**Mildness** is assessed by protein denaturation (the **zein test** measures dissolved corn protein), irritation tests on reconstructed epidermis, and TEWL after repeated washing. Mixed micelles (anionic + amphoteric or non-ionic) lower the free-monomer concentration and so reduce irritation.

## 3.3 Emollients: classification and application

Emollients are oily or waxy materials that make the skin soft and smooth by filling spaces between desquamating corneocytes; many also reduce water loss (**occlusives**). Moisturisers combine **emollients, occlusives and humectants**.

**Classification by chemistry**

| Group | Examples | Feel and use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrocarbons | Mineral oil, petrolatum, microcrystalline wax, squalane, hydrogenated polyisobutene | Petrolatum is the most occlusive (reduces TEWL by about 98%); heavy feel; inexpensive |
| Triglycerides (vegetable oils and butters) | Almond, olive, sunflower, coconut, jojoba (actually a wax ester), shea butter, cocoa butter, caprylic/capric triglyceride | Nourishing, may oxidise (need antioxidants); rich feel |
| Esters (synthetic) | Isopropyl myristate, isopropyl palmitate, C12–15 alkyl benzoate, cetearyl ethylhexanoate, ethylhexyl palmitate | Light, fast spreading; some (isopropyl myristate) can be comedogenic; good solvents for UV filters |
| Fatty alcohols | Cetyl, stearyl, cetostearyl, behenyl alcohol | Body, stability and "soaping" feel in creams; co-emulsifiers |
| Fatty acids | Stearic, palmitic, myristic | Vanishing creams, soaps, cleansing creams |
| Silicones | Dimethicone, cyclopentasiloxane (volatile; its use is being restricted in the EU for environmental reasons), amodimethicone | Silky, non-greasy, breathable film; hair shine and conditioning |
| Lanolin and derivatives | Lanolin, lanolin alcohols, acetylated lanolin | Excellent emollient; possible allergy in some users |
| Waxes | Beeswax, carnauba, candelilla, ceresin | Structure for sticks and balms |
| Skin-identical lipids | Ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids, phytosphingosine | Barrier repair in dry or atopic skin |

**Classification by spreading and feel:** fast-spreading (volatile silicones, light esters) → medium (isopropyl myristate, triglycerides) → slow-spreading, rich (petrolatum, butters, lanolin). Formulators often use an **emollient cascade** – a blend of fast, medium and slow emollients – to give a pleasant feel that changes during rub-in.

**Selection criteria:** polarity (affects emulsion stability and solubility of actives), spreading value, occlusivity, comedogenicity, oxidative stability, odour and colour, compatibility with UV filters, regulatory and natural-certification status, cost.

## 3.4 Rheological additives: classification and application

**Why rheology matters:** viscosity gives the product body; **shear-thinning** (pseudoplastic) flow lets a lotion pour or a cream spread easily while it stays thick at rest; a **yield value** keeps particles and droplets suspended; **thixotropy** lets a gel recover after shaking.

| Class | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural gums and polysaccharides | Xanthan gum, guar gum, carrageenan, gum acacia, gellan, sclerotium gum, alginates, starches | Tolerate salts; xanthan has a high yield value; may feel slightly sticky |
| Semi-synthetic (cellulose derivatives) | Sodium carboxymethylcellulose, hydroxyethylcellulose, hydroxypropylmethylcellulose, methylcellulose | Clear gels; CMC is the classic toothpaste binder |
| Synthetic polymers | Carbomers (crosslinked polyacrylic acid), acrylates/C10–30 alkyl acrylate crosspolymer, sodium polyacrylate, polyacrylamide-type thickeners | Need neutralisation (carbomers) to swell; crystal-clear gels; sensitive to electrolytes |
| Associative thickeners | Hydrophobically modified polymers (HASE types, PEG-150 distearate) | Thicken surfactant systems by linking micelles |
| Electrolytes | Sodium chloride, ammonium chloride | Thicken SLES/betaine shampoos by growing rod micelles; viscosity rises then falls past a "salt curve" maximum |
| Inorganic clays and silicas | Bentonite, magnesium aluminium silicate, hectorite, fumed silica, hydrated silica | Gel structure, suspension, mattifying; silica thickens toothpaste |
| Lipid thickeners | Fatty alcohols, waxes, hydrogenated castor oil, stearic acid | Structure creams (liquid-crystal gel networks) and anhydrous sticks |

**Application examples:** carbomer for clear facial gels; xanthan + fatty alcohol for creams; salt for shampoos; CMC or xanthan + silica for toothpaste; clays for face packs and suspensions of zinc oxide.

## 3.5 Antimicrobials used as preservatives

Water, nutrients (proteins, sugars, plant extracts) and repeated opening make cosmetics ideal for microbes. Contamination can spoil the product (odour, colour change, emulsion breaking) and harm users, especially near the eyes. Preservatives protect against contamination **during use**; they do not replace GMP.

**Ideal preservative:** broad spectrum (Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, yeasts, moulds); effective at low concentration across the product's pH; partitions into the water phase; stable to heat and storage; compatible with ingredients and packaging; non-toxic, non-irritant, non-sensitising; colourless and odourless; permitted by regulation; affordable.

| Preservative | Merits | Demerits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parabens (methyl, ethyl, propyl, butyl esters of p-hydroxybenzoic acid) | Broad spectrum (esp. fungi), effective from pH about 3–8, long safety record, cheap | Lower activity with increasing chain length in water; bound by non-ionics; public concern over endocrine effects (see 3.10); some long-chain esters banned in EU |
| Phenoxyethanol | Broad, stable, compatible | Weak against some moulds and Pseudomonas alone; used with boosters (ethylhexylglycerin) |
| Organic acids: benzoic acid/sodium benzoate, sorbic acid/potassium sorbate, dehydroacetic acid | Mild, accepted in natural products | Work only in **undissociated form** at low pH (about ≤ 5.5); weak against bacteria |
| Benzyl alcohol | Mild, accepted in natural products | Weak; odour; listed fragrance allergen |
| Formaldehyde releasers (DMDM hydantoin, imidazolidinyl urea, diazolidinyl urea, quaternium-15, bronopol) | Very effective against bacteria, cheap | Sensitisation; formaldehyde concerns (see 3.10) |
| Isothiazolinones (methylchloroisothiazolinone/methylisothiazolinone) | Very active at ppm levels, broad | High rates of contact allergy; restricted to rinse-off products in the EU |
| Quaternary ammonium compounds (benzalkonium chloride) | Active against bacteria | Inactivated by anionics; eye irritation |
| Chlorphenesin, o-cymen-5-ol, piroctone olamine | Useful partners | Limited spectrum alone |
| Multifunctional "preservative-free" boosters (caprylyl glycol, glyceryl caprylate, ethylhexylglycerin, 1,2-hexanediol) | Support preservation, not listed as preservatives in some markets | Need higher levels; may affect feel |

Preservatives are usually combined to widen the spectrum and allow lower levels of each.

## 3.6 Factors affecting preservative efficacy

1. **pH and pKa.** Weak-acid preservatives act in the undissociated form; efficacy falls sharply when pH exceeds pKa (benzoic acid pKa about 4.2, sorbic acid about 4.8).
2. **Oil–water partition.** Microbes grow in the water phase; a lipophilic preservative (long-chain paraben) migrates into oil and leaves too little in water. Effective concentration in water depends on the oil:water ratio and the partition coefficient.
3. **Binding to surfactants (micellar solubilisation).** Non-ionic surfactants such as polysorbates bind parabens and phenols in micelles, so the free (active) fraction drops; a higher total level is then needed.
4. **Interaction with other ingredients.** Cationic preservatives are inactivated by anionic surfactants; proteins, cellulose derivatives, powders (talc, kaolin, titanium dioxide) and plant extracts can adsorb or inactivate preservatives.
5. **Packaging.** Preservatives can sorb into or permeate plastics and rubber; open jars expose product to fingers and air, whereas airless pumps and tubes reduce contamination.
6. **Water activity (aw).** Microbes need free water; high glycerin, sugar or salt content and anhydrous products lower aw (most bacteria need aw above about 0.9).
7. **Temperature and storage time.** Preservatives may degrade or evaporate; heat during manufacture can hydrolyse esters.
8. **Initial microbial load.** Contaminated water, raw materials (especially natural ones) or poor hygiene can overwhelm any preservative – GMP comes first.
9. **Product structure.** Multiple emulsions, high-viscosity regions and solid particles can create pockets where preservative is low.
10. **Synergy and boosters.** Chelating agents (disodium EDTA) weaken Gram-negative cell walls; glycols and alcohol contribute activity; combinations allow a "hurdle" approach (low pH + low aw + preservative + good packaging).

**Preservative efficacy (challenge) test.** The product is inoculated separately with standard organisms – *Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Candida albicans* and *Aspergillus brasiliensis* – and the count is followed over 28 days (for example at 7, 14 and 28 days). The internationally used cosmetic method is **ISO 11930**, which sets criteria for log reduction of bacteria and fungi. A product that fails is reformulated or packed in protective packaging.

## 3.7 Building blocks of classic products

The formulas below show the **type and typical range** of each block, not a licensed recipe.

### Moisturising cream (o/w)

| Block | Examples | Typical % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water phase | Purified water | to 100 |
| Humectants | Glycerin, propylene glycol, sorbitol, sodium PCA, urea | 3–10 |
| Emollients / occlusives | Light liquid paraffin, caprylic/capric triglyceride, isopropyl myristate, dimethicone, shea butter | 10–25 |
| Consistency factors | Cetostearyl alcohol, stearic acid, glyceryl stearate | 2–6 |
| Emulsifiers | Ceteareth-20, glyceryl stearate SE, cetearyl glucoside | 2–5 |
| Thickener | Xanthan gum or carbomer (neutralised) | 0.2–0.5 |
| Barrier actives | Ceramides, niacinamide, panthenol, allantoin | 0.1–5 |
| Preservative, chelator, antioxidant | Phenoxyethanol, disodium EDTA, tocopherol | as permitted |
| Perfume, pH adjuster | – | small |

Method: heat oil phase and water phase separately to about 70–75 °C, add the oil to water (or water to oil) with homogenisation, cool with stirring, add heat-sensitive actives, perfume and preservative below about 40 °C, adjust pH (about 5–6).

### Vanishing cream (o/w, stearate type)

A cream that "vanishes" on rubbing, leaving a dry, matt, non-greasy film – because its oil phase is mainly **stearic acid**, part of which is neutralised to a soap that acts as the emulsifier.

| Block | Examples | Typical % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fatty acid | Stearic acid (triple-pressed) | 15–25 |
| Alkali (forms soap in situ) | Potassium hydroxide, sodium hydroxide or triethanolamine (neutralising about 15–25% of the stearic acid) | about 0.5–1 |
| Humectant | Glycerin, sorbitol | 5–10 |
| Others | Cetyl alcohol (body), preservative, perfume | small |
| Water | Purified water | to 100 |

The unneutralised stearic acid crystallises into fine plates that give a **pearly lustre** after ageing. Used as a day cream and make-up base.

### Cold cream (w/o, beeswax–borax type)

A cream that gives a **cooling** sensation as its water evaporates, used for cleansing and as a night/massage cream.

| Block | Examples | Typical % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wax | White beeswax | 10–20 |
| Oil | Liquid paraffin, almond oil | 40–55 |
| Emulsifier formed in situ | Borax reacts with free cerotic acid of beeswax to form sodium cerotate | 0.5–1 |
| Water | Purified water, rose water | 20–35 |
| Preservative, antioxidant, perfume | – | small |

Beeswax–borax creams are usually w/o; with a high water content and the right borax ratio they can invert to o/w. Modern cold creams also use w/o emulsifiers such as polyglyceryl esters.

### Shampoo

| Block | Examples | Typical % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary surfactant | SLES, ammonium lauryl sulphate, alpha-olefin sulphonate, SCI (sulphate-free) | 8–15 (active) |
| Secondary surfactant / foam booster | Cocamidopropyl betaine, cocamide MEA, coco glucoside | 2–5 |
| Conditioning agents | Polyquaternium-10, guar hydroxypropyltrimonium chloride, dimethicone, panthenol | 0.1–2 |
| Thickener | Sodium chloride, PEG-150 distearate, acrylates copolymer | 0.5–3 |
| Pearlising / opacifier | Glycol distearate | 0.5–2 |
| Actives | Zinc pyrithione, piroctone olamine, climbazole (anti-dandruff); suspended with acrylate polymers | as permitted |
| pH adjuster | Citric acid (pH about 5–6.5) | q.s. |
| Preservative, chelator, perfume, colour | – | small |
| Water | – | to 100 |

"Two-in-one" (conditioning) shampoos rely on **coacervate deposition**: cationic polymer and anionic surfactant form a complex that separates on dilution during rinsing and deposits silicone onto hair.

### Toothpaste

| Block | Examples | Typical % |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abrasive (cleaning, stain removal) | Calcium carbonate, dicalcium phosphate dihydrate, hydrated silica, alumina | 20–50 |
| Humectant (stops drying in the tube) | Sorbitol, glycerin, PEG | 20–40 |
| Binder / thickener | Sodium CMC, xanthan, carrageenan, thickening silica | 0.5–2 |
| Foaming agent | Sodium lauryl sulphate, cocamidopropyl betaine | 1–2 |
| Active | Sodium fluoride or sodium monofluorophosphate (fluoride ≤ 1000 ppm in India), potassium nitrate (sensitivity), zinc citrate (tartar, gums) | as permitted |
| Sweetener, flavour | Sodium saccharin; peppermint, spearmint, menthol | small |
| Preservative, colour, water | Sodium benzoate or parabens | small, to 100 |

Abrasivity is controlled (relative dentine abrasivity) so the paste cleans without wearing dentine. Fluoride must stay **available**: calcium carbonate abrasives bind free sodium fluoride, so they are paired with monofluorophosphate; silica is compatible with sodium fluoride.

## 3.8 Soaps and syndet bars

**Soap** = alkali-metal salt of long-chain fatty acids, made by **saponification**:

```text
Triglyceride + 3 NaOH  ──►  Glycerol + 3 RCOONa (soap)
(fat / oil)                            (hard bar with Na; soft/liquid with K)
```

| Raw material | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Tallow or palm oil (C16–C18) | Hardness, long-lasting creamy lather, mild |
| Coconut or palm kernel oil (C12–C14) | Quick, abundant lather, more soluble; more drying |
| Typical blend | About 80:20 tallow/palm : coconut |

Processes: full boiling (kettle), cold process, semi-boiled, and continuous saponification or neutralisation of fatty acids. Toilet soaps are milled, plodded and stamped; additives include glycerin, perfume, colour, antioxidant (to prevent rancidity) and chelator. BIS grades toilet soaps by **total fatty matter (TFM)**: Grade 1 at least 76%, Grade 2 at least 70%, Grade 3 at least 60%.

Drawbacks of soap: **alkaline pH (about 9–10)**, forms insoluble calcium and magnesium soaps (scum) in hard water, removes barrier lipids and swells the stratum corneum, which can dry and irritate the skin.

**Syndet bars** (synthetic detergent bars) are based mainly on mild synthetic surfactants, chiefly **sodium cocoyl isethionate**, plus stearic acid, paraffin or fatty alcohols as plasticisers and binders, with fillers (starch, dextrin) and emollients. **Combars** combine soap and syndet.

| Property | Soap | Syndet bar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pH of solution | About 9–10 | About 5.5–7 |
| Hard water | Scum | No scum |
| Effect on barrier | More lipid and protein damage | Milder; less TEWL rise |
| Lather | Good | Creamy, slightly less |
| Wear and mush | Usually lower mush | Needs careful formulation |
| Cost | Low | Higher |
| Best for | Normal skin | Dry, sensitive, acne-prone, atopic skin, babies |

## 3.9 Perfumes

**Classification by origin**

| Source | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Natural plant materials (essential oils, absolutes, resinoids) | Rose, jasmine, sandalwood, vetiver, lavender, citrus oils, patchouli |
| Animal (now replaced by synthetics) | Musk, civet, ambergris, castoreum |
| Synthetic and nature-identical aroma chemicals | Linalool, citronellol, geraniol, hexyl cinnamal, coumarin, vanillin, Iso E Super-type woody materials, macrocyclic and polycyclic musks |

**Classification by olfactory family** (fragrance wheel): **floral** (single flower, floral bouquet), **fresh** (citrus, green, aquatic, aromatic), **oriental/amber** (vanilla, resins, spices), **woody** (sandalwood, cedar, vetiver), **chypre** (citrus top on oakmoss–patchouli base), **fougère** (lavender, coumarin, oakmoss – typical of men's fragrances), and gourmand.

**Classification by concentration of perfume oil** (typical ranges, not legal limits)

| Type | Perfume concentrate |
| --- | --- |
| Parfum / extrait | About 20–30% |
| Eau de parfum | About 15–20% |
| Eau de toilette | About 5–15% |
| Eau de cologne | About 2–5% |
| Body mist / splash | About 1–3% |

**Structure of a perfume (notes)**

```text
 TOP notes     │ smelled first, evaporate in minutes  │ citrus, light herbs, aldehydes
 MIDDLE (heart)│ the character, last hours             │ floral, spices
 BASE notes    │ fixatives, last longest               │ woods, musks, vanilla, resins
```

**Perfuming cosmetics:** the fragrance must suit the base (alkaline soaps destroy some esters), not discolour (vanillin and indole darken in some bases), be solubilised in clear products, and stay within allergen and safety limits. The International Fragrance Association (IFRA) issues industry standards that restrict or ban certain fragrance materials by product category.

### Perfume ingredients listed as allergens in EU regulation

Under Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, Annex III lists fragrance substances that are known contact allergens. They must be **named in the ingredient list** when present above **0.001% in leave-on** or **0.01% in rinse-off** products. The original list has **26 substances**:

| Type | Substances |
| --- | --- |
| Aroma chemicals (24) | Amyl cinnamal, amylcinnamyl alcohol, anise alcohol, benzyl alcohol, benzyl benzoate, benzyl cinnamate, benzyl salicylate, cinnamal, cinnamyl alcohol, citral, citronellol, coumarin, eugenol, farnesol, geraniol, hexyl cinnamal, hydroxycitronellal, hydroxyisohexyl 3-cyclohexene carboxaldehyde (HICC), isoeugenol, limonene, linalool, methyl 2-octynoate, alpha-isomethyl ionone, butylphenyl methylpropional (lilial) |
| Natural extracts (2) | Oakmoss extract (Evernia prunastri), treemoss extract (Evernia furfuracea) |

Later changes students should know:

- **HICC** and the oakmoss/treemoss sensitisers **atranol and chloroatranol** have been **banned** in the EU because of their high allergy rates.
- **Butylphenyl methylpropional (lilial)** was banned in 2022 after classification as toxic to reproduction.
- **Regulation (EU) 2023/1545** expanded the list of fragrance allergens requiring labelling to about 80 substances (adding many essential-oil allergens and further aroma chemicals), with transition periods; new products had to comply from 31 July 2026.

Formulators in India follow these lists voluntarily or when exporting; they are also a good guide to safer fragrance design for sensitive-skin products.

## 3.10 Controversial ingredients

### Parabens

**Concern:** weak oestrogen-like activity in laboratory tests (much weaker than oestradiol; increases with chain length) and a much-publicised 2004 study that detected parabens in breast tumour tissue (it did not show that parabens caused cancer).

**Scientific and regulatory position (EU):**

| Paraben | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Methylparaben, ethylparaben | Permitted up to 0.4% (as acid) for a single ester and 0.8% for mixtures of esters |
| Propylparaben, butylparaben | Permitted, total not more than 0.14% (as acid); not allowed in leave-on products for the nappy area of children under 3 years |
| Isopropyl-, isobutyl-, phenyl-, benzyl- and pentylparaben | Banned (insufficient safety data) |

**Formulator's view:** short-chain parabens remain among the best-studied, least sensitising preservatives. "Paraben-free" products often swap them for alternatives (isothiazolinones, formaldehyde releasers) that have **higher** allergy rates – a point examiners like to see.

### Formaldehyde and formaldehyde liberators

Formaldehyde is a known sensitiser and is classified as carcinogenic (category 1B) in the EU. Direct use as a preservative has been withdrawn in the EU; it may still be present as a **released** substance. **Formaldehyde liberators** (DMDM hydantoin, imidazolidinyl urea, diazolidinyl urea, quaternium-15, sodium hydroxymethylglycinate, bronopol) slowly release formaldehyde in water-based products.

| Issue | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Allergy | Common cause of contact dermatitis in sensitised users |
| Labelling (EU) | Regulation (EU) 2022/1181 requires the warning **"releases formaldehyde"** when total formaldehyde released by all releasers exceeds **0.001% (10 ppm)** (earlier threshold 0.05%); applied to new products from 31 July 2024 and to all products from 31 July 2026 |
| Hair-smoothing ("keratin") treatments | Some release formaldehyde gas on heating with irons – an inhalation and eye hazard for salon workers |
| Bronopol | Can form nitrosamines with amines in the formula |

### 1,4-Dioxane

1,4-Dioxane is **not added deliberately**; it is a **by-product of ethoxylation** (made from ethylene oxide) and appears as a trace impurity in ethoxylated ingredients such as SLES, PEGs, polysorbates and ceteths. It is classified as a possible human carcinogen.

Control measures:

1. **Vacuum stripping** of ethoxylated raw materials by the supplier.
2. Specifying low dioxane limits in raw-material specifications and testing (headspace GC–MS).
3. Using non-ethoxylated alternatives (sulphates without ether, acyl isethionates, alkyl polyglucosides, amino-acid surfactants).

In the EU 1,4-dioxane is a prohibited substance; only technically unavoidable traces from raw materials are tolerated if the product is safe. Some regulators outside Europe (for example, New York State in the USA) have set numerical limits on dioxane in personal care products.

**Teaching point:** "controversial" ingredients are judged by **hazard, exposure and risk**. An ingredient may be hazardous yet safe at the low levels used; the formulator's job is to document the margin of safety (Unit 4) and to avoid replacing a well-understood ingredient with a poorly studied one.

## Remember

- Building blocks: vehicle + functional excipients + actives + stabilisers/sensory; start from the target product profile.
- Surfactants: anionic (cleanse, foam), cationic (condition, antimicrobial), amphoteric (mild, booster), non-ionic (emulsify, solubilise). HLB 3–6 w/o, 8–18 o/w, 15–18 solubilisers; packing parameter predicts micelle shape.
- Emollients: hydrocarbons, triglycerides, esters, fatty alcohols/acids, silicones, lanolin, waxes, skin-identical lipids; petrolatum most occlusive.
- Rheology: gums, cellulose derivatives, carbomers (neutralise), associative polymers, salt (shampoos), clays/silica, fatty alcohols; shear-thinning with yield value is ideal.
- Preservative efficacy depends on pH (undissociated acid), oil–water partition, micellar binding by non-ionics, interactions, packaging, aw, load, structure; test by challenge test (ISO 11930), 28 days, five organisms.
- Vanishing cream = stearic acid partly neutralised (o/w, pearly); cold cream = beeswax–borax (w/o, cooling).
- Toothpaste: abrasive + humectant + binder + SLS + fluoride (≤ 1000 ppm India) + flavour; CaCO3 pairs with MFP.
- Soap pH 9–10, TFM grades 76/70/60%; syndet (SCI) pH 5.5–7, milder.
- Perfume: by origin, family, concentration (parfum 20–30% … cologne 2–5%), notes top/heart/base.
- EU allergens: 26 listed, label above 0.001% leave-on / 0.01% rinse-off; expanded to about 80 by Regulation 2023/1545; HICC, atranol, chloroatranol, lilial banned.
- Parabens: methyl/ethyl 0.4%/0.8%; propyl + butyl ≤ 0.14% total; five long/branched parabens banned (EU).
- Formaldehyde releasers: "releases formaldehyde" above 0.001% (Reg. 2022/1181). 1,4-dioxane: ethoxylation by-product, removed by vacuum stripping.
