# Unit 5: Herbal cosmetics and natural/organic standards

India has a long tradition of plant-based beauty care – amla and shikakai for hair, turmeric and sandalwood for skin, neem and babool twigs for teeth. Consumers today want "natural" products, and global brands want natural and organic certification. For the formulator this creates two tasks: choosing herbal ingredients that genuinely help, and building stable, safe products **without** many of the synthetic building blocks of Unit 3. This unit covers herbal ingredients for hair, skin and oral care, the COSMOS standard's rules for preservatives, emollients, foaming agents, emulsifiers and rheology modifiers, and the practical challenges of herbal formulation.

## 5.1 What is a herbal cosmetic?

A **herbal cosmetic** is a cosmetic in which one or more ingredients of plant origin (powders, extracts, juices, essential oils, fixed oils, gums) are used for a cosmetic purpose. Terms used in the market:

| Term | Usual meaning | Legal status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Herbal / botanical | Contains plant ingredients (any amount) | Not defined in Indian cosmetic law; claims must not mislead |
| Natural | Mostly natural or naturally derived ingredients | Defined only by private standards (COSMOS, NATRUE) or by ISO 16128 calculations |
| Organic | Natural, with a minimum share of organically farmed ingredients, certified | Private certification (COSMOS ORGANIC etc.) |
| Ayurvedic | Formulated on Ayurvedic principles | If it makes medicinal claims it is an Ayurvedic drug, licensed under the Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani provisions of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act; the Cosmetics Rules, 2020 have no separate "Ayurvedic cosmetic" category |

**ISO 16128** (Parts 1 and 2) gives internationally agreed definitions and a method to calculate **natural, natural-origin, organic and organic-origin indices** of ingredients and products. It does not set minimum levels or a logo; brands use it to support percentage claims.

Herbal toothpastes still need the basic blocks of Unit 3 (abrasive, humectant, binder, foaming agent) and, for anti-caries claims, fluoride.

## 5.5 Private standards for natural and organic cosmetics: COSMOS

**Background.** The **COSMOS standard** (COSMetic Organic and Natural Standard) was developed by five European organisations – **BDIH** (Germany), **Cosmebio** and **Ecocert** (France), **ICEA** (Italy) and the **Soil Association** (UK) – and is run by the COSMOS-standard association based in Brussels. It replaced the founders' individual standards and is now one of the most widely used natural/organic certification schemes worldwide. The current series is **version 4** (from 1 January 2023, with later revisions). Other schemes include **NATRUE** and national labels; the USDA National Organic Program applies to organic agricultural ingredients in the USA.

**Certification signatures**

| Signature | Applies to | Main criteria |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **COSMOS ORGANIC** | Finished products | At least **20% organic** content of the total product (at least **10%** for rinse-off products, non-emulsified aqueous products and products with at least 80% mineral ingredients); at least **95% of the physically processed agro-ingredients organic**; specified chemically processed agro-ingredients must be organic; **not more than 2% petrochemical moieties** |
| **COSMOS NATURAL** | Finished products | No minimum organic content; same ingredient and process rules; not more than 2% petrochemical moieties |
| **COSMOS CERTIFIED** | Raw materials containing organic ingredients | Raw materials that can be used in COSMOS products |
| **COSMOS APPROVED** | Raw materials without organic content (e.g. minerals, base formulas) | Approved for use in COSMOS products |

**Ingredient categories in COSMOS:** water; **minerals and ingredients of mineral origin**; **physically processed agro-ingredients (PPAI)** – plant, animal or microbial materials obtained by physical processes (pressing, distillation, extraction with permitted solvents, drying); **chemically processed agro-ingredients (CPAI)** – obtained from agro-ingredients by **permitted chemical processes**; and **other ingredients** – a short positive list of preservatives and nature-identical substances, including some with petrochemical parts (counted against the 2% limit).

**General rules:**

1. **Permitted chemical processes** follow "green chemistry" – for example esterification, hydrolysis, saponification, hydrogenation, amidation, neutralisation, condensation and fermentation. **Ethoxylation and propoxylation are not allowed**, so PEGs, polysorbates, ceteareths and other ethoxylated materials (and the 1,4-dioxane issue that comes with them) are excluded.
2. **GMOs and irradiation are forbidden**; sourcing of palm oil and its derivatives must be organic or certified sustainable.
3. **Petrochemical-derived oils and waxes** (mineral oil, petrolatum, paraffin) and **silicones** cannot be used.
4. Nano-forms of ingredients are restricted (assessed against EU scientific opinions).
5. Biodegradability and aquatic toxicity of ingredients are considered; packaging should be recyclable or reduced.
6. Version 4 introduced tighter rules on some sulphated surfactants and emulsifiers, with phase-out dates, and calculation changes for organic percentages – always check the current version.

## 5.6 COSMOS rules for the five excipient groups named in the syllabus

| Building block | Accepted under COSMOS (typical) | Not accepted |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Preservatives** | Only a short positive list: mainly **organic acids and their salts** (benzoic acid/sodium benzoate, sorbic acid/potassium sorbate, dehydroacetic acid/sodium dehydroacetate, salicylic acid and others on the list) and **benzyl alcohol**; plus ethanol at high levels, and naturally derived "multifunctionals" such as glyceryl caprylate | Parabens, formaldehyde releasers, isothiazolinones, phenoxyethanol, quaternium preservatives |
| **Emollients** | Vegetable oils and butters, plant waxes (jojoba, carnauba, candelilla), beeswax and lanolin (animal by-products allowed), esters made by esterification of natural fatty acids and alcohols (coco-caprylate/caprate, caprylic/capric triglyceride, cetearyl alcohol), plant-derived squalane, natural hydrocarbons from plants | Mineral oil, petrolatum, paraffin, silicones (dimethicone, cyclomethicone), synthetic esters from petrochemical feedstock beyond the 2% moiety limit |
| **Foaming agents (surfactants)** | Sugar-based alkyl polyglucosides (decyl, coco, lauryl glucoside), amino-acid surfactants (e.g. sodium cocoyl glutamate), betaines derived from natural fatty acids, true soaps (saponification), plant saponins (reetha, shikakai, quillaja), some isethionates and sulphated surfactants as allowed by the current version | Ethoxylated surfactants (SLES, laureths, PEG-based foam boosters), surfactants made by forbidden processes |
| **Emulsifiers** | Glyceryl stearate, glyceryl oleate, sucrose esters, polyglyceryl esters, sorbitan esters, cetearyl glucoside, cetearyl olivate/sorbitan olivate, lecithin, beeswax (with natural co-emulsifiers) | Ethoxylated emulsifiers (polysorbates, ceteareths, PEG-100 stearate) |
| **Rheology modifiers** | Natural gums and polysaccharides (xanthan, guar, gellan, sclerotium, carrageenan, agar, alginate), starches, natural celluloses, clays (bentonite, hectorite), silica, fatty alcohols and waxes | Carbomers and other synthetic acrylate polymers, PEG-based thickeners, ethoxylated cellulose derivatives |

**Formulator's consequences:** natural emulsions often need combinations of polyglyceryl esters and glucosides plus gums to match the stability of PEG systems; natural shampoos foam less and are harder to thicken with salt; preservation relies on low pH (acids work below about pH 5.5), lower water activity, chelators (e.g. sodium phytate) and protective packaging.

## 5.7 Challenges in formulating herbal cosmetics

| Challenge | Why it happens | How it is managed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Variability of raw material** | Species, chemotype, region, season, harvest time, drying and storage change constituent levels | Correct botanical identification (macroscopy, microscopy, DNA barcoding), specifications, **standardised extracts** with marker compounds (e.g. % glycyrrhizin, % curcuminoids), approved suppliers |
| **Adulteration and substitution** | Expensive herbs (saffron, sandalwood) and essential oils are diluted or replaced; "black henna" with PPD | Chromatographic fingerprinting (HPTLC, HPLC, GC), authentication |
| **Microbial contamination** | Plant powders carry high counts of bacteria, moulds and spores | Microbial limits on raw materials, extracts instead of crude powders, sterilisation methods compatible with natural rules, GMP |
| **Preservation** | Few preservatives allowed in natural products; plant extracts feed microbes and bind preservatives | Hurdle approach: low pH, organic acids, glycols and glyceryl esters, lower water activity, airless packs, challenge testing |
| **Contaminants** | Heavy metals (lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium), pesticide residues, aflatoxins | Testing against limits; organic sourcing; supplier audits |
| **Stability** | Natural colours fade, oils oxidise (rancidity), chlorophyll and curcumin degrade in light, extracts precipitate, emulsions without PEG emulsifiers separate | Antioxidants (tocopherol, rosemary extract), chelators, opaque packaging, encapsulation, robust emulsifier blends, accelerated stability testing |
| **Colour and odour** | Extracts are brown, green or yellow and smell strongly; turmeric stains | Decolourised extracts, low use levels, derivatives (tetrahydrocurcumin), fragrance masking within allergen limits |
| **Solubility and delivery** | Many actives are poorly soluble (curcumin) or poorly penetrating | Solvent systems, liposomes/phytosomes (phospholipid complexes), nanoemulsions (within natural standards) |
| **Sensory and performance gaps** | Lower foam, sticky gums, heavier oils, weaker SPF without synthetic filters | Careful blending; for sun protection only mineral filters fit natural standards, which raises whitening problems |
| **Safety myths** | "Natural" does not mean safe: essential oils contain allergens (limonene, linalool, eugenol, citral), some herbs are phototoxic (bergapten in bergamot oil), oxidised terpenes sensitise | Allergen limits and labelling, IFRA standards, bergapten-free oils, safety assessment as for any cosmetic |
| **Evidence for claims** | Traditional use is not proof of efficacy | In vitro and clinical efficacy studies, honest claims; avoid drug claims |
| **Regulation and claims** | No legal definition of "herbal"/"natural"/"organic" in Indian cosmetic law; drug claims need drug licences | Accurate labelling (Rule 36), certification for "organic", ISO 16128 calculations for percentages |
| **Sustainability and cost** | Over-harvesting (sandalwood, jatamansi), organic supply limits, price swings | Cultivated sources, fair-trade and sustainability certification, alternative species or biotechnology (plant cell culture) |

## 5.8 Example: designing a herbal anti-dandruff shampoo to natural standards

```text
Target: mild, natural-certifiable anti-dandruff shampoo
  Cleansers   : coco glucoside + sodium cocoyl glutamate + coco-betaine
  Herbal      : neem leaf extract (antimicrobial), rosemary extract
                (antioxidant), fenugreek mucilage (conditioning),
                shikakai/reetha extract (saponin co-foamers, low pH)
  Thickener   : xanthan gum (salt works poorly with these surfactants)
  Conditioner : guar-based cationic polymer (if allowed by the chosen scheme)
                or plant proteins
  Preservation: sodium benzoate + potassium sorbate at pH about 5.0–5.5,
                chelator (sodium phytate)
  Fragrance   : essential-oil blend within allergen limits
  Tests       : viscosity and foam, pH, challenge test (ISO 11930),
                stability, flaking score study for the claim
```

If a strong anti-dandruff claim is needed, a cosmetic antifungal (piroctone olamine, zinc pyrithione) may be necessary; these are not permitted under all natural standards, which shows the trade-off between "natural" positioning and proven performance.

## Remember

- "Herbal", "natural" and "organic" have no legal definition in Indian cosmetic law; Ayurvedic products with medicinal claims are drugs under the ASU provisions. ISO 16128 gives natural/organic index calculations.
- Hair: amla, bhringraj, hibiscus, henna (lawsone), shikakai and reetha (saponins), neem, methi, rosemary, aloe.
- Skin: turmeric (curcumin), aloe, neem, sandalwood (santalols), saffron, liquorice (glabridin), green tea (EGCG), centella (asiaticoside), papaya (papain), tea tree (terpinen-4-ol).
- Oral: neem, babool (tannins), clove (eugenol), miswak (Salvadora persica), mint (menthol), thyme (thymol), liquorice, bakul.
- COSMOS: founded by BDIH, Cosmebio, Ecocert, ICEA, Soil Association; ORGANIC (≥ 20% organic, ≥ 10% rinse-off; ≥ 95% PPAI organic; ≤ 2% petrochemical moieties), NATURAL, CERTIFIED, APPROVED.
- COSMOS: no ethoxylation/propoxylation (no PEGs, polysorbates, SLES), no GMOs, no irradiation, no mineral oil or silicones; preservatives from a short list (benzoic, sorbic, dehydroacetic, salicylic acids and salts, benzyl alcohol).
- Natural emulsifiers: glyceryl stearate, sucrose and polyglyceryl esters, glucosides, olivates, lecithin; thickeners: xanthan, guar, gellan, clays.
- Challenges: variability, adulteration, microbial load, preservation, contaminants, stability, colour/odour, solubility, sensory gaps, allergen and phototoxicity myths, evidence, regulation, sustainability.
