# Unit 5: Declaration of Helsinki

The Declaration of Helsinki is the World Medical Association's (WMA) statement of ethical principles for medical research involving human participants. It is the best-known ethical code for clinical research worldwide: ICH Good Clinical Practice says trials should follow principles that have their origin in it, Indian rules and ICMR guidelines are built on it, and journals ask authors to state that their human studies conformed to it. The syllabus asks for its history, introduction, the basic principles for all medical research, and the additional principles for research combined with medical care. Those three headings come from the 2000 version; the current version (2024) arranges the same ideas under more detailed headings, and both arrangements are explained here.

This unit restates the principles in plain words for study. For any formal use, read the current official text on the WMA website.

## 5.1 The World Medical Association

The WMA is an international confederation of national medical associations, founded in 1947 after the Second World War to set ethical and professional standards for physicians. Its main ethical documents are the Declaration of Geneva (1948, the physician's pledge), the International Code of Medical Ethics (1949) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964).

## 5.2 History

**Background.** The Nuremberg Code (1947) was written by judges after the trial of Nazi doctors. It stressed consent but was a legal response to atrocities, did not address patients who cannot consent, and was not written by the medical profession. Physicians needed their own code for research carried out alongside patient care. The WMA drafted one through the late 1950s and early 1960s.

**Adoption and revisions:**

| Year | Place | Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| June 1964 | 18th General Assembly, **Helsinki, Finland** | Adopted. Distinguished clinical (therapeutic) research combined with care from non-therapeutic research on volunteers |
| 1975 | Tokyo, Japan | Major revision: introduced review of the protocol by an **independent committee**, stronger consent, and the principle that the interests of science and society must never take precedence over the well-being of the participant |
| 1983 | Venice, Italy | Minor amendment |
| 1989 | Hong Kong | Minor amendment |
| 1996 | Somerset West, South Africa | Minor amendment; placebo wording in the context of trials where no proven method exists |
| 2000 | Edinburgh, Scotland | Major restructuring into **A. Introduction, B. Basic principles for all medical research, C. Additional principles for medical research combined with medical care**; stricter placebo rule; post-trial access; duty to publish negative results |
| 2002 | Washington DC, USA | Note of clarification on placebo use (paragraph 29) |
| 2004 | Tokyo, Japan | Note of clarification on post-study access (paragraph 30) |
| 2008 | Seoul, South Korea | Revision: registration of trials, vulnerable groups, research on identifiable material and data |
| 2013 | Fortaleza, Brazil | Revision into thematic headings; compensation and treatment for research-related harm; post-trial provisions |
| October 2024 | 75th General Assembly, **Helsinki, Finland** | Current version, 60 years after adoption: 37 paragraphs; speaks of "participants" and "free and informed consent"; stronger attention to vulnerability of individuals, groups and communities, and to community engagement |

**Why it keeps changing:** new research settings (multinational trials in developing countries), controversy over placebo-controlled trials, demands for post-trial access, data and biobank research, and the need to keep pace with ICH-GCP and national laws.

## 5.3 Introduction (purpose and scope)

The introductory part explains what the Declaration is and to whom it applies:

1. It is a statement of ethical principles for **medical research involving human participants, including research on identifiable human material and data**.
2. It is addressed primarily to **physicians**, but others involved in medical research (pharmacists, scientists, nurses, sponsors) are encouraged to adopt these principles.
3. It should be read as a whole; each paragraph is applied with consideration of all others.
4. The physician's duty to promote and safeguard the health, well-being and rights of patients, including those in research, comes first — reflecting the Declaration of Geneva ("the health of my patient will be my first consideration").
5. Medical progress depends on research, which ultimately must include studies in humans; its primary purpose is to understand the causes, development and effects of disease and to improve prevention, diagnosis and treatment.
6. **The goal of generating new knowledge can never take precedence over the rights and interests of individual participants.**
7. Ethical standards in the Declaration are a minimum. National or international ethical, legal or regulatory requirements must not reduce or eliminate any of its protections.

## 5.4 Basic principles for all medical research

These apply to every study, whether or not it is combined with care. They are grouped below under the headings used in the current text.

### 5.4.1 General principles

- Physicians involved in research must protect the **life, health, dignity, integrity, autonomy, privacy and confidentiality** of participants.
- Responsibility for protecting participants always rests with the physician or researcher, **never with the participant**, even though the participant has consented.
- Research must be conducted only by individuals with appropriate **ethics and scientific education, training and qualifications**; research on patients requires supervision by a competent, qualified physician or health professional.
- Groups that are under-represented in research should be given appropriate access to participation.
- Research must be designed and conducted in a way that minimises possible harm to the **environment**, and the welfare of animals used in research must be respected.
- Research must comply with local laws and with ethical norms, but no national requirement may weaken the protections of the Declaration.
- Participants harmed as a result of research must receive appropriate **compensation and treatment**.

### 5.4.2 Risks, burdens and benefits

- Research involving humans may be done only if the **importance of the objective outweighs the risks and burdens** to participants.
- Every study must be preceded by a careful **assessment of predictable risks and burdens** compared with foreseeable benefits to participants and to others affected.
- Measures to minimise risk must be built in, and risks must be **continuously monitored, assessed and documented**.
- Physicians may not take part unless they are confident that risks have been adequately assessed and can be managed. When risks are found to outweigh benefits, or there is conclusive proof of results, the researcher must decide whether to **continue, modify or stop** the study immediately.

### 5.4.3 Vulnerability

- Some individuals, groups and communities are more vulnerable (for example those unable to consent, people in dependent relationships, the very poor, prisoners, children) and may face increased risk of being wronged or harmed. They need **specifically considered protection**.
- Research with a vulnerable group is justified only if it responds to that group's health needs or priorities, cannot be done in a less vulnerable group, and the group stands to benefit from the knowledge or interventions that result.
- At the same time, excluding vulnerable groups entirely can deprive them of the benefits of research; exclusion must also be justified.

### 5.4.4 Scientific requirements and research protocols

- Research must conform to **generally accepted scientific principles**, based on thorough knowledge of the scientific literature, other relevant sources, adequate laboratory and, where appropriate, animal experimentation.
- Poor science is unethical, because it exposes participants to risk without producing reliable knowledge.
- The design and performance of each study must be described in a **research protocol**, stating the ethical considerations, funding, sponsors, institutional affiliations, potential conflicts of interest, incentives for participants, provisions for treating and compensating injury, and post-trial provisions in clinical trials.

### 5.4.5 Research ethics committees

- The protocol must be submitted for consideration, comment, guidance and approval to a **research ethics committee before the study begins**.
- The committee must be transparent, independent of the researcher and the sponsor and free from undue influence, qualified, and must respect local laws as well as international norms.
- It has the **right to monitor ongoing studies**; researchers must provide monitoring information, especially serious adverse events.
- **No amendment to the protocol may be made without the committee's review and approval.** At the end of the study, a final report with findings and conclusions is submitted to the committee.

### 5.4.6 Privacy and confidentiality

Every precaution must be taken to protect the privacy of participants and the confidentiality of their personal information.

### 5.4.7 Free and informed consent

- Participation by individuals capable of giving informed consent must be **voluntary**. Family or community leaders may be consulted, but no capable individual may be enrolled without his or her own free agreement.
- Each potential participant must be adequately informed of the aims, methods, funding, conflicts of interest, institutional affiliations, expected benefits and risks, discomforts, post-trial provisions and any other relevant aspects, and of the **right to refuse or withdraw at any time without reprisal**. Information must be suited to the individual's needs, and the physician must check understanding.
- Consent should preferably be **in writing**; if not, non-written consent must be formally documented and witnessed.
- Participants should be offered information on the general outcome and results of the study.
- If the potential participant is in a **dependent relationship** with the physician, or may consent under duress, consent should be sought by an appropriately qualified person independent of that relationship.
- For a person **incapable of consent**, consent is sought from the legally authorised representative. Such persons must not be included in research unlikely to benefit them unless it aims to promote the health of the group they represent, cannot be done with capable persons, and involves only minimal risk and burden.
- A person incapable of consenting who is nevertheless able to agree (a child) gives **assent**, which must be sought in addition to the representative's consent; their **dissent should be respected**.
- Research with participants physically or mentally incapable of consenting (unconscious patients) may be done only if the condition that prevents consent is a necessary characteristic of the research group; the ethics committee must approve, and consent is obtained from the participant or representative as soon as possible.
- Refusal to take part or a decision to withdraw must **never adversely affect the patient–physician relationship**.
- For research using **identifiable human material or data** (biobanks, records), consent for collection, storage and re-use is normally required; exceptions are allowed only after ethics committee consideration and approval where consent is impossible or impracticable.

### 5.4.8 Registration, publication and dissemination of results

- Every research study involving human participants must be **registered in a publicly accessible database before recruitment of the first participant** (in India, the Clinical Trials Registry – India, CTRI).
- Researchers, authors, sponsors, editors and publishers all have ethical obligations about publication: reports must be complete and accurate; **negative and inconclusive as well as positive results** must be published or made public; funding sources, affiliations and conflicts of interest must be declared.
- Reports of research not in accordance with the Declaration should not be accepted for publication.

## 5.5 Additional principles for medical research combined with medical care

These apply when a physician combines research with the treatment of patients (clinical research on patients who also need care). They are the heart of the original 1964 Declaration and were grouped as Section C in 2000.

### 5.5.1 Justification

A physician may combine research with medical care **only to the extent that the research is justified by its potential preventive, diagnostic or therapeutic value**, and when the physician has good reason to believe that participation will not adversely affect the health of the patients who serve as participants.

### 5.5.2 Comparison with the best proven intervention and use of placebo

The benefits, risks, burdens and effectiveness of a new intervention must be tested against those of the **best proven intervention(s)**. Placebo, or no intervention, is acceptable only:

1. where **no proven intervention exists**; or
2. where, for **compelling and scientifically sound methodological reasons**, a placebo (or a less effective intervention, or no intervention) is necessary to determine efficacy or safety, **and** the patients who receive it will not be subject to additional risk of serious or irreversible harm as a result of not receiving the best proven intervention.

Extreme care must be taken to avoid abuse of this option.

```text
Is there a proven effective treatment for the condition?
├── No  → placebo control is acceptable
└── Yes → compare with the best proven treatment
          Placebo allowed only if:
          compelling scientific reason  AND  no risk of serious or irreversible harm
          (example: short trial in mild seasonal allergic rhinitis with rescue medication)
          Otherwise: active control, or add-on design (standard therapy + test drug or placebo)
```

This paragraph is one of the most debated in research ethics. Regulators sometimes prefer placebo-controlled trials as more scientifically sensitive; the Declaration sets ethical limits on them (Unit 1, placebo).

### 5.5.3 Post-trial provisions

In advance of a clinical trial, sponsors, researchers and host country governments should make provisions for **post-trial access** for all participants who still need an intervention identified as beneficial and reasonably safe in the trial. Exceptions must be approved by the ethics committee, and the information must be disclosed to participants during informed consent. This protects participants, especially in low-income settings, from being left without a treatment that helped them once the trial ends.

### 5.5.4 Informing the patient and freedom to refuse

The physician must fully inform patients which aspects of their care are related to the research. A patient's refusal to participate, or decision to withdraw, must never interfere with the patient–physician relationship or with the standard care they receive.

### 5.5.5 Unproven interventions in clinical practice

In the treatment of an individual patient, where proven interventions do not exist or other known interventions have been ineffective, the physician — after seeking expert advice, with informed consent from the patient or representative — may use an **unproven intervention** if in the physician's judgement it offers hope of saving life, re-establishing health or alleviating suffering. Such an intervention should subsequently be made the object of research designed to evaluate its safety and efficacy, and new information recorded and, where appropriate, made publicly available. This is the ethical basis for "compassionate use" of unapproved drugs.

## 5.6 The original 1964 structure (for comparison)

| 1964 heading | Main ideas |
| --- | --- |
| Basic principles | Conform to scientific principles; qualified persons; proportionality of objective to risk; risk assessment; caution with personality-altering research |
| Clinical research combined with professional care | Doctor free to use new measures if they offer hope; consent where possible; research only justified by therapeutic value for the patient |
| Non-therapeutic clinical research | Physician remains protector of the participant; full explanation and free consent of volunteers; right to withdraw; stop if harmful |

The 2000 version replaced the "therapeutic versus non-therapeutic" division with "all research" and "research combined with care", because the old division suggested that patients in therapeutic research needed less protection.

## 5.7 Declaration of Helsinki, ICH-GCP and Indian rules

| Aspect | Declaration of Helsinki | ICH E6 GCP | India |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Ethical statement of the medical profession (not a law) | International technical guideline for trial conduct and data quality | Law (New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019) and national guidelines (ICMR 2017, Indian GCP) |
| Focus | Rights and welfare of participants | Ethics plus quality, roles of sponsor, investigator, monitor, documentation | Approval by CDSCO, registered ethics committees, consent, compensation, CTRI registration |
| Ethics committee | Independent review before start, monitoring, amendments | IRB/IEC responsibilities and composition | Registered EC with defined composition |
| Placebo | Restricted as in 5.5.2 | Refers to ethical principles | Follows ICMR guidance consistent with Helsinki |
| Compensation for injury | Required | Sponsor's responsibility per local law | Statutory compensation for trial-related injury or death |

## 5.8 Remember

- WMA (1947); Declaration of Helsinki adopted **June 1964 at Helsinki** (18th General Assembly); revised 1975 (Tokyo — independent committee review), 1983, 1989, 1996, **2000 Edinburgh (A/B/C structure)**, 2002 and 2004 notes of clarification, 2008 Seoul, 2013 Fortaleza, **2024 Helsinki (current, 37 paragraphs)**.
- Introduction: addressed mainly to physicians; covers identifiable human material and data; knowledge never takes precedence over participants' rights.
- Basic principles: protect life, health, dignity and privacy; responsibility on the researcher, not the participant; sound science and protocol; prior ethics committee approval and monitoring; risk–benefit; vulnerable groups; free, informed, preferably written consent; assent; registration before first enrolment; publish negative results.
- Research combined with care: justified only by potential value to patients; compare with best proven intervention; placebo only if no proven treatment or compelling reasons with no serious or irreversible harm; post-trial provisions; refusal never affects care; unproven interventions as last resort with consent.
