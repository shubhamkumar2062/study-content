# Unit 1: General research methodology

This unit covers how a research question becomes a sound study: what research is, how objectives are framed, what a researcher needs, the practical difficulties faced in Indian laboratories and hospitals, how to review the literature, and how study design, controls, randomization, crossover designs, placebo and blinding protect a study from error and bias. Your M.Pharm dissertation protocol (presented in the same semester) uses every idea in this unit.

All numbers in the worked examples are example numbers for practice, not real data.

## 1.1 Research: meaning and characteristics

**Research** is a careful, systematic and objective search for new knowledge, or for a new way of using existing knowledge, carried out by a planned method so that others can check and repeat it. In pharmacy it ranges from making a new nanoparticle formulation to comparing two antihypertensive drugs in patients.

Characteristics of good research:

| Characteristic | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Systematic | Follows planned steps; nothing left to chance |
| Logical and objective | Conclusions follow from evidence, not opinion |
| Empirical | Based on observation or experiment |
| Replicable | Method described well enough to be repeated |
| Valid | Measures what it claims to measure (internal validity: free of bias; external validity: applies beyond the sample) |
| Reliable | Gives consistent results on repetition |
| Ethical | Protects human participants, animals and data integrity |

### 1.1.1 Types of research

| Basis | Types and pharmacy examples |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | **Basic (fundamental)**: new knowledge (mechanism of drug permeation); **applied**: solves a practical problem (taste-masking a paediatric syrup) |
| Approach | **Quantitative** (numbers, statistics: dissolution, blood pressure); **qualitative** (meanings, interviews: patients' reasons for non-adherence); **mixed methods** |
| Method | **Descriptive** (what exists: prescribing survey); **analytical** (why: risk factors); **experimental** (investigator assigns the intervention) |
| Time | **Cross-sectional** (one point in time); **longitudinal** (followed over time); **retrospective** (looks back at records); **prospective** (follows forward) |
| Setting | Laboratory (in vitro), pre-clinical (animal), clinical (human), field or community |
| Others | Conceptual vs empirical; exploratory (generates hypotheses) vs confirmatory (tests a pre-stated hypothesis); translational ("bench to bedside") |

### 1.1.2 The research process

```text
Identify a problem → Review the literature → Define the problem and objectives
      ↓
Formulate hypothesis (H0 and H1) → Choose study design → Decide sample and sample size
      ↓
Write protocol → Ethics approval (IEC / IAEC) → Collect data (pilot first)
      ↓
Analyse data (statistics) → Test hypothesis → Interpret and generalise → Report and publish
```

The steps loop back: a pilot study may change the design, and the literature review is updated until writing ends.

## 1.2 Research objective

The **aim** is the broad purpose ("to develop a gastro-retentive tablet of drug X"). **Objectives** are the specific, measurable steps that achieve it. Good objectives are **SMART**: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

- **Primary objective:** the single main question that decides the sample size and the main conclusion (for example, "to compare the floating lag time and 12-hour drug release of formulations F1 to F9").
- **Secondary objectives:** supporting questions (stability, release kinetics, effect of polymer ratio).
- **Exploratory objectives:** ideas for future work; never used to claim success.

A **research question** is often framed with **PICO**: Population (adult hypertensive patients), Intervention (new fixed-dose combination), Comparison (standard therapy), Outcome (fall in systolic pressure at 8 weeks). For laboratory work the same logic applies: system, factor varied, control, response.

A **hypothesis** is a testable prediction. The **null hypothesis (H0)** states no difference or no relationship; the **alternative hypothesis (H1)** states that there is one (Unit 2).

## 1.3 Requirements of research

1. **A clear, feasible problem** — important, original and answerable with available resources.
2. **Knowledge of the field** — through an up-to-date literature review.
3. **Sound design and methodology** — appropriate design, controls, sample size and statistics fixed before data collection.
4. **Resources** — funds (UGC, AICTE, DST, ICMR, DBT, industry), equipment, chemicals, animals or patients, library and software access.
5. **Skilled people** — guide, co-investigators, statistician, technicians.
6. **Time plan** — a Gantt chart for each stage.
7. **Ethical and regulatory clearance** — Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) for humans, Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) for animals, biosafety committee for hazardous material, and CDSCO permission for regulated clinical trials.
8. **Good documentation** — laboratory notebooks, raw data and audit trail, so that results are traceable.
9. **Honesty** — no fabrication, falsification or plagiarism; proper credit to others' work.

## 1.4 Practical difficulties in research

| Difficulty | Example in pharmacy research | Way out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Poor problem selection | Repeating a topic done many times | Thorough literature search; discuss with guide |
| Lack of funds and equipment | No access to particle size analyser or LC-MS | Collaboration, central instrument facilities, grants |
| Limited access to literature | Paywalled journals | Institutional databases, open-access sources, interlibrary loan |
| Delay in approvals | IEC or IAEC meeting cycles, CDSCO timelines | Plan early; submit complete protocols |
| Recruitment and dropouts | Patients do not return for follow-up | Realistic sample size with dropout allowance, reminders, convenient visits |
| Variability and error | Batch-to-batch excipient variation, analyst error | SOPs, calibration, randomization, replication |
| Inadequate statistical knowledge | Wrong test applied | Plan analysis with a statistician before collecting data |
| Time pressure | Two-semester dissertation | Pilot early, realistic objectives |
| Ethical constraints | Placebo not acceptable, vulnerable groups | Active control, extra safeguards |
| Publication barriers | Negative results not accepted | Register studies, publish in journals accepting negative results |

## 1.5 Review of literature

The **review of literature** is a critical summary of what is already known about the problem. It is not a list of abstracts; it groups studies by theme, compares methods and findings and ends by showing the **gap** your study will fill.

**Purposes:** avoid duplication; refine the question and hypothesis; borrow validated methods and outcome measures; find the expected effect size and variability for sample size calculation; anticipate problems; place your results in context in the discussion.

**Sources:**

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | Original research papers, theses, patents, conference proceedings |
| Secondary | Review articles, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, monographs, textbooks |
| Tertiary | Encyclopaedias, pharmacopoeias (IP, BP, USP), drug compendia, handbooks |
| Databases | PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, patent databases, Clinical Trials Registry – India (CTRI), ClinicalTrials.gov |

**Search strategy:**

1. Break the question into key concepts (PICO).
2. List synonyms and controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms in PubMed).
3. Combine with **Boolean operators**: AND narrows ("floating tablet" AND famotidine), OR widens (nanoparticle OR nanocarrier), NOT excludes; use truncation (formulat*) and quotation marks for phrases.
4. Apply limits (years, language, human or animal).
5. Screen titles, then abstracts, then full texts against inclusion criteria; record numbers at each step.
6. Manage references with software (Mendeley, Zotero, EndNote) and cite in a standard style (Vancouver numbered style in most pharmacy journals).

**Critical appraisal** asks: Was the question clear? Was the design appropriate? Were groups comparable and randomized? Was assessment blinded? Were dropouts handled? Are the statistics right and the conclusions justified? Is there a conflict of interest?

A **systematic review** follows a pre-registered protocol, searches exhaustively, appraises every study and reports with the PRISMA flow diagram. A **meta-analysis** pools the numerical results of comparable studies to give one weighted estimate (shown as a forest plot), with checks for heterogeneity and publication bias (funnel plot).

### Hierarchy of evidence

```text
          Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs
                Randomized controlled trials
                     Cohort studies
                  Case–control studies
          Cross-sectional studies, case series, case reports
       Expert opinion, animal and in vitro studies (pre-clinical)
```

Higher levels are less prone to bias, but the right design depends on the question: harm from a rare exposure is studied by case–control, not by an RCT.

## 1.6 Study design and types of studies

The **study design** is the overall plan that decides who is studied, how they are grouped, what is measured and when. The key division is whether the investigator assigns the exposure (experimental) or only observes (observational).

```text
Studies
├── Observational (investigator only observes)
│   ├── Descriptive: case report, case series, cross-sectional survey, ecological study
│   └── Analytical: case–control, cohort (prospective or retrospective), analytical cross-sectional
└── Experimental (investigator assigns intervention)
    ├── Pre-clinical: in vitro, in vivo (animal)
    ├── Clinical: randomized controlled trial (parallel, crossover, factorial, cluster), non-randomized trial
    └── Quasi-experimental: before–after, controlled before–after
```

### 1.6.1 Observational studies

| Design | How it works | Measure of association | Strengths | Weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case report / series | Describes one or a few patients (new adverse reaction) | None | Early signal, cheap | No comparison group |
| Cross-sectional | Exposure and outcome measured at the same time (prevalence of self-medication among students) | Prevalence, prevalence ratio | Quick, cheap | Cannot show which came first |
| Ecological | Groups (districts, countries), not individuals | Correlation | Uses existing data | Ecological fallacy |
| Case–control | Starts with people who have the disease (cases) and without (controls); looks back for exposure | **Odds ratio** | Good for rare diseases, quick | Recall and selection bias |
| Cohort | Starts with exposed and unexposed people without the disease; follows forward for outcome | **Relative risk**, incidence | Shows time order, several outcomes | Long, costly, losses to follow-up; poor for rare diseases |

**Worked example (cohort).** 200 workers exposed to a solvent: 30 develop dermatitis; 400 unexposed: 20 develop it. Risk in exposed = 30/200 = 0.15; in unexposed = 20/400 = 0.05. **Relative risk = 0.15/0.05 = 3.0**: exposed workers have three times the risk.

**Worked example (case–control).** 100 cases of liver injury: 40 used a herbal product. 200 controls: 40 used it. Odds of exposure: cases 40/60, controls 40/160. **Odds ratio = (40 × 160)/(60 × 40) = 2.67**.

### 1.6.2 Experimental studies and the randomized controlled trial

In a **randomized controlled trial (RCT)** eligible participants are randomly allocated to intervention or control, followed under the same conditions, and outcomes are compared. Randomization balances known and unknown factors, so a difference in outcome can be attributed to the intervention. The RCT is the gold standard for efficacy.

**Worked example (RCT measures).** Stroke in 2 years: control 40/200 (control event rate CER = 0.20), new drug 24/200 (experimental event rate EER = 0.12).

| Measure | Formula | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relative risk (RR) | EER/CER | 0.12/0.20 = **0.60** |
| Relative risk reduction (RRR) | 1 − RR | **40%** |
| Absolute risk reduction (ARR) | CER − EER | **0.08 (8%)** |
| Number needed to treat (NNT) | 1/ARR | 12.5, rounded up to **13** |

Thirteen patients must be treated for two years to prevent one stroke. NNT is always rounded up.

Variants of the RCT:

- **Parallel group:** each participant gets one treatment throughout.
- **Crossover:** each participant gets all treatments in sequence (section 1.9).
- **Factorial:** two or more interventions tested together (2 × 2: A alone, B alone, both, neither).
- **Cluster randomized:** whole wards, schools or villages are randomized; used when the intervention acts on a group.
- **Superiority, equivalence and non-inferiority trials:** decide in advance whether the aim is to show the new treatment is better, the same within a margin, or not worse by more than a pre-set margin.
- **Adaptive designs:** pre-planned changes (dropping an arm, re-estimating sample size) based on interim analysis.

**Phases of clinical trials** (New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019, and ICH): Phase I (safety, tolerability and pharmacokinetics, usually in healthy volunteers), Phase II (exploratory efficacy and dose finding in patients), Phase III (confirmatory efficacy and safety in large numbers), Phase IV (post-marketing studies). Bioequivalence studies of generics are usually crossover studies in healthy volunteers.

## 1.7 Errors and bias, and strategies to eliminate them

### 1.7.1 Random error and systematic error

- **Random error** is chance variation (measurement noise, biological variation). It makes results imprecise but not consistently wrong. It is reduced by **larger sample size, replication and precise instruments**, and is quantified by confidence intervals and P values.
- **Systematic error (bias)** pushes results consistently in one direction. A bigger sample does **not** remove it; only good design does.

```text
           Precise                 Imprecise
Unbiased   tight cluster on target  scattered around target
Biased     tight cluster off target scattered and off target
```

### 1.7.2 Types of bias

| Bias | What happens | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selection bias | Groups differ at the start (sicker patients given the new drug) | Randomization, allocation concealment, clear eligibility criteria |
| Allocation (channelling) bias | Investigator knows the next allocation and steers patients | Concealment: central or web randomization, sealed opaque envelopes, coded identical packs |
| Performance bias | Groups receive different care apart from the intervention | Blinding of participants and carers, standard protocol |
| Detection (observer, measurement) bias | Outcome assessed differently according to group | Blinding of assessors, objective end points, calibrated instruments |
| Attrition bias | Dropouts differ between groups | Minimise losses, intention-to-treat analysis |
| Recall bias | Cases remember exposures better than controls | Records instead of memory, structured questionnaires |
| Reporting and publication bias | Only positive outcomes or studies published | Trial registration (CTRI), pre-specified outcomes, publish all results |
| Confounding | A third factor linked to both exposure and outcome (age, smoking) distorts the association | Randomization, restriction, matching, stratification, multivariable analysis |
| Hawthorne effect | Behaviour changes because people know they are observed | Control group observed in the same way |
| Regression to the mean | Extreme baseline values drift back on re-measurement | Concurrent control group, repeated baseline readings |

### 1.7.3 Strategies to eliminate error and bias (summary)

1. **At the design stage:** randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, appropriate controls, restriction (narrow eligibility), matching, adequate sample size, standardised procedures and calibrated instruments, validated analytical methods.
2. **During conduct:** SOPs, training, monitoring, source data verification, keeping losses low, recording all deviations.
3. **At analysis:** pre-specified statistical analysis plan, intention-to-treat, stratified or multivariable analysis for confounders, sensitivity analysis.
4. **At reporting:** follow reporting checklists (CONSORT for RCTs, STROBE for observational studies, ARRIVE for animal studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews); report all outcomes.

**Intention-to-treat (ITT)** analysis analyses every randomized participant in the group to which they were allocated, whether or not they completed treatment; it preserves the balance created by randomization. **Per-protocol** analysis includes only those who followed the protocol; it estimates efficacy under ideal conditions but can be biased. Non-inferiority trials usually report both.

## 1.8 Controls and randomization

### 1.8.1 Controls

A **control** is the comparison that shows what would have happened without the intervention.

| Type of control | Description | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Placebo (negative) control | Inactive look-alike | When no proven treatment exists or delay is safe |
| Active (positive) control | Standard proven treatment | Most trials where effective therapy exists; non-inferiority trials |
| No-treatment control | Observation only | When blinding is impossible or unnecessary |
| Dose–response control | Different doses of the same drug | Dose finding (Phase II) |
| Historical (external) control | Patients treated earlier or elsewhere | Rare diseases; weakest because groups may differ |
| Add-on design | All receive standard therapy, plus test drug or placebo | Ethical use of placebo when standard therapy cannot be withheld |

In laboratory work: a **vehicle control** (solvent alone), a **positive control** (known active compound to show the test system works) and a **blank** in analysis play the same roles. In animal experiments a normal control and a disease (model) control are both used.

### 1.8.2 Randomization

**Randomization** gives each participant a known chance (usually equal) of receiving each treatment, decided by chance and not by anyone's choice.

Why randomize: it removes selection bias, balances known and unknown confounders on average, and justifies the statistical tests used.

| Method | How | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple | Coin, random number table, computer list for each participant | Groups may be unequal in small trials |
| Block | Random order within blocks (block of 4: AABB, ABAB, ABBA, BAAB, BABA, BBAA) | Keeps group sizes equal over time; vary block size to prevent guessing |
| Stratified | Separate block lists within strata (age, sex, centre, disease severity) | Balances important prognostic factors |
| Cluster | Groups (wards, villages) randomized | Needs larger samples (design effect) |
| Minimization | Each new patient allocated to the arm that best balances factors, with a random element | Useful in small trials with many factors |
| Unequal (2:1) | More participants on new drug | Gives more safety data |

Randomization must be combined with **allocation concealment**: the person enrolling cannot know or change the next allocation. Concealment protects the randomization before allocation; blinding protects the comparison after allocation.

## 1.9 Crossover design

In a **crossover design** each participant receives every treatment in sequence, separated by a **washout period**, and so acts as their own control.

```text
Two-period, two-sequence (2 × 2, AB/BA) crossover

Sequence 1:  Randomize → Period 1: A → Washout → Period 2: B
Sequence 2:  Randomize → Period 1: B → Washout → Period 2: A
```

**Advantages:** removes between-subject variation, so fewer participants are needed; each subject compares treatments directly; standard design for bioavailability and bioequivalence studies (test vs reference product).

**Requirements and problems:**

- **Stable, chronic condition** — the disease must return to baseline (asthma, hypertension, pain), so it is unsuitable for curative treatments or acute illness.
- **Carryover effect** — effect of the first treatment persists into the second period. Prevented by an adequate washout; in bioequivalence studies this is usually at least five elimination half-lives of the drug.
- **Period effect** — outcomes differ between periods regardless of treatment (season, learning). Balanced by randomizing the sequence.
- **Sequence (treatment × period interaction)** — checked statistically.
- **Dropouts** are costly, because a subject who completes only one period contributes little.
- Longer total duration for each participant.

Extensions: three-period designs (ABB/BAA), Latin square designs for three or more products, and replicate designs (each product given twice) for highly variable drugs.

Analysis of a crossover uses the within-subject differences (paired t-test or ANOVA with subject, period, sequence and treatment as factors).

## 1.10 Placebo

A **placebo** is an inert preparation made to look, taste and feel identical to the active treatment. The **placebo effect** is an improvement that follows from receiving any treatment and the expectation of benefit; the **nocebo effect** is the reporting of side effects due to negative expectation.

**Uses:** separates the specific drug effect from placebo effect, natural course and regression to the mean; allows blinding; gives the most sensitive test of efficacy; helps judge whether reported adverse effects are drug-related.

**Ethics of placebo** (Unit 5): a placebo is acceptable when no proven treatment exists, or when compelling and sound methodological reasons require it and patients face no added risk of serious or irreversible harm. Otherwise the new treatment should be compared with the best proven one, or placebo should be used as an add-on to standard care. Participants must be told in the consent form that they may receive placebo, and **rescue medication** and clear withdrawal rules are provided.

Related terms: **sham procedure** (imitation surgery or device use), **double-dummy** technique (when two treatments look different, each group takes one active and one placebo of the other's form, so all take identical-looking regimens).

## 1.11 Blinding (masking) techniques

**Blinding** means keeping one or more parties unaware of which treatment each participant receives, so that knowledge cannot influence behaviour, care, assessment or analysis.

| Level | Who is unaware | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open-label | Nobody blinded | When blinding is impossible (surgery vs drug); use objective outcomes |
| Single-blind | Usually the participant | Reduces placebo and reporting effects |
| Double-blind | Participant and investigator/outcome assessor | Standard for drug trials |
| Triple-blind | Also the data analyst or monitoring committee | Protects the analysis |
| PROBE | Prospective, Randomized, Open, Blinded End-point: treatment open, assessor blinded | Practical for long outcome trials |

**How blinding is achieved:**

1. Identical appearance, size, colour, taste and packaging of test, comparator and placebo (over-encapsulation of a marketed tablet in an opaque capsule).
2. Double-dummy technique when dosage forms differ.
3. Coded containers prepared by an independent pharmacist; the code list held by a third party.
4. Central assessment of outcomes (ECG, imaging) by blinded readers.
5. Emergency unblinding procedure (sealed code envelopes or a 24-hour system) for serious adverse events, with documentation of every code break.

**Checking blinding:** at the end, participants and assessors may be asked which treatment they think was given. Distinctive side effects (dry mouth, flushing) can unblind a trial.

## 1.12 Remember

- Research = systematic, objective, replicable search for knowledge; aim → SMART objectives → hypothesis (H0, H1); frame with PICO.
- Literature review shows the gap; use databases, MeSH, Boolean AND/OR/NOT; systematic review uses PRISMA; meta-analysis gives a forest plot.
- Observational: cross-sectional (prevalence), case–control (backward, odds ratio, rare diseases), cohort (forward, relative risk). Experimental: RCT is the gold standard.
- RR = EER/CER; RRR = 1 − RR; ARR = CER − EER; NNT = 1/ARR rounded up.
- Random error falls with sample size; bias does not — only design removes bias.
- Randomization (simple, block, stratified, cluster, minimization) + allocation concealment prevent selection bias; blinding prevents performance and detection bias; ITT prevents attrition bias.
- Crossover: each subject is own control; needs stable disease and adequate washout; watch carryover, period and sequence effects; standard for bioequivalence.
- Placebo only when no proven therapy or compelling reasons with no serious risk; double-dummy for different dosage forms.
- Blinding levels: open, single, double, triple, PROBE; keep an emergency unblinding system.
