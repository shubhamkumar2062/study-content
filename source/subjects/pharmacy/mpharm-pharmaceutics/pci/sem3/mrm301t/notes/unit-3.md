# Unit 3: Medical research and medical ethics

Medical ethics is the set of moral principles that guide clinical practice and research involving people. Every M.Pharm student who runs a clinical, pharmacy-practice or survey study must have it approved by an ethics committee, obtain informed consent and protect data, so this unit is practical as well as theoretical. The syllabus list follows the standard headings of medical ethics; Indian law and guidelines are added where they apply.

## 3.1 Medical research and the need for ethics

**Medical research** is systematic investigation in human beings, or using human data and tissue, to improve the understanding, prevention, diagnosis and treatment of disease. It includes clinical trials, observational studies, pharmacokinetic and bioequivalence studies, surveys, and research on stored samples and records.

Research differs from treatment: in treatment the only goal is the patient's benefit; in research the goal is generalisable knowledge, and the participant may bear risk for others' benefit. This conflict is why research needs independent review, consent and safeguards.

## 3.2 History of medical ethics and research ethics

| Period / event | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Ancient | Hippocratic Oath (Greece): do no harm, confidentiality, duty to the patient. Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita (India) laid down the conduct expected of a physician |
| 1803 | Thomas Percival's *Medical Ethics* (England) — the term enters common use |
| 1847 | American Medical Association's first Code of Ethics |
| 1947 | **Nuremberg Code** — after the Doctors' Trial of Nazi physicians who conducted cruel experiments on prisoners; ten principles, the first being that voluntary consent is absolutely essential |
| 1948 | World Medical Association (WMA) **Declaration of Geneva** — a modern physician's pledge |
| 1950s–60s | Thalidomide tragedy — birth defects led to stronger drug regulation and testing |
| 1964 | **Declaration of Helsinki** (WMA) — ethical principles for medical research (Unit 5) |
| 1932–1972 | **Tuskegee syphilis study** (USA) — treatment withheld from Black men for decades without informed consent; its exposure led to US reforms |
| 1979 | **Belmont Report** (USA): respect for persons, beneficence, justice. Beauchamp and Childress publish the four principles of biomedical ethics |
| 1982 onward | CIOMS international guidelines (Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences, with WHO) for research in low-resource settings |
| 1996 | ICH E6 Good Clinical Practice guideline (revised as E6(R2) in 2016 and E6(R3) in 2025) |
| India | ICMR Policy Statement (1980); ICMR Ethical Guidelines (2000, 2006); **ICMR National Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical and Health Research Involving Human Participants (2017)**; Schedule Y of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules (revised 2005); **New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019** (current law for clinical trials); Indian GCP guidelines from CDSCO |

## 3.3 Values in medical ethics

The widely used framework has four principles, often extended with two more.

| Value | Meaning | Example in practice or research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Autonomy** | Respect for the right of a competent person to make informed decisions about their own body | Patient may refuse surgery; participant may withdraw from a trial at any time without penalty |
| **Beneficence** | Act in the best interest of the patient; promote well-being | Choose the most effective therapy; design research to give useful results |
| **Non-maleficence** | "First, do no harm" — avoid causing harm or exposing to unjustified risk | Minimise invasive procedures, monitor adverse events, stop a trial if harm emerges |
| **Justice** | Fairness in distributing benefits, burdens and scarce resources | Do not recruit only the poor for risky trials; fair selection; post-trial access |
| **Dignity** | Patient and practitioner both have a right to dignity | Privacy during examination, respectful language |
| **Truthfulness and honesty** | Patients should be told the truth; basis of informed consent | Honest disclosure of diagnosis and of research risks |

The principles do not rank automatically. Real cases require weighing them; when they clash, the conflict is resolved by discussion, consultation and (where needed) an ethics committee.

## 3.4 Principle of double effect

The **doctrine of double effect** holds that an action with one good and one bad effect may be ethical if:

1. The act itself is good or morally neutral.
2. The good effect is intended; the bad effect is foreseen but not intended.
3. The bad effect is not the means of achieving the good effect.
4. There is a proportionate reason — the good outweighs the bad.

**Classic example:** giving opioid analgesics to relieve severe pain in a terminally ill patient, knowing that high doses may hasten death by respiratory depression. Relief of suffering is the aim; earlier death is a foreseen side effect, not the purpose. Other examples: chemotherapy in pregnancy that may harm the fetus, palliative sedation in the last days of life.

## 3.5 Conflicts between autonomy and beneficence/non-maleficence

Autonomy may clash with what the doctor thinks is best for the patient.

| Situation | Conflict | Approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Competent adult refuses a life-saving blood transfusion on religious grounds | Autonomy vs beneficence | Respect informed refusal; explain consequences; document; offer alternatives |
| Patient demands antibiotics for a viral cold | Autonomy vs non-maleficence (resistance, side effects) | Explain; a doctor need not give treatment that is not indicated |
| Patient lacks capacity (unconscious, severe dementia, child) | Who decides? | Legally authorised representative or guardian decides in the patient's best interest; previous wishes (advance directive) respected |
| Suicidal patient refuses treatment | Autonomy may be impaired by illness | Capacity assessment; protective care under mental health law |
| Paternalism | Doctor overrides the patient's choice "for their good" | Generally unacceptable for competent adults; "soft" paternalism limited to protecting those whose choice is not truly informed or voluntary |

**Capacity** means the patient can understand the information, retain it, weigh it, and communicate a decision. Capacity is decision-specific and must be assessed, not assumed absent.

## 3.6 Euthanasia

**Euthanasia** is the deliberate ending of a life to relieve suffering.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Active euthanasia | A positive act (administering a lethal substance) to cause death |
| Passive euthanasia | Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment (ventilator, artificial feeding), allowing death from the illness |
| Voluntary | At the request of a competent patient |
| Non-voluntary | Patient cannot express a wish (coma); decision by others |
| Involuntary | Against the patient's wishes — regarded as homicide everywhere |
| Physician-assisted suicide | Doctor provides the means; the patient performs the final act |

**Arguments for:** autonomy, relief of unbearable suffering, dignity in dying. **Arguments against:** sanctity of life, risk of abuse and pressure on vulnerable people (the "slippery slope"), possible misdiagnosis, availability of palliative care, conflict with the doctor's role as healer.

**Position in India:** active euthanasia and assisted suicide are illegal. The Supreme Court in *Aruna Shanbaug v Union of India* (2011) allowed passive euthanasia under strict safeguards, and in *Common Cause v Union of India* (2018) recognised the right to die with dignity under Article 21 and gave legal force to **advance directives (living wills)** and withdrawal of life support under a procedure involving medical boards; the procedure was simplified by the Court in 2023. Some countries (for example the Netherlands and Belgium) permit active euthanasia under strict laws.

Withdrawing futile treatment in the dying, while giving full palliative care, is not regarded as euthanasia in medical ethics (see futility, section 3.20).

## 3.7 Informed consent

**Informed consent** is a voluntary decision by a competent person to take part in treatment or research after receiving and understanding all relevant information. It is a process, not just a signature.

**Elements:**

1. **Disclosure** — purpose, procedures, duration, foreseeable risks and discomforts, expected benefits, alternatives, confidentiality, compensation for injury, whom to contact, and that participation is voluntary.
2. **Comprehension** — information in simple language the participant understands (regional language in India), with time to ask questions.
3. **Voluntariness** — no coercion or undue inducement; payment should cover time and expenses, not overpower judgement.
4. **Competence (capacity)** to decide.
5. **Documentation** — signed and dated consent form; a copy given to the participant.
6. **Right to withdraw** at any time without loss of benefits.

**Special situations:**

- **Children:** parental or guardian consent plus the child's **assent** where the child can understand (ICMR guidelines suggest assent from about 7 years of age).
- **Illiterate participants:** an impartial literate witness attends the consent discussion and signs.
- **Unconscious or incompetent adults:** consent from the legally acceptable representative.
- **Audio-video recording of consent** — required by Indian rules for certain regulated clinical trials, especially those involving vulnerable participants (as amended since 2013 and carried into the 2019 Rules).
- **Waiver of consent** may be granted by an ethics committee only for minimal-risk research where consent is impracticable (for example, anonymised records), or in specified emergencies with later consent.
- **Re-consent** when new information may affect willingness to continue.

## 3.8 Confidentiality

**Confidentiality** is the duty not to disclose information shared by a patient or participant without permission. It builds trust; without it patients withhold information.

Measures in research: coding or anonymising data, removing identifiers before analysis, restricted access, encrypted storage, limits on data sharing set out in the consent form, and secure destruction after the retention period.

**Justified exceptions** (disclosure limited to what is necessary): notifiable diseases, court orders, serious risk of harm to others (a patient threatening violence, or an infection posing a specific risk to an identified partner, handled carefully under applicable law and guidance), child abuse reporting, and regulatory inspection of trial records (with the participant informed beforehand in the consent form).

Indian law adds duties for patient data: the HIV and AIDS (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017 protects HIV status; the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 governs digital personal data.

## 3.9 Criticisms of orthodox medical ethics

- **Too individualistic** — focuses on the individual patient's autonomy and neglects family and community decision-making common in many cultures, including India.
- **Western origin** — principles developed in Europe and North America may not fit other value systems.
- **Principlism is too abstract** — four principles give no method to settle conflicts between them; critics favour virtue ethics, care ethics or case-based reasoning.
- **Paternalism in practice** — despite talk of autonomy, decisions remain doctor-centred.
- **Neglect of justice and public health** — little attention to access, cost and global inequity.
- **Influence of industry** — guidelines and research priorities shaped by commercial interests.
- **Rigid rules** can obstruct low-risk research (excessive paperwork for surveys).

## 3.10 Importance of communication

Most ethical problems and complaints arise from poor communication. Good communication:

- makes informed consent genuine;
- allows the patient to express values and preferences;
- builds trust, adherence and satisfaction;
- prevents misunderstanding and litigation;
- helps break bad news humanely (the SPIKES approach: Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions and empathy, Strategy and summary).

Use plain language, the patient's own language, open questions, active listening, and check understanding ("teach-back").

## 3.11 Conflict ("control") resolution

When doctors, patients, relatives or team members disagree about care:

1. Clarify the facts and the clinical options.
2. Hold a meeting; listen to all parties and identify their values.
3. Seek a second opinion where useful.
4. Use mediation or a hospital (clinical) ethics committee or ethics consultation service.
5. Document the process and the decision.
6. As a last resort, refer to legal procedures (courts in disputes over withdrawal of life support).

## 3.13 Ethics committees

An **ethics committee (EC)** — Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) or Institutional Review Board (IRB) — is an independent body that reviews research protocols to protect the rights, safety and well-being of participants.

**Functions:** initial review of protocol, consent documents, investigator's qualifications and site; risk–benefit assessment; continuing review (progress reports, amendments, protocol deviations); review of serious adverse events and compensation; monitoring site visits; approval of final report; record keeping.

**Composition in India (NDCT Rules, 2019, and ICMR 2017):** at least seven members, including a medical scientist (preferably a pharmacologist), a clinician, a legal expert, a social scientist or representative of a non-governmental organisation or philosopher, ethicist or theologian, and a lay person from the community. Members should include both genders, at least half should be non-affiliated with the institution, and the chairperson should be from outside the institution. A member secretary (from the institution) runs the office. Members with a conflict of interest withdraw from that decision. ECs reviewing regulated clinical trials must be **registered with the Central Licensing Authority (CDSCO)**; those reviewing other biomedical research register with the Department of Health Research.

**Types of review (ICMR):** exempt (negligible risk), expedited (minimal risk), and full committee review (more than minimal risk).

## 3.14 Cultural concerns

Respect for culture affects consent, disclosure and decision-making:

- Family-centred decisions are common in India; the patient's own consent is still required, but family involvement may be welcome.
- Gender norms may require a female investigator or chaperone.
- Religious beliefs affect acceptance of blood products, gelatin capsules, organ donation and end-of-life care.
- Collection and export of biological samples raise community concerns; benefit sharing should be considered.
- Language and literacy differences require translated and tested consent forms.

Cultural sensitivity does not override core rights: individual consent and protection from harm remain essential.

## 3.15 Truth telling

Patients have a right to honest information about their diagnosis, prognosis and treatment, and research participants about risks. Earlier practice in many countries hid serious diagnoses (such as cancer) "to protect" the patient; this is now regarded as unjustified paternalism. Truth should be told sensitively, at the patient's pace, and the patient may choose not to know and name someone to receive information. The narrow "therapeutic privilege" to withhold information when disclosure itself would cause serious harm is rarely justified. Honesty also includes **disclosure of medical errors** to the patient.

## 3.16 Online business practices

Online consultations, websites and social media raise ethical issues:

- **Telemedicine:** India issued Telemedicine Practice Guidelines in 2020; identification of patient and practitioner, consent, record keeping and limits on which medicines may be prescribed remotely.
- **Advertising and self-promotion:** professional codes forbid misleading claims and soliciting patients; testimonials and paid promotion raise concerns.
- **Online sale of medicines and e-pharmacies:** must follow drug laws; prescription verification is essential.
- **Privacy:** patient photographs or data must not be posted without consent; secure platforms are needed.
- **Online research:** recruitment through social media and online surveys require consent, data protection and honest advertising.

## 3.17 Conflicts of interest

A **conflict of interest** exists when a secondary interest (financial gain, career, personal relationships) could unduly influence professional judgement on a primary interest (patient welfare, validity of research).

Examples: an investigator paid per enrolled patient; holding shares in the sponsor company; consultancy fees; authorship pressure; a reviewer judging a competitor's paper.

Management: **disclosure** to the EC, journal and participants; independent oversight (data safety monitoring board); limiting payments; recusal from decisions; separation of clinical care from recruitment decisions. Journals require a declaration of competing interests in every paper.

## 3.18 Referral

Doctors should refer a patient to another practitioner when the problem lies beyond their competence or facilities, or when the patient asks for a second opinion. Ethical problems include **fee-splitting or "cuts"** (commissions for referring patients to laboratories, imaging centres or specialists), which professional codes in India prohibit, and delayed referral that harms the patient. Referral should be based only on the patient's interest, with adequate information passed on.

## 3.19 Vendor relationships

Relationships with pharmaceutical and device companies (vendors) must not compromise care or research:

- Gifts, travel, hospitality and cash from industry to doctors are restricted. In India, the Medical Council regulations (amended in 2009) set a code of conduct for doctors in their relationship with the pharmaceutical and allied health sector, and the Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices (Department of Pharmaceuticals, revised in 2024) restricts what companies may give.
- Free samples must be for patient benefit, not resale.
- Continuing education sponsored by industry should be transparent and free of promotional bias.
- In research: clear contracts, investigator's freedom to publish, payments approved by the EC.

## 3.20 Treatment of family members, sexual relationships and futility

**Treatment of family members:** most codes discourage doctors from treating their own family except in emergencies or minor problems, because emotional involvement can distort judgement, intimate examinations and questions may be avoided, confidentiality is difficult, and the relative may find it hard to refuse or to seek another opinion.

**Sexual relationships:** any sexual or romantic relationship between a doctor and a current patient is unethical. It exploits the trust and power imbalance of the relationship and harms the patient. Relationships with former patients and with relatives of patients are also discouraged; professional bodies treat sexual misconduct as serious misconduct leading to removal from the register. The same principle applies to investigators and research participants.

**Futility** (listed as "fatality" in some copies of the syllabus): treatment is futile when it has no reasonable chance of achieving a meaningful benefit for the patient (prolonging an irreversible dying process in multi-organ failure). Doctors are not obliged to provide futile treatment; decisions should be made with the patient or family after honest discussion, often with a second opinion, and with continued comfort care. Do-not-attempt-resuscitation orders are an example. Disagreements about futility are among the commonest reasons for ethics consultation.

## 3.21 Remember

- Landmarks: Hippocratic Oath → Percival 1803 → Nuremberg Code 1947 → Geneva 1948 → Helsinki 1964 → Tuskegee exposed 1972 → Belmont 1979 → ICH-GCP 1996 → ICMR 2017 → NDCT Rules 2019.
- Values: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, plus dignity and truthfulness.
- Double effect: good intention, bad effect foreseen not intended, not the means, proportionate reason (opioids in terminal pain).
- India: active euthanasia illegal; passive euthanasia and living wills allowed (Aruna Shanbaug 2011, Common Cause 2018).
- Consent: disclosure, comprehension, voluntariness, capacity, documentation, right to withdraw; assent for children; impartial witness for illiterate participants; audio-video consent where rules require.
- EC: at least 7 members (medical scientist, clinician, legal expert, social scientist or ethicist, lay person), both genders, half non-affiliated, outside chairperson; registered with CDSCO for clinical trials.
- Avoid conflicts of interest (disclose, recuse), fee-splitting, unethical vendor gifts, treating own family, any sexual relationship with patients; futile treatment need not be given, comfort care always.
