# Urine analysis: abnormal constituents

**Aim:** To detect abnormal constituents – glucose, proteins, ketone bodies, bile pigments (bilirubin), bile salts, excess urobilinogen and blood – in the given urine sample and to state their clinical significance.

> The syllabus asks for 4 experiments. Colleges usually give simulated "pathological" urine samples, each containing one or two abnormal constituents. Wear gloves throughout.

## Principle

| Constituent | Test | Principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glucose | Benedict's test | Reducing sugar reduces Cu2+ to red Cu2O on boiling |
| Protein | Heat coagulation test | Heat denatures proteins; acetic acid brings them to their isoelectric point and dissolves phosphates, so a white cloud that stays is protein |
| Protein | Sulphosalicylic acid test | The acid precipitates proteins as a white turbidity |
| Ketone bodies | Rothera's test | Acetoacetate and acetone react with sodium nitroprusside in alkaline (ammonia) medium to give a purple ring |
| Bilirubin | Fouchet's test | Barium chloride carries bilirubin down with barium sulphate; ferric chloride in trichloroacetic acid oxidises bilirubin to green biliverdin (or blue cholecyanin) |
| Bile salts | Hay's sulphur test | Bile salts lower surface tension, so fine sulphur powder sinks instead of floating |
| Urobilinogen | Ehrlich's test | Urobilinogen gives a red colour with p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in HCl |
| Blood | Benzidine / peroxidase test | Haem has peroxidase-like action; it releases oxygen from H2O2, which oxidises the chromogen to a blue/green colour |

## Requirements

Test tubes, water bath, burner, filter paper, funnel. Simulated urine samples. Reagents: Benedict's qualitative reagent, 1% acetic acid, 20% sulphosalicylic acid, solid ammonium sulphate, 5% sodium nitroprusside (fresh), liquor ammonia, 10% barium chloride, Fouchet's reagent, sulphur powder, Ehrlich's aldehyde reagent, hydrogen peroxide with a peroxidase chromogen (benzidine in older manuals; ortho-tolidine or a reagent strip in most laboratories now), urine reagent strips (optional).

## Procedure

1. **Benedict's test (glucose):** 5 ml Benedict's reagent + 8 drops urine; boil 2 minutes or place in a boiling water bath for about 3–5 minutes; cool.
2. **Heat coagulation test (protein):** fill a test tube two-thirds with clear (filtered) urine. Heat only the upper part. If turbidity appears, add 2–3 drops of 1% acetic acid.
3. **Sulphosalicylic acid test (protein):** 3 ml urine + a few drops of 20% sulphosalicylic acid.
4. **Rothera's test (ketones):** 5 ml urine + solid ammonium sulphate to saturation + 2–3 drops fresh sodium nitroprusside; mix; layer 1–2 ml liquor ammonia carefully along the side.
5. **Fouchet's test (bilirubin):** 10 ml urine + 5 ml 10% BaCl2; mix and filter. Spread the filter paper and add a drop of Fouchet's reagent to the precipitate.
6. **Hay's test (bile salts):** sprinkle a pinch of fine sulphur powder on the surface of urine in a test tube (and on water in a control tube).
7. **Ehrlich's test (urobilinogen):** 5 ml fresh urine + 0.5 ml Ehrlich's reagent; stand for 5 minutes. A pink colour that is faint (normal traces) is noted; a deep red colour indicates excess.
8. **Test for blood:** mix a few drops of urine with the chromogen solution and hydrogen peroxide (or dip a reagent strip and read at the stated time).
9. **Reagent strip (optional):** dip, remove excess, read each pad at its stated time against the colour chart.

## Observations

| Test | Observation | Inference | Clinical significance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Benedict's | Green / yellow / orange / brick-red precipitate (roughly increasing amounts) | Reducing sugar present (glucose) | Diabetes mellitus, renal glycosuria, pregnancy |
| Heat coagulation | White cloud that persists after acetic acid | Protein present | Nephrotic syndrome, glomerulonephritis, pre-eclampsia |
| Heat coagulation | Cloud that disappears with acetic acid | Phosphates, not protein | Normal |
| Sulphosalicylic acid | White turbidity | Protein present | As above |
| Rothera's | Purple (permanganate-coloured) ring | Ketone bodies present | Diabetic ketoacidosis, starvation, prolonged vomiting |
| Fouchet's | Green or blue colour | Bilirubin present | Hepatic or obstructive jaundice |
| Hay's | Sulphur sinks | Bile salts present | Obstructive jaundice |
| Hay's | Sulphur floats | Bile salts absent | – |
| Ehrlich's | Deep red | Excess urobilinogen | Haemolytic jaundice, early hepatitis |
| Blood test | Blue / green colour | Blood (haemoglobin) present | Stones, infection, glomerulonephritis, haemolysis |

**Benedict's grading (semi-quantitative, approximate):** green ≈ trace, yellow ≈ +, orange ≈ ++, brick-red ≈ +++ / ++++. Exact percentage values vary between manuals.

## Result

The given urine sample contains ________ (abnormal constituents found), suggesting ________.

## Precautions

1. Use fresh urine – urobilinogen and bilirubin break down on standing and in light; ketones evaporate.
2. Filter turbid urine before the heat test.
3. Sodium nitroprusside solution must be freshly prepared.
4. Large doses of vitamin C and some drugs can give a false positive Benedict's test.
5. Benzidine is carcinogenic; use safer chromogens or reagent strips if available.

## Viva questions

1. **What is the renal threshold for glucose?** Commonly quoted as about 180 mg/dl of blood glucose.
2. **Why is acetic acid added in the heat test?** To dissolve phosphate turbidity and bring proteins to their isoelectric point.
3. **Which ketone body is not detected by Rothera's test?** β-Hydroxybutyrate.
4. **Why is bilirubin absent in urine in haemolytic jaundice?** The raised bilirubin is unconjugated, which is bound to albumin and not water-soluble.
5. **What are Bence-Jones proteins?** Immunoglobulin light chains in multiple myeloma; they precipitate at about 40–60 °C and redissolve near boiling.
6. **Why does sulphur sink in Hay's test?** Bile salts lower the surface tension of urine.
