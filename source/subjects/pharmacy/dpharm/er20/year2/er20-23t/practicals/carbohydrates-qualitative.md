# Qualitative analysis of carbohydrates

**Aim:** To identify the carbohydrate present in the given unknown solution (for example glucose, fructose, lactose, maltose, sucrose or starch) by systematic qualitative tests.

> The syllabus asks for 4 experiments. Most colleges give one unknown sugar per experiment, chosen from glucose, fructose, lactose/maltose, sucrose and starch. Follow your lab manual and teacher where they differ.

## Principle

- **Molisch's test:** concentrated sulphuric acid dehydrates any carbohydrate to furfural (pentoses) or hydroxymethylfurfural (hexoses), which condense with α-naphthol to form a violet ring. It is a general test for carbohydrates.
- **Iodine test:** iodine fits into the coils of amylose, giving a blue complex (starch); glycogen and dextrins give red-brown.
- **Benedict's / Fehling's test:** sugars with a free aldehyde or ketone group reduce blue cupric ions (Cu2+) in alkaline solution to red cuprous oxide (Cu2O) on boiling.
- **Barfoed's test:** copper acetate in a weakly acidic medium is reduced only by strong reducing agents. Monosaccharides reduce it within about 2–3 minutes; disaccharides take much longer.
- **Seliwanoff's test:** ketoses are dehydrated by HCl much faster than aldoses; the product gives a cherry-red colour with resorcinol.
- **Bial's test:** pentoses give furfural, which forms a green-blue colour with orcinol and ferric chloride.
- **Osazone test:** reducing sugars react with excess phenylhydrazine on heating to form yellow osazone crystals of a typical shape.
- **Hydrolysis test (for sucrose):** boiling with dilute HCl splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, which then reduce Benedict's reagent.

## Requirements

Test tubes and stand, test-tube holder, water bath, spirit lamp/Bunsen burner, droppers, measuring cylinder, glass slides, microscope.

Reagents: Molisch's reagent (α-naphthol in alcohol), concentrated H2SO4, dilute iodine solution, Benedict's qualitative reagent, Fehling's A and B, Barfoed's reagent, Seliwanoff's reagent (resorcinol in HCl), Bial's reagent, phenylhydrazine hydrochloride, sodium acetate, dilute HCl, sodium carbonate/NaOH for neutralising.

## Procedure

1. **Molisch's test:** to 2 ml of the test solution add 2 drops of Molisch's reagent and mix. Hold the tube inclined and carefully pour about 1–2 ml of concentrated H2SO4 along the side so it forms a layer below. Observe the junction.
2. **Iodine test:** to 1 ml of test solution add 1–2 drops of dilute iodine solution. Note the colour.
3. **Benedict's test:** to 5 ml of Benedict's reagent add 8 drops of the test solution. Boil for about 2 minutes (or keep in a boiling water bath for about 3–5 minutes). Cool and note the colour and precipitate.
4. **Fehling's test:** mix 1 ml each of Fehling's A and B, add 2 ml of test solution, boil and observe.
5. **Barfoed's test:** to 2 ml of Barfoed's reagent add 1 ml of test solution. Keep in a boiling water bath and note the time at which a red precipitate appears (look at about 2–3 minutes).
6. **Seliwanoff's test:** to 3 ml of Seliwanoff's reagent add 1 ml of test solution. Heat in a boiling water bath for about 1 minute and observe; do not boil for long.
7. **Bial's test (if pentose suspected):** to 2 ml of Bial's reagent add a few drops of test solution, heat and observe.
8. **Hydrolysis of a non-reducing sugar:** to 5 ml of test solution add 1 ml of dilute HCl, boil for a few minutes, cool, neutralise with sodium carbonate, and repeat Benedict's and Seliwanoff's tests.
9. **Osazone test:** to 5 ml of test solution add a small spatula each of phenylhydrazine hydrochloride and sodium acetate (about twice as much sodium acetate), warm to dissolve, and keep in a boiling water bath. Note the time of crystal formation; cool slowly and examine a drop under the microscope.

## Observations

| Test | Observation | Inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Molisch's | Violet ring at junction | Carbohydrate present |
| Iodine | Blue | Starch |
| Iodine | No blue colour | Not starch – go on to reducing tests |
| Benedict's | Brick-red / orange precipitate | Reducing sugar present |
| Benedict's | Blue solution remains | Non-reducing sugar (e.g. sucrose) or polysaccharide |
| Barfoed's | Red precipitate within about 2–3 minutes | Monosaccharide |
| Barfoed's | No red precipitate in about 2–3 minutes | Reducing disaccharide (maltose or lactose) |
| Seliwanoff's | Cherry red quickly | Ketose – fructose (or sucrose after hydrolysis) |
| Bial's | Green-blue | Pentose |
| Osazone | Needle-shaped crystals in sheaves (broom-like), formed quickly in hot solution | Glucose or fructose |
| Osazone | Sunflower / petal-shaped crystals (on cooling) | Maltose |
| Osazone | Powder-puff / cotton-ball crystals (on cooling) | Lactose |
| Hydrolysis then Benedict's | Positive after hydrolysis only | Sucrose |

### Identification scheme

```text
 Molisch +  ─→ Iodine blue ─────────────────────────────→ STARCH
            └→ Iodine negative → Benedict's
                 ├─ negative → hydrolyse → Benedict + and Seliwanoff + → SUCROSE
                 └─ positive → Barfoed's
                      ├─ quick red → Seliwanoff quick cherry red → FRUCTOSE
                      │             Seliwanoff negative → (osazone needles) GLUCOSE
                      └─ no quick red → osazone shape → MALTOSE (sunflower) or LACTOSE (powder puff)
```

## Result

The given unknown solution contains ________ (write the sugar identified from the tests).

## Precautions

1. Add concentrated H2SO4 slowly along the side; do not shake after adding it.
2. Use clean test tubes and the reagent in excess over the sample in Benedict's test.
3. Do not overheat in Barfoed's and Seliwanoff's tests – long heating gives false positives (disaccharides reduce Barfoed's; aldoses react with Seliwanoff's).
4. Neutralise acid after hydrolysis before Benedict's test.
5. Phenylhydrazine is toxic; avoid skin contact and handle with care.

## Viva questions

1. **Which is the general test for carbohydrates?** Molisch's test.
2. **Why is sucrose non-reducing?** Both anomeric carbons are used in the α1→β2 glycosidic bond, so no free carbonyl group is left.
3. **What is the composition of Benedict's reagent?** Copper sulphate, sodium citrate and sodium carbonate.
4. **Why is Benedict's reagent preferred to Fehling's?** It is a single stable solution and is not easily reduced by uric acid and creatinine.
5. **Which sugars give the same osazone?** Glucose, fructose and mannose.
6. **Why does starch give a blue colour with iodine?** Iodine enters the helix of amylose.
7. **What does a positive Barfoed's test show?** A reducing monosaccharide.
