# Estimation of glucose in urine (Benedict's quantitative method)

**Aim:** To estimate the amount of reducing sugar (glucose) in the given urine sample by Benedict's quantitative method.

## Principle

Benedict's **quantitative** reagent contains copper sulphate, sodium carbonate, sodium citrate, **potassium thiocyanate** and a small amount of **potassium ferrocyanide**. On boiling, glucose reduces the blue cupric ions. Instead of a red precipitate of cuprous oxide, the thiocyanate forms a **white precipitate of cuprous thiocyanate**; ferrocyanide keeps cuprous oxide from separating. The **end point** is the complete disappearance of the blue colour.

The reagent is standardised so that **25 ml of Benedict's quantitative reagent is completely reduced by 50 mg of glucose** (that is, 10 ml by 20 mg). Urine is run from a burette into a measured volume of boiling reagent; the volume of urine used contains that fixed amount of glucose.

## Requirements

Burette and stand, 25 ml pipette, conical flask (or porcelain dish), wire gauze, burner, anhydrous sodium carbonate, porous pieces (pumice). Benedict's quantitative reagent; urine sample (positive for sugar by Benedict's qualitative test).

## Procedure

1. Do the qualitative Benedict's test first. If it gives an orange or brick-red precipitate (high sugar), dilute the urine (commonly 1 in 10 with distilled water) and note the dilution.
2. Fill the burette with the urine (or diluted urine) and note the initial reading.
3. Pipette **25 ml** of Benedict's quantitative reagent into a conical flask. Add about **2–3 g of anhydrous sodium carbonate** and a few porous pieces.
4. Heat the flask to boiling until the carbonate dissolves.
5. While keeping the solution boiling, add urine from the burette quickly at first, about 1 ml at a time, until a chalky white precipitate starts forming and the blue colour begins to fade.
6. Then add urine drop by drop, boiling for a few seconds (about 10–15 seconds) after each drop, until the **last trace of blue colour disappears**. This is the end point.
7. Note the burette reading. Repeat to get two concordant readings (within about 0.1 ml).

## Calculations

```text
 25 ml of reagent ≡ 50 mg of glucose = 0.05 g
 x ml of urine contains 0.05 g glucose
 100 ml of urine contains (0.05 × 100) / x  = 5 / x  g

 Glucose (g/100 ml, i.e. g/dl) = 5 / x
 If the urine was diluted 1 in D:  Glucose (g/dl) = (5 / x) × D
```

**Worked example:** x = 9.9 ml, urine diluted 1 in 10 → glucose = (5 / 9.9) × 10 = **5.05 g/dl** of urine.

(If 10 ml of reagent is used, it equals 20 mg glucose, and glucose (g/dl) = 2 / x × D.)

To express the 24-hour output: glucose per day (g) = glucose (g/dl) × 24-hour urine volume (dl).

## Precautions

1. Keep the solution **boiling throughout** the titration; cooling lets air re-oxidise cuprous ions and the blue colour returns.
2. Add urine dropwise near the end point, boiling between drops.
3. Keep enough sodium carbonate; add a little more if the volume becomes large.
4. Use porous pieces to prevent bumping.
5. Dilute the urine when the qualitative test is strongly positive; otherwise the end point is reached with only a millilitre or two of urine and the reading error becomes large.
6. Benedict's method measures all reducing substances, not glucose alone.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is potassium thiocyanate added to the quantitative reagent?** To form white cuprous thiocyanate instead of red cuprous oxide, making the end point (disappearance of blue) clear.
2. **What is the role of sodium carbonate?** Provides the alkaline medium.
3. **What is the role of potassium ferrocyanide?** It prevents cuprous oxide from precipitating.
4. **How much glucose reduces 25 ml of the reagent?** 50 mg.
5. **Why is the solution kept boiling?** To prevent re-oxidation of cuprous ions by air.
6. **Name a more specific method for urine glucose.** Glucose oxidase strip or GOD-POD method.
