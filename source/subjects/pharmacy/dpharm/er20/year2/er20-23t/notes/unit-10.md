# Unit 10: Minerals, water and electrolytes

This unit covers syllabus chapters 9 (minerals, 5 hours) and 10 (water and electrolytes, 5 hours). They are joined because sodium, potassium and chloride belong to both.

> The daily requirements below are **approximate adult values** often quoted in Indian textbooks. ICMR–NIN recommendations (latest 2020) differ with age, sex, pregnancy, lactation and activity, so learn them as "about".

## 10.1 Minerals: definition and types

**Minerals** are **inorganic elements** needed by the body for structure and for many body functions. They cannot be made in the body and must come from food and water. They make up about 4% of body weight.

| Type | Daily need | Elements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Macrominerals** (major / principal elements) | More than about 100 mg per day | **Calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, sodium, potassium, chloride, sulphur** |
| **Microminerals** (trace elements) | Less than about 100 mg per day | **Iron, iodine, zinc, copper, manganese, fluoride, selenium, cobalt, chromium, molybdenum** |
| Ultra-trace / possibly essential | Very small amounts | Nickel, vanadium, silicon, boron |
| Toxic (no known need) | – | Lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic, aluminium |

### General functions of minerals

1. **Structural:** calcium, phosphorus and magnesium in bones and teeth; sulphur in proteins (keratin).
2. **Water and acid–base balance:** sodium, potassium, chloride, phosphate, bicarbonate.
3. **Nerve and muscle function:** sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium.
4. **Parts of enzymes and other molecules:** iron (haemoglobin, cytochromes), zinc (carbonic anhydrase), copper (cytochrome oxidase), iodine (thyroxine), cobalt (vitamin B12), selenium (glutathione peroxidase).
5. **Enzyme activators:** magnesium (kinases, all ATP reactions), manganese, zinc.
6. **Blood clotting:** calcium.

## 10.2 Macrominerals

### Calcium

- **Body content:** the most abundant mineral, about 1–1.2 kg in an adult; **99% in bones and teeth** (as hydroxyapatite), 1% in body fluids.
- **Sources:** **milk and milk products** (best), ragi (finger millet), green leafy vegetables, sesame seeds, small fish eaten with bones, pulses.
- **Absorption:** in the upper small intestine; **increased by vitamin D (calcitriol)**, acidic pH, lactose, protein; **decreased by phytates** (cereals), **oxalates** (spinach), excess fat and phosphate, alkaline pH.
- **Functions:** bones and teeth; **blood clotting** (factor IV); **muscle contraction** (including heart); **nerve transmission** and release of neurotransmitters; second messenger for hormones; activates enzymes; controls cell membrane permeability; secretion of hormones (insulin).
- **Regulation of plasma calcium:** commonly quoted as about **9–11 mg/dl** (ranges vary by laboratory), kept steady by **parathyroid hormone** (raises), **calcitriol** (raises) and **calcitonin** (lowers).
- **RDA:** about 600–1000 mg per day (more in pregnancy, lactation, adolescence and old age).
- **Deficiency:** **rickets** (children), **osteomalacia** (adults), **osteoporosis** (thin, fragile bones in the elderly, especially post-menopausal women), **tetany** (muscle spasms, carpopedal spasm, tingling) with low plasma calcium.
- **Excess (hypercalcaemia):** in hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D excess, cancers; kidney stones, constipation, confusion.

### Phosphorus

- **Body content:** about 80% in bones and teeth with calcium.
- **Sources:** milk, cereals, pulses, meat, eggs, nuts (widely present).
- **Functions:** bones and teeth; part of **ATP, ADP, creatine phosphate** (energy); **nucleic acids** (DNA, RNA) and phospholipids; **phosphate buffer**; activation of molecules by phosphorylation (glucose-6-phosphate); coenzymes (NADP, TPP, PLP).
- **RDA:** about 600–1000 mg per day (often taken as equal to calcium).
- **Deficiency:** rare; rickets, muscle weakness. High levels in kidney failure.

### Magnesium

- **Body content:** about 60% in bones; the rest mostly inside cells (second most abundant intracellular cation).
- **Sources:** green leafy vegetables (part of chlorophyll), whole cereals, nuts, pulses.
- **Functions:** **cofactor for all enzymes that use ATP** (kinases), many others; DNA and protein synthesis; nerve and muscle function (calms nerve excitability); bone structure; release of parathyroid hormone.
- **RDA:** about 300–440 mg per day.
- **Deficiency:** in malabsorption, chronic alcoholism, diuretics, diarrhoea; **tremors, muscle cramps, tetany, convulsions**, heart rhythm problems; may cause low calcium and potassium.
- **Pharmacy link:** magnesium sulphate (purgative; treatment of eclampsia), magnesium hydroxide (antacid).

### Sodium

- **Main cation of extracellular fluid (ECF)**; plasma sodium commonly quoted as about **135–145 mEq/L**.
- **Sources:** **common salt (NaCl)**, salted and processed foods, pickles, papads, baking soda.
- **Functions:** controls **ECF volume and osmotic pressure** (water follows sodium); acid–base balance (sodium bicarbonate); **nerve impulse** and muscle contraction (action potential); absorption of glucose and amino acids in the gut (sodium co-transport). Regulated by **aldosterone** (retains Na+) and atrial natriuretic peptide.
- **Intake:** WHO advises less than about **5 g of salt per day** (about 2 g sodium) for adults.
- **Hyponatraemia (low sodium):** vomiting, diarrhoea, excess sweating, diuretics, Addison's disease, excess water intake, SIADH; causes weakness, confusion, convulsions.
- **Hypernatraemia (high sodium):** water loss (dehydration), diabetes insipidus, Cushing's syndrome; causes thirst, confusion. High salt intake raises **blood pressure**.

### Potassium

- **Main cation of intracellular fluid (ICF)**; plasma potassium commonly quoted as about **3.5–5.0 mEq/L**.
- **Sources:** fruits (**banana**, orange, coconut water, dates), vegetables, potatoes, pulses, dry fruits.
- **Functions:** maintains **intracellular volume and osmotic pressure**; **resting membrane potential** – **heart rhythm**, nerve and muscle function; acid–base balance; needed for glycogen and protein synthesis.
- **Intake:** about 3.5 g (about 90 mEq) per day.
- **Hypokalaemia:** vomiting, diarrhoea, **diuretics** (thiazides, furosemide), insulin therapy, alkalosis; causes **muscle weakness**, cramps, paralytic ileus, **cardiac arrhythmias** (ECG changes); increases digoxin toxicity.
- **Hyperkalaemia:** **kidney failure**, Addison's disease, potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors, massive cell damage; causes muscle weakness and dangerous **arrhythmias, cardiac arrest**.

### Chloride

- **Main anion of ECF**; plasma chloride commonly quoted as about **96–106 mEq/L**.
- **Sources:** common salt.
- **Functions:** with sodium, maintains **osmotic pressure and water balance**; acid–base balance (**chloride shift** in red cells); forms **hydrochloric acid** in the stomach; activates salivary amylase.
- **Deficiency:** with prolonged vomiting (loss of HCl) → **hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis**.

### Sulphur

Taken in as sulphur-containing amino acids (methionine, cysteine). Part of proteins (disulphide bonds in keratin, insulin), **coenzyme A, biotin, thiamine, glutathione, heparin, chondroitin sulphate**; used in detoxification (sulphate conjugation). No specific deficiency disease apart from protein deficiency.

## 10.4 Water: distribution in the body

**Water** is the most abundant substance in the body: about **60% of body weight** in an adult man (about 42 litres in a 70 kg man), about **50–55% in women** (more fat, less muscle) and up to about 75% in newborns. It falls with age and obesity because fat holds little water.

```text
        TOTAL BODY WATER  ≈ 60% of body weight (≈ 42 L in a 70 kg man)
        ┌──────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────┐
        │  INTRACELLULAR FLUID (ICF)   │ EXTRACELLULAR FLUID (ECF)   │
        │  ≈ 2/3  (≈ 40% body weight,  │ ≈ 1/3  (≈ 20% body weight,  │
        │          ≈ 28 L)             │          ≈ 14 L)            │
        │                              │ ├ Interstitial fluid        │
        │  Main ions: K+, Mg2+,        │ │   ≈ 15% body wt (≈ 10.5 L)│
        │  phosphate, proteins         │ ├ Plasma ≈ 5% (≈ 3.5 L)     │
        │                              │ └ Transcellular (CSF, joint,│
        │                              │   eye, GI fluids) small     │
        │                              │ Main ions: Na+, Cl−, HCO3− │
        └──────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────┘
```

## 10.5 Functions of water

1. **Universal solvent:** dissolves nutrients, gases, waste products and drugs; all biochemical reactions take place in water.
2. **Transport medium:** blood plasma and lymph carry nutrients, hormones, oxygen and wastes.
3. **Body temperature control:** high specific heat; sweating (evaporation) removes heat.
4. **Excretion:** removes urea and other wastes in urine; keeps stools soft.
5. **Lubricant:** saliva, mucus, synovial (joint) fluid, pleural and pericardial fluids.
6. **Protection:** cerebrospinal fluid cushions the brain; amniotic fluid protects the fetus.
7. **Participates in reactions:** hydrolysis (digestion), hydration reactions (fumarase, enoyl CoA hydratase).
8. **Maintains shape and turgidity** of cells and blood volume (blood pressure).
9. **Medium for digestion and absorption** (digestive juices).

## 10.6 Water turnover and balance

**Water balance** means water intake equals water output, so body water stays constant. An adult turns over about **2.5 litres of water per day** (figures are approximate and vary with climate, diet and activity).

| Water intake (per day) | Amount (about) | Water output (per day) | Amount (about) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drinking water and beverages | 1.2–1.5 L | **Urine** | 1.5 L |
| Water in solid food | 0.7–1.0 L | Skin (insensible perspiration and sweat) | 0.5 L (much more in heat or fever) |
| **Metabolic water** (from oxidation of food) | 0.3 L | Lungs (expired air) | 0.4 L |
| | | Faeces | 0.1–0.2 L |
| **Total** | **≈ 2.5 L** | **Total** | **≈ 2.5 L** |

- **Obligatory urine volume** (the minimum needed to remove daily wastes) is about 500 ml.
- **Insensible water loss** (skin and lungs, not felt) is about 800–900 ml per day.
- Metabolic water: oxidation of 100 g of fat gives more water than 100 g of carbohydrate or protein.

### Regulation of water balance

```text
 Water loss → plasma osmolality ↑ → osmoreceptors (hypothalamus)
         ├→ THIRST → more drinking
         └→ ADH (antidiuretic hormone, vasopressin) from posterior pituitary
               → kidney collecting ducts reabsorb more water → concentrated urine
 Excess water → osmolality ↓ → thirst and ADH ↓ → dilute urine
 Low blood volume → renin → angiotensin II → ALDOSTERONE → Na+ and water retained
```

Disorders: **diabetes insipidus** (lack of ADH action → large volumes of dilute urine), **SIADH** (excess ADH → water retention, low sodium), **oedema** (excess interstitial fluid – in heart failure, kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, low plasma albumin).

## 10.7 Electrolyte composition of body fluids

**Electrolytes** are substances that separate into ions in solution and conduct electricity: Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, HCO3−, HPO4^2−, SO4^2−, proteins and organic acids. In each compartment the total cations equal the total anions (**electrical neutrality**).

| Ion | Plasma (ECF), mEq/L (about) | Intracellular fluid, mEq/L (about) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Na+ | 140 | 10–15 |
| K+ | 4–5 | 140–150 |
| Ca2+ | 5 | Very low (free) |
| Mg2+ | 2 | 25–40 |
| Cl− | 100–105 | 5–10 |
| HCO3− | 24–28 | 10 |
| Phosphate | 2 | High (main anion with proteins) |
| Proteins | 16 | High |

Key points: **ECF is rich in sodium and chloride; ICF is rich in potassium, magnesium, phosphate and proteins.** The difference is kept by the **Na+/K+-ATPase pump** (pumps 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, using ATP). Plasma has more protein than interstitial fluid, which holds water in vessels (oncotic pressure).

**Normal plasma osmolality** is commonly quoted as about 285–295 mOsm/kg water; it is mainly due to sodium and its anions.

## 10.8 Dietary intake of electrolytes and electrolyte balance

- **Intake:** sodium and chloride mainly from common salt (Indian diets often supply 8–10 g of salt or more per day, above the advised limit); potassium from fruits and vegetables; bicarbonate is made in the body from CO2.
- **Output:** mainly **kidneys** (urine), also sweat, faeces; abnormal losses in vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, drainage tubes and diuretic use.
- **Regulation:**
  - **Kidney** is the main regulator: filters and reabsorbs Na+, Cl−, HCO3−; secretes K+ and H+.
  - **Aldosterone** (adrenal cortex): retains Na+ and water, excretes K+ and H+.
  - **ADH:** retains water.
  - **Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP):** increases Na+ and water excretion.
  - **Parathyroid hormone, calcitriol, calcitonin:** calcium and phosphate.
  - **Insulin and adrenaline** move K+ into cells.
- **Acid–base balance** depends on electrolytes: blood pH is kept at about **7.35–7.45** by buffers (bicarbonate, phosphate, proteins, haemoglobin), lungs (CO2) and kidneys (H+ and HCO3−).

## 10.9 Dehydration

**Dehydration** is a condition in which **water loss exceeds water intake**, so total body water decreases – usually with loss of electrolytes (especially sodium) as well.

### Causes

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Excess loss from gut | **Diarrhoea** (cholera, gastroenteritis), **vomiting** |
| Excess loss through kidneys | Uncontrolled **diabetes mellitus** (osmotic diuresis), **diabetes insipidus**, diuretic overuse, Addison's disease |
| Excess loss through skin and lungs | Heavy **sweating** (heat, exercise), **fever**, **burns**, rapid breathing |
| Low intake | Inability to drink (coma, elderly, infants), lack of water, painful mouth or throat |
| Others | Haemorrhage, drainage from fistulas, ascites removal |

### Types

| Type | Loss | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isotonic | Water and sodium in equal proportion | Diarrhoea, vomiting, haemorrhage |
| Hypertonic (water loss > salt) | Mainly water | Fever, sweating, diabetes insipidus, poor intake |
| Hypotonic (salt loss > water) | Mainly sodium | Excess diuretics, Addison's disease, replacing losses with plain water |

### Features and grading

- **Mild:** thirst, dry mouth, less urine.
- **Moderate (some dehydration):** restlessness or irritability, **sunken eyes**, **skin pinch goes back slowly**, very thirsty, dark scanty urine; sunken fontanelle in infants.
- **Severe:** lethargy or unconsciousness, very sunken eyes, unable to drink, skin pinch goes back very slowly, rapid weak pulse, low blood pressure, cold limbs, little or no urine – **shock**; needs intravenous fluids (Ringer's lactate or normal saline).
- **Laboratory:** raised haematocrit, raised blood urea, high urine specific gravity (unless kidney cause), electrolyte changes.

## 10.10 Oral rehydration therapy (ORT)

**ORT** is the replacement of water and electrolytes lost in diarrhoea by giving a glucose–electrolyte solution **by mouth**. It is one of the most important health measures of the 20th century and has saved millions of children.

**Principle:** sodium and glucose are absorbed together by the **sodium–glucose co-transporter (SGLT1)** in the small intestine, and this transport keeps working in diarrhoea (even cholera). Water follows the absorbed sodium and glucose by osmosis. Glucose must be in the right ratio with sodium; too much sugar makes diarrhoea worse.

### WHO/UNICEF low-osmolarity ORS (current formula)

| Ingredient | g per litre |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium chloride | 2.6 |
| Glucose, anhydrous | 13.5 |
| Potassium chloride | 1.5 |
| Trisodium citrate, dihydrate | 2.9 |

| Component | mmol per litre |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium | 75 |
| Chloride | 65 |
| Glucose | 75 |
| Potassium | 20 |
| Citrate | 10 |
| **Total osmolarity** | **245** |

Role of each: **sodium chloride** replaces sodium and chloride; **glucose** helps sodium (and water) absorption; **potassium chloride** replaces potassium lost in stools; **trisodium citrate** corrects acidosis (and improves shelf life over the older bicarbonate). The low-osmolarity formula (since 2002) reduces stool output, vomiting and the need for intravenous fluids compared with the older formula (osmolarity 311).

**How to use:** dissolve one packet in the stated volume (usually 1 litre) of clean drinking water; do not boil the solution or add sugar; use within 24 hours; give small frequent sips (a spoon every 1–2 minutes for small children); continue breast-feeding and normal food. **Zinc** (10–20 mg daily for 10–14 days) is given along with ORS to children with diarrhoea under national guidelines.

**Home fluids** (when ORS packets are not available): rice water, lassi, lemon water with a little salt, coconut water or a salt–sugar solution made exactly as taught by the local health worker. Wrong proportions can be harmful, so the packet ORS is always preferred.

**Contra-indications for ORS alone:** severe dehydration with shock, unconsciousness, persistent vomiting, paralytic ileus – these need intravenous fluids.

## Remember

- Macrominerals (> 100 mg/day): Ca, P, Mg, Na, K, Cl, S; trace: Fe, I, Zn, Cu, F, Se, Mn, Co, Cr, Mo.
- Ca: 99% in bone; vitamin D aids absorption, phytate and oxalate reduce it; rickets, osteomalacia, osteoporosis, tetany; plasma about 9–11 mg/dl.
- Fe: haemoglobin; deficiency microcytic hypochromic anaemia. I: thyroxine; goitre, cretinism. Zn: carbonic anhydrase, wound healing. Cu: ceruloplasmin; excess in Wilson's disease. F: caries (low), fluorosis (high). Se: glutathione peroxidase, Keshan disease.
- Body water 60% (ICF 40%, ECF 20% = interstitial 15% + plasma 5%); turnover about 2.5 L/day.
- ECF: Na+ and Cl−; ICF: K+, Mg2+, phosphate; kept by the Na+/K+-ATPase.
- Dehydration: diarrhoea, vomiting, sweating, burns, diabetes; sunken eyes, slow skin pinch, scanty urine.
- Low-osmolarity ORS per litre: NaCl 2.6 g, glucose 13.5 g, KCl 1.5 g, trisodium citrate 2.9 g; osmolarity 245; works by sodium–glucose co-transport.
