# Unit 11: Organ function tests

This unit covers syllabus chapter 12 (6 hours): kidney function tests, liver function tests and the lipid profile.

> **About normal values:** the reference ranges in this unit are values **commonly quoted** in Indian textbooks and laboratory reports. Every laboratory sets its own range depending on its method, instrument and population, so **ranges vary by laboratory**. Always read a patient's result against the range printed on that report.

**Organ function tests** are laboratory tests on blood and urine that show how well an organ is working. They help in diagnosis, in judging severity, in following the course of disease and the response to treatment, and in **adjusting drug doses** (for example lowering doses of drugs excreted by the kidney when kidney function is poor).

## 11.1 Functions of the kidney

```text
 Blood → glomerulus (filtration, ~120 ml/min) → tubules (reabsorption + secretion)
       → urine (~1–2 L/day)
```

| Function | Details |
| --- | --- |
| **Excretion of waste products** | Urea, creatinine, uric acid, drugs and their metabolites, toxins |
| **Water balance** | Concentrates or dilutes urine under ADH |
| **Electrolyte balance** | Regulates Na+, K+, Cl−, Ca2+, phosphate (aldosterone, PTH) |
| **Acid–base balance** | Excretes H+ (as ammonium and phosphate), reabsorbs and makes bicarbonate |
| **Endocrine functions** | **Erythropoietin** (red cell production), **renin** (blood pressure via angiotensin–aldosterone), **calcitriol** (active vitamin D), prostaglandins |
| **Metabolic functions** | Gluconeogenesis in fasting; breakdown of small proteins and hormones (insulin) |
| **Blood pressure control** | Through salt and water balance and the renin–angiotensin system |

**Glomerular filtration rate (GFR):** the volume of plasma filtered by both kidneys per minute – about **120–125 ml/min** in a healthy young adult (about 180 L/day). It is the best overall index of kidney function. It falls with age and in kidney disease.

## 11.2 Kidney (renal) function tests

```text
 Kidney function tests
  ├─ Urine examination (routine and microscopic)
  ├─ Blood tests: urea, BUN, creatinine, uric acid, electrolytes
  ├─ Clearance tests (GFR): creatinine clearance, eGFR, inulin and urea clearance
  ├─ Tubular function tests: concentration and dilution tests
  └─ Special: urine protein / albumin (microalbuminuria), imaging, biopsy
```

### A. Routine urine examination

- **Volume:** normal about 1–2 litres per day. Polyuria (more than about 2.5–3 L/day) in diabetes mellitus, diabetes insipidus; oliguria (less than about 400 ml/day) and anuria (less than about 100 ml/day) in acute kidney injury, dehydration, shock.
- **Specific gravity:** reflects concentrating ability – usually about 1.003–1.030. A fixed low specific gravity (about 1.010, isosthenuria) means the kidney cannot concentrate or dilute – seen in chronic kidney failure.
- **Protein (proteinuria):** suggests glomerular damage (nephrotic syndrome, glomerulonephritis, diabetic kidney disease, hypertension).
- **Microalbuminuria:** small amounts of albumin (about 30–300 mg per day) – an early sign of diabetic kidney damage.
- **Blood (haematuria), casts, pus cells:** glomerulonephritis, stones, infection, tumours.
- **Glucose, ketones:** usually show diabetes, not kidney disease (see Unit 12).

### B. Blood urea

- Urea is the main end product of protein (amino acid) breakdown, made in the liver and excreted by the kidney.
- **Normal:** commonly quoted as about **15–40 mg/dl** (about 20–40 in many Indian laboratories); **blood urea nitrogen (BUN)** about **7–20 mg/dl**. (Urea ≈ BUN × 2.14.)
- **Raised (uraemia, azotaemia):**
  - **Pre-renal:** dehydration, vomiting, diarrhoea, shock, heart failure, burns, high-protein diet, gastrointestinal bleeding, increased protein breakdown (fever, steroids).
  - **Renal:** acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, glomerulonephritis.
  - **Post-renal:** obstruction – stones, enlarged prostate, tumours.
- **Lowered:** severe liver disease (less urea made), low-protein diet, pregnancy, overhydration.
- **Limitation:** affected by diet, hydration and liver function, so it is less specific than creatinine. It rises only after much of kidney function is lost.

### C. Serum creatinine

- **Creatinine** is formed at a nearly **constant rate** from **creatine phosphate** in muscle and is excreted almost entirely by glomerular filtration (not reabsorbed; slightly secreted).
- **Normal:** commonly quoted as about **0.6–1.2 mg/dl** in men and slightly lower (about 0.5–1.1 mg/dl) in women; ranges vary by laboratory.
- **Better than urea** because it does not depend much on diet or hydration. It depends on muscle mass (lower in women, children, elderly and wasted patients).
- **Raised in:** kidney failure (acute and chronic), urinary obstruction, dehydration (slight), muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis).
- **Note:** serum creatinine rises above normal only after about half of the GFR is lost, so it can be "normal" in early kidney disease.
- **Urea : creatinine ratio** (BUN : creatinine about 10–20 : 1) helps to separate pre-renal (ratio high) from renal causes.

### D. Serum uric acid

- End product of **purine** breakdown (xanthine oxidase), excreted by the kidney.
- **Normal:** commonly quoted as about **3.5–7.0 mg/dl** in men and about **2.5–6.0 mg/dl** in women.
- **Raised (hyperuricaemia):** **gout**, kidney failure, leukaemia and cancer chemotherapy (cell breakdown), thiazide diuretics, low-dose aspirin, alcohol, starvation, psoriasis.

### E. Serum electrolytes

Sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate (see Unit 10). In kidney failure, **potassium and phosphate rise**, **bicarbonate falls** (metabolic acidosis) and calcium falls.

### F. Clearance tests

**Clearance** of a substance is the **volume of plasma completely cleared of that substance by the kidneys per minute**.

```text
             U × V
   C  =  ─────────
               P

  C = clearance (ml/min)
  U = concentration of the substance in urine (mg/dl)
  V = urine flow rate (ml/min)
  P = concentration of the substance in plasma (mg/dl)
```

| Test | Substance | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inulin clearance** | Inulin (a fructose polymer given by IV infusion) | Freely filtered, **neither reabsorbed nor secreted** → equals the true GFR; **gold standard**, but needs infusion, so used mainly in research |
| **Creatinine clearance** (endogenous) | Body's own creatinine | Most commonly used clearance test; **normal commonly quoted as about 90–140 ml/min** (lower in women and with age); overestimates GFR slightly because a little creatinine is secreted. A **24-hour urine collection** is made with a blood sample during that period |
| **Urea clearance** | Urea | Older test; urea is partly reabsorbed, so it underestimates GFR; normal about 70–75 ml/min (maximum clearance) |
| **Estimated GFR (eGFR)** | Calculated from serum creatinine with age and sex (Cockcroft–Gault, MDRD or CKD-EPI equations) | No urine collection needed; used to stage chronic kidney disease and to adjust drug doses |

**Worked example (creatinine clearance):** a patient passes 1440 ml urine in 24 hours (V = 1440 / 1440 = 1 ml/min). Urine creatinine = 120 mg/dl, plasma creatinine = 1.0 mg/dl.
C = (120 × 1) / 1.0 = **120 ml/min** – within the commonly quoted normal range.

**Cockcroft–Gault equation (estimated creatinine clearance, ml/min):**

```text
           (140 − age in years) × body weight (kg)
   CrCl = ─────────────────────────────────────────   × 0.85 for women
             72 × serum creatinine (mg/dl)
```

Pharmacists use this to adjust doses of drugs such as aminoglycosides, vancomycin and digoxin.

### G. Tubular function tests

- **Urine concentration test (water deprivation test):** fluids are withheld (commonly overnight or for a set time) and urine specific gravity or osmolality is measured. A normal kidney concentrates urine (specific gravity commonly above about 1.020–1.025); failure to do so shows tubular damage or diabetes insipidus.
- **Urine dilution test:** a water load is given and the kidney should pass dilute urine (specific gravity falling to about 1.003).
- **Others:** acidification test (ammonium chloride), urine osmolality, β2-microglobulin.

## 11.3 Functions of the liver

The liver is the largest gland and the main metabolic organ of the body.

| Function | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Carbohydrate metabolism** | Glycogen storage and release, gluconeogenesis – keeps blood glucose steady |
| **Lipid metabolism** | Fatty acid oxidation and synthesis, **cholesterol synthesis**, **lipoprotein** (VLDL, HDL) formation, **ketone body** formation, bile acid synthesis |
| **Protein metabolism** | Synthesis of **plasma proteins** – **albumin**, most globulins, **clotting factors** (fibrinogen, prothrombin, factors V, VII, IX, X); transamination and deamination; **urea formation** |
| **Excretory function** | Forms **bile**; excretes **bilirubin**, cholesterol, drugs and toxins in bile |
| **Detoxification and drug metabolism** | Phase I (oxidation by cytochrome P450) and phase II (conjugation with glucuronic acid, sulphate, glycine, acetyl) reactions; ammonia → urea; alcohol metabolism |
| **Storage** | Glycogen, vitamins A, D, B12, iron (ferritin), copper |
| **Haematological** | Formation of red cells in fetus; breakdown of old red cells (Kupffer cells); clotting factors |
| **Immune / defence** | Kupffer cells remove bacteria and particles from portal blood |
| **Hormone metabolism** | Inactivates steroid hormones, insulin; activates vitamin D (25-hydroxylation) |

## 11.4 Liver function tests (LFT)

```text
 Liver function tests
  ├─ Excretory function: serum bilirubin (total, direct, indirect), urine bilirubin,
  │                       urine urobilinogen, bile salts in urine
  ├─ Liver enzymes:  hepatocellular damage → ALT (SGPT), AST (SGOT)
  │                   cholestasis (obstruction) → ALP, GGT, 5′-nucleotidase
  ├─ Synthetic function: serum total protein, albumin, A/G ratio, prothrombin time (INR)
  └─ Metabolic / others: blood ammonia, blood glucose, serum cholesterol, viral markers
```

### A. Tests of excretory function (bilirubin)

| Test | Commonly quoted normal value | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Serum total bilirubin** | About 0.2–1.0 mg/dl (up to about 1.2 in some laboratories) | Raised in all types of jaundice; visible jaundice above about 2–2.5 mg/dl |
| **Direct (conjugated) bilirubin** | Up to about 0.3 mg/dl | Raised in hepatic and obstructive jaundice |
| **Indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin** = total − direct | About 0.2–0.8 mg/dl | Raised in haemolytic jaundice, neonatal jaundice, Gilbert's syndrome |
| **Van den Bergh reaction** | – | Direct reaction (conjugated) vs indirect reaction (after alcohol, unconjugated) |
| **Urine bilirubin** (Fouchet's / Gmelin's test, dipstick) | Absent | Present in hepatic and obstructive jaundice (only conjugated bilirubin passes into urine) |
| **Urine urobilinogen** (Ehrlich's test) | Traces present normally | Increased in haemolytic jaundice; **absent in complete obstruction** |
| **Bile salts in urine** (Hay's sulphur test) | Absent | Present in obstructive jaundice |

### B. Serum enzymes

| Enzyme | Commonly quoted normal | Raised in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ALT (SGPT)** – mainly in liver cytosol | About 5–40 U/L (some laboratories up to about 55) | **Hepatocellular damage** – viral hepatitis (very high, often many times normal), drug and toxin injury (paracetamol overdose), fatty liver; **more specific for the liver** than AST |
| **AST (SGOT)** – liver, heart, muscle, kidney | About 5–40 U/L | Liver damage, **myocardial infarction**, muscle injury; **AST : ALT ratio above about 2** suggests alcoholic liver disease |
| **Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)** – liver bile ducts, bone, placenta | About 40–130 U/L in adults (higher in growing children and in pregnancy) | **Obstructive (cholestatic) jaundice** – markedly raised; also bone disease (rickets, Paget's disease, bone tumours, healing fractures) |
| **γ-Glutamyl transferase (GGT)** | Roughly below about 50 U/L (lower in women) | **Alcohol** use and alcoholic liver disease, cholestasis, enzyme-inducing drugs (phenytoin); helps confirm that a raised ALP comes from the liver |
| 5′-Nucleotidase | – | Cholestasis (liver origin of raised ALP) |

**Pattern rule:** ALT and AST much higher than ALP → **hepatocellular** (hepatitis); ALP and GGT much higher than ALT/AST → **cholestatic/obstructive**.

### C. Tests of synthetic function

| Test | Commonly quoted normal | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Serum total protein** | About 6.0–8.0 g/dl | Low in chronic liver disease, malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome |
| **Serum albumin** | About 3.5–5.0 g/dl | **Low in chronic liver disease (cirrhosis)** – albumin has a long half-life (about 20 days), so it falls slowly; also low in nephrotic syndrome and malnutrition; low albumin → oedema and ascites |
| **Globulins** | About 2.0–3.5 g/dl | Raised in chronic liver disease and chronic infections |
| **Albumin : globulin (A/G) ratio** | About 1.2–2.0 : 1 (normally above 1) | **Reversed (below 1) in cirrhosis**, multiple myeloma, chronic infections |
| **Prothrombin time (PT) / INR** | PT about 11–16 seconds (laboratory-specific); INR about 1 | **Prolonged** in severe liver damage (clotting factors not made) – a sensitive guide to severity; also in vitamin K deficiency (obstructive jaundice), when vitamin K injection corrects it but not in liver cell failure; and in warfarin therapy |

### D. Other tests

- **Blood ammonia:** raised in liver failure (hepatic encephalopathy).
- **Blood glucose:** may be low in severe liver failure.
- **Serum cholesterol:** raised in obstructive jaundice; may be low in severe liver failure.
- **Viral markers:** HBsAg (hepatitis B), anti-HCV (hepatitis C), anti-HAV IgM, anti-HEV IgM.
- **α-Fetoprotein:** tumour marker for liver cancer.
- Imaging (ultrasound) and liver biopsy.

### Summary of LFT in the three types of jaundice

| Test | Haemolytic | Hepatocellular | Obstructive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serum bilirubin | Unconjugated ↑ | Both ↑ | Conjugated ↑↑ |
| Urine bilirubin | Absent | Present | Present |
| Urine urobilinogen | ↑ | Variable | Absent |
| ALT / AST | Normal | ↑↑↑ | Mild ↑ |
| ALP | Normal | Mild ↑ | ↑↑↑ |
| Albumin | Normal | ↓ (chronic) | Normal |
| Prothrombin time | Normal | Prolonged (not corrected by vitamin K) | Prolonged (corrected by vitamin K) |

## 11.5 Lipid profile

The **lipid profile** is a group of blood tests for fats that help to assess the **risk of coronary heart disease and stroke**, and to diagnose and monitor dyslipidaemia and its treatment.

**Sample:** usually after an **overnight fast of about 9–12 hours** (water allowed), because a recent meal raises triglycerides; avoid alcohol and heavy fatty meals the day before. Some guidelines now accept non-fasting samples for screening.

| Test | Commonly quoted desirable / reference values (adults) |
| --- | --- |
| **Total cholesterol** | Desirable below about **200 mg/dl**; borderline high about 200–239; high about 240 or more |
| **LDL-cholesterol** ("bad") | Optimal below about **100 mg/dl**; near optimal 100–129; borderline 130–159; high 160–189; very high 190 or more (lower targets for people with heart disease or diabetes) |
| **HDL-cholesterol** ("good") | Low (risk) below about **40 mg/dl** in men and below about **50 mg/dl** in women; about **60 mg/dl or more is protective** |
| **Triglycerides** | Normal below about **150 mg/dl**; borderline 150–199; high 200–499; very high 500 or more |
| **VLDL-cholesterol** | About 5–40 mg/dl (calculated as triglycerides ÷ 5) |
| **Total cholesterol : HDL ratio** | Below about 4.5–5 desirable |
| **LDL : HDL ratio** | Below about 3 desirable |

These cut-offs come from widely used guidelines (such as the US NCEP ATP III); laboratories and newer guidelines may differ, and **ranges vary by laboratory**.

**Friedewald formula** (when LDL is not measured directly):

```text
   LDL-cholesterol = Total cholesterol − HDL-cholesterol − (Triglycerides ÷ 5)      (all in mg/dl)
   Valid only when triglycerides are below 400 mg/dl.
```

**Worked example:** TC 220, HDL 40, TG 150 mg/dl → VLDL = 150/5 = 30; LDL = 220 − 40 − 30 = **150 mg/dl** (borderline high). TC/HDL = 5.5 (raised risk).

**Methods:** cholesterol by the enzymatic **CHOD-PAP** method; triglycerides by the **GPO-PAP** method (lipase, glycerol kinase, glycerol phosphate oxidase, peroxidase); HDL measured after precipitating the other lipoproteins (phosphotungstate–magnesium) or directly.

### Clinical significance of the lipid profile

| Finding | Seen in |
| --- | --- |
| **High LDL / total cholesterol** | Familial hypercholesterolaemia, diet rich in saturated and trans fat, **hypothyroidism**, **nephrotic syndrome**, **diabetes**, **obstructive jaundice**, pregnancy; leads to atherosclerosis, coronary heart disease |
| **High triglycerides** | Obesity, uncontrolled diabetes, alcohol, high-carbohydrate diet, kidney failure, some drugs (steroids, oestrogens, β-blockers); very high levels risk **acute pancreatitis** |
| **Low HDL** | Smoking, obesity, inactivity, type 2 diabetes; increases heart disease risk |
| **High HDL** | Exercise, moderate habits, oestrogen; protective |
| **Low cholesterol** | Hyperthyroidism, severe liver disease, malnutrition, malabsorption |

**Uses:** screening adults for cardiovascular risk (along with blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, family history), choosing and monitoring **lipid-lowering drugs** (statins, fibrates, ezetimibe), checking response to diet and lifestyle change. Statin therapy is also monitored with liver enzymes (ALT) and, if muscle symptoms occur, creatine kinase.

## Remember

- GFR about 120 ml/min; inulin clearance = gold standard; creatinine clearance commonly quoted about 90–140 ml/min; C = UV/P.
- Blood urea about 15–40 mg/dl (BUN about 7–20); serum creatinine about 0.6–1.2 mg/dl; uric acid about 3.5–7 (men) – all "about", ranges vary by laboratory.
- Creatinine is a better kidney marker than urea (less affected by diet and hydration) but rises only after about half the GFR is lost.
- LFT: bilirubin (total about 0.2–1.0, direct up to 0.3 mg/dl); ALT/AST about 5–40 U/L (ALT more liver-specific); ALP about 40–130 U/L (↑↑ in obstruction and bone disease); albumin about 3.5–5 g/dl; A/G reversed in cirrhosis; PT prolonged in liver failure.
- Lipid profile after a 9–12 hour fast: TC desirable < 200, LDL optimal < 100, HDL ≥ 60 protective (< 40 men / < 50 women low), TG < 150 mg/dl.
- Friedewald: LDL = TC − HDL − TG/5 (TG < 400).
