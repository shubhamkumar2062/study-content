# Unit 5: Enzymes

This unit covers syllabus chapter 6 (5 hours).

## 5.1 Definition

**Enzymes** are **biological catalysts**: protein molecules (with very few exceptions) made by living cells that **speed up chemical reactions** in the body without being used up or permanently changed in the reaction.

- The substance on which an enzyme acts is the **substrate**; the substance formed is the **product**.
- The region of the enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction occurs is the **active site**.
- **Ribozymes** are RNA molecules that act as enzymes (for example the rRNA that forms peptide bonds) – the exception to "all enzymes are proteins".

### Parts of an enzyme

```text
   Holoenzyme (active)  =  Apoenzyme (protein part, inactive alone)
                           +  Cofactor (non-protein part)
                                 ├─ Metal ion activator (Zn2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, Cu2+)
                                 └─ Coenzyme (organic, often from a B vitamin)
                                       loosely bound   → coenzyme (NAD+, CoA)
                                       tightly bound   → prosthetic group (FAD, biotin)
```

| Coenzyme | Vitamin source | Carries |
| --- | --- | --- |
| NAD+, NADP+ | Niacin | Hydrogen (hydride) |
| FAD, FMN | Riboflavin | Hydrogen |
| Thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP) | Thiamine | Aldehyde groups (decarboxylation) |
| Pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) | Pyridoxine | Amino groups (transamination) |
| Coenzyme A | Pantothenic acid | Acyl groups |
| Biotin | Biotin | CO2 (carboxylation) |
| Tetrahydrofolate | Folic acid | One-carbon groups |

## 5.2 Properties of enzymes

1. **Catalytic power:** they raise reaction rates enormously (often a million times or more).
2. **Specificity:** each enzyme acts on one substrate or a group of related substrates.
   - **Absolute specificity:** one substrate only – urease acts only on urea.
   - **Group specificity:** a type of bond in many substrates – trypsin breaks peptide bonds next to lysine or arginine.
   - **Stereo-specificity:** only one optical isomer – L-amino acid oxidase acts only on L-amino acids.
   - **Reaction specificity:** one type of reaction only.
3. **Not consumed:** a small amount of enzyme handles a large amount of substrate.
4. **Do not change the equilibrium** of a reversible reaction; they only help it to be reached faster.
5. **Lower the activation energy** of the reaction.
6. **Sensitive to temperature and pH:** each has an optimum; being proteins they are denatured by heat and extremes of pH.
7. **Many need cofactors** (coenzymes or metal ions).
8. **Regulated:** activity is controlled by allosteric effectors, covalent modification (phosphorylation), induction and repression of synthesis.
9. **Some are made as inactive precursors** called **zymogens (proenzymes)** – pepsinogen, trypsinogen, prothrombin – which are activated when needed. This protects the tissue that makes them.
10. **Isoenzymes (isozymes):** different molecular forms of the same enzyme that catalyse the same reaction but differ in structure, tissue location and properties. Examples: **lactate dehydrogenase** (LDH1–LDH5; LDH1 rich in heart), **creatine kinase** (CK-MM in muscle, CK-MB in heart, CK-BB in brain). They help locate tissue damage.

## 5.3 IUB–MB classification of enzymes

The International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB) classifies enzymes into classes according to the **type of reaction** they catalyse. Each enzyme gets a four-number **EC (Enzyme Commission) number**, such as EC 2.7.1.1 for hexokinase (class 2, transferase).

| Class | Name | Reaction | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **EC 1** | **Oxidoreductases** | Oxidation–reduction (transfer of H or electrons) | Lactate dehydrogenase, alcohol dehydrogenase, cytochrome oxidase, catalase |
| **EC 2** | **Transferases** | Transfer of a group (amino, phosphate, methyl) from one molecule to another | Hexokinase, transaminases (ALT, AST), creatine kinase |
| **EC 3** | **Hydrolases** | Breaking of bonds by adding water | Amylase, lipase, pepsin, trypsin, urease, acetylcholinesterase |
| **EC 4** | **Lyases** | Removal or addition of a group to a double bond without hydrolysis or oxidation | Aldolase, fumarase, carbonic anhydrase, decarboxylases |
| **EC 5** | **Isomerases** | Interconversion of isomers | Triose phosphate isomerase, phosphoglucomutase, epimerases |
| **EC 6** | **Ligases** (synthetases) | Joining two molecules using energy from ATP | Glutamine synthetase, DNA ligase, acetyl CoA carboxylase, pyruvate carboxylase |
| EC 7 | Translocases | Moving ions or molecules across membranes | Na+/K+-ATPase (class added in 2018; many textbooks still list six classes) |

Memory aid for the six classical classes: **"OTHLIL"** – Oxidoreductases, Transferases, Hydrolases, Lyases, Isomerases, Ligases.

## 5.4 Factors affecting enzyme activity

```text
 Rate                    Rate                     Rate
  │      ___ Vmax         │     /\                 │      /\
  │    /                  │    /  \                │     /  \
  │   /                   │   /    \               │    /    \
  │  /                    │  /      \              │   /      \
  │ /                     │ /        \             │  /        \
  └──────────── [S]       └──────────── Temp       └──────────── pH
   substrate conc.         optimum ~37 °C           optimum pH
   (hyperbola)             (denatured above)         (bell-shaped)
```

1. **Substrate concentration:** at low substrate concentration, rate rises almost in proportion; then the rise slows, and at high concentration the enzyme is **saturated** and the rate reaches a maximum (**Vmax**). The curve is a **rectangular hyperbola** (Michaelis–Menten kinetics).
   - **Km (Michaelis constant)** = the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax.
   - Low Km means **high affinity** of the enzyme for its substrate.
2. **Enzyme concentration:** with excess substrate, the rate is **directly proportional** to the enzyme concentration (a straight line). This is used to measure enzymes in serum.
3. **Temperature:** rate increases with temperature up to an **optimum** (about 37–40 °C for most human enzymes); above this the enzyme is denatured and activity falls sharply. Low temperature slows enzymes without destroying them (hence storage of enzymes and serum samples in the cold). Q10 (temperature coefficient): the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise in the working range.
4. **pH:** each enzyme has an **optimum pH**; activity falls on either side (bell-shaped curve) because ionisation of the active site and substrate changes. Pepsin works best at about pH 1.5–2, salivary amylase at about pH 6.8, trypsin at about pH 8, alkaline phosphatase at about pH 9–10.
5. **Product concentration:** accumulation of product slows the reaction (feedback or product inhibition).
6. **Activators:** metal ions (Mg2+ for kinases, Cl− for salivary amylase, Zn2+ for carbonic anhydrase).
7. **Inhibitors:** reduce activity (see 5.6).
8. **Time:** activity is constant over the early period and then falls as substrate is used up and the enzyme is slowly inactivated.
9. **Light and radiation:** UV and ionising radiation can inactivate enzymes.

## 5.5 Mechanism of action of enzymes

### Lowering the activation energy

Every reaction needs a minimum energy, the **activation energy**, for molecules to reach the reactive **transition state**. An enzyme provides an alternative pathway with a **lower activation energy**, so many more molecules react at body temperature.

```text
 Energy
   │        /‾‾\        ← without enzyme (high activation energy)
   │       /    \
   │      / /‾\  \      ← with enzyme (lower activation energy)
   │ S ──/ /   \  \
   │          ES  \
   │                \── P
   └───────────────────────── reaction progress
```

### Enzyme–substrate complex

The enzyme (E) binds the substrate (S) at its active site to form an **enzyme–substrate complex (ES)**, which changes to product (P) and releases the enzyme unchanged:

```text
   E + S  ⇌  ES  →  E + P
```

### Models of binding

**1. Lock and key model (Emil Fischer, 1894):** the active site has a rigid shape that exactly fits the substrate, as a key fits a lock. It explains specificity but not how some enzymes act on several related substrates.

**2. Induced fit model (Daniel Koshland, 1958):** the active site is flexible. When the substrate binds, the enzyme changes shape to fit it closely, like a glove fitting a hand. This brings catalytic groups into place and strains the substrate bonds. It is the accepted model today.

```text
 Lock and key:   [E with fixed pocket] + (S) → [E·S] → [E] + (P)
 Induced fit:    [E open pocket] + (S) → [E closes around S] → [E] + (P)
```

**How the enzyme speeds up the reaction:** bringing substrates close together in the right direction (proximity and orientation), straining bonds, acid–base catalysis by side chains, covalent catalysis and metal ion catalysis.

## 5.6 Enzyme inhibitors

An **inhibitor** is a substance that lowers the rate of an enzyme reaction.

```text
                 Enzyme inhibition
      ┌─────────────────┼────────────────────┐
  Reversible                         Irreversible
   ├ Competitive                      (covalent, permanent)
   ├ Non-competitive                 Suicide (mechanism-based)
   └ Uncompetitive                    Allosteric (regulatory)
```

### 1. Competitive inhibition

- The inhibitor **resembles the substrate** (structural analogue) and competes for the **same active site**.
- **Reversed by increasing substrate** concentration.
- **Km increases** (lower apparent affinity); **Vmax unchanged**.

| Inhibitor (drug) | Enzyme | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Malonate | Succinate dehydrogenase (TCA cycle) | Classic laboratory example |
| **Statins** (atorvastatin) | HMG-CoA reductase | Lower cholesterol |
| **Methotrexate** | Dihydrofolate reductase | Cancer, rheumatoid arthritis |
| **Sulphonamides** | Dihydropteroate synthase (bacterial; compete with PABA) | Antibacterial |
| **Allopurinol** | Xanthine oxidase | Gout (lowers uric acid) |
| Neostigmine | Acetylcholinesterase | Myasthenia gravis |
| Captopril | Angiotensin-converting enzyme | Hypertension |
| Ethanol | Alcohol dehydrogenase (competes with methanol) | Treatment of methanol poisoning |

### 2. Non-competitive inhibition

- The inhibitor binds at a site **other than the active site** (or to the ES complex), changing the enzyme so it works less well.
- **Not reversed** by adding more substrate.
- **Km unchanged**, **Vmax decreased**.
- Examples: heavy metals (Hg2+, Pb2+, Ag+) binding –SH groups; **cyanide** on cytochrome oxidase; fluoride on enolase (Mg2+ removal).

### 3. Uncompetitive inhibition

- The inhibitor binds only to the **enzyme–substrate complex**.
- **Both Km and Vmax decrease.** Example: lithium on inositol monophosphatase.

### 4. Irreversible inhibition

- The inhibitor forms a **covalent bond** with the enzyme and destroys its activity permanently; activity returns only when new enzyme is made.
- Examples: **organophosphate** insecticides and nerve gases on acetylcholinesterase; **aspirin** acetylates cyclooxygenase (so platelets cannot make thromboxane for their whole life); iodoacetate on glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase; disulfiram on aldehyde dehydrogenase.

### 5. Suicide (mechanism-based) inhibition

The enzyme converts an inactive substance into a reactive form that then destroys the enzyme. Example: **allopurinol** is changed by xanthine oxidase into **alloxanthine (oxypurinol)**, which binds tightly to the enzyme; **5-fluorouracil**; clavulanic acid on β-lactamase.

### 6. Allosteric (feedback) inhibition

A regulatory molecule binds at an **allosteric site** and changes enzyme activity. In **feedback inhibition**, the end product of a pathway inhibits the first enzyme: ATP and citrate inhibit phosphofructokinase; cholesterol inhibits HMG-CoA reductase.

### Comparison

| Feature | Competitive | Non-competitive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Binding site | Active site | Another site |
| Structure | Similar to substrate | Not similar |
| Effect of more substrate | Inhibition reversed | Not reversed |
| Km | Increased | Unchanged |
| Vmax | Unchanged | Decreased |
| Example | Malonate, statins | Heavy metals, cyanide |

## 5.7 Therapeutic and pharmaceutical importance of enzymes

### A. Enzymes used as drugs (therapeutic enzymes)

| Enzyme | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Streptokinase**, **urokinase**, **alteplase (tPA)**, tenecteplase | Dissolve blood clots (thrombolytics) in heart attack, pulmonary embolism |
| **L-Asparaginase** | Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (removes asparagine needed by tumour cells) |
| **Pancreatin / pancrelipase** (amylase, lipase, protease) | Digestive aid in pancreatic insufficiency, cystic fibrosis |
| **Papain**, **bromelain** | Digestive aids, meat tenderiser, wound debridement |
| **Pepsin** | Digestive aid |
| **Serratiopeptidase**, **trypsin–chymotrypsin** | Reduce inflammation and swelling |
| **Hyaluronidase** | Increases spread and absorption of injected fluids and drugs |
| **Collagenase** | Removes dead tissue from burns and ulcers |
| **Dornase alfa (DNase)** | Thins the mucus in cystic fibrosis |
| **Lactase** | Lactose intolerance |
| Penicillinase | Treat penicillin allergic reactions (historical) |
| Enzyme replacement (imiglucerase) | Gaucher's disease |

### B. Diagnostic enzymes (clinical enzymology)

When cells are damaged, their enzymes leak into the blood. Raised serum levels help diagnosis:

| Enzyme | Raised in |
| --- | --- |
| **ALT (SGPT)** | Liver damage – hepatitis (more specific for liver) |
| **AST (SGOT)** | Liver disease, heart attack, muscle damage |
| **Alkaline phosphatase (ALP)** | Obstructive jaundice, bone disease (rickets, Paget's disease, bone cancer) |
| **Acid phosphatase** | Prostate cancer (now replaced largely by PSA) |
| **Amylase**, **lipase** | Acute pancreatitis |
| **CK-MB**, **LDH1** | Myocardial infarction (cardiac troponins are now preferred markers; they are proteins, not enzymes) |
| **γ-Glutamyl transferase (GGT)** | Alcoholic liver disease, obstructive jaundice |
| Cholinesterase (serum) | Lowered in organophosphate poisoning and liver disease |

### C. Enzymes as laboratory reagents (analytical enzymes)

Used in kits for estimating substances in blood and urine:

- **Glucose oxidase–peroxidase (GOD-POD)** – blood glucose, also urine dipsticks.
- **Urease** – blood urea.
- **Cholesterol oxidase and esterase** – serum cholesterol.
- **Uricase** – uric acid.
- **Horseradish peroxidase and alkaline phosphatase** – labels in ELISA.
- **Restriction enzymes, DNA ligase, Taq polymerase** – genetic engineering and PCR.

### D. Enzymes in pharmaceutical industry

Production of antibiotics (penicillin acylase to make semi-synthetic penicillins), steroid conversion, high-fructose syrup (glucose isomerase), cleaning agents and contact lens cleaners (proteases).

### E. Enzymes as drug targets

Most drugs act by inhibiting enzymes (see the inhibitor table): NSAIDs (cyclooxygenase), ACE inhibitors, statins, proton pump inhibitors (H+/K+-ATPase), MAO inhibitors, HIV protease inhibitors.

## Remember

- Enzymes are protein biological catalysts; they lower activation energy and do not change the equilibrium.
- Holoenzyme = apoenzyme + cofactor (coenzyme or metal ion); most coenzymes come from B vitamins.
- IUBMB classes: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases (OTHLIL); translocases added in 2018.
- Factors: substrate (hyperbola, Km = [S] at half Vmax), enzyme concentration, temperature (optimum ~37 °C), pH (bell curve), activators, inhibitors.
- Lock and key (Fischer) and induced fit (Koshland).
- Competitive: Km ↑, Vmax same (malonate, statins, methotrexate); non-competitive: Km same, Vmax ↓ (heavy metals, cyanide); irreversible: organophosphates, aspirin; suicide: allopurinol.
- Therapeutic: streptokinase, asparaginase, pancreatin, hyaluronidase; diagnostic: ALT, AST, ALP, amylase, CK-MB.
