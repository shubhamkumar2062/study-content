# Unit 1: Introduction to biochemistry and carbohydrates

This unit covers syllabus chapters 1 (introduction, 2 hours) and 2 (carbohydrates, 5 hours).

## 1.1 What is biochemistry?

**Biochemistry** is the study of the chemical substances present in living things and of the chemical reactions that take place in living cells. It explains life in the language of chemistry: what the body is made of (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, vitamins, minerals, water) and how these substances are built up, broken down and controlled.

The word comes from "bios" (life) and "chemistry". The chemical reactions of the body taken together are called **metabolism**.

## 1.2 Scope of biochemistry in pharmacy

A pharmacist needs biochemistry in almost every area of work.

| Area | How biochemistry helps |
| --- | --- |
| How drugs act | Many drugs act on enzymes or receptors (statins block HMG-CoA reductase, allopurinol blocks xanthine oxidase, aspirin blocks cyclooxygenase). |
| Drug metabolism | Liver enzymes (cytochrome P450 in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum) change drugs into other forms; this decides dose and drug interactions. |
| Diagnosis | Blood and urine tests (glucose, urea, creatinine, cholesterol, bilirubin, enzymes such as SGPT) are biochemical tests. Pharmacists in hospitals and laboratories read these reports. |
| Nutrition and supplements | Knowledge of vitamins, minerals, amino acids and oral rehydration salts helps in counselling and in choosing supplements. |
| Biotechnology products | Insulin, vaccines, interferons and monoclonal antibodies are made by recombinant DNA technology. |
| Disease understanding | Diabetes mellitus, jaundice, phenylketonuria, gout and fatty liver are disorders of metabolism. |
| Formulation | Enzymes and proteins in formulations (pancreatin, papain) need suitable pH and temperature for stability. |
| Clinical pathology laboratory | A D.Pharm holder may help in a clinical laboratory; the practical part of this subject trains for it. |

## 1.3 The cell and its biochemical organization

The **cell** is the structural and functional unit of life. Cells are of two types.

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Example | Bacteria | Human, animal, plant and fungal cells |
| Nucleus | No true nucleus; DNA lies free in the cytoplasm (nucleoid) | True nucleus with a nuclear membrane |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Present (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes) |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in cytoplasm (70S in mitochondria) |
| Size | Small, about 1–10 µm | Larger, about 10–100 µm |

### Organelles and their biochemical functions

```text
                 ┌──────────────── Plasma membrane ────────────────┐
                 │   Cytosol: glycolysis, HMP shunt,                │
                 │   fatty acid synthesis, glycogen metabolism      │
                 │                                                  │
                 │   ┌──────────┐    Rough ER ═══ (ribosomes)       │
                 │   │ Nucleus  │    Smooth ER ── drug metabolism   │
                 │   │  DNA,    │                                   │
                 │   │ nucleolus│    Golgi  ))) packing, glycosylation
                 │   └──────────┘                                   │
                 │   (Mitochondrion) TCA, β-oxidation, ETC, ATP     │
                 │   (Lysosome) hydrolytic enzymes                  │
                 │   (Peroxisome) catalase, H2O2 breakdown          │
                 └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Part of the cell | Biochemical function |
| --- | --- |
| **Plasma membrane** | Lipid bilayer of phospholipids with cholesterol and proteins (fluid mosaic model, Singer and Nicolson, 1972). Selective permeability, transport of nutrients and drugs, receptors for hormones. |
| **Nucleus** | Holds DNA (genetic material). DNA replication and transcription (RNA synthesis) take place here. The **nucleolus** makes ribosomal RNA. |
| **Mitochondria** ("power house") | Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, β-oxidation of fatty acids, electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation (ATP formation), ketone body formation, part of the urea cycle. Have their own DNA. |
| **Rough endoplasmic reticulum** | Ribosomes on its surface make proteins, especially proteins for export (secretory proteins). |
| **Smooth endoplasmic reticulum** | Synthesis of lipids and steroids; metabolism of drugs and toxins by cytochrome P450 enzymes (the "microsomal" enzymes). |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Modifies proteins (adds sugar chains – glycosylation), packs them into vesicles and sends them to their destination. |
| **Lysosomes** ("suicide bags") | Contain acid hydrolases (proteases, lipases, nucleases) that digest worn-out parts and engulfed bacteria. |
| **Peroxisomes** | Contain catalase and oxidases; destroy hydrogen peroxide; oxidise very long chain fatty acids. |
| **Ribosomes** | Site of protein synthesis (translation of mRNA). |
| **Cytosol** | Glycolysis, hexose monophosphate (HMP) shunt, fatty acid synthesis, glycogen synthesis and breakdown. |

## 1.4 Carbohydrates: definition

**Carbohydrates** are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones, or compounds that give these on hydrolysis.

Many have the general formula **Cn(H2O)n**, which is why they were once called "hydrates of carbon". The formula is not a strict rule: deoxyribose (C5H10O4) does not fit it, while lactic acid (C3H6O3) fits it but is not a carbohydrate.

## 1.5 Classification of carbohydrates

```text
                       Carbohydrates
        ┌──────────────┬──────┴────────┬──────────────────┐
 Monosaccharides  Disaccharides  Oligosaccharides   Polysaccharides
 (one sugar unit) (two units)    (3–10 units)       (more than 10 units)
                                                    ┌──────┴──────┐
                                              Homopolysaccharides  Heteropolysaccharides
```

### Monosaccharides

They cannot be hydrolysed into simpler sugars. They are classified by the **number of carbon atoms** and by the **functional group** (aldehyde = aldose; ketone = ketose).

| Carbon atoms | Name | Aldose example | Ketose example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | Triose | Glyceraldehyde | Dihydroxyacetone |
| 4 | Tetrose | Erythrose | Erythrulose |
| 5 | Pentose | Ribose, xylose, arabinose | Ribulose, xylulose |
| 6 | Hexose | Glucose, galactose, mannose | Fructose |
| 7 | Heptose | Glucoheptose | Sedoheptulose |

### Disaccharides

Two monosaccharides joined by a **glycosidic bond**: maltose, lactose, sucrose (details in 1.8).

### Oligosaccharides

3 to 10 monosaccharide units, for example raffinose (a trisaccharide) and the sugar chains of glycoproteins.

### Polysaccharides (glycans)

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Homopolysaccharides | Made of one kind of monosaccharide | Starch, glycogen, cellulose, dextrin (all glucose); inulin (fructose) |
| Heteropolysaccharides | Made of two or more kinds of units | Heparin, hyaluronic acid, chondroitin sulphate (mucopolysaccharides), agar, gums |

## 1.6 Chemical properties of carbohydrates

1. **Reducing property.** Sugars with a free (or potentially free) aldehyde or ketone group reduce mild oxidising agents such as alkaline copper sulphate (Benedict's and Fehling's reagents). Cupric ions (blue) are reduced to cuprous oxide (red). All monosaccharides, maltose and lactose are reducing; sucrose is non-reducing.
2. **Osazone formation.** Reducing sugars heated with phenylhydrazine give yellow crystals called osazones. Their shape helps identify the sugar. Glucose, fructose and mannose give the same osazone because the reaction involves carbons 1 and 2 only.
3. **Mutarotation.** A freshly made solution of glucose slowly changes its optical rotation until it reaches a fixed value. This happens because the α and β forms of glucose change into each other through the open-chain form until an equilibrium is reached (about 1/3 α and 2/3 β).
4. **Oxidation.** Mild oxidation of the aldehyde group gives aldonic acids (glucose → gluconic acid); oxidation of the last carbon gives uronic acids (glucuronic acid, used by the liver to conjugate bilirubin and drugs).
5. **Reduction.** The carbonyl group is reduced to an alcohol: glucose → sorbitol, mannose → mannitol (used as an osmotic diuretic).
6. **Dehydration by strong acids.** Concentrated acids remove water from sugars to give furfural (from pentoses) or hydroxymethylfurfural (from hexoses). These condense with phenols to give coloured products – the basis of Molisch's, Seliwanoff's and Bial's tests.
7. **Glycoside formation.** The anomeric –OH of a sugar joins with an –OH or –NH of another molecule to form a glycoside (for example digoxin is a cardiac glycoside).
8. **Fermentation.** Yeast ferments glucose to ethanol and carbon dioxide.
9. **Ester formation.** Sugars form phosphate esters, such as glucose-6-phosphate, which are the active forms in metabolism.

## 1.7 Structures of monosaccharides

### Glucose (dextrose, grape sugar)

C6H12O6, an **aldohexose**. It is the main sugar of blood and the main fuel of the brain.

```text
 Open chain (Fischer)          Ring form (Haworth), α-D-glucopyranose

      H–C=O   C1                          CH2OH (C6)
        |                                  |
     H–C–OH   C2                      C5 ──O──
        |                             /         \
    HO–C–H    C3                    C4           C1 ─ OH (α: OH down)
        |                             \         /
     H–C–OH   C4                      C3 ───── C2
        |                             (OH)     (OH)
     H–C–OH   C5   (D-sugar: OH on right at C5)
        |
       CH2OH  C6
```

In water, glucose exists mainly as a six-membered ring (**pyranose**) formed when the –OH on C5 reacts with the aldehyde at C1. C1 then becomes a new chiral centre, called the **anomeric carbon**; this gives α and β forms.

### Fructose (laevulose, fruit sugar)

C6H12O6, a **ketohexose** (keto group at C2). It forms a five-membered ring (**furanose**) in sucrose. It is the sweetest common sugar and is found in fruits, honey and semen.

```text
   Open chain           Haworth (β-D-fructofuranose)
      CH2OH  C1             HOCH2(C6)    O     CH2OH (C1)
      C=O    C2                   \    /   \  /
   HO–C–H    C3                    C5      C2
    H–C–OH   C4                     \       /  \
    H–C–OH   C5                     C4 ── C3    OH
      CH2OH  C6
```

### Galactose

An aldohexose like glucose. It differs from glucose only in the position of the –OH at **C4** (it is the C4 **epimer** of glucose). It comes from lactose of milk and is a part of glycolipids and glycoproteins.

**Epimers** are sugars that differ only in the arrangement around one carbon atom other than the anomeric carbon: glucose and galactose (C4), glucose and mannose (C2).

## 1.8 Structures of disaccharides

| Disaccharide | Units | Linkage | Reducing? | Source / note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Maltose** (malt sugar) | Glucose + glucose | α(1→4) | Yes | Formed when amylase digests starch |
| **Lactose** (milk sugar) | Galactose + glucose | β(1→4) | Yes | Milk; lactase deficiency causes lactose intolerance |
| **Sucrose** (cane sugar, table sugar) | Glucose + fructose | α1 → β2 | No (both anomeric carbons are used in the bond) | Sugar cane, beet; hydrolysis gives "invert sugar" |

```text
 Maltose:   Glc ─α(1→4)─ Glc      (free anomeric C1 on the second glucose → reducing)
 Lactose:   Gal ─β(1→4)─ Glc      (free anomeric C1 on glucose → reducing)
 Sucrose:   Glc ─α1↔β2─ Fru       (no free anomeric carbon → non-reducing)
```

**Invert sugar:** sucrose is dextrorotatory. On hydrolysis (by acid or the enzyme invertase/sucrase) it gives equal amounts of glucose and fructose. Fructose is strongly laevorotatory, so the mixture turns to the left. This change of sign is called inversion, and the mixture is called invert sugar (honey is mostly invert sugar).

## 1.9 Polysaccharides: starch and glycogen

### Starch

The storage carbohydrate of **plants** (rice, wheat, potato, maize). It is a mixture of two glucose polymers.

| Part | Structure | Share | Iodine colour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Amylose** | Long unbranched chain of glucose with α(1→4) bonds; coils into a helix | About 15–20% | Deep blue |
| **Amylopectin** | α(1→4) chains with α(1→6) branches about every 24–30 glucose units | About 80–85% | Reddish-violet |

Starch gives a blue colour with iodine because iodine fits inside the amylose helix. Starch is not reducing (very few free reducing ends). Amylase hydrolyses it stepwise: starch → dextrins → maltose.

### Glycogen ("animal starch")

The storage carbohydrate of **animals**, stored mainly in the **liver** (keeps blood glucose up between meals) and in **skeletal muscle** (fuel for the muscle itself).

- Made of glucose units with α(1→4) bonds and α(1→6) branches.
- **More branched than amylopectin** – a branch about every 8–12 glucose units. The many branch ends allow fast release of glucose.
- Gives a **red-brown (reddish-violet)** colour with iodine.

```text
 Amylose:      o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o     (straight α1→4)

 Amylopectin:  o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o
                       |  (α1→6)
                       o-o-o-o-o-o         (few branches)

 Glycogen:     o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o
                 |     |     |
                 o-o-o o-o-o o-o-o         (many short branches)
                   |     |
                   o-o   o-o
```

Other polysaccharides worth knowing: **cellulose** (β(1→4) glucose; plant cell walls; humans cannot digest it but it gives dietary fibre), **inulin** (fructose polymer; used to measure GFR), **dextrans** (plasma expanders), **heparin** (anticoagulant), **hyaluronic acid** (joint fluid, used in eye surgery).

## 1.10 Qualitative tests for carbohydrates

| Test | Reagent | Positive result | Shows |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Molisch's** | α-naphthol in alcohol + conc. H2SO4 along the side | Violet (purple) ring at the junction | Any carbohydrate (general test) |
| **Iodine** | Dilute iodine solution | Blue (starch); red-brown (glycogen, dextrins) | Polysaccharides |
| **Benedict's** | Alkaline copper sulphate with sodium citrate; boil | Green → yellow → orange → brick-red precipitate | Reducing sugars |
| **Fehling's** | Fehling's A (CuSO4) + Fehling's B (alkaline sodium potassium tartrate); boil | Red precipitate of Cu2O | Reducing sugars |
| **Barfoed's** | Copper acetate in acetic acid; boil 2–3 minutes | Brick-red precipitate quickly (within about 2–3 minutes) | Reducing monosaccharides (disaccharides react only after longer heating) |
| **Seliwanoff's** | Resorcinol in HCl; boil | Cherry-red colour quickly | Ketoses (fructose, and sucrose after hydrolysis) |
| **Bial's** | Orcinol in HCl with ferric chloride | Green or blue-green colour | Pentoses |
| **Osazone** | Phenylhydrazine HCl + sodium acetate; boil | Yellow crystals, examined under microscope | Glucose/fructose: needle-shaped (broom-like); maltose: sunflower/petal shaped; lactose: powder-puff or cotton-ball shaped |
| **Fermentation** | Yeast | Gas (CO2) | Fermentable sugars |

```text
 Systematic scheme
 Molisch +  →  Iodine?  blue → starch;  red-brown → glycogen/dextrin
             →  No colour with iodine → Benedict's
                   negative → non-reducing (sucrose: hydrolyse, then Benedict +, Seliwanoff +)
                   positive → Barfoed's
                       quick red → monosaccharide → Seliwanoff (cherry red = fructose)
                                                   → Bial (green = pentose)
                                                   → else glucose/galactose (osazone)
                       late or no red → disaccharide (maltose/lactose: osazone)
```

## 1.11 Biological role of carbohydrates

1. **Main source of energy.** Glucose is oxidised to give ATP; 1 g of carbohydrate gives about 4 kcal. The brain and red blood cells depend on glucose.
2. **Storage of energy** as glycogen (animals) and starch (plants).
3. **Structural role.** Cellulose in plant cell walls; chitin in insects; mucopolysaccharides (chondroitin sulphate, hyaluronic acid) in cartilage, bone and joints.
4. **Components of important molecules:** ribose and deoxyribose in RNA and DNA; ribose in ATP, NAD, FAD and coenzyme A.
5. **Cell recognition and immunity.** Sugar chains on glycoproteins and glycolipids form blood group antigens and receptor sites.
6. **Anticoagulant:** heparin.
7. **Dietary fibre** (cellulose, pectin) helps bowel movement and lowers cholesterol absorption.
8. **Protein sparing:** enough carbohydrate in the diet stops the body from using protein for energy.
9. **Detoxification:** glucuronic acid joins with bilirubin, steroids and many drugs so they can be excreted.
10. **Pharmaceutical uses:** lactose and starch as tablet diluents and disintegrants, sucrose in syrups, dextrose in intravenous fluids, mannitol as an osmotic diuretic.

## Remember

- Biochemistry = chemistry of life; metabolism = all chemical reactions of the body.
- Mitochondria: TCA, β-oxidation, ETC; cytosol: glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis; smooth ER: drug metabolism.
- Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones; general formula Cn(H2O)n.
- Glucose = aldohexose (pyranose ring); fructose = ketohexose (furanose ring); galactose = C4 epimer of glucose.
- Maltose Glc-α(1→4)-Glc and lactose Gal-β(1→4)-Glc are reducing; sucrose Glc-α1↔β2-Fru is non-reducing.
- Amylose (unbranched, blue with iodine) + amylopectin (branched) = starch; glycogen is more highly branched (red-brown with iodine).
- Molisch = all carbohydrates; Benedict = reducing; Barfoed = monosaccharides; Seliwanoff = ketoses; Bial = pentoses; iodine = starch.
