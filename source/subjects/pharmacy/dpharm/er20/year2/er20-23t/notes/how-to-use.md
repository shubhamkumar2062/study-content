# How to use this guide

Biochemistry and Clinical Pathology (ER20-23T) is a second-year D.Pharm subject with 75 theory hours and a 50-hour practical (ER20-23P). The syllabus has 13 chapters. This guide follows them closely, with four changes:

- Chapter 1 (introduction, 2 hours) is joined with chapter 2 (carbohydrates) in **Unit 1**.
- Chapter 11 (introduction to biotechnology, 1 hour) is joined with chapter 5 (nucleic acids) in **Unit 4**, because biotechnology is built on DNA and RNA.
- Chapter 8 (metabolism, 20 hours) is the largest chapter, so it is split into **Units 7, 8 and 9** (carbohydrates with biological oxidation, lipids, amino acids with jaundice).
- Chapters 9 and 10 (minerals; water and electrolytes) are joined in **Unit 10**.

Metabolism carries almost a quarter of the theory hours, so give Units 7–9 the most time. Study one unit every two or three days: read the notes, draw each pathway once from memory, learn the tables, and then answer the question bank without looking.

## Exam pattern (ER-2020 theory paper)

D.Pharm ER-2020 exams are held yearly by the state boards under the PCI Education Regulations 2020. The theory paper is **80 marks, 3 hours**; another 20 marks come from sessional (internal) assessment. The usual ER-2020 board paper looks like this. Confirm the exact pattern with your college, because boards sometimes change the format.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Objective questions (MCQs / one-word / fill in the blanks) – all compulsory | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| II | Short answers – attempt 10 of 11 | 10 × 3 = 30 |
| III | Long answers – attempt 6 of 7 | 6 × 5 = 30 |

In this guide the **long** questions are 5-mark answers and the **short** questions are 3-mark answers, to match this pattern. A few **very-short** (2-mark) questions are included for quick revision and sessional tests.

## Probability ratings

- **High** – asked in most years; prepare the full answer with pathway diagrams and tables.
- **Medium** – asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** – rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

Ratings are an estimate from the syllabus weight and the kind of questions usually set in D.Pharm papers. They are not a promise.

## Normal laboratory values

Normal values for glucose, urea, creatinine, bilirubin, enzymes, the lipid profile, blood counts and urine are given as **commonly quoted reference ranges** and are marked "about". **Ranges vary by laboratory**, method, age and sex. In the exam, write the range your textbook or teacher uses, and add "varies with the laboratory" – examiners accept small differences.

## Scoring tips for Biochemistry and Clinical Pathology

1. **Begin with a definition.** Glycolysis, transamination, jaundice, ketosis, lipoprotein, enzyme inhibitor – one or two clear lines first.
2. **Draw the pathway.** For glycolysis, TCA cycle, urea cycle, β-oxidation, ketogenesis, glycogen metabolism and the electron transport chain, a neat flow diagram with enzyme names on the arrows earns most of the marks. Structures are **not** needed in metabolism (the syllabus says "without chemical structures").
3. **Always add site, key enzyme, energy yield and significance** to a pathway answer. Examiners look for these four points.
4. **Use tables for comparisons:** DNA vs RNA, competitive vs non-competitive inhibition, fat- vs water-soluble vitamins, kwashiorkor vs marasmus, the three types of jaundice, ICF vs ECF, type 1 vs type 2 diabetes.
5. **Classify with examples.** Every classification (carbohydrates, proteins, amino acids, lipids, enzymes, vitamins, minerals) should carry at least one example for each class.
6. **Link to pharmacy.** Mention drugs where they fit: statins (HMG-CoA reductase), allopurinol (xanthine oxidase), methotrexate (dihydrofolate reductase), isoniazid (vitamin B6), warfarin (vitamin K), lactulose (hyperammonaemia), streptokinase and asparaginase (therapeutic enzymes). This makes an average answer a good one.
7. **For clinical questions** (organ function tests, urine and blood abnormalities), write the test, its approximate normal value, and what a rise or fall means.
8. **For a 5-mark long answer**, aim for about one and a half pages: definition, diagram or table, 4–6 explained points, significance or clinical correlation.
9. **For a 3-mark short answer**, write 5–6 crisp points or a small table.
10. **Practical knowledge scores marks in theory too.** Molisch, Benedict, biuret, Rothera's, Fouchet's, Hay's and Ehrlich's tests often appear as short questions; the practical guides give their principle and colour change.

## Practicals and assignments

The practical syllabus (ER20-23P) asks for qualitative tests of carbohydrates, proteins and lipids, urine analysis, estimation of urine glucose, creatinine and chlorides, estimation of blood and serum constituents (glucose, creatinine, cholesterol, calcium, urea, SGOT/SGPT) and the hydrolysis of starch. The printed syllabus lists "creatine" among the blood tests; colleges almost always run **serum creatinine**, so that is the guide given here. Most estimations are done with commercial kits on a colorimeter or semi-auto analyser, so always follow the kit insert for volumes, timings and wavelength.

The syllabus also asks for at least **three written assignments on pathology laboratory reports** (one per sessional period). Collect sample reports (CBC, LFT, KFT, lipid profile, urine routine), write the normal range beside each value, mark the abnormal ones, and explain what they suggest using Units 11 and 12.
