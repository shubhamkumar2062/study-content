# Unit 9: Amino acid metabolism and jaundice

This unit covers the amino acid (protein) part of syllabus chapter 8, together with the disorders listed there: disorders of ammonia metabolism, phenylketonuria, alkaptonuria and jaundice. Pathways are studied without chemical structures.

## 9.1 Overview: the amino acid pool

Dietary proteins are digested by **pepsin** (stomach), **trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, carboxypeptidase** (pancreas) and **aminopeptidases and dipeptidases** (intestine) into free amino acids, which are absorbed by active transport.

```text
   Dietary protein ─┐                         ┌─→ Body proteins (synthesis)
   Tissue protein ──┼─→  AMINO ACID POOL  ────┼─→ Special products (haem, hormones,
   breakdown         │   (about 100 g)         │   neurotransmitters, creatine, purines)
   Synthesis of ─────┘                         └─→ Breakdown:
   non-essential                                     amino group → NH3 → UREA (liver)
   amino acids                                       carbon skeleton → glucose, fat or energy
```

The body has no special store for amino acids; excess amino acids are broken down. Breakdown has two parts: **removal of the amino group** (transamination and deamination) and **use of the carbon skeleton**.

**Nitrogen balance:** intake of nitrogen minus loss. Positive in growth, pregnancy and recovery; negative in starvation, fever, burns, surgery, cancer and protein deficiency.

## 9.2 Transamination

**Definition:** the **transfer of an amino group (–NH2) from an α-amino acid to an α-keto acid**, forming a new amino acid and a new keto acid. No free ammonia is released.

```text
   Amino acid 1  +  α-Keto acid 2   ⇌   α-Keto acid 1  +  Amino acid 2
                 (transaminase / aminotransferase, coenzyme PLP)

 Most common acceptor:  α-ketoglutarate  →  glutamate
```

### Two important transaminases

```text
 ALT (alanine transaminase, SGPT):
   Alanine + α-Ketoglutarate  ⇌  Pyruvate + Glutamate

 AST (aspartate transaminase, SGOT):
   Aspartate + α-Ketoglutarate ⇌  Oxaloacetate + Glutamate
```

### Features

- Catalysed by **transaminases (aminotransferases)**, which need **pyridoxal phosphate (PLP, from vitamin B6)** as coenzyme. PLP accepts the amino group (becoming pyridoxamine phosphate) and then hands it on.
- **Reversible**, so it works both in breakdown and in synthesis of amino acids.
- All amino acids except **lysine, threonine** (and proline, hydroxyproline) take part.
- Takes place in the cytosol and mitochondria of most tissues, especially liver, heart, muscle and kidney.

### Significance

1. **Collects amino groups** from many amino acids into **glutamate**, which is then deaminated to release ammonia for urea formation.
2. **Synthesis of non-essential amino acids** from keto acids (pyruvate → alanine; oxaloacetate → aspartate).
3. **Links amino acid and carbohydrate metabolism** – carbon skeletons enter glycolysis and TCA cycle (pyruvate, oxaloacetate, α-ketoglutarate).
4. **Diagnostic importance:** ALT and AST leak into blood when cells are damaged – **ALT rises mainly in liver disease (hepatitis)**; AST in liver disease, heart attack and muscle injury (Unit 11).

## 9.3 Deamination

**Definition:** the **removal of the amino group as free ammonia (NH3)** from an amino acid, leaving a keto acid.

### Oxidative deamination (main type)

```text
 Glutamate + NAD+ (or NADP+) + H2O  ──glutamate dehydrogenase──→  α-Ketoglutarate + NH3 + NADH
```

- **Glutamate dehydrogenase** is found in the **mitochondria of the liver** (and kidney); it is unusual because it can use either NAD+ or NADP+.
- It is reversible.
- **Transdeamination** = transamination (collecting amino groups as glutamate) + oxidative deamination of glutamate. This is the main route of ammonia formation from most amino acids.
- Minor oxidative deamination by **L-amino acid oxidase** and **D-amino acid oxidase** (FMN/FAD) in the liver and kidney.

### Non-oxidative deamination

Some amino acids lose ammonia without oxidation, for example **serine and threonine by dehydratases** (PLP), **histidine by histidase**, **cysteine by desulphhydrase**.

### Significance

- Releases **ammonia**, which is detoxified to **urea** in the liver.
- The keto acid left behind is used for energy, glucose or fat.
- Ammonia from deamination in the kidney (from glutamine by glutaminase) helps excrete acid (acid–base balance).

## 9.4 Ammonia: sources, transport and toxicity

| Sources of ammonia | Removal |
| --- | --- |
| Deamination of amino acids (main) | **Urea formation in the liver (main)** |
| Bacterial action on proteins and urea in the large intestine | Glutamine formation (glutamine synthetase) in brain, muscle and other tissues |
| Breakdown of purines and pyrimidines, amines | Excretion as ammonium ions by the kidney |
| Glutaminase in the kidney | |

- Ammonia is **toxic**, especially to the **brain**. Normal blood ammonia is kept very low.
- It travels safely in blood as **glutamine** (from most tissues) and **alanine** (from muscle, the glucose–alanine cycle).
- **Why ammonia is toxic to the brain:** excess ammonia combines with α-ketoglutarate to form glutamate and glutamine, depleting α-ketoglutarate → TCA cycle slows → less ATP; glutamine accumulation causes brain cell swelling; neurotransmitter balance (glutamate, GABA) is disturbed.

## 9.5 Urea cycle (Krebs–Henseleit cycle, ornithine cycle)

**Definition:** the cyclic pathway in the **liver** by which **toxic ammonia** is converted into **urea**, which is non-toxic, water-soluble and excreted in urine. Proposed by Hans Krebs and Kurt Henseleit (1932).

**Site:** liver only; the first two steps are in the **mitochondria**, the rest in the **cytosol**.

```text
  MITOCHONDRIA
  NH3 + CO2 + 2 ATP ──① carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS-I; activator N-acetylglutamate)──→ Carbamoyl phosphate
  Carbamoyl phosphate + ORNITHINE ──② ornithine transcarbamoylase──→ CITRULLINE
                                                                          │ (moves to cytosol)
  CYTOSOL                                                                 ▼
  Citrulline + ASPARTATE + ATP ──③ argininosuccinate synthetase──→ ARGININOSUCCINATE (+ AMP + PPi)
  Argininosuccinate ──④ argininosuccinate lyase──→ ARGININE + FUMARATE (→ TCA cycle)
  Arginine + H2O ──⑤ arginase──→ UREA + ORNITHINE (returns to mitochondria to restart the cycle)
```

```text
            NH3 + CO2
                │ ①
        Carbamoyl phosphate
                │ ②
   Ornithine ──→ Citrulline
      ▲              │ ③ ← Aspartate (second N)
      │ ⑤            ▼
   Arginine ←─④─ Argininosuccinate
      │               └→ Fumarate → TCA
      ▼
     UREA → blood → kidney → urine
```

### Key points

- **Sources of the two nitrogen atoms of urea:** one from **free ammonia** (via carbamoyl phosphate), one from **aspartate**. The carbon comes from **CO2** (bicarbonate).
- **Energy:** 3 ATP are used, but 4 high-energy phosphate bonds (step 3 makes AMP + PPi).
- **Rate-limiting enzyme:** carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I, which needs **N-acetylglutamate** as an allosteric activator. A protein-rich diet increases N-acetylglutamate and the cycle enzymes.
- **Link with the TCA cycle** ("Krebs bicycle"): fumarate from step 4 enters the TCA cycle to form oxaloacetate, which is transaminated to aspartate for step 3.
- An adult excretes about 20–30 g of urea per day on a usual diet; it is the main nitrogenous waste of urine.

### Significance

- Detoxifies ammonia; the main route for disposal of nitrogen.
- Arginine is formed in the cycle (semi-essential amino acid).
- Blood urea rises in kidney failure (cannot excrete urea) and falls in severe liver disease (cannot make urea).

## 9.6 Decarboxylation

**Definition:** the removal of the **carboxyl group as CO2** from an amino acid to form the corresponding **biogenic amine**. Catalysed by **decarboxylases**, which need **pyridoxal phosphate**.

| Amino acid | Amine formed | Function of the amine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Histidine** | **Histamine** | Allergy and inflammation, gastric acid secretion, vasodilatation |
| **Tyrosine** (via DOPA) | **Dopamine** → noradrenaline → adrenaline | Neurotransmitters and hormones (Parkinson's disease = dopamine deficiency; levodopa is given with carbidopa, a decarboxylase inhibitor) |
| **Tryptophan** (via 5-hydroxytryptophan) | **Serotonin (5-HT)** → melatonin | Neurotransmitter (mood, sleep), vasoconstriction, platelets |
| **Glutamate** | **GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid)** | Main inhibitory neurotransmitter of the brain (convulsions in B6 deficiency) |
| Cysteine | Cysteamine (→ taurine) | Part of coenzyme A; bile salt conjugation |
| Serine | Ethanolamine | Phospholipids (cephalin) |
| Lysine | Cadaverine | Formed by bacteria (putrefaction) |
| Ornithine | Putrescine | Cell growth (polyamines) |

**Significance:** forms many hormones, neurotransmitters and local hormones from amino acids; several drugs act on these pathways (carbidopa, antihistamines, MAO inhibitors).

## 9.7 Fate of the carbon skeleton (brief)

After removal of nitrogen, the carbon skeletons of the 20 amino acids form seven products: **pyruvate, acetyl CoA, acetoacetyl CoA, α-ketoglutarate, succinyl CoA, fumarate, oxaloacetate**. Those forming glucose are **glucogenic**; those forming acetyl CoA or acetoacetyl CoA are **ketogenic** (leucine and lysine only); some are both (isoleucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, threonine).

## 9.8 Diseases related to abnormal amino acid metabolism

### Disorders of ammonia metabolism (hyperammonaemia)

**Hyperammonaemia** = raised blood ammonia.

| Type | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| **Acquired** (more common) | **Liver failure, cirrhosis**, hepatitis (liver cannot make urea); portal-systemic shunting (ammonia from the gut bypasses the liver); GI bleeding (blood protein digested by bacteria); constipation; high-protein meal in liver disease |
| **Inherited (urea cycle disorders)** | Deficiency of a urea cycle enzyme: **hyperammonaemia type I** (CPS-I), **type II** (ornithine transcarbamoylase – the most common, X-linked), **citrullinaemia** (argininosuccinate synthetase), **argininosuccinic aciduria** (argininosuccinate lyase), **hyperargininaemia** (arginase); N-acetylglutamate synthase deficiency |

- **Features:** vomiting, refusal of protein feeds, lethargy, irritability, slurred speech, **flapping tremor (asterixis)**, blurred vision, confusion, convulsions, coma (**hepatic encephalopathy** in liver disease); in newborns with urea cycle defects, symptoms start soon after milk feeding begins.
- **Treatment:** low-protein diet with essential amino acids; **lactulose** (acidifies colon contents, traps ammonia as ammonium and acts as a laxative); antibiotics such as rifaximin or neomycin to reduce gut bacteria; sodium benzoate and sodium phenylbutyrate (remove nitrogen as hippurate and phenylacetylglutamine); arginine supplements in some urea cycle defects; dialysis in severe cases.

### Phenylketonuria (PKU)

- **Defect:** inherited (autosomal recessive) deficiency of **phenylalanine hydroxylase**, the liver enzyme that converts **phenylalanine → tyrosine** (needs tetrahydrobiopterin). Rarely, a defect in the cofactor.
- **Biochemistry:** phenylalanine accumulates in blood and is diverted to **phenylpyruvate, phenyllactate and phenylacetate**, which appear in urine ("phenyl-keto-uria"). Tyrosine becomes deficient, so less melanin, dopamine and noradrenaline are made.
- **Features:** **severe mental retardation** if untreated, seizures, hyperactivity, eczema; **light skin, fair hair, blue eyes** (less melanin); **mousy (musty) odour** of urine and body (phenylacetate).
- **Diagnosis:** newborn screening by **Guthrie test** (bacterial inhibition assay) or modern blood methods; **ferric chloride test** on urine (transient green colour with phenylpyruvate).
- **Treatment:** **low-phenylalanine diet** started early in infancy and continued long-term (special formulas); tyrosine becomes essential; avoid **aspartame** (it contains phenylalanine) – medicines and foods with aspartame carry a warning for PKU patients. Mothers with PKU need strict control during pregnancy.

### Alkaptonuria

- **Defect:** inherited deficiency of **homogentisate oxidase (homogentisic acid 1,2-dioxygenase)** in the tyrosine breakdown pathway. Described by Archibald Garrod as one of the first "inborn errors of metabolism" (1902).
- **Biochemistry:** **homogentisic acid** accumulates and is excreted in urine; on exposure to air (oxidation), especially in alkaline urine, it forms a black pigment (alkapton).
- **Features:** **urine turns black on standing** (often the first sign, noticed on nappies); usually harmless in childhood; in adults pigment deposits in connective tissue (**ochronosis** – bluish-black ears, sclera, cartilage), **arthritis** of spine and large joints, heart valve problems.
- **Tests:** urine darkens on standing or on adding alkali; ferric chloride gives a transient blue-black colour; Benedict's reagent is reduced (homogentisic acid is a reducing substance – a false positive for sugar).
- **Treatment:** no cure; low-protein (low phenylalanine and tyrosine) diet, vitamin C; nitisinone reduces homogentisic acid production.

### Other inborn errors (for MCQs)

| Disorder | Defect |
| --- | --- |
| Albinism | Tyrosinase (no melanin) |
| Maple syrup urine disease | Branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase (valine, leucine, isoleucine) – urine smells like burnt sugar |
| Homocystinuria | Cystathionine β-synthase |
| Tyrosinaemia | Enzymes of tyrosine breakdown |
| Hartnup disease | Defective intestinal and renal transport of neutral amino acids (tryptophan) – pellagra-like rash |
| Cystinuria | Defective renal reabsorption of cystine, ornithine, lysine, arginine – cystine stones |

## 9.9 Jaundice

### Bilirubin formation and excretion

```text
 Old red cells (120 days) → spleen/reticuloendothelial system
   Haemoglobin → globin (amino acids) + haem
   Haem ──haem oxygenase──→ biliverdin (green) + Fe (reused) + CO
   Biliverdin ──biliverdin reductase──→ BILIRUBIN (unconjugated, indirect)
        │ carried in blood bound to ALBUMIN (water-insoluble, not filtered by kidney)
        ▼
 LIVER: uptake → CONJUGATION with glucuronic acid
        (UDP-glucuronyl transferase) → bilirubin diglucuronide (conjugated, direct, water-soluble)
        → secreted into bile
        ▼
 INTESTINE: bacteria → UROBILINOGEN
        ├→ most → stercobilinogen → stercobilin (brown colour of faeces)
        └→ some reabsorbed → portal blood → liver (re-excreted) / kidney → urine urobilinogen
                                                    → urobilin (yellow colour of urine)
```

About 250–350 mg of bilirubin is formed each day, mainly from haemoglobin.

**Normal serum bilirubin** is commonly quoted as about **0.2–1.0 mg/dl total** (up to about 1.2 mg/dl in some laboratories), with conjugated (direct) bilirubin below about 0.3 mg/dl; ranges vary by laboratory.

### Definition and classification

**Jaundice (icterus)** is the **yellow colour of the skin, sclera and mucous membranes** due to a raised level of **bilirubin** in the blood (hyperbilirubinaemia). It becomes visible when serum bilirubin rises above about **2–2.5 mg/dl**; between the normal upper limit and this level it is called **latent (subclinical) jaundice**.

| Feature | **Pre-hepatic (haemolytic)** | **Hepatic (hepatocellular)** | **Post-hepatic (obstructive, cholestatic)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Excess breakdown of red cells: haemolytic anaemias (sickle cell, thalassaemia, G6PD deficiency, malaria, incompatible transfusion), **neonatal (physiological) jaundice** | Liver cell damage: **viral hepatitis**, alcohol, cirrhosis, drugs (paracetamol overdose, isoniazid), Gilbert's and Crigler–Najjar syndromes | Blocked bile flow: **gallstones**, carcinoma of head of pancreas, stricture of bile duct |
| Raised bilirubin | **Unconjugated (indirect)** | **Both** conjugated and unconjugated | **Conjugated (direct)** |
| Van den Bergh reaction | Indirect positive | Biphasic | Direct positive |
| Bilirubin in urine | **Absent** (acholuric jaundice – unconjugated is not water-soluble) | Present | **Present** (dark, tea-coloured urine) |
| Urine urobilinogen | **Increased** | Normal or increased (early), may decrease | **Absent** (bile does not reach the gut) |
| Faeces colour | Dark (more stercobilin) | Normal or pale | **Pale, clay-coloured** (no stercobilin); fatty (steatorrhoea) |
| Bile salts in urine | Absent | May be present | **Present** – cause **itching (pruritus)** and slow pulse |
| Serum enzymes | Normal | **ALT and AST markedly raised** | **Alkaline phosphatase (and GGT) markedly raised**; ALT/AST mildly raised |
| Other | Anaemia, high reticulocyte count | Low albumin, prolonged prothrombin time in severe disease | Prolonged prothrombin time corrected by vitamin K (fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption) |

**Van den Bergh reaction:** bilirubin reacts with **diazotised sulphanilic acid** to give a reddish-purple azobilirubin. Conjugated bilirubin reacts **directly** in water ("direct"); unconjugated bilirubin reacts only after adding **alcohol (methanol)** ("indirect" = total − direct).

### Neonatal (physiological) jaundice

Common in newborns between the 2nd and about the 7th day: more red cell breakdown (fetal haemoglobin cells) and immature liver conjugation (low UDP-glucuronyl transferase). Usually mild and clears by itself. High levels of unconjugated bilirubin can cross the immature blood–brain barrier and damage the brain (**kernicterus**). Treatment: **phototherapy** (blue light changes bilirubin into water-soluble forms), exchange transfusion if severe. Drugs such as sulphonamides that push bilirubin off albumin should be avoided in newborns.

### Inherited hyperbilirubinaemias (brief)

- **Gilbert's syndrome:** mild unconjugated rise from reduced conjugation; harmless.
- **Crigler–Najjar syndrome:** absent (type I) or reduced (type II) UDP-glucuronyl transferase; severe unconjugated jaundice.
- **Dubin–Johnson** and **Rotor syndromes:** defective excretion of conjugated bilirubin.

## Remember

- Transamination: amino group to α-ketoglutarate → glutamate; transaminases need PLP (B6); ALT (alanine ⇌ pyruvate), AST (aspartate ⇌ oxaloacetate).
- Oxidative deamination: glutamate dehydrogenase (liver mitochondria) → α-ketoglutarate + NH3.
- Urea cycle: liver; CPS-I (rate-limiting, N-acetylglutamate) and OTC in mitochondria; ASS, ASL, arginase in cytosol; N from NH3 and aspartate; 3 ATP (4 high-energy bonds); fumarate links to TCA.
- Decarboxylation (PLP): histidine → histamine, tyrosine → dopamine, tryptophan → serotonin, glutamate → GABA.
- Hyperammonaemia: liver failure or urea cycle enzyme defects; flapping tremor, coma; treat with low protein, lactulose.
- PKU: phenylalanine hydroxylase; mental retardation, mousy odour, fair skin; Guthrie test, ferric chloride test; low-phenylalanine diet, avoid aspartame.
- Alkaptonuria: homogentisate oxidase; urine turns black on standing; ochronosis, arthritis.
- Jaundice: bilirubin above about 2–2.5 mg/dl; haemolytic (unconjugated, urobilinogen ↑, no bilirubinuria), hepatic (both, ALT ↑), obstructive (conjugated, bilirubinuria, clay stools, ALP ↑, urobilinogen absent).
