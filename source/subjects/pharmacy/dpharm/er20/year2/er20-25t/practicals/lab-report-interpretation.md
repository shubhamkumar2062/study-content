# Interpretation of laboratory reports to optimise drug therapy

**Aim:** To interpret the laboratory reports of two clinical cases and suggest how the drug therapy should be adjusted.

> The cases below are worked examples. Use the reference range printed on the report you are given; values here are typical adult ranges.

## Principle

Laboratory values show how well the kidney, liver, blood, thyroid and heart are working. Many drugs must be **chosen, dosed or monitored** according to these values – for example renally cleared drugs by creatinine clearance, warfarin by INR and potassium-affecting drugs by serum potassium. The pharmacist compares each value with the reference range, links abnormal values to the disease or a drug, and makes a recommendation.

## Requirements

Case sheets with laboratory reports, reference range chart, a renal dosing reference, calculator, case work-up form.

## Procedure

1. Read the patient details: age, sex, weight, diagnosis and drug chart.
2. List every laboratory value with its reference range; mark high (H) and low (L).
3. Calculate derived values: creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault), anion gap, corrected values where needed.
4. Decide what each abnormal value means: disease, drug effect, or both.
5. Check each drug for dose adjustment, contraindication or monitoring need.
6. Write recommendations and a monitoring plan in SOAP format.

## Observations and calculations

**Case 1.** Man, 65 years, 60 kg, admitted with pneumonia and heart failure. Drugs: gentamicin 80 mg IV every 8 h, furosemide 40 mg twice daily, digoxin 0.25 mg once daily, enalapril 5 mg twice daily.

| Test | Result | Reference | Remark |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serum creatinine | 1.8 mg/dL | 0.6–1.2 | H |
| Blood urea | 68 mg/dL | 15–40 | H |
| Potassium | 3.0 mmol/L | 3.5–5.0 | L |
| Sodium | 136 mmol/L | 135–145 | Normal |

```text
CrCl = [(140 − 65) × 60] / (72 × 1.8) = (75 × 60) / 129.6 = 4500 / 129.6 ≈ 35 mL/min
```

Interpretation and recommendations:

1. Reduced kidney function (CrCl about 35 mL/min) → gentamicin and digoxin accumulate. Suggest a longer gentamicin dosing interval or another antibiotic, with serum gentamicin levels; review digoxin dose and check a digoxin level.
2. Low potassium (likely from furosemide) increases the risk of **digoxin toxicity** → potassium replacement and monitoring.
3. Monitor creatinine, potassium and urine output daily; watch for nausea, visual changes or irregular pulse (digoxin toxicity).

**Case 2.** Woman, 52 years, on warfarin for atrial fibrillation, recently started on a course of metronidazole for a dental infection. Complains of gum bleeding.

| Test | Result | Reference / target | Remark |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| INR | 5.2 | target 2–3 | H |
| Haemoglobin | 10.8 g/dL | 12–15 | L |
| Platelets | 2.4 lakh/µL | 1.5–4.5 lakh | Normal |

Interpretation and recommendations:

1. INR far above target with bleeding → **drug interaction**: metronidazole inhibits warfarin metabolism.
2. Recommend: withhold warfarin as per protocol, give vitamin K as decided by the physician, choose an alternative antibiotic if possible, recheck INR daily until back in range, then restart warfarin at an adjusted dose.
3. Low haemoglobin – monitor for continued blood loss.
4. Counsel the patient to report bleeding and to tell every doctor that she takes warfarin.

## Result

Laboratory reports of two cases were interpreted, abnormal values were linked to the disease and drugs, and dose and monitoring recommendations were made.

## Precautions

1. Always use the laboratory's own reference range and units.
2. Look at trends, not a single value.
3. Use actual body weight carefully in obese or oedematous patients.
4. Recommendations go to the prescriber; the pharmacist does not change orders alone.

## Viva questions

1. **Write the Cockcroft–Gault equation.** CrCl = (140 − age) × weight / (72 × SCr), × 0.85 for women.
2. **Why is low potassium dangerous with digoxin?** It increases digoxin binding and toxicity (arrhythmias).
3. **What is the usual INR target for warfarin?** 2–3 for most indications.
4. **Name two drugs that raise serum potassium.** ACE inhibitors and spironolactone.
5. **What does a high ALT suggest?** Liver-cell damage (hepatitis, drug-induced liver injury).
