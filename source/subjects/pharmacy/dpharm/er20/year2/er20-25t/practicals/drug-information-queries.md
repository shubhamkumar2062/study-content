# Systematic approach to drug information queries

**Aim:** To answer two drug information queries in a systematic way using primary, secondary and tertiary sources of information, and to document the response.

> The cases below are worked examples. Your teacher will give you your own queries; follow the same steps and record them in the query form.

## Principle

A good answer depends on understanding the **real question** behind the request and on using reliable, current sources in a logical order. The **modified systematic approach** has seven steps:

```text
1. Secure demographics of the requester (name, profession, contact, urgency)
2. Obtain background information (patient age, weight, diagnosis,
   other drugs, kidney/liver function, why the question is asked)
3. Determine and categorise the ultimate question
   (dose, ADR, interaction, pregnancy/lactation, compatibility, poisoning…)
4. Develop a search strategy and search:
   tertiary → secondary → primary
5. Evaluate, analyse and put together the information
6. Formulate and give the response (verbal and written, with references)
7. Follow up and document
```

## Requirements

Drug information request/response form, standard references (National Formulary of India, CIMS/IDR, Martindale, BNF, Goodman & Gilman, a pregnancy and lactation reference, an interaction reference), computer with internet access to PubMed and other databases, telephone, register or database for documentation.

## Procedure

1. Receive the query in person or by telephone; write the requester's details and the time.
2. Ask open questions to collect background details; restate the question to confirm it.
3. Classify the query and note how urgent it is.
4. Search a tertiary source first for a quick, reliable answer; if more recent or detailed information is needed, search PubMed (secondary) for primary articles.
5. Compare sources; note agreements and differences; judge quality.
6. Write a clear answer: direct answer first, then the reasons, with references.
7. Communicate the answer within the time needed; record it on the form.
8. Follow up with the requester (was the advice used, what was the outcome?).

## Observations (worked cases)

**Case 1 – pregnancy query.** A doctor in the antenatal clinic asks: "Can a pregnant woman at 10 weeks take ondansetron for vomiting?"

| Step | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Requester | Resident doctor, obstetrics OPD; answer needed in 30 minutes |
| Background | 26-year-old, 10 weeks pregnant, persistent vomiting, no other drugs, normal kidney function |
| Category | Drug use in pregnancy (first trimester) |
| Tertiary search | National Formulary of India; pregnancy and lactation reference |
| Secondary/primary | PubMed: recent studies on ondansetron in early pregnancy |
| Evaluation | First-line advice is lifestyle measures and drugs with long safety experience; ondansetron is used when others fail; some studies suggest a small possible risk of certain birth defects in the first trimester while others do not |
| Response | Try first-line options as per the obstetric protocol; if vomiting continues, the obstetrician may use ondansetron after discussing the small uncertain risk; ensure fluids and electrolytes |
| Follow-up | Called next day – first-line drug given, vomiting controlled |

**Case 2 – IV compatibility query.** A staff nurse in the ICU asks whether phenytoin can be infused in 5% dextrose.

| Step | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Requester | ICU staff nurse, urgent |
| Background | Adult with seizures, phenytoin loading dose ordered; only dextrose line running |
| Category | IV compatibility |
| Tertiary search | Injectable drug compatibility reference |
| Evaluation | Phenytoin precipitates in dextrose solutions |
| Response | Do not mix with dextrose; dilute in 0.9% sodium chloride, give through a separate line or flush the line with saline before and after, use an in-line filter if required, and give at the recommended slow rate with monitoring |
| Follow-up | Infusion given in saline without problems |

## Result

Two drug information queries were answered systematically using tertiary, secondary and primary sources, and the responses were documented.

## Precautions

1. Always find the real question and the patient details before searching.
2. Use current editions and reliable sources; note the edition and date.
3. Do not give an answer you are unsure of – say when you will call back.
4. Keep patient information confidential.
5. Record every query, even simple ones.

## Viva questions

1. **What are tertiary sources? Give examples.** Compiled summaries – textbooks, compendia, databases (Martindale, BNF, NFI, CIMS).
2. **Why start with tertiary sources?** They are quick, easy and reliable for most questions.
3. **Give one advantage and one disadvantage of primary literature.** Most current and detailed; needs skill to evaluate and is voluminous.
4. **What is the "ultimate question"?** The real need behind the request, found from background information.
5. **Why is follow-up needed?** To know whether the advice helped and to improve the service.
