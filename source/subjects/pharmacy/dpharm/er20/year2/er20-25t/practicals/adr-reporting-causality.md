# Filling the IPC ADR reporting form and causality assessment

**Aim:** To fill the Suspected Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Form of the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (PvPI) for two cases and to assess causality using the WHO-UMC scale and the Naranjo algorithm.

> Use the current version of the form available from IPC or your ADR monitoring centre. The cases below are worked examples.

## Principle

Spontaneous reporting of suspected ADRs is the backbone of pharmacovigilance. Under the **Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)**, health professionals report on the IPC form to an ADR monitoring centre, which sends it to the National Coordination Centre at IPC, Ghaziabad. **Causality assessment** judges how likely it is that the drug caused the event. Certainty is **not** needed before reporting.

## Requirements

Blank IPC Suspected ADR Reporting Forms, case details, WHO-UMC causality table, Naranjo questionnaire, pen.

## Procedure

1. Read the case carefully; list all drugs with dates.
2. Fill the form section by section:
   - **Patient information** – initials (not the full name), age or date of birth, sex, weight.
   - **Suspected adverse reaction** – date of onset and recovery, description of the reaction, relevant tests, seriousness (death, life-threatening, hospitalisation, disability, congenital anomaly, other medically important), outcome (recovered, recovering, not recovered, fatal, unknown).
   - **Suspected medication(s)** – brand and generic name, manufacturer, batch number, expiry date, dose, route, frequency, start and stop dates, reason for use; action taken (withdrawn, dose reduced, no change); whether the reaction reduced after stopping (dechallenge) and reappeared on restarting (rechallenge); concomitant drugs and relevant history.
   - **Reporter details** – name, profession, address, contact, signature and date.
3. Assess causality using the WHO-UMC scale and the Naranjo algorithm.
4. Submit the form to the ADR monitoring centre (or online); keep a copy.

## Observations and calculations

**Case 1.** A 34-year-old man (62 kg) started co-trimoxazole for a urinary infection. On day 2 he developed an itchy red rash over the trunk. No other new drugs. Co-trimoxazole was stopped and the rash disappeared in 4 days. Not restarted.

| Naranjo question | Answer | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Previous conclusive reports on this reaction? | Yes | +1 |
| 2. Event appeared after the suspected drug was given? | Yes | +2 |
| 3. Improved when drug stopped or antagonist given? | Yes | +1 |
| 4. Reappeared when drug given again? | Not done | 0 |
| 5. Alternative causes that could cause the reaction? | No | +2 |
| 6. Reappeared with placebo? | Not done | 0 |
| 7. Drug detected in blood in toxic concentration? | Not done | 0 |
| 8. Dose-related severity? | Not known | 0 |
| 9. Similar reaction to the same or similar drug before? | Not known | 0 |
| 10. Confirmed by objective evidence? | Yes (seen by doctor) | +1 |
| **Total** | | **7 – probable** |

WHO-UMC: reasonable time relationship, unlikely to be due to disease or other drugs, improved on withdrawal, no rechallenge → **probable/likely**. Seriousness: non-serious. Outcome: recovered.

**Case 2.** A 58-year-old woman on enalapril 5 mg twice daily for 6 weeks developed a dry, irritating cough. She is a non-smoker with no chest infection; chest X-ray normal. Enalapril was replaced by losartan; the cough settled in 3 weeks. Earlier, she had stopped enalapril for a week and the cough had eased, returning when she restarted it.

| Naranjo question | Answer | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Previous conclusive reports? | Yes | +1 |
| 2. After the drug was given? | Yes | +2 |
| 3. Improved on stopping? | Yes | +1 |
| 4. Reappeared on re-administration? | Yes | +2 |
| 5. Alternative causes? | No | +2 |
| 6. Placebo? | Not done | 0 |
| 7. Toxic blood levels? | Not done | 0 |
| 8. Dose-related? | Not known | 0 |
| 9. Previous similar reaction? | Not known | 0 |
| 10. Objective evidence? | Yes (clinical exam, normal X-ray) | +1 |
| **Total** | | **9 – definite** |

WHO-UMC: positive dechallenge and rechallenge, no other cause → **certain**. Type A ADR (related to the drug's action on bradykinin).

**Naranjo interpretation:** ≥9 definite; 5–8 probable; 1–4 possible; ≤0 doubtful.

## Result

Two suspected ADRs were reported on the IPC form; causality was assessed as probable (co-trimoxazole rash) and definite/certain (enalapril cough).

## Precautions

1. Use only patient initials to protect privacy.
2. Fill batch number and dates accurately.
3. Report even when unsure – causality is assessed later.
4. Report serious reactions as early as possible.
5. Do not deliberately rechallenge a patient just to confirm a reaction.

## Viva questions

1. **Where is the National Coordination Centre of PvPI?** Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad.
2. **What is dechallenge?** Stopping the suspected drug and observing whether the reaction improves.
3. **Name two causality scales.** WHO-UMC and Naranjo.
4. **What makes an ADR "serious"?** Death, life-threatening, hospitalisation, disability, congenital anomaly or other medically important event.
5. **Can a patient report an ADR?** Yes – a consumer form and helpline (1800-180-3024) are available.
