# Unit 3: Drug lists, purchase procedures and inventory control

This unit covers the first half of syllabus chapter 4 (supply chain and inventory control): preparation of special drug lists (high-risk drugs, emergency drugs, Schedule H1 drugs, NDPS drugs, reserved antibiotics), procedures of drug purchase (selection, short-term and long-term purchase, tender and e-tender, quotations), and inventory control techniques – economic order quantity, reorder level, inventory turnover, and ABC, VED and related analyses, with worked examples.

## 3.1 Supply chain in a hospital

The **supply chain** is the whole path a medicine takes from the manufacturer to the patient:

```text
Manufacturer → Distributor / supplier → Hospital central drug store
     → Sub-stores / satellite pharmacies → Wards, ICU, OT, OPD → Patient
         (information, money and returns flow back along the chain)
```

The aim is the **six rights of supply**: the right medicine, in the right quantity, of the right quality, at the right place, at the right time, at the right cost. Too little stock → stock-outs and harm to patients; too much stock → money blocked, expiry and wastage.

## 3.2 Preparation of special drug lists

Besides the formulary, the pharmacy keeps short lists of drugs that need special control. Each list is approved by the P&T Committee and displayed where needed.

### (a) High-risk (high-alert) drugs

**High-alert medications** are drugs that carry a higher risk of causing serious harm if used wrongly (the error may not be more common, but its result is more serious). Examples (based on ISMP lists):

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Concentrated electrolytes | Potassium chloride concentrate, sodium chloride stronger than 0.9%, magnesium sulfate injection |
| Anticoagulants and thrombolytics | Heparin, warfarin, low molecular weight heparins, alteplase |
| Insulin and oral hypoglycaemics | All insulins |
| Opioids | Morphine, fentanyl, pethidine |
| Sedatives and anaesthetics | Midazolam, propofol, ketamine |
| Neuromuscular blockers | Succinylcholine, vecuronium, atracurium |
| Cytotoxic drugs | Methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, vincristine |
| Inotropes / vasopressors | Adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, dobutamine |
| Others | IV oxytocin, digoxin injection, hypertonic dextrose |

Controls: separate storage with red "HIGH ALERT" labels, concentrated KCl removed from wards, double-checking before administration, standard concentrations.

### (b) Emergency drugs

Drugs needed immediately to save life; they must always be available in **crash carts / emergency trolleys** in every ward, ICU, OT and casualty, checked daily and resealed after use. Typical list:

- **Cardiac arrest:** adrenaline, amiodarone, atropine, lignocaine, sodium bicarbonate, calcium gluconate/chloride, adenosine;
- **Anaphylaxis / shock:** adrenaline, hydrocortisone, chlorpheniramine, IV fluids (normal saline, Ringer lactate);
- **Respiratory:** salbutamol nebules, aminophylline, oxygen;
- **Neurological:** diazepam or midazolam, phenytoin;
- **Metabolic:** 25% or 50% dextrose, insulin;
- **Antidotes:** naloxone, atropine (organophosphate), flumazenil;
- **Others:** furosemide, nitroglycerin, dopamine, noradrenaline.

### (c) Schedule H1 drugs

**Schedule H1** of the Drugs Rules lists certain antibiotics (higher cephalosporins, carbapenems and others), anti-TB drugs and some habit-forming drugs. Requirements:

- sold only on the prescription of a registered medical practitioner;
- label carries the symbol **Rx in red** and a boxed warning in red that it is dangerous to take the preparation except in accordance with medical advice and that it must not be sold without prescription;
- a **separate register** with the name and address of the prescriber, name of the patient, name of the drug and quantity supplied, kept for **three years**.

### (d) NDPS drugs

Drugs controlled under the **Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985** and its Rules – for example morphine, pethidine, fentanyl, codeine-containing preparations above set limits, buprenorphine, and benzodiazepines/barbiturates under psychotropic control. The hospital keeps a list of each NDPS drug, its strength and its storage place. They are kept in **locked cupboards (double lock)**, issued only on written orders, and recorded in a stock register with daily balance.

### (e) Reserved (restricted) antibiotics

Antibiotics kept for proven or strongly suspected multi-drug-resistant infections, for example **colistin/polymyxin B, linezolid, tigecycline, ceftazidime–avibactam**, and in many hospitals carbapenems. They are issued only with a **restricted antibiotic request form** signed by the treating consultant and approved by the microbiologist/infectious disease physician, usually after a culture report.

## 3.3 Procedures of drug purchase

### Drug selection

Purchase starts from the **hospital formulary** and NLEM. The P&T Committee decides which drugs are needed; the pharmacist estimates quantities from past consumption, morbidity pattern, bed strength and budget.

**Methods of estimating quantity:**

1. **Consumption method** – based on past use (corrected for stock-outs and wastage).
2. **Morbidity method** – based on number of expected cases × standard treatment.
3. **Service-level projection** – based on similar facilities.

### Purchase systems

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Short-term (local / emergency) purchase** | Small quantity, for urgent needs or items not covered by contract; bought from local suppliers on quotations; costlier per unit |
| **Long-term (annual) purchase** | Whole year's need estimated; bought through tender / rate contract; better price and quality control; supplies in staggered deliveries |
| **Rate contract** | Price fixed for a period (usually 1–2 years); hospital orders as and when needed at that rate |
| **Centralised purchase** | One agency (state medical corporation) buys for all hospitals – bulk discount, quality testing |
| **Direct purchase from manufacturer** | Removes middlemen; possible for large hospitals |

### Tender system

A **tender** is a formal written invitation to suppliers to quote prices for specified items under stated conditions; the contract goes to the qualified bidder with the lowest acceptable price (**L1**).

**Types of tender:**

1. **Open (advertised) tender** – published in newspapers/portals; anyone eligible may bid; most transparent.
2. **Limited (restricted) tender** – sent only to registered or approved suppliers.
3. **Single tender** – only one source exists (proprietary or patented drug).

**Steps in the tender process:**

```text
Prepare list of drugs with specifications (generic name, strength, IP/BP
standard, pack size, shelf life on delivery) and estimated quantity
        ↓
Tender notice issued (newspaper / e-procurement portal), with last date
        ↓
Bidders submit: earnest money deposit (EMD) + technical bid + price bid
(two-bid system: sealed separately)
        ↓
Technical bid opened first – check licences, GMP certificate, turnover,
market standing, non-conviction, sample/quality reports
        ↓
Price bids of technically qualified firms opened → comparative statement
        ↓
Purchase committee approves L1 → supply / rate contract signed,
performance security taken, EMD of others returned
        ↓
Purchase orders placed → receipt → quality check → payment
```

**E-tender (e-procurement):** the same process done online on a government portal – for example the **Central Public Procurement Portal (CPPP)**, state e-procurement portals, or the **Government e-Marketplace (GeM)**. Advantages: transparency, wider participation, less paperwork, no physical tampering, faster processing, digital record. Disadvantages: needs internet, digital signatures and trained staff.

### Quotation method

Used for small or urgent purchases. The pharmacy invites written **quotations** from at least three suppliers, prepares a **comparative statement**, and orders from the supplier with the lowest acceptable rate. It is quicker than a tender but less competitive.

### Purchase order and receipt

A **purchase order (PO)** states the item, specification, quantity, rate, delivery date, place and terms. On receipt, the store checks the goods against the PO and invoice: name, strength, quantity, batch, manufacturing and expiry dates, physical condition, temperature (cold chain), and the manufacturer's test report. Goods are entered in the **goods received note (GRN)** and stock register.

## 3.4 Inventory control

**Inventory** = the stock of medicines and supplies held by the hospital. **Inventory control** = the scientific way of keeping just enough stock: never running out, but not tying up money in excess stock.

**Costs involved:**

- **Ordering (procurement) cost** – cost of placing and receiving one order (paperwork, staff time, transport, inspection).
- **Carrying (holding) cost** – cost of keeping stock: interest on money blocked, storage space, insurance, deterioration, expiry and pilferage; often expressed as a % of the item's value per year.
- **Stock-out cost** – cost of not having the item (emergency purchase, patient harm).

### 3.4.1 Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

**EOQ** is the order size that gives the **lowest total of ordering cost + carrying cost** in a year.

```text
        EOQ = √( 2 × D × S / H )

   D = annual demand (units per year)
   S = ordering cost per order (Rs)
   H = carrying cost per unit per year (Rs)  [= unit price × carrying % if given as %]
```

At EOQ, the yearly ordering cost equals the yearly carrying cost.

```text
 Cost
  |\                       ______ total cost
  | \           _____-----
  |  \ ____----   ↑ minimum at EOQ      ___ carrying cost
  |   \__                       ___---
  |      ‾‾--__          ___---
  |            ‾‾--__---‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾ ordering cost
  |__________________|_________________________ Order quantity
                    EOQ
```

**Worked example 1.** A hospital uses 3,600 vials of ceftriaxone 1 g a year. The cost of placing one order is Rs 200, and the carrying cost is Rs 4 per vial per year. Find EOQ, number of orders per year and the total cost.

```text
EOQ = √(2 × 3600 × 200 / 4) = √(1,440,000 / 4) = √360,000 = 600 vials

Orders per year = D / EOQ = 3600 / 600 = 6 orders (one every 2 months)

Ordering cost per year  = 6 × 200          = Rs 1,200
Average stock           = EOQ / 2 = 300 vials
Carrying cost per year  = 300 × 4          = Rs 1,200
Total of the two costs  =                    Rs 2,400 (minimum)
```

Check: if the hospital ordered 1,200 vials each time → 3 orders = Rs 600 ordering, average stock 600 × 4 = Rs 2,400 carrying, total Rs 3,000 – higher than at EOQ.

**Worked example 2 (carrying cost as %).** Annual demand of an injection = 2,000 ampoules; price Rs 50 per ampoule; ordering cost Rs 125 per order; carrying cost 20% of value per year.

```text
H = 20% of Rs 50 = Rs 10 per ampoule per year
EOQ = √(2 × 2000 × 125 / 10) = √50,000 ≈ 224 ampoules
```

Limitations of EOQ: assumes steady demand, fixed price (no discounts), instant delivery; hospital demand is often seasonal and unpredictable, so EOQ is used as a guide.

### 3.4.2 Reorder level and related stock levels

- **Lead time** = time between placing an order and receiving the goods.
- **Safety (buffer) stock** = extra stock kept to cover unexpected high use or delayed delivery.
- **Reorder level (ROL)** = the stock level at which a fresh order must be placed.
- **Reorder quantity (ROQ)** = the quantity ordered each time (often the EOQ).

```text
Safety stock  = (Maximum daily usage − Normal daily usage) × Lead time
Reorder level = (Normal daily usage × Lead time) + Safety stock
              (= Maximum daily usage × Maximum lead time, in the simple method)
Minimum stock = Reorder level − (Normal usage × Normal lead time)
Maximum stock = Reorder level + Reorder quantity − (Minimum usage × Minimum lead time)
```

**Worked example 3.** Normal use of an antibiotic injection is 50 vials a day, maximum use 70 vials a day, minimum use 30 vials a day, lead time 10 days (taken as constant), EOQ 600 vials.

```text
Safety stock  = (70 − 50) × 10        = 200 vials
Reorder level = (50 × 10) + 200       = 700 vials   → order when stock falls to 700
Minimum stock = 700 − (50 × 10)       = 200 vials   (= safety stock)
Maximum stock = 700 + 600 − (30 × 10) = 1,000 vials
```

```text
 Stock
 1000 ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─  maximum level
   |\              /\
   | \            /  \           order quantity arrives (vertical rise)
 700 ─\─ ─ ─ ─ ─ /─ ─ \─ ─ ─ ─ ─  reorder level (order placed here)
   |   \        /      \
 200 ─ ─\─ ─ ─/─ ─ ─ ─ ─\─ ─ ─ ─  safety stock (minimum level)
   |     \___/            \__
   |_______________________________ Time
         |←lead time→|
```

### 3.4.3 Inventory turnover

**Inventory turnover ratio** shows how many times the average stock is used up (and replaced) in a year.

```text
Inventory turnover = Cost of drugs consumed (issued) in the year ÷ Average inventory value
Average inventory  = (Opening stock + Closing stock) ÷ 2
Days of stock held = 365 ÷ Inventory turnover
```

**Worked example 4.** Drugs worth Rs 60 lakh were issued in a year. Opening stock was Rs 8 lakh and closing stock Rs 12 lakh.

```text
Average inventory = (8 + 12) / 2 = Rs 10 lakh
Turnover = 60 / 10 = 6 times a year
Days of stock = 365 / 6 ≈ 61 days (about two months' stock held on average)
```

A **high** turnover means stock moves quickly and little money is blocked (but too high may mean frequent stock-outs). A **low** turnover means overstocking, slow-moving items and risk of expiry.

### 3.4.4 ABC analysis (Always Better Control)

ABC analysis groups items by **annual consumption value** (annual quantity × unit cost), based on the Pareto principle that a few items take most of the money.

| Class | % of items (approx.) | % of annual value (approx.) | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | 10–20% | about 70% | Strict: frequent small orders, close monitoring, senior officer controls |
| **B** | 20–30% | about 20% | Moderate control |
| **C** | 60–70% | about 10% | Simple control, bulk orders, larger safety stock |

**Steps:** list items with annual quantity and unit cost → calculate annual value → arrange in descending order → work out cumulative % of value and cumulative % of items → cut-off into A, B and C.

**Worked example 5.** A hospital's 14 items with annual consumption value (Rs thousand):

| Rank | Item | Annual value | Cumulative value | Cumulative % value | Class |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Meropenem injection | 900 | 900 | 30.0 | A |
| 2 | Human albumin 20% | 700 | 1,600 | 53.3 | A |
| 3 | Human insulin | 400 | 2,000 | 66.7 | A |
| 4 | Ceftriaxone injection | 250 | 2,250 | 75.0 | B |
| 5 | Normal saline 500 mL | 200 | 2,450 | 81.7 | B |
| 6 | Paracetamol tablets | 120 | 2,570 | 85.7 | B |
| 7 | Pantoprazole injection | 110 | 2,680 | 89.3 | B |
| 8 | Amoxicillin capsules | 90 | 2,770 | 92.3 | C |
| 9 | Metformin tablets | 70 | 2,840 | 94.7 | C |
| 10 | Absorbent cotton and gauze | 60 | 2,900 | 96.7 | C |
| 11 | ORS sachets | 40 | 2,940 | 98.0 | C |
| 12 | Povidone-iodine solution | 30 | 2,970 | 99.0 | C |
| 13 | Vitamin B-complex tablets | 20 | 2,990 | 99.7 | C |
| 14 | Folic acid tablets | 10 | 3,000 | 100.0 | C |

Result: **A** = 3 items (21% of items) take 66.7% of money; **B** = 4 items (29%) take 22.7%; **C** = 7 items (50%) take only 10.7%. Most control effort should go to the three A items.

### 3.4.5 VED analysis

VED groups items by how **critical** they are to patient care, not by cost:

- **V – Vital:** life-saving; a stock-out cannot be tolerated even for a short time (adrenaline, insulin, anti-snake venom, oxygen, IV fluids).
- **E – Essential:** important; a short stock-out is tolerable but undesirable (most antibiotics, antihypertensives).
- **D – Desirable:** a stock-out causes little harm (vitamins, tonics, cough syrups).

### 3.4.6 ABC–VED matrix

Combining both gives nine groups placed in three categories:

| | V | E | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | AV – I | AE – I | AD – I |
| **B** | BV – I | BE – II | BD – II |
| **C** | CV – I | CE – II | CD – III |

- **Category I** (AV, AE, AD, BV, CV) – top management attention; vital items never out of stock, costly items tightly controlled.
- **Category II** (BE, CE, BD) – middle-level control.
- **Category III** (CD) – lowest control.

In the example above, meropenem would be A and (for ICU) V → **AV**, category I; vitamin B-complex is C and D → **CD**, category III.

### 3.4.7 Other analyses (brief)

| Analysis | Basis | Groups |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **FSN** | Rate of movement (issues) | Fast-moving, Slow-moving, Non-moving – find dead stock |
| **SDE** | Ease of procurement | Scarce, Difficult, Easy to obtain |
| **HML** | Unit price | High, Medium, Low cost per unit |

## Remember

- Special lists: high-alert, emergency (crash cart), Schedule H1 (register kept 3 years), NDPS (double lock, register), reserved antibiotics (restricted form).
- Tender types: open, limited, single; two-bid system; L1 wins; e-tender on CPPP/GeM; quotations for small urgent buys (at least three).
- EOQ = √(2DS/H); at EOQ ordering cost = carrying cost.
- ROL = normal use × lead time + safety stock; safety stock = (max − normal use) × lead time.
- Turnover = consumption ÷ average inventory; days of stock = 365 ÷ turnover.
- ABC by value (A ≈ 10–20% items, 70% value); VED by criticality; matrix categories I, II, III; FSN by movement.
