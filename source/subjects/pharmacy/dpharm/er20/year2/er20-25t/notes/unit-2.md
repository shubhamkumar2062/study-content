# Unit 2: Committees in the hospital and the hospital formulary

This unit covers syllabus chapter 2: the Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee (objectives, composition and functions), the hospital formulary (definition, procedure for development and use), and the Infection Control Committee with the role of the pharmacist in preventing antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

## 2.1 Why hospitals have committees

A hospital has many departments, and decisions about medicines affect all of them. **Committees** bring together people from different professions to make policies that everyone follows. Common hospital committees include:

| Committee | Main work |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacy and Therapeutics (P&T) Committee | Drug policy, formulary, rational drug use |
| Infection Control Committee | Prevention and control of hospital-acquired infections, antibiotic policy |
| Antimicrobial Stewardship Committee (often a sub-committee) | Monitoring and improving antibiotic use |
| Medical Records Committee | Standards for case records |
| Ethics Committee | Approval and monitoring of research on humans |
| Quality and Patient Safety Committee | Accreditation, error and incident review |
| Blood Transfusion Committee | Safe use of blood and blood products |
| Hospital Waste Management Committee | Bio-medical waste management |

The pharmacist is a member of several of these, and is usually the **secretary** of the P&T Committee.

## 2.2 Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee (PTC)

### Definition

The **Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee** is an advisory and policy-making body of the hospital, made up of physicians, pharmacists, nurses and administrators, that acts as the link between the medical staff and the pharmacy and decides all matters concerning the selection and use of drugs in the hospital.

### Objectives

1. **Advisory:** to advise the medical staff and administration on all matters relating to drugs.
2. **Educational:** to plan programmes that keep doctors, nurses and pharmacists up to date on drugs and their safe use.
3. **Drug policy:** to develop and maintain the hospital formulary and policies for drug use, purchase and distribution.
4. **Rational use and safety:** to promote cost-effective, safe and rational use of medicines and to reduce ADRs and medication errors.

### Composition

| Member | Role in the committee |
| --- | --- |
| Chairperson – a senior physician (or the Medical Superintendent) | Leads meetings, gives clinical authority |
| Members – heads of major clinical departments (medicine, surgery, paediatrics, obstetrics, anaesthesia, etc.) | Represent prescribers' needs |
| **Secretary – Chief Pharmacist** | Prepares agenda, collects drug data, keeps minutes, prepares formulary |
| Nursing superintendent | Represents administration of drugs on wards |
| Hospital administrator / finance officer | Budget and purchase aspects |
| Microbiologist, clinical pharmacologist, clinical pharmacist (as needed) | Antibiotic and safety advice |

Typically there are about 8–12 members. The committee usually meets **at least once every two or three months**, and more often when the formulary is being prepared. Decisions are recorded in **minutes** and circulated.

### Functions

1. Prepare, review and revise the **hospital formulary** – add new drugs, delete obsolete or unsafe drugs.
2. Evaluate **new drug requests** from doctors on the basis of efficacy, safety, cost and need.
3. Frame **policies** on:
   - generic prescribing and therapeutic substitution;
   - use of non-formulary drugs;
   - drug samples and medical representatives;
   - investigational drugs and clinical trials;
   - automatic stop orders (for example antibiotics, opioids, anticoagulants stopped after a set period unless re-ordered);
   - self-medication and patients' own drugs.
4. **Monitor and review ADRs and medication errors**; recommend preventive steps.
5. Carry out **drug use evaluation (DUE)** – check whether high-cost or high-risk drugs are used appropriately.
6. Develop **standard treatment guidelines** and antibiotic policy (jointly with the Infection Control Committee).
7. Approve **emergency drug lists** for crash carts and wards.
8. Plan **education** – drug bulletins, continuing medical education, newsletters.
9. Advise on **purchase** of drugs and their quality (with the purchase committee).

## 2.3 Hospital formulary

### Definition

A **hospital formulary** is a continually revised list of drugs approved for use in the hospital by the P&T Committee, together with important information about each drug, reflecting the current clinical judgement of the medical staff.

It is both a **list** (what may be prescribed) and a **reference book** (how to use it). The policy of using it is called the **formulary system**.

### Objectives of a formulary

- Promote rational, safe, effective and economical drug therapy.
- Avoid duplication of drugs that do the same job → smaller inventory, lower cost.
- Give prescribers quick, reliable information.
- Allow bulk purchase at better prices.

### Contents (organisation) of a hospital formulary

1. **Title page and introduction** – hospital name, edition, members of the P&T Committee.
2. **Information on hospital policies and procedures** – formulary policy, how to request a non-formulary drug, prescription writing rules, pharmacy working hours, automatic stop orders, narcotic rules, ADR reporting procedure.
3. **Drug products listing** – the main part, arranged by therapeutic category (with an alphabetical index). For each drug:
   - generic name (and common brand names);
   - dosage forms and strengths stocked;
   - usual adult and paediatric dose;
   - indications, contraindications, important adverse effects and interactions;
   - special notes (storage, high-alert status, restricted use).
4. **Special information** – tables of drug interactions, IV compatibility, doses in renal failure, poisoning antidotes, normal laboratory values, conversion tables, abbreviations, nutritional products.
5. **Indexes** – generic, brand and therapeutic index.

### Procedure for development of a hospital formulary

```text
P&T Committee decides to prepare formulary
            ↓
Pharmacist lists all drugs now stocked / used (usage and cost data)
            ↓
Drugs grouped by therapeutic category
            ↓
Clinical departments review each group; suggest additions/deletions
            ↓
Committee selects drugs (efficacy, safety, cost, need, availability,
national essential medicines list) – avoid duplicates
            ↓
Pharmacist prepares monographs and policy sections
            ↓
Draft circulated → comments → final approval by committee
            ↓
Printing / electronic copy → distribution to all wards and doctors
            ↓
Regular revision (new drug requests, deletions, ADR data) – usually yearly
```

**Criteria used to select a drug for the formulary:**

- proven efficacy and safety (good-quality evidence);
- real therapeutic need, not a copy of a drug already listed;
- cost of the whole treatment, not only the unit price;
- availability of quality-assured products;
- preference for drugs in the **National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)**;
- convenience of dosage form for hospital use.

### Profile of a drug for inclusion (new drug request)

When a doctor requests a new drug, the pharmacist prepares a **drug monograph / profile** for the committee: generic and brand names, pharmacology, approved indications, comparison with formulary drugs, dose, adverse effects, interactions, cost comparison, and a recommendation (add, add with restriction, or reject).

### Use of a hospital formulary

- Prescribers choose drugs from it; non-formulary drugs need a special request form with justification.
- Pharmacy stocks and purchases only formulary drugs (plus approved non-formulary requests).
- Nurses and pharmacists use it as a reference for dose, storage and administration.
- It supports **generic substitution** – the pharmacist may supply any approved brand of the same generic drug.
- It is used in training of students and new staff.

**Advantages:** rational and economical therapy, smaller inventory, fewer errors, better purchase terms, quick information.

**Limitations:** may restrict prescriber freedom; needs regular updating; may delay access to new drugs.

## 2.4 Infection Control Committee (ICC)

### Definition and purpose

The **Infection Control Committee** (also called Hospital Infection Control Committee, HICC) is a multidisciplinary committee responsible for planning, carrying out and monitoring the hospital's programme to prevent and control **hospital-acquired (nosocomial) infections**.

**Hospital-acquired infection** = an infection that was not present or incubating when the patient was admitted, and appears after admission (commonly taken as 48 hours or more after admission), or an infection acquired by staff at work. Common ones: catheter-associated urinary tract infection, ventilator-associated pneumonia, central-line bloodstream infection, surgical site infection.

### Composition

- Chairperson – Medical Superintendent or a senior clinician
- Member secretary – usually the **microbiologist** (or infection control officer)
- Infection control nurse(s)
- Heads of medicine, surgery, paediatrics, ICU, operation theatre
- **Chief Pharmacist**
- Nursing superintendent, CSSD in-charge, housekeeping and engineering representatives

### Functions

1. Frame infection control policies: hand hygiene, isolation, sterilisation and disinfection, bio-medical waste, linen and kitchen hygiene.
2. Surveillance of hospital-acquired infections and outbreaks; investigate outbreaks.
3. Prepare the **antibiotic policy** with the P&T Committee; monitor antibiotic use and resistance patterns (antibiogram).
4. Select disinfectants and antiseptics, and set their correct dilutions and uses.
5. Staff training and vaccination of health workers (for example hepatitis B).
6. Needle-stick injury and post-exposure prophylaxis policy.

## 2.5 Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and the pharmacist

**Antimicrobial resistance** is the ability of microorganisms to survive and multiply in the presence of an antimicrobial that would normally kill them or stop their growth. Resistant infections need costlier drugs, stay longer in hospital and cause more deaths.

**Causes of AMR:** unnecessary antibiotics for viral illness, wrong dose or duration, broad-spectrum use when narrow-spectrum would do, over-the-counter sale without prescription, poor infection control, antibiotic use in animals, poor patient adherence.

**India's response (for awareness):**

- **National Action Plan on AMR (2017)** under a "One Health" approach.
- **Schedule H1** of the Drugs Rules for many higher antibiotics – sale only on prescription with a separate register.
- **"Red Line" campaign** – medicines with a red vertical line on the pack should be used only on a doctor's prescription.
- **ICMR** guidelines on antimicrobial stewardship and treatment.
- The **WHO AWaRe classification** groups antibiotics as **Access** (first choice, for example amoxicillin), **Watch** (higher resistance risk, for example ceftriaxone, azithromycin, meropenem) and **Reserve** (last resort, for example colistin, linezolid, tigecycline).

### Role of the pharmacist in preventing AMR

1. **Antibiotic policy and formulary** – help write it; classify antibiotics as unrestricted and restricted (reserve antibiotics need approval of a microbiologist or infectious disease specialist).
2. **Prescription review** – check indication, dose, route, duration, renal adjustment and allergy for every antibiotic order.
3. **Antimicrobial stewardship** – take part in the stewardship team: suggest de-escalation after culture reports, IV-to-oral switch, stopping unnecessary combinations, optimum duration.
4. **Automatic stop orders** for antibiotics (review after a fixed number of days).
5. **Surveillance** – track antibiotic consumption (for example defined daily doses per 100 bed-days) and share with the ICC.
6. **Dispensing control** – no antibiotics without prescription; Schedule H1 register.
7. **Patient counselling** – complete the full course, do not share or save antibiotics.
8. **Education** of doctors, nurses and students; awareness weeks.
9. **Infection control** – correct dilution and storage of disinfectants; sterile preparation in the pharmacy.

## Remember

- P&T Committee: physicians + pharmacist (secretary) + nursing + administration; link between medical staff and pharmacy.
- Four P&T objectives: advisory, educational, drug policy, rational use and safety.
- Formulary = continually revised list of approved drugs + information; formulary system = the policy.
- Formulary parts: policies, drug listing (by therapeutic class), special information, indexes.
- Drug selection: efficacy, safety, need, total cost, NLEM, no duplication.
- ICC: microbiologist usually secretary; surveillance, policies, antibiotic policy, disinfectant selection.
- Pharmacist vs AMR: policy, review, stewardship, stop orders, surveillance, H1 control, counselling.
