# Unit 8: Clinical pharmacy – development, clinical terms and daily activities

Syllabus chapter 9 (12 hours) is large, so it is split into two units in this guide. This unit covers the first part: definition, scope and development of clinical pharmacy in India and other countries; technical definitions and common clinical terms (paediatrics, geriatrics, antenatal and postnatal care and others) with simple paediatric dose calculations; and the daily activities of clinical pharmacists – ward round participation, treatment chart review, ADR monitoring, drug and poison information, medication history, patient counselling and inter-professional collaboration. Pharmaceutical care, drug-related problems, MTM and home medication review are in Unit 9.

## 8.1 Definition of clinical pharmacy

**Clinical pharmacy** is the area of pharmacy in which pharmacists provide direct patient care that optimises the use of medicines and promotes health, wellness and disease prevention. In simple words, it is pharmacy practice at the **patient's bedside** (or with the patient) rather than at the product counter.

| Traditional pharmacy | Clinical pharmacy |
| --- | --- |
| Product-oriented (the medicine) | Patient-oriented (the person) |
| Dispensing what is prescribed | Checking that the therapy is right for this patient |
| Works mainly in the pharmacy | Works on wards, clinics, with the team |
| Outcome: correct product supplied | Outcome: best therapeutic result with least harm |

### Goals

1. Maximise the benefit of drug therapy (right drug, dose, duration).
2. Minimise risks – ADRs, interactions, errors.
3. Minimise cost to patient and hospital.
4. Respect the patient's choices and improve quality of life.

## 8.2 Scope of clinical pharmacy

- Ward rounds and treatment chart review
- Medication history and medicine reconciliation (admission, transfer, discharge)
- ADR monitoring and reporting (pharmacovigilance)
- Drug and poison information services
- Patient counselling and adherence support
- Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) and pharmacokinetic dose adjustment (digoxin, phenytoin, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, lithium)
- Antimicrobial stewardship
- Drug utilisation evaluation and audits
- Special clinics – diabetes, hypertension, anticoagulation, asthma, HIV, TB
- Clinical research and education of health professionals
- Medication therapy management and home medication review (Unit 9)

## 8.3 Development of clinical pharmacy

### In other countries

- **USA:** in the 1960s pharmacists in some university hospitals began working on wards; unit-dose systems and drug information centres followed. Pharmacists became part of the clinical team, and the **Pharm.D** became the professional degree for practice. Professional bodies such as ACCP (American College of Clinical Pharmacy) and ASHP promote clinical services.
- **UK:** "ward pharmacy" developed from the late 1960s and 1970s; today clinical pharmacists work on almost every NHS ward and many are independent prescribers.
- **Australia, Canada, Europe:** structured clinical pharmacy, medication reconciliation and home medication reviews; pharmacists in primary care.
- **WHO and FIP** promote the pharmacist's role in rational drug use and patient care through Good Pharmacy Practice.

### In India

- Until the 1990s hospital pharmacy in India was almost only dispensing and store-keeping.
- In the **1990s**, a few teaching hospitals began clinical pharmacy services with pharmacy colleges (ward rounds, drug information centres, ADR monitoring).
- **2008** – the Pharmacy Council of India introduced the six-year **Pharm.D** programme (and Pharm.D Post-Baccalaureate), with clinical clerkship and internship in hospitals.
- **2010** – the **Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)** started; many ADR monitoring centres are run with clinical pharmacists.
- **2015** – PCI's **Pharmacy Practice Regulations** described the professional role of pharmacists.
- **ER-2020** added hospital and clinical pharmacy to the D.Pharm course.
- NABH accreditation and corporate hospitals now employ clinical pharmacists; posts in government hospitals are still few.

**Barriers in India:** few sanctioned posts, lack of recognition by other professionals, heavy dispensing workload, limited training opportunities, and lack of legal recognition of clinical roles.

## 8.4 Common clinical terms and their significance

### Age groups

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters for drugs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paediatrics** | Medical care of children from birth to adolescence | Doses by weight or body surface area; immature liver and kidney function in newborns; special dosage forms (drops, syrups) |
| Preterm newborn | Born before 37 weeks of pregnancy | Very immature organs; extremely careful dosing |
| Term newborn (neonate) | Birth to 27 days | Low drug metabolism (e.g. chloramphenicol "grey baby" risk) |
| Infant and toddler | 28 days to 23 months | Rapid growth; doses change often |
| Child | 2 to 11 years | Doses by weight; taste and formulation matter |
| Adolescent | 12 to about 16–18 years | Adult-like doses for many drugs; adherence issues |
| **Geriatrics** | Care of elderly people (usually 65 years and above) | Reduced kidney and liver function, more sensitivity (sedatives, anticoagulants), polypharmacy, falls, poor memory → "start low, go slow" |

### Pregnancy and childbirth

| Term | Meaning | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Antenatal (ante-natal) care (ANC)** | Care of the mother and foetus during pregnancy | Regular check-ups (India: at least four ANC visits), iron–folic acid and calcium supplements, tetanus–diphtheria vaccination, screening (BP, haemoglobin, glucose, urine), avoiding teratogenic drugs (for example isotretinoin, warfarin, valproate, ACE inhibitors) |
| Trimester | Each three-month period of pregnancy | The first trimester is the main period of organ formation → highest risk of birth defects |
| **Postnatal (post-natal) care (PNC)** | Care of mother and newborn after delivery, mainly the first six weeks | Watch for bleeding, infection, anaemia, depression; breastfeeding support; drugs safe in lactation; newborn vaccines |
| Lactation | Breastfeeding period | Many drugs pass into milk; choose drugs safe for the infant |

### Other common terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Diagnosis | Identification of a disease from history, examination and tests |
| Prognosis | Likely course and outcome of a disease |
| Aetiology | Cause of a disease |
| Sign / symptom | What the doctor finds (fever, rash) / what the patient feels (pain, nausea) |
| Acute / chronic | Sudden and short / long-lasting |
| Morbidity / mortality | Illness rate / death rate |
| Comorbidity | Another disease present at the same time (diabetes with hypertension) |
| Iatrogenic | Caused by medical treatment (drug-induced) |
| Nosocomial | Hospital-acquired |
| Idiopathic | Of unknown cause |
| Prophylaxis | Prevention (antibiotic before surgery) |
| Palliative care | Care to relieve symptoms and improve quality of life when cure is not possible |
| Indication / contraindication | Reason to use a drug / reason not to use it |
| Adherence (compliance) | How well the patient takes medicines as agreed |
| Polypharmacy | Use of many medicines at once (often taken as 5 or more) |
| Therapeutic index | Ratio of toxic dose to effective dose; narrow index → monitoring needed |
| Off-label use | Use outside the approved indication, age or dose |
| In-patient / out-patient | Admitted to a ward / treated without admission |
| Vital signs | Temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure (and oxygen saturation, pain) |
| BMI | Weight (kg) ÷ height (m)²; for Indian adults a value of 23 or more is usually taken as overweight |

**Common prescription abbreviations:** OD (once daily), BD (twice daily), TDS (three times), QID (four times), HS (at bedtime), AC (before food), PC (after food), PRN/SOS (when needed), STAT (at once), NPO / NBM (nothing by mouth), PO (by mouth), IV, IM, SC.

### Paediatric dose calculations (worked example)

When only an adult dose is known, older rules give an estimate; modern practice prefers **mg/kg** or **body surface area (BSA)** doses from a paediatric formulary.

```text
Young's rule (1–12 years):   Child dose = [Age / (Age + 12)] × Adult dose
Clark's rule (by weight):    Child dose = [Weight in lb / 150] × Adult dose
BSA method:                  Child dose = [BSA (m²) / 1.73] × Adult dose
```

**Worked example.** A 6-year-old child weighs 20 kg (about 44 lb) and has a BSA of about 0.8 m². The adult dose of a drug is 500 mg.

```text
Young's rule  = 6 / (6 + 12) × 500 = 0.333 × 500 ≈ 167 mg
Clark's rule  = 44 / 150 × 500     = 0.293 × 500 ≈ 147 mg
BSA method    = 0.8 / 1.73 × 500   = 0.462 × 500 ≈ 231 mg
```

The rules give different answers – which is why weight-based doses are preferred. For example, paracetamol is commonly dosed at about **15 mg/kg per dose**: 15 × 20 = **300 mg per dose** for this child, repeated at 4–6 hour intervals within the daily maximum.

## 8.5 Daily activities of clinical pharmacists

### 8.5.1 Ward round participation

- **Definition:** visiting patients on the ward along with doctors, nurses and other team members to review each patient's condition and treatment.
- **Goal:** to make the pharmacist part of decisions about drug therapy at the time they are made, and to catch drug-related problems early.
- **Procedure:**
  1. *Before the round* – read the case notes, treatment chart and lab results; prepare a list of points.
  2. *During the round* – listen to the presentation; give brief, evidence-based suggestions (dose, interaction, cheaper alternative, renal adjustment); answer questions.
  3. *After the round* – follow up recommendations, document interventions (patient, problem, suggestion, accepted or not), update pharmacy profile, counsel patients.

### 8.5.2 Treatment chart review

- **Definition:** systematic checking of each patient's medication chart (case sheet/drug chart) for appropriateness, safety and accuracy.
- **Goal:** to find and resolve drug-related problems and medication errors.
- **Procedure – check:**
  - patient details, weight, allergies, diagnosis;
  - each drug: indication, dose, frequency, route, duration, dosage form;
  - dose suited to kidney and liver function, age;
  - interactions, duplications, contraindications;
  - correct administration records (missed doses);
  - need for monitoring (levels, lab tests);
  - legibility, abbreviations, signatures.
  Problems are discussed with the prescriber, and the intervention is documented.

### 8.5.3 Adverse drug reaction monitoring

- **Definition:** systematic detection, assessment, documentation and reporting of adverse drug reactions in patients.
- **Goal:** to protect individual patients and contribute data for safer drug use (pharmacovigilance).
- **Procedure:** detect (from rounds, nurses, lab results, "trigger" drugs such as antidotes) → collect details → assess causality (WHO-UMC or Naranjo scale), severity and preventability → inform the doctor → manage → document in the patient record → report on the national ADR form → feedback and education. (Details in Unit 12.)

### 8.5.4 Drug information and poison information

- **Definition:** providing accurate, unbiased, evaluated information about drugs (or poisons) in response to queries, and proactively through bulletins.
- **Goal:** rational, safe use of drugs and quick help in poisoning.
- **Procedure:** receive query → classify and find background → search primary/secondary/tertiary sources → evaluate → reply (verbal and written) → document → follow up. (Details in Unit 11 and practical guide.)

### 8.5.5 Medication history

- **Definition:** a detailed, accurate record of all medicines the patient is taking or has taken recently, including allergies and past ADRs.
- **Goal:** to avoid omissions, duplications and interactions at admission, and to support **medicine reconciliation**.
- **Procedure (medication history interview):**
  1. Introduce yourself and explain purpose; get consent.
  2. Use more than one source – patient, family, old prescriptions, medicine packs brought in, previous records.
  3. Ask about: prescription drugs (name, strength, dose, how taken, since when), OTC drugs, herbal/Ayurvedic/homeopathic products, supplements, vaccines, allergies and ADRs (what happened), adherence, alcohol, tobacco, recreational drugs, recent stopping of drugs.
  4. Compare with the admission orders; report differences to the doctor.
  5. Document on a medication history form.

### 8.5.6 Patient counselling

- **Definition:** giving the patient (or caregiver) information and advice about their disease and medicines in a way they can understand, to improve adherence and outcomes.
- **Goal:** right use of medicines, better adherence, fewer ADRs, better quality of life.
- **Procedure:**
  1. *Preparation* – read the prescription and history; choose a quiet place.
  2. *Opening* – greet, introduce, confirm identity, explain purpose, check what the patient already knows.
  3. *Content* – name and purpose of each medicine, dose and timing, how to use (inhaler, insulin pen, eye drops), duration, common side effects and what to do, precautions (alcohol, driving), storage, missed dose, follow-up and lifestyle advice.
  4. *Check understanding* – ask the patient to repeat (teach-back).
  5. *Closing* – give written information or pictograms; invite questions; document.
  Barriers to handle: language, low literacy, hearing or vision problems, anxiety, lack of time.

### 8.5.7 Inter-professional collaboration

- **Definition:** health professionals from different disciplines working together with patients and families to provide the best care.
- **Goal:** safe, coordinated, patient-centred care with shared decisions.
- **Procedure / practice:** take part in rounds and team meetings; communicate recommendations clearly (verbal and written, using SBAR – situation, background, assessment, recommendation); respect each role; share information (lab results, ADRs); joint protocols (antibiotic policy, anticoagulation); joint training sessions.

## Remember

- Clinical pharmacy = patient-oriented practice that optimises medicines and promotes health.
- India: services from the 1990s, Pharm.D 2008, PvPI 2010, Pharmacy Practice Regulations 2015.
- Age groups (ICH): term newborn 0–27 days, infant 28 days–23 months, child 2–11 years, adolescent 12–16/18 years; geriatric 65 years and above.
- ANC = care in pregnancy (at least four visits in India); PNC = first six weeks after delivery.
- Young's = age/(age+12); Clark's = lb/150; BSA/1.73; mg/kg preferred.
- Seven daily activities: ward round, chart review, ADR monitoring, DI/PI, medication history, counselling, inter-professional collaboration – know definition, goal and steps of each.
