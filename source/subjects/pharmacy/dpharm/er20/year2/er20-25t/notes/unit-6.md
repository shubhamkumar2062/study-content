# Unit 6: Compounding in hospitals, IV admixtures and total parenteral nutrition

This unit covers syllabus chapter 6: compounding in hospitals, bulk compounding, IV admixture services and incompatibilities, and total parenteral nutrition (TPN). Worked examples cover dilution, infusion rate, weight-based infusion dose and TPN calories.

## 6.1 Compounding in hospitals

**Compounding** is the preparation, mixing, packing and labelling of a medicine by a pharmacist from its ingredients, either for an individual patient (extemporaneous compounding) or in larger batches for the hospital (bulk compounding).

**Why do hospitals still compound when ready-made products exist?**

1. Products not available commercially (special strengths for children, preservative-free eye drops, oral liquids made from tablets).
2. Cheaper in bulk (antiseptic solutions, disinfectant dilutions, mouthwashes, ointments).
3. Patient-specific sterile preparations – IV admixtures, TPN, cytotoxic infusions.
4. Quick supply in emergencies.

**Requirements:**

- a separate, clean compounding area; a separate aseptic (clean room) area for sterile products;
- equipment – balances, measures, mixers, ointment slab and mill, water still or purified water plant, filling equipment, autoclave, laminar air flow (LAF) workbench;
- written **master formula** and **SOPs**; **batch manufacturing record** for each batch;
- quality checks (appearance, volume/weight, pH, clarity, sterility and pyrogen tests for sterile products, as applicable);
- labels with name, strength, batch number, date of preparation, expiry (or beyond-use date), storage and "For hospital use only";
- trained pharmacists; compliance with the Drugs and Cosmetics Act and Rules where hospital manufacture is licensed.

## 6.2 Bulk compounding

**Bulk compounding** is the preparation of commonly used products in large batches for stock, to be issued to wards and departments.

| Non-sterile bulk products | Sterile bulk products |
| --- | --- |
| Antiseptic and disinfectant dilutions (chlorhexidine, povidone-iodine, cetrimide–chlorhexidine) | Bladder irrigation solutions |
| Mouthwashes and gargles | Eye drops and eye washes |
| Calamine lotion, liniments | Sterile water/saline for irrigation |
| Ointments and creams (simple ointment, Whitfield's ointment) | Dialysis solutions (in some centres) |
| Oral liquids (syrups, suspensions from tablets) | |
| Hand rubs (alcohol-based) | |

**Steps of bulk compounding:**

```text
Master formula approved → requisition of raw materials (FEFO, checked)
→ weighing with a second check → mixing / processing as per SOP
→ in-process checks → filling and labelling → QC sampling
→ release by pharmacist → storage and issue; batch record kept
```

**Worked example 1 – dilution for an antiseptic.** Prepare 10 litres of 0.05% w/v chlorhexidine gluconate solution for wound cleaning from a 5% w/v concentrate.

```text
C1 × V1 = C2 × V2
5% × V1 = 0.05% × 10,000 mL
V1 = (0.05 × 10,000) / 5 = 100 mL of concentrate
Take 100 mL of 5% concentrate and make up to 10 litres with purified water.
```

Advantages of bulk compounding: low cost, steady supply, products made to the hospital's own needs. Disadvantages: needs space, staff and quality control; risk of contamination if poorly done; stability data may be limited, so short expiry dates are given.

## 6.3 IV admixtures and IV admixture service

An **IV admixture** is a sterile solution made by adding one or more drugs to a large-volume parenteral (LVP) fluid such as 5% dextrose or 0.9% sodium chloride, for intravenous infusion.

An **IV admixture service** is a **centralised pharmacy service** in which trained pharmacy staff prepare these admixtures under **aseptic conditions** in the pharmacy instead of nurses mixing them on the wards.

### Why prepare in the pharmacy?

| On the ward (nurse) | In the pharmacy (IV admixture service) |
| --- | --- |
| Busy, open environment → contamination risk | Clean room with laminar air flow workbench |
| Calculation done in a hurry | Calculations and doses double-checked |
| Compatibility not always known | Pharmacist checks compatibility and stability |
| Variable labelling | Standard full labels |

### Facilities and procedure

1. **Clean room** with HEPA-filtered air and a **laminar air flow (LAF) workbench**:
   - **horizontal LAF** for ordinary admixtures (air flows from the back filter towards the operator);
   - **vertical LAF / biological safety cabinet (Class II)** for **cytotoxic drugs**, so that the airflow protects the operator as well as the product.
   The work area is maintained at a very clean grade (ISO class 5, earlier called "Class 100").
2. Staff wear clean gowns, caps, masks and sterile gloves; hands washed; surfaces wiped with 70% alcohol.
3. **Aseptic technique** – work at least about 15 cm (6 inches) inside the hood, never block the airflow between the filter and the critical site, swab vial tops and ports with alcohol, use sterile syringes and needles, use filter needles for ampoules.
4. Check the order: patient, drug, dose, diluent, volume, rate, compatibility.
5. Prepare, then **inspect** the bag against a light and dark background for particles, haze or colour change.
6. **Label**: patient name and ward, drug(s) and dose, base fluid and volume, rate of infusion, date and time prepared, expiry (beyond-use) time, storage, initials of preparer and checker.
7. Store as required (some admixtures under refrigeration) and deliver promptly.

**Worked example 2 – infusion rate (drops per minute).** 1,000 mL of normal saline is to be infused over 8 hours using a giving set that delivers 20 drops/mL.

```text
mL per hour   = 1000 / 8 = 125 mL/h
Drops per min = (Volume in mL × drop factor) / (time in minutes)
              = (1000 × 20) / (8 × 60) = 20,000 / 480 ≈ 42 drops per minute
(For a micro-drip set of 60 drops/mL, drops/min equals mL/h: 125 drops/min.)
```

**Worked example 3 – weight-based infusion.** Dopamine 400 mg is added to 250 mL of 5% dextrose. A 60 kg patient needs 5 µg/kg/min. Find the pump rate in mL/h.

```text
Concentration = 400 mg / 250 mL = 1.6 mg/mL = 1,600 µg/mL
Dose per minute = 5 × 60 = 300 µg/min
Dose per hour = 300 × 60 = 18,000 µg/h
Rate = 18,000 / 1,600 = 11.25 mL/h (set about 11.3 mL/h)
```

## 6.4 Incompatibilities in IV admixtures

An **incompatibility** is an undesirable reaction between two or more drugs, or between a drug and the fluid or container, that changes the product so that it is less effective or unsafe.

### Types

1. **Physical incompatibility** – visible change: precipitation, haze, colour change, gas formation, or loss of drug by **sorption** to the container or tubing.
2. **Chemical incompatibility** – chemical change, often invisible: hydrolysis, oxidation, reduction, or breakdown caused by pH; leads to loss of potency or toxic products.
3. **Therapeutic incompatibility** – two drugs given together that act against each other or cause an unwanted effect in the body (for example a bacteriostatic and a bactericidal antibiotic used together in some infections, or two drugs that together prolong the QT interval).

### Important examples

| Combination / situation | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Calcium salts + phosphate (TPN) | Calcium phosphate precipitate – can block lung capillaries |
| Calcium salts + sodium bicarbonate | Calcium carbonate precipitate |
| Ceftriaxone + calcium-containing solutions | Ceftriaxone–calcium precipitate (dangerous in neonates) |
| Phenytoin in 5% dextrose | Crystallises; give in normal saline and flush line |
| Diazepam diluted with aqueous fluids | Precipitates (drug dissolved in a co-solvent) |
| Amphotericin B (conventional) in normal saline | Precipitates; diluted in 5% dextrose |
| Ampicillin in 5% dextrose | Breaks down faster than in normal saline |
| Catecholamines (adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine) + sodium bicarbonate | Inactivated in alkaline solution |
| Aminoglycoside (gentamicin) + penicillins in the same container | Aminoglycoside inactivated – give separately |
| Insulin, nitroglycerin, diazepam in PVC bags/sets | **Sorption** (loss of drug onto plastic) – use non-PVC sets or account for loss |
| Light-sensitive drugs (sodium nitroprusside, amphotericin B) | Degrade in light – protect bag from light |

### Factors that affect compatibility

pH of the fluid and drugs, concentration, order of mixing, temperature, time since mixing, light, type of container, and presence of other additives.

### How to prevent incompatibilities

1. Check a compatibility chart or reference (Trissel's Handbook on Injectable Drugs and similar references) before mixing.
2. Add only one drug at a time and mix well after each addition; inspect after each.
3. Do **not** add drugs to blood, blood products, mannitol, sodium bicarbonate, lipid emulsions or amino acid solutions unless specifically allowed.
4. Use the recommended diluent; give incompatible drugs through separate lines or flush the line between them.
5. Use admixtures soon after preparation; follow the beyond-use time.
6. Prepare in the pharmacy IV admixture service where possible.

## 6.5 Total parenteral nutrition (TPN)

### Definition

**Parenteral nutrition** is the intravenous supply of nutrients when the gut cannot be used or cannot absorb enough. **Total parenteral nutrition (TPN)** supplies **all** the patient's daily requirement of water, energy, protein, electrolytes, vitamins and trace elements by vein. **Partial (supplemental) parenteral nutrition** supplies only part, along with oral or tube feeding.

### Indications

- Severe malabsorption (short bowel syndrome), intestinal obstruction, prolonged ileus;
- severe acute pancreatitis or high-output fistula when enteral feeding is not possible;
- major surgery or trauma, burns, sepsis with a non-functioning gut;
- premature neonates who cannot feed;
- severe malnutrition before surgery when enteral feeding fails.

Rule: **"If the gut works, use it"** – enteral feeding is preferred whenever possible.

### Components of TPN

| Component | Source | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Base fluid | About 30–35 mL/kg/day in adults (adjusted) |
| Carbohydrate | Dextrose (glucose) 5–70% | Main energy source; about 3.4 kcal/g as dextrose monohydrate |
| Protein | Crystalline amino acids (essential + non-essential) | 4 kcal/g; for tissue repair, not mainly for energy |
| Fat | Lipid emulsion (10%, 20%) from soybean, olive, fish oils | 9 kcal/g; supplies essential fatty acids; concentrated energy |
| Electrolytes | Na, K, Ca, Mg, phosphate, chloride, acetate | Adjusted daily from blood reports |
| Vitamins | Water- and fat-soluble preparations | Added just before use |
| Trace elements | Zinc, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium | Added as a mixture |

### Types and routes

- **Central PN** – through a catheter in a large central vein (subclavian, jugular, PICC); can use concentrated, high-osmolarity solutions.
- **Peripheral PN** – through a peripheral vein; only lower-osmolarity solutions (commonly kept below about 900 mOsm/L) to avoid thrombophlebitis; short-term use.
- **2-in-1** (dextrose + amino acids, lipid given separately) and **3-in-1 / total nutrient admixture** (all three in one bag).

### Preparation (pharmacist's role)

```text
Doctor's order (weight, fluids, kcal, protein, electrolytes)
→ pharmacist checks calculations, osmolarity, Ca–PO4 compatibility
→ compounding in LAF hood under aseptic conditions
   (amino acids and dextrose first, phosphate added early, other
    electrolytes next, calcium near the end and well diluted,
    lipid last, vitamins and trace elements just before use)
→ inspect (no particles; emulsion not "cracked"/separated)
→ label: patient, contents, volume, rate, start time, expiry
→ refrigerate if not used at once; protect from light; use within
   the stated time (usually 24 h after hanging)
```

**Worked example 4 – TPN calories.** One day's bag contains dextrose 200 g (as monohydrate), amino acids 60 g and lipid 50 g.

```text
Dextrose  200 g × 3.4 kcal/g = 680 kcal
Amino acids 60 g × 4 kcal/g  = 240 kcal
Lipid      50 g × 9 kcal/g   = 450 kcal
Total energy                 = 1,370 kcal/day
Non-protein calories (NPC) = 680 + 450 = 1,130 kcal
Nitrogen = protein ÷ 6.25 = 60 ÷ 6.25 = 9.6 g
NPC : N ratio = 1,130 ÷ 9.6 ≈ 118 : 1 (a commonly used range is about 100–150 : 1)
```

### Monitoring

Weight, fluid balance, blood glucose (several times a day at first), electrolytes, urea and creatinine, liver function tests, triglycerides, signs of infection at the catheter site.

### Complications

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Catheter-related | Infection and sepsis, thrombosis, air embolism, pneumothorax during insertion |
| Metabolic | Hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia (if stopped suddenly), electrolyte imbalance, **refeeding syndrome** (low phosphate, potassium and magnesium when a starved patient is fed too fast), fatty liver and cholestasis, high triglycerides |
| Formulation | Precipitation (Ca–PO4), lipid emulsion separation, contamination |

## Remember

- Compounding = pharmacist prepares from ingredients; bulk compounding = batches for stock (antiseptics, mouthwashes, lotions, ointments, irrigations); keep master formula and batch record.
- C1V1 = C2V2 for dilutions; drops/min = volume × drop factor ÷ minutes.
- IV admixture service = centralised aseptic mixing in LAF (horizontal for routine, vertical/Class II cabinet for cytotoxics), with full labels and compatibility checks.
- Incompatibilities: physical (precipitate, colour, gas, sorption), chemical (hydrolysis, oxidation, pH), therapeutic.
- Key examples: Ca + PO4, Ca + bicarbonate, ceftriaxone + Ca, phenytoin in dextrose, amphotericin B in saline, insulin/nitroglycerin sorption to PVC.
- TPN = all nutrients IV; central (concentrated) or peripheral (<about 900 mOsm/L); dextrose 3.4, amino acids 4, lipid 9 kcal/g; watch infection, glucose and refeeding syndrome.
