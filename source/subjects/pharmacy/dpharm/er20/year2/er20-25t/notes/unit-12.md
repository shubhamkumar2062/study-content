# Unit 12: Pharmacovigilance, medication errors and drug interactions

This unit joins syllabus chapter 12 (pharmacovigilance – definition, aim, scope and overview) and chapter 13 (medication errors – definition, types, consequences, strategies to minimise them, LASA drugs and Tall Man lettering as per ISMP; drug interactions – definition, types and clinical significance). A worked causality assessment is included.

## Part A – Pharmacovigilance

## 12.1 Definition, aim and scope

**Pharmacovigilance (PV)** is the science and activities relating to the **detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem** (WHO definition).

An **adverse drug reaction (ADR)** is a response to a drug that is noxious and unintended, and which occurs at doses normally used in humans for prevention, diagnosis or treatment of disease, or for modification of physiological function (WHO).

**Why PV is needed:** clinical trials before marketing involve a limited number of selected patients for a short time; rare ADRs (for example 1 in 10,000), ADRs in children, the elderly and pregnant women, long-term effects and interactions appear only after a drug is used widely.

### Aims

1. Improve patient care and safety in relation to medicines.
2. Detect new ADRs and changes in frequency of known ADRs early ("signals").
3. Assess the benefit–risk balance of medicines and support regulatory action (label changes, restrictions, withdrawal).
4. Promote rational and safe use of medicines.
5. Educate health professionals and the public.

### Scope

ADRs, medication errors, lack of efficacy, counterfeit and substandard medicines, misuse and abuse, poisoning, drug interactions, and – under wider programmes – vaccines (AEFI), medical devices (materiovigilance), herbal products and blood products (haemovigilance).

## 12.2 Overview of pharmacovigilance

### Methods

| Method | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Spontaneous (voluntary) reporting** | Health professionals and patients report suspected ADRs on a form; main method worldwide; cheap, covers all drugs, but under-reporting is common |
| Intensive / active monitoring | Specially trained staff look for ADRs in defined patients or wards |
| Cohort and case–control studies | Compare exposed and unexposed groups |
| Record linkage, registries | Use of health databases |
| Periodic safety update reports (PSURs) | Reports submitted by manufacturers at set intervals |

### Classification of ADRs (Rawlins–Thompson, extended)

| Type | Name | Features | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | **Augmented** | Exaggerated normal action; dose-related, common, predictable | Hypoglycaemia with insulin; bleeding with warfarin |
| B | **Bizarre** | Not dose-related, uncommon, unpredictable, often immune | Anaphylaxis to penicillin; Stevens–Johnson syndrome |
| C | Chronic | Due to long-term use | Osteoporosis with steroids |
| D | Delayed | Appear long after use | Cancer with cytotoxics; teratogenicity |
| E | End of use | Withdrawal effects | Rebound hypertension on stopping clonidine |
| F | Failure | Unexpected failure of therapy | Contraceptive failure with enzyme inducers |

### Seriousness and severity

A **serious** ADR is one that results in death, is life-threatening, requires or prolongs hospitalisation, causes persistent disability, or a congenital anomaly, or needs intervention to prevent permanent damage. **Severity** (mild, moderate, severe) describes intensity (for example by the Hartwig and Siegel scale).

### Causality assessment

| WHO-UMC category | Main idea |
| --- | --- |
| **Certain** | Plausible time relationship; cannot be explained by disease or other drugs; response to withdrawal clinically plausible; definitive on re-challenge (pharmacologically or phenomenologically) |
| **Probable / likely** | Reasonable time relationship; unlikely due to disease/other drugs; improves on withdrawal; re-challenge not needed |
| **Possible** | Reasonable time relationship but could also be explained by disease or other drugs; withdrawal information lacking or unclear |
| **Unlikely** | Time relationship improbable; other causes more likely |
| **Conditional / unclassified** | More data needed |
| **Unassessable / unclassifiable** | Information insufficient or contradictory |

**Naranjo algorithm** – ten questions, each scored; total score: **9 or more = definite, 5–8 = probable, 1–4 = possible, 0 or less = doubtful**.

**Worked example – Naranjo score.** A patient develops an itchy rash 2 days after starting co-trimoxazole. The rash fades after the drug is stopped. No re-challenge; no other new drugs; the reaction is well documented in literature.

```text
Q1 Previous conclusive reports of this reaction?         Yes  → +1
Q2 Event appeared after the suspected drug was given?    Yes  → +2
Q3 Improved when the drug was stopped?                   Yes  → +1
Q4 Reappeared on re-administration?                      Not done → 0
Q5 Alternative causes that could have caused it?         No   → +2
Q6 Reaction reappeared with placebo?                     Not done → 0
Q7 Drug detected in blood in toxic concentration?        Not done → 0
Q8 More severe with higher dose / less with lower dose?  Not known → 0
Q9 Similar reaction to the same/similar drug before?     Not known → 0
Q10 Confirmed by objective evidence?                     Yes (seen by doctor) → +1
Total = 7 → PROBABLE
```

### Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)

- Started by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in **2010**; the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad** is the **National Coordination Centre (NCC)**.
- **ADR Monitoring Centres (AMCs)** in medical colleges and hospitals collect reports and send them to the NCC, which analyses them and forwards data to the **WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre** (Sweden), whose global database is VigiBase.
- Reports are made on the **"Suspected Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Form"** of IPC (for health professionals; a simpler consumer form also exists), through the online system, mobile app, or toll-free helpline (1800-180-3024).
- Recommendations go to the **CDSCO** (regulator) for action such as label changes or bans.
- Anyone – doctor, pharmacist, nurse, patient – can report; **reporting does not need certainty** that the drug caused the reaction.

### Role of the pharmacist in PV

Detect and document ADRs during rounds and dispensing; fill the ADR form and assess causality; report to the AMC; give feedback and education; counsel patients to recognise and report reactions; maintain ADR records for the hospital and P&T Committee.

## Part B – Medication errors

## 12.3 Definition

A **medication error** is any **preventable** event that may cause or lead to inappropriate medication use or patient harm while the medication is in the control of the health-care professional, patient or consumer (definition of the US National Coordinating Council for Medication Error Reporting and Prevention, NCC MERP). It can occur in prescribing, order communication, labelling, packaging, dispensing, distribution, administration, education, monitoring or use.

**ADR versus medication error:** an ADR can occur even when everything is done correctly; a medication error is a preventable mistake. A **near miss** is an error that was caught before reaching the patient.

## 12.4 Types of medication errors

| By stage | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Prescribing errors** | Wrong drug, dose or frequency; illegible handwriting; unsafe abbreviations; ignoring allergy, kidney function or interaction; incomplete order |
| **Transcribing errors** | Mistakes when copying orders to the nursing chart or entering into the computer |
| **Dispensing errors** | Wrong drug (LASA), wrong strength or dosage form, wrong label, wrong quantity, expired drug, wrong patient |
| **Administration errors** | Wrong patient, wrong route, wrong time, omitted dose, wrong rate of infusion, crushing a sustained-release tablet |
| **Monitoring errors** | Not checking INR, glucose, drug levels or side effects |
| **Patient / compliance errors** | Patient takes wrong dose, double doses, wrong technique |

By type: omission, wrong dose (over/under), wrong drug, wrong dosage form, wrong route, wrong time, wrong patient, wrong technique, deteriorated drug, monitoring error.

**NCC MERP index** groups errors by harm, from **category A** (circumstances that could cause error) through **categories B–D** (error occurred, no harm), **E–H** (harm, from temporary harm to intervention needed to sustain life) to **category I** (error contributed to death).

## 12.5 Consequences

- To the **patient** – no effect, temporary harm, prolonged hospital stay, permanent disability, death; loss of trust.
- To the **hospital** – extra treatment cost, legal cases, damage to reputation, loss of accreditation.
- To the **staff** – stress, disciplinary action ("second victim").

## 12.6 Strategies to minimise medication errors

1. **System approach, not blame** – encourage reporting of errors and near misses; analyse causes (root cause analysis); learn and redesign.
2. **Prescribing:** CPOE and e-prescriptions with decision support; generic names; legible capital letters; no dangerous abbreviations ("U" for units → write "units"; "µg" → write "mcg"; **no trailing zero** – write 1 mg, not 1.0 mg; **always a leading zero** – write 0.5 mg, not .5 mg).
3. **Dispensing:** pharmacist checks every order; independent double check; barcode scanning; separate LASA drugs; Tall Man lettering on shelf labels; counsel at handover.
4. **Distribution:** unit-dose system; remove concentrated KCl from wards; standard concentrations for infusions; ready-to-administer products.
5. **Administration:** the **"rights" of medication administration** – right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, plus right documentation, right reason and right response; patient identification with two identifiers; smart infusion pumps; BCMA.
6. **High-alert drugs:** extra labels and double checks.
7. **Education** of staff and patients; patients encouraged to know their medicines.
8. **Monitoring:** lab checks for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.

## 12.7 LASA drugs and Tall Man lettering (ISMP)

**LASA (look-alike, sound-alike) drugs** are drugs whose names look or sound similar, or whose packs look similar, so that one is easily mistaken for the other.

The **Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP)** recommends **Tall Man lettering** – writing the differing part of similar names in UPPER-CASE letters so the difference stands out.

| LASA pair (Tall Man form) | Risk if confused |
| --- | --- |
| hydrALAZINE / hydrOXYzine | Antihypertensive vs antihistamine |
| DOPamine / DOBUTamine | Different inotropes and doses |
| predniSONE / prednisoLONE | Different steroids |
| vinBLAStine / vinCRIStine | Different cytotoxics – vincristine is fatal if given intrathecally |
| glipiZIDE / glyBURIDE | Different sulfonylureas |
| chlorproMAZINE / chlorproPAMIDE | Antipsychotic vs antidiabetic |
| NIFEdipine / niCARdipine | Different calcium channel blockers |
| cycloSERINE / cycloSPORINE | Anti-TB drug vs immunosuppressant |

**Other LASA strategies:** store apart, use different colours or "LASA" stickers on bins, include both brand and generic name and the indication on prescriptions, read back verbal orders, use barcodes, keep a hospital LASA list (including Indian brand-name pairs).

## Part C – Drug interactions

## 12.8 Definition

A **drug interaction** occurs when the effect of one drug is changed (increased or decreased) by the presence of another drug, food, drink, herbal product or disease. The drug whose effect changes is the **object drug**; the one causing the change is the **precipitant drug**.

## 12.9 Types of drug interactions

### 1. Pharmaceutical (physical/chemical) interactions

Occur outside the body, mainly when drugs are mixed (IV incompatibilities – Unit 6).

### 2. Pharmacokinetic interactions – change in what the body does to the drug

| Process | Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Absorption** | Chelation / binding | Tetracyclines or ciprofloxacin + antacids, iron, calcium → reduced absorption (separate by 2–4 h) |
| | Change in gastric pH | PPIs reduce absorption of ketoconazole and itraconazole |
| **Distribution** | Displacement from plasma protein binding | Warfarin displaced by highly protein-bound drugs (for example some NSAIDs) – usually short-lived |
| **Metabolism** | **Enzyme induction** (more metabolism → less effect) | Rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital → failure of oral contraceptives, lower warfarin effect |
| | **Enzyme inhibition** (less metabolism → toxicity) | Erythromycin/clarithromycin, ketoconazole, fluconazole, cimetidine, grapefruit juice → raised levels of simvastatin (muscle damage), warfarin (bleeding), theophylline |
| **Excretion** | Competition for renal tubular secretion | Probenecid raises penicillin levels (used therapeutically) |
| | Reduced renal clearance | Thiazides or NSAIDs raise lithium levels → toxicity |

### 3. Pharmacodynamic interactions – change in what the drug does to the body

| Kind | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Additive / synergistic** (same direction) | Alcohol + benzodiazepines → severe sedation; warfarin + aspirin → bleeding; ACE inhibitor + spironolactone → high potassium; two QT-prolonging drugs → arrhythmia |
| **Antagonistic** (opposite direction) | Beta-agonist salbutamol + non-selective beta-blocker propranolol; NSAIDs reduce effect of antihypertensives |
| Indirect | Diuretic-induced low potassium increases digoxin toxicity |
| Serotonin syndrome | SSRIs + tramadol or MAO inhibitors |
| Hypertensive crisis | MAO inhibitors + tyramine-rich food (cheese reaction) |
| Severe hypotension | Sildenafil + nitrates |

### 4. Other interactions

- **Drug–food:** levothyroxine on an empty stomach; alcohol + metronidazole (disulfiram-like reaction); grapefruit juice.
- **Drug–disease:** NSAIDs in heart failure and peptic ulcer; non-selective beta-blockers in asthma; anticholinergics in glaucoma or enlarged prostate.
- **Drug–laboratory test:** drugs interfering with test results (for example biotin with some immunoassays).

## 12.10 Clinical significance

An interaction matters clinically when it changes the outcome for the patient. Significance depends on:

1. **Severity** – **major** (life-threatening or permanent damage; avoid the combination), **moderate** (may worsen condition; needs monitoring or dose change), **minor** (little effect; usually no change needed).
2. **Documentation** – how well proven (established, probable, suspected, possible, unlikely).
3. **Onset** – rapid (within 24 h) or delayed (days to weeks; enzyme induction takes about 1–2 weeks to develop fully).
4. **Patient factors** – elderly, kidney or liver disease, polypharmacy, narrow-therapeutic-index drugs (warfarin, digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, theophylline, aminoglycosides).

Not every interaction is harmful: some are used deliberately (probenecid + penicillin; levodopa + carbidopa; atropine + pralidoxime in poisoning).

**Managing interactions:** avoid the combination, choose an alternative, adjust dose, separate administration times, monitor (INR, drug levels, potassium), and counsel the patient.

## Remember

- PV (WHO): detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or other drug-related problems.
- ADR types A (augmented, dose-related) and B (bizarre, unpredictable), then C, D, E, F.
- Causality: WHO-UMC (certain, probable, possible, unlikely, conditional, unassessable); Naranjo ≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful.
- PvPI 2010; NCC at IPC Ghaziabad; AMCs; data to WHO-UMC; helpline 1800-180-3024; regulator CDSCO.
- Medication error = preventable event; stages: prescribing, transcribing, dispensing, administration, monitoring; NCC MERP categories A–I.
- Prevention: CPOE, unit dose, BCMA, double checks, rights of administration, no trailing zero, leading zero, no "U".
- LASA + Tall Man lettering (hydrALAZINE/hydrOXYzine, DOPamine/DOBUTamine, vinBLAStine/vinCRIStine).
- Interactions: pharmaceutical, pharmacokinetic (absorption, distribution, metabolism – inducers/inhibitors, excretion), pharmacodynamic (additive, antagonistic); significance by severity, documentation, onset and patient factors.
