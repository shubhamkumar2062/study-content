# Unit 1: Hospital pharmacy – organisation, standards and accreditation

This unit covers syllabus chapter 1: definition and scope of hospital pharmacy, the national and international scenario, organisational structure, professional responsibilities, qualification and job specifications, workload, inter-professional relationships, Good Pharmacy Practice (GPP) in hospitals, hospital pharmacy standards (FIP Basel Statements and ASHP standards), and an introduction to NQAS guidelines and NABH accreditation with the role of the pharmacist.

## 1.1 Hospital and hospital pharmacy

### What is a hospital?

A **hospital** is an institution that provides diagnosis, treatment, care and rehabilitation to sick or injured people, with beds for in-patients, an out-patient department (OPD), emergency services, and trained medical, nursing and paramedical staff. Hospitals also train health workers and carry out research.

**Classification of hospitals**

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership | Government (central, state, municipal), private, trust/charitable, corporate, military/railway/ESI |
| Level of care | Primary (PHC, CHC), secondary (district and sub-district hospitals), tertiary (medical colleges, super-speciality hospitals) |
| Type of service | General hospitals, speciality hospitals (cancer, TB, mental health, eye, maternity, children) |
| Length of stay | Short-stay (acute care) and long-stay (chronic care, rehabilitation) |
| Teaching status | Teaching (attached to medical colleges) and non-teaching |

### Definition of hospital pharmacy

**Hospital pharmacy** is the department or service in a hospital, run by registered pharmacists, that is responsible for the selection, procurement, storage, preparation, distribution and safe use of medicines and medical supplies for in-patients and out-patients, and for giving drug information to staff and patients.

In simple words, it is the practice of pharmacy within a hospital, under the control of a qualified pharmacist, so that every patient gets the right medicine, in the right dose, at the right time, at the lowest cost to the hospital.

### Scope of hospital pharmacy

1. **Procurement and inventory control** – selection, purchase, receipt, storage and stock control of medicines, surgical items and devices.
2. **Dispensing and distribution** – to OPD patients, wards, ICUs, operation theatres and emergency.
3. **Manufacturing and compounding** – bulk non-sterile preparations (antiseptic solutions, mouthwashes), sterile preparations, IV admixtures, total parenteral nutrition, cytotoxic reconstitution.
4. **Clinical services** – ward rounds, treatment chart review, ADR monitoring, therapeutic drug monitoring, patient counselling, medication history and reconciliation.
5. **Drug information** – answering queries of doctors, nurses and patients; publishing newsletters.
6. **Committee work** – Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee (hospital formulary), Infection Control Committee, antimicrobial stewardship.
7. **Special services** – radiopharmacy, central sterile supply department (CSSD) support, poison information.
8. **Education and research** – training of pharmacy and nursing students, drug use evaluation studies, clinical trials (storage and dispensing of trial drugs).
9. **Quality and safety** – medication error prevention, accreditation (NABH, NQAS) activities.

## 1.2 National and international scenario

### In India

- Hospital pharmacy in most government hospitals is still largely **store-keeping and dispensing**; clinical services exist mainly in teaching and corporate hospitals.
- The Pharmacy Act, 1948 requires that medicines be dispensed only by **registered pharmacists**; the **Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015** of the Pharmacy Council of India (PCI) describe the duties and conduct of pharmacists, including in hospitals.
- The **Pharm.D** course (started by PCI in 2008) and the ER-2020 D.Pharm syllabus have added clinical pharmacy, so the role of hospital pharmacists is expanding.
- **NABH** accreditation of private hospitals and **NQAS** certification of public health facilities have made hospitals pay attention to medication management, which needs pharmacists.
- State drug procurement corporations (for example the Tamil Nadu Medical Services Corporation model) and free-drug schemes have increased the need for pharmacists trained in supply chain management.
- Problems: shortage of pharmacists in public hospitals, poor recognition, few posts for clinical pharmacists, and lack of standard staffing norms in many states.

### In other countries

- In the **USA**, hospital (health-system) pharmacy is well developed; the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP) sets practice standards; pharmacists take part in ward rounds, run anticoagulation and pharmacokinetic services, and use unit-dose and automated systems.
- In the **UK**, NHS hospitals have clinical pharmacists on most wards; pharmacists can train as independent prescribers.
- In **Australia** and **Canada**, pharmacists do medication reconciliation at admission and discharge and home medication reviews.
- Internationally, the **International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP)** and the **World Health Organization (WHO)** publish guidance such as the **FIP Basel Statements** and the joint FIP/WHO **Good Pharmacy Practice** guidelines.

## 1.3 Organisational structure

The hospital is headed by a **Medical Superintendent / Director / Chief Executive Officer**, supported by medical, nursing, administrative and support departments. The pharmacy is a **service department** reporting to the medical superintendent (or to a deputy for medical services).

```text
                 Governing body / Board

        __________________|_________________________
       |            |            |                  |
   Medical      Nursing     Administration     Pharmacy department
   services     services    (accounts, HR,     (Chief Pharmacist /
                            purchase)          Director of Pharmacy)
                                                     |
       ____________________________________________________________________
      |            |              |              |             |           |
  Purchase &   Dispensing      In-patient /    Compounding   Clinical &   Drug
  central      (OPD)           ward supply     & IV          DI services  information
  drug store                   (satellite      admixture                  / ADR cell
                               pharmacies)
```

**Staff of a hospital pharmacy (typical):**

| Post | Main duties |
| --- | --- |
| Chief Pharmacist / Director of Pharmacy | Overall management, policy, budget, committees, staff supervision |
| Deputy / Senior Pharmacist | In charge of a section (store, dispensing, compounding) |
| Clinical Pharmacist | Ward rounds, chart review, ADR and DI services |
| Pharmacist | Dispensing, ward supply, record keeping, counselling |
| Store keeper / store assistant | Physical handling and arrangement of stock under pharmacist control |
| Clerical staff, attendants | Records, billing, cleaning, transport |

## 1.4 Professional responsibilities of the hospital pharmacist

1. Prepare and follow the hospital formulary and drug policies.
2. Purchase good-quality medicines at fair prices; maintain correct storage conditions.
3. Dispense prescriptions correctly with clear labels and counselling.
4. Supply medicines to wards by an efficient distribution system (unit dose where possible).
5. Control narcotic, psychotropic and Schedule H1 drugs as per law.
6. Prepare sterile and non-sterile products, IV admixtures and TPN under aseptic conditions.
7. Provide drug information; monitor and report ADRs; prevent medication errors.
8. Take part in P&T Committee, Infection Control Committee and antimicrobial stewardship.
9. Train pharmacy students, nurses and junior staff; carry out drug utilisation studies.
10. Keep records as required by the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, NDPS rules and the hospital.

## 1.5 Qualification, experience, job specification and workload

### Qualification and experience

- **Pharmacist:** D.Pharm or B.Pharm and **registration with the State Pharmacy Council** (compulsory under the Pharmacy Act, 1948).
- **Senior / Chief Pharmacist:** usually B.Pharm or M.Pharm (or Pharm.D) with several years of hospital experience; exact requirements are laid down by each state's recruitment rules or by the hospital.
- **Clinical Pharmacist:** Pharm.D or M.Pharm (Pharmacy Practice) preferred, with clinical training.

### Job specification

A **job specification** is a written statement of the minimum qualities a person needs for a post: education, registration, experience, skills (communication, computer, accuracy), physical fitness and personal qualities (honesty, responsibility). It is different from a **job description**, which lists the duties of the post.

| Job description | Job specification |
| --- | --- |
| Describes the job | Describes the person needed |
| Lists duties, responsibilities, reporting line, working hours | Lists qualification, experience, skills, attitude |
| Used for work allotment and appraisal | Used for recruitment and selection |

### Workload requirements

Staffing of a hospital pharmacy is planned from the **workload**, not guessed. Points considered:

- number of beds and bed occupancy;
- number of OPD prescriptions per day and number of items per prescription;
- type of distribution system (unit dose needs more staff than floor stock);
- 24-hour services (emergency, ICU), number of shifts and leave reserve;
- extra services – compounding, IV admixture, TPN, clinical rounds, drug information;
- national norms such as the **Indian Public Health Standards (IPHS)** for public hospitals, and NABH requirements in accredited hospitals.

A simple workload estimate: if one pharmacist can properly dispense and counsel about a fixed number of prescriptions per hour, then pharmacists needed per shift = (prescriptions per shift ÷ prescriptions one pharmacist can handle per shift), rounded up, plus reserve for leave.

### Inter-professional relationships

The pharmacist works with:

- **Doctors** – advice on drug choice, dose, interactions, formulary alternatives; P&T Committee.
- **Nurses** – supply to wards, administration techniques, storage on wards, IV compatibility, error reporting.
- **Laboratory staff** – drug levels, culture reports (antibiotic choice), lab values for dose adjustment.
- **Administration** – budget, purchase, tenders, staffing.
- **Patients and caregivers** – counselling and follow-up.

Good relationships rest on mutual respect, clear communication, shared goals (patient safety), and documentation of every recommendation.

## 1.6 Good Pharmacy Practice (GPP) in hospital

**GPP** is the practice of pharmacy that meets the needs of people using pharmaceutical services by providing optimal, evidence-based care. The joint FIP/WHO guidelines on GPP describe four main roles of pharmacists, each with minimum national standards:

1. **Prepare, obtain, store, secure, distribute, administer, dispense and dispose of medical products.**
2. **Provide effective medication therapy management** (assessing therapy, monitoring outcomes).
3. **Maintain and improve professional performance** (continuing education).
4. **Contribute to improving the effectiveness of the health-care system and public health** (health promotion, rational drug use).

**GPP applied in a hospital means:**

- the patient's welfare comes first;
- every medicine is supplied with assured quality and correct information;
- storage areas are clean, temperature-controlled and secure;
- written **standard operating procedures (SOPs)** exist for dispensing, ward supply, cold chain, recall and disposal;
- prescriptions are checked for legality, appropriateness, dose and interactions before dispensing;
- errors and ADRs are reported and learned from;
- the pharmacist takes part in rational drug use and continuing education.

## 1.7 Hospital pharmacy standards

### FIP Basel Statements

The **Basel Statements on the Future of Hospital Pharmacy** were agreed at an FIP global conference held in Basel, Switzerland in 2008 and were **revised in 2014**. They are a set of short statements describing what good hospital pharmacy practice should look like. They are grouped as **overarching statements** plus six themes:

| Theme | Example of what it expects |
| --- | --- |
| Procurement | Transparent purchase of quality medicines; pharmacists involved in procurement decisions |
| Influences on prescribing | Formulary system, P&T Committee, pharmacist review of prescriptions |
| Preparation and delivery | Safe compounding, labelling, unit-dose supply, IV admixtures |
| Administration | Safe administration systems, five rights, support to nurses |
| Monitoring of medicines use | ADR and error reporting, outcome monitoring, drug use evaluation |
| Human resources, training and development | Enough trained pharmacists, competency-based training |

The overarching idea is that **every medicine in the hospital is the responsibility of the pharmacy**, and hospital pharmacists should ensure the "seven rights": right patient, right medicine, right dose, right route, right time, with right information and right documentation.

### ASHP standards

The **American Society of Health-System Pharmacists (ASHP)** publishes minimum standards and guidelines for pharmacies in hospitals. They cover:

- leadership and practice management of the pharmacy department;
- medication-use policy (formulary, P&T Committee);
- optimising medication therapy (clinical services, monitoring);
- drug distribution and control (unit dose, IV admixture, controlled substances);
- facilities, equipment and information resources;
- research and quality improvement.

Indian hospitals use these as references because there are few detailed national standards for hospital pharmacy.

## 1.8 NQAS guidelines

**NQAS (National Quality Assurance Standards)** are quality standards for **public health facilities** (district hospitals, CHCs, PHCs, urban PHCs) developed under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare by the **National Health Systems Resource Centre (NHSRC)**. Facilities are assessed and certified at state and national level.

NQAS assessment is arranged in **eight areas of concern**:

| Area | Covers |
| --- | --- |
| A. Service provision | Availability of services, including pharmacy and diagnostics |
| B. Patient rights | Information, privacy, free drugs, grievance redressal |
| C. Inputs | Infrastructure, staff, drugs and equipment |
| D. Support services | Equipment maintenance, laundry, stores, records |
| E. Clinical services | Clinical care, drug administration, protocols |
| F. Infection control | Hand hygiene, biomedical waste, sterilisation |
| G. Quality management | Quality team, audits, SOPs, indicators |
| H. Outcome | Measured indicators of productivity, efficiency, quality |

**Pharmacist's role:** maintain the essential drug list and stock (no stock-outs), proper storage and cold chain, FEFO, expiry checking, records, dispensing with counselling, supply of emergency drugs, and help in audits.

## 1.9 NABH accreditation and role of pharmacists

**NABH (National Accreditation Board for Hospitals and Healthcare Providers)** is a constituent board of the **Quality Council of India (QCI)**. It accredits hospitals that meet its standards; accreditation is voluntary but is valued by patients, insurers and government schemes.

NABH standards are arranged in chapters on patient-centred and organisation-centred areas. The chapter most related to pharmacy is **Management of Medication (MOM)**. Other related chapters are Hospital Infection Control (HIC) and Patient Safety and Quality Improvement (PSQ).

**Medication management requirements that involve the pharmacist:**

1. A **hospital formulary** prepared by a multidisciplinary committee and reviewed regularly.
2. Safe **storage** – temperature control, security, separate storage of look-alike/sound-alike and high-risk medicines, emergency drug trolleys checked regularly.
3. **Prescription** standards – legible, dated, signed, generic names where possible, no unsafe abbreviations.
4. **Dispensing** – checked by a pharmacist, labelled, FEFO.
5. **Administration** and monitoring – right patient verification, ADR monitoring and reporting.
6. **High-risk medications** identified and handled with extra care.
7. **Medication errors**, near misses and ADRs captured, analysed and acted on.
8. Policy on narcotics, recall of drugs, disposal of expired drugs, use of patients' own medicines.

The pharmacist prepares SOPs, trains staff, collects data on errors and ADRs, and presents them at quality meetings – so the pharmacist is a key person in NABH accreditation.

## Remember

- Hospital pharmacy = selection, procurement, storage, preparation, distribution and safe use of medicines in a hospital under a registered pharmacist.
- The pharmacy is a service department under the medical superintendent; sections: store, OPD dispensing, ward supply, compounding, clinical/DI.
- Job description = the job; job specification = the person needed.
- GPP (FIP/WHO) has four roles: supply, medication therapy management, professional development, public health.
- FIP Basel Statements: 2008, revised 2014; overarching statements + six themes (procurement, prescribing, preparation and delivery, administration, monitoring, human resources).
- NQAS = public facilities, NHSRC, eight areas of concern (A–H).
- NABH = part of QCI; Management of Medication (MOM) chapter is the pharmacist's main area.
