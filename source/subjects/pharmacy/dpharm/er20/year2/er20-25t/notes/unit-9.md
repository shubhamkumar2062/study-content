# Unit 9: Pharmaceutical care, drug-related problems, MTM and home medication review

This unit covers the second part of syllabus chapter 9: pharmaceutical care – definition, classification of drug-related problems (DRPs), principles and procedure to provide pharmaceutical care – and Medication Therapy Management (MTM) and Home Medication Review (HMR). A worked case shows how DRPs are found and written up.

## 9.1 Pharmaceutical care

### Definition

**Pharmaceutical care** is the responsible provision of drug therapy for the purpose of achieving definite outcomes that improve a patient's quality of life. (This classic definition was given by Hepler and Strand in 1990.)

**The definite outcomes are:**

1. cure of the disease;
2. elimination or reduction of the patient's symptoms;
3. arresting or slowing the disease process;
4. preventing a disease or symptoms.

Key words in the definition:

- **Responsible** – the pharmacist accepts responsibility for the outcome of drug therapy, not only for supplying the product.
- **Provision** – it is a direct service to the patient.
- **Drug therapy** – the whole therapy: selection, dose, monitoring.
- **Definite outcomes** – measurable goals agreed for each patient.
- **Quality of life** – the final aim.

### Difference from clinical pharmacy

Clinical pharmacy is the **discipline/activities** (ward rounds, chart review); pharmaceutical care is the **philosophy and process of practice** in which the pharmacist takes responsibility for the patient's drug-related needs. Pharmaceutical care can be given in hospitals and community pharmacies.

## 9.2 Drug-related problems (DRPs)

A **drug-related problem** is an event or situation involving drug therapy that actually or potentially interferes with the desired health outcome of the patient.

- **Actual DRP** – already present (patient has hypoglycaemia from too much insulin).
- **Potential DRP** – likely to happen if not prevented (a new prescription of clarithromycin for a patient on simvastatin).

### Classification of DRPs (Strand and colleagues, eight categories)

| No. | Category | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Untreated indication** – patient has a condition needing a drug but receives none | Diabetic patient with high BP and no antihypertensive; no aspirin/statin after myocardial infarction |
| 2 | **Improper drug selection** – wrong drug for the condition or patient | NSAID in a patient with peptic ulcer; beta-blocker that is non-selective in an asthmatic |
| 3 | **Sub-therapeutic dose** – too little drug | Antibiotic dose too low for a severe infection |
| 4 | **Failure to receive drug** – patient does not get the drug (cost, non-availability, non-adherence, not given by nurse) | Patient cannot afford insulin; dose missed on ward |
| 5 | **Over-dosage** – too much drug | Gentamicin full dose in kidney failure; paracetamol from two products |
| 6 | **Adverse drug reaction** | Rash with co-trimoxazole; cough with ACE inhibitor |
| 7 | **Drug interaction** – drug–drug, drug–food, drug–disease, drug–lab test | Warfarin + metronidazole → bleeding |
| 8 | **Drug use without indication** – drug with no valid reason | Antibiotic for a viral cold; continuing PPI with no reason |

A later grouping (Cipolle, Strand and Morley) puts DRPs into **seven categories under four needs**:

| Patient need | DRP categories |
| --- | --- |
| **Indication** | Unnecessary drug therapy; needs additional drug therapy |
| **Effectiveness** | Ineffective drug; dosage too low |
| **Safety** | Adverse drug reaction; dosage too high |
| **Adherence (compliance)** | Non-adherence |

Other systems such as the **PCNE (Pharmaceutical Care Network Europe)** classification also record causes and interventions.

**Causes of DRPs:** wrong prescribing, transcription and dispensing errors, poor communication at transfer of care, polypharmacy, old age, kidney or liver disease, poor patient understanding, cost.

## 9.3 Principles of pharmaceutical care

1. **Patient-centred** – the patient's needs, beliefs and preferences guide decisions.
2. **Responsibility** – the pharmacist is accountable for drug-therapy outcomes.
3. **Therapeutic relationship** – trust and partnership between pharmacist and patient.
4. **Systematic process** – assessment → care plan → follow-up, done the same way every time.
5. **Collaboration** – with doctors, nurses and family.
6. **Documentation** – every assessment, plan and result recorded.
7. **Continuity** – care continues over time, not only at one visit.
8. **Ethics** – confidentiality, respect, honesty.

## 9.4 Procedure for providing pharmaceutical care

The pharmaceutical care process has three main steps, repeated in a cycle:

```text
     ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
     ▼                                             │
 1. ASSESSMENT                                     │
   Collect patient data → identify DRPs            │
     ▼                                             │
 2. CARE PLAN                                      │
   Set goals → choose interventions → agree        │
   monitoring plan with patient and doctor         │
     ▼                                             │
 3. FOLLOW-UP EVALUATION                           │
   Check outcomes → new DRPs? → adjust plan ───────┘
 Document at every step
```

### Step 1 – Assessment

- Collect data: demographics (age, sex, weight, height), presenting complaint, medical history, diagnosis, current and past medicines (with OTC and herbal), allergies, social history (smoking, alcohol, occupation), lab values, vital signs, the patient's concerns and expectations.
- Make a list of the patient's medical problems and match each drug to a problem.
- Identify DRPs using the categories above, in order: indication → effectiveness → safety → adherence.

### Step 2 – Care plan

- Write **therapeutic goals** for each condition (for example BP below the target value, HbA1c target, pain score reduced).
- Choose **interventions** – recommend starting, stopping or changing a drug or dose; patient education; referral.
- Decide a **monitoring plan** – what parameter, how often, who will check.
- Discuss and agree with the patient and prescriber.

### Step 3 – Follow-up evaluation

- Check whether goals are achieved and whether new problems have appeared.
- Record status of each problem: resolved, improved, unchanged, worse, failed.
- Revise the plan; continue the cycle.

### Documentation formats

- **SOAP note:** **S**ubjective (what the patient says), **O**bjective (signs, lab values, drug chart), **A**ssessment (pharmacist's judgement – the DRP), **P**lan (recommendation and monitoring).
- **Pharmacist's work-up of drug therapy (PWDT)** and **FARM** (Findings, Assessment, Recommendation, Monitoring) are other formats.

### Worked case – finding and writing up DRPs

A 70-year-old woman (55 kg) with type 2 diabetes, hypertension and knee osteoarthritis. Serum creatinine 1.8 mg/dL. Medicines: metformin 1 g twice daily, enalapril 10 mg once daily, ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily for knee pain, and amlodipine 5 mg once daily. She complains of stomach pain. BP 148/90 mmHg.

```text
Creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault, female):
CrCl = [(140 − 70) × 55] / (72 × 1.8) × 0.85
     = (70 × 55) / 129.6 × 0.85 = 3850 / 129.6 × 0.85 ≈ 29.7 × 0.85 ≈ 25 mL/min
```

| DRP found | Category | Recommendation (to prescriber) | Monitoring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metformin 2 g/day with CrCl about 25 mL/min | Improper drug selection / dose too high (safety) – risk of lactic acidosis | Review metformin – current guidance advises against it at this level of kidney function; consider another agent | Blood glucose, kidney function |
| Ibuprofen with ACE inhibitor in an elderly patient with poor kidney function | Drug interaction + ADR risk (kidney injury, high BP, GI bleed); likely cause of stomach pain | Stop ibuprofen; use paracetamol for osteoarthritis pain; physiotherapy | Creatinine, potassium, pain score, stomach symptoms |
| BP 148/90 above target | Effectiveness – may partly be due to NSAID | Reassess after stopping NSAID; adjust antihypertensive if still high | BP at each visit |

SOAP note (short): **S** – stomach pain, knee pain. **O** – age 70, SCr 1.8, CrCl ≈ 25 mL/min, BP 148/90, drugs as above. **A** – metformin unsafe at this CrCl; NSAID–ACE inhibitor interaction and probable NSAID gastritis. **P** – recommend review of metformin, stop ibuprofen, start paracetamol, recheck BP, creatinine and potassium in one week, counsel on avoiding over-the-counter painkillers.

## 9.5 Medication Therapy Management (MTM)

### Definition

**Medication Therapy Management** is a distinct service, or group of services, provided by pharmacists (and other qualified providers) to optimise therapeutic outcomes for individual patients. It is independent of, but can be linked with, the supply of a medicine. MTM was developed in the **USA** (it became part of Medicare drug benefits under a 2003 law), mainly for patients with many chronic diseases and many medicines.

### Patients who benefit most

- multiple chronic diseases (diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, asthma/COPD);
- many medicines (polypharmacy) or high-risk drugs (warfarin, insulin);
- recent hospital discharge;
- poor adherence or high drug costs.

### Five core elements of an MTM service

1. **Medication therapy review (MTR)** – a full review of all medicines (prescription, OTC, herbal) with the patient, to identify DRPs. It can be comprehensive or targeted to one problem.
2. **Personal medication record (PMR)** – an up-to-date list of all the patient's medicines, given to the patient to carry and show to every provider.
3. **Medication-related action plan (MAP)** – a simple, patient-centred list of what the patient will do (for example "check sugar before breakfast every day").
4. **Intervention and referral** – the pharmacist solves problems directly or contacts/refers to the doctor.
5. **Documentation and follow-up** – records of the service and a follow-up visit planned.

**Benefits:** better adherence and control of chronic disease, fewer ADRs and hospital admissions, lower costs, patients understand their medicines.

## 9.6 Home Medication Review (HMR)

### Definition

**Home Medication Review** (also called domiciliary medication management review) is a structured review of a patient's medicines carried out by a trained pharmacist in the **patient's home**, usually on referral from the patient's doctor, to identify and resolve medication-related problems. It is well established in **Australia** (Home Medicines Review programme).

### Who needs it

Elderly people on many medicines, patients recently discharged from hospital, those using high-risk drugs, those with confusion about their medicines, many changes to therapy, or suspected non-adherence or ADRs.

### Procedure

```text
Doctor identifies need → referral to pharmacist (with consent)
     ↓
Pharmacist visits home (with caregiver present if needed)
  – collects all medicines in the house ("brown bag"),
  – checks how they are stored and taken,
  – expired or duplicate medicines, OTC/herbal use,
  – understanding, adherence aids, home conditions (falls risk)
     ↓
Pharmacist writes a report with findings and recommendations to the doctor
     ↓
Doctor and patient agree a medication management plan
     ↓
Follow-up; plan implemented; documentation
```

**Advantages:** the real picture of medicine use is seen at home (old stock, shared medicines, poor storage, incorrect inhaler use); fewer ADRs and readmissions; the patient is more confident.

**Limitations:** time and travel, need for trained pharmacists, payment model, privacy, needs good cooperation with the doctor.

### MTM versus HMR

| | MTM | HMR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Pharmacy, clinic, telephone or home | Patient's home |
| Origin | USA | Australia |
| Started by | Pharmacist or payer programme | Usually doctor's referral |
| Output | PMR + action plan for patient | Report to doctor → management plan |
| Common aim | Optimise medicines, find and fix DRPs, improve adherence | Same |

## Remember

- Pharmaceutical care (Hepler and Strand, 1990): responsible provision of drug therapy for definite outcomes that improve quality of life; four outcomes – cure, symptom relief, arrest/slow disease, prevention.
- DRP = event involving drug therapy that interferes with desired outcome; actual or potential.
- Eight DRP categories: untreated indication, improper drug selection, sub-therapeutic dose, failure to receive, overdose, ADR, interaction, drug without indication.
- Seven categories in four needs: indication, effectiveness, safety, adherence.
- Process: assessment → care plan → follow-up; document in SOAP format.
- MTM five core elements: MTR, PMR, MAP, intervention and referral, documentation and follow-up.
- HMR: pharmacist reviews medicines at home on doctor's referral; report to doctor.
