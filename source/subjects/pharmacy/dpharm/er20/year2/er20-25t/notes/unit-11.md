# Unit 11: Poisoning, and drug and poison information centres

This unit covers syllabus chapter 11: types of poisoning, clinical manifestations and antidotes; and drug and poison information centres and their services – definition, requirements, information resources with examples, and their advantages and disadvantages. A worked example on a paracetamol overdose is included.

## Part A – Poisoning

## 11.1 Definitions

- **Poison** – any substance which, when taken into or applied to the body in a sufficient quantity, damages health or causes death. ("The dose makes the poison" – almost any drug is a poison in overdose.)
- **Poisoning** – the harmful effects produced by a poison.
- **Toxicology** – the science dealing with poisons, their effects, detection and treatment.
- **Antidote** – a substance that counteracts the effect of a poison.

## 11.2 Types of poisoning

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| **Intent** | **Accidental** (children swallowing tablets or kerosene, wrong medicine, occupational exposure to pesticides), **suicidal (intentional self-poisoning)** – very common in India with pesticides, **homicidal** (rare), and therapeutic/iatrogenic (medication overdose by error) |
| **Duration** | **Acute** – single large exposure, rapid effects; **chronic** – repeated small exposures (lead, arsenic, mercury) |
| **Route** | Ingestion (commonest), inhalation (carbon monoxide), skin contact (organophosphates), injection, bites and stings |
| **Type of agent** | Drugs (paracetamol, benzodiazepines, opioids, tricyclic antidepressants, iron), agricultural chemicals (organophosphates, carbamates, aluminium phosphide, paraquat), household products (kerosene, corrosives, detergents), plants (datura, oleander), metals (lead, arsenic), animal toxins (snake bite, scorpion sting), alcohols (methanol) |
| **Action** | Corrosives, irritants, neurotoxics (CNS depressants, convulsants), cardiotoxics, asphyxiants (carbon monoxide, cyanide) |

## 11.3 General management of poisoning

```text
1. Resuscitation and stabilisation – Airway, Breathing, Circulation (ABC),
   check glucose, control seizures
        ↓
2. History and examination – what, how much, when, route; look for a
   toxidrome (group of signs); keep containers, samples
        ↓
3. Decontamination – remove clothes and wash skin; eye irrigation;
   activated charcoal or gastric lavage only when indicated
        ↓
4. Enhanced elimination – urine alkalinisation, haemodialysis,
   multiple-dose charcoal (selected poisons)
        ↓
5. Specific antidote when available
        ↓
6. Supportive care and monitoring; psychiatric assessment after
   self-poisoning; report to authorities where required (medico-legal case)
```

**Decontamination notes:**

- **Activated charcoal** adsorbs many drugs in the gut; most useful within about 1 hour of ingestion; usual adult dose 50 g (about 1 g/kg in children). It does **not** adsorb alcohols, iron, lithium, corrosives or hydrocarbons. Not given if the airway is not protected.
- **Gastric lavage** – only for a life-threatening amount taken within about an hour, with airway protection; **never for corrosives or kerosene/hydrocarbons** (risk of perforation and aspiration pneumonitis).
- **Induced vomiting (syrup of ipecac)** is no longer recommended.

### Common toxidromes

| Toxidrome | Signs | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cholinergic** | Salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, vomiting, sweating, **pinpoint pupils**, bradycardia, bronchorrhoea, muscle twitching | Organophosphate and carbamate insecticides |
| **Anticholinergic** | Dry hot flushed skin, **dilated pupils**, fast pulse, urine retention, delirium ("hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter") | Datura, atropine, antihistamines, tricyclics |
| **Opioid** | Coma, **pinpoint pupils**, **slow breathing** | Morphine, heroin, tramadol, codeine |
| **Sedative–hypnotic** | Drowsiness, slurred speech, ataxia, coma, normal pupils | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol |
| **Sympathomimetic** | Agitation, sweating, dilated pupils, fast pulse, high BP, fever | Amphetamines, cocaine, high-dose theophylline |

## 11.4 Clinical manifestations and antidotes of important poisons

| Poison | Main clinical features | Antidote / specific treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paracetamol** overdose | Few early symptoms (nausea); liver damage after 24–72 h (raised ALT, jaundice) | **N-acetylcysteine** (restores glutathione) – best within 8 h |
| **Organophosphates** | Cholinergic toxidrome, respiratory failure | **Atropine** in repeated doses until secretions dry (atropinisation) + **pralidoxime** (oxime) |
| **Opioids** | Coma, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression | **Naloxone** |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Sedation, coma | **Flumazenil** (used with caution; may cause seizures) |
| **Heparin** | Bleeding | **Protamine sulfate** |
| **Warfarin** (and superwarfarin rodenticides) | Bleeding, high INR | **Vitamin K1 (phytomenadione)**; fresh frozen plasma or prothrombin complex for serious bleeding |
| **Iron** | Vomiting, GI bleeding, shock, metabolic acidosis, liver failure | **Desferrioxamine (deferoxamine)** |
| **Methanol** (spurious liquor) | Drunkenness, then visual blurring, blindness, severe metabolic acidosis | **Fomepizole** or **ethanol** (block alcohol dehydrogenase), sodium bicarbonate, haemodialysis, folinic acid |
| **Cyanide** | Rapid collapse, convulsions, bitter almond smell, severe acidosis | **Hydroxocobalamin**, or nitrites (amyl / sodium nitrite) followed by **sodium thiosulfate** |
| **Digoxin** | Nausea, visual changes (yellow vision), arrhythmias, high potassium | **Digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Fab)** |
| **Lead** | Abdominal colic, anaemia (basophilic stippling), lead line on gums, neuropathy, encephalopathy | **Calcium disodium EDTA**, **succimer (DMSA)**, dimercaprol in severe cases |
| **Arsenic, mercury** | GI symptoms, skin changes (arsenic), kidney damage | **Dimercaprol (BAL)**, succimer |
| **Copper** (Wilson's disease, copper sulfate) | Haemolysis, liver damage | **Penicillamine** |
| **Beta-blockers** | Bradycardia, hypotension | **Glucagon**, atropine |
| **Calcium channel blockers** | Hypotension, bradycardia | **Calcium gluconate / chloride**, high-dose insulin |
| **Isoniazid** | Seizures, acidosis | **Pyridoxine** |
| **Nitrites, dapsone** (methaemoglobinaemia) | Cyanosis not corrected by oxygen, chocolate-brown blood | **Methylene blue** |
| **Datura / atropine** (anticholinergic) | Anticholinergic toxidrome | Supportive; **physostigmine** in severe cases |
| **Snake bite** (cobra, krait, Russell's viper, saw-scaled viper) | Neurotoxic (ptosis, paralysis) or haemotoxic (bleeding, kidney injury) features | **Polyvalent anti-snake venom (ASV)** (covers these four snakes); neostigmine with atropine for neurotoxic bites |
| **Scorpion sting** | Pain, sweating, high BP, pulmonary oedema | **Prazosin**; supportive care |
| **Kerosene / hydrocarbons** | Coughing, choking, aspiration pneumonitis, drowsiness | No antidote; **no emesis or lavage**; oxygen, supportive care |
| **Corrosives** (acids, alkalis) | Burns of mouth, throat, stomach; perforation | No neutralisation, no lavage; supportive, endoscopy |
| **Aluminium phosphide** (celphos) | Vomiting, shock, severe acidosis, arrhythmias | No specific antidote; intensive supportive care |
| **Carbon monoxide** | Headache, confusion, cherry-red colour, coma | **100% oxygen** (hyperbaric oxygen where available) |

### Worked example – paracetamol overdose

A 50 kg woman swallowed 20 tablets of paracetamol 500 mg about 3 hours ago.

```text
Total dose  = 20 × 500 mg = 10,000 mg (10 g)
Dose per kg = 10,000 / 50 = 200 mg/kg
A single dose above about 150 mg/kg is commonly taken as potentially toxic
→ check a 4-hour serum paracetamol level and plot it on the treatment
  nomogram; start N-acetylcysteine (do not wait if the level will be late
  or the time is uncertain)
Activated charcoal: 50 g if within about 1 hour (here 3 h → limited value)

Standard 21-hour IV N-acetylcysteine regimen (by body weight):
  150 mg/kg over 1 h  → 150 × 50 = 7,500 mg
   50 mg/kg over 4 h  →  50 × 50 = 2,500 mg
  100 mg/kg over 16 h → 100 × 50 = 5,000 mg
  Total 300 mg/kg     →            15,000 mg (15 g)
```

The pharmacist checks the calculation, prepares or supervises the infusions, and watches for anaphylactoid reactions (flushing, wheeze) during the first bag.

## Part B – Drug and poison information centres

## 11.5 Definitions

- **Drug information** – current, critically evaluated and unbiased information about drugs and their use, given in answer to a request or in advance, to promote rational drug therapy.
- **Drug Information Centre (DIC)** – a facility, usually in a hospital or college, staffed by trained pharmacists and equipped with references, that provides drug information to health professionals and the public.
- **Poison Information Centre (PIC)** – a specialised service, ideally available **24 hours a day**, that gives information and advice on the diagnosis, management and prevention of poisoning, and keeps records of poisoning cases. In India, the **National Poisons Information Centre** works at AIIMS, New Delhi, and several medical colleges run regional centres.

## 11.6 Services of DIC and PIC

| Drug Information Centre | Poison Information Centre |
| --- | --- |
| Answer queries (dose, ADR, interaction, compatibility, pregnancy and lactation, availability, identification of tablets) | 24-hour telephone advice on management of poisoning |
| Support the P&T Committee (drug monographs, formulary) | Information on toxic doses, antidotes, decontamination |
| Drug bulletins and newsletters | Keep list and location of antidotes in the region |
| ADR monitoring and reporting | Toxicovigilance – recording trends of poisoning |
| Education of staff and students; patient education | Education and prevention campaigns (safe storage of pesticides, child-proof containers) |
| Drug use evaluation and research | Analytical services (in some centres) |

## 11.7 Requirements of a drug/poison information centre

1. **Space** – a quiet room near the pharmacy or wards, with shelves and work tables.
2. **Staff** – trained drug information pharmacists (clinical pharmacists), with support of clinical pharmacologists and physicians; for PIC, staff on call round the clock.
3. **Information resources** – up-to-date primary, secondary and tertiary references (print and electronic).
4. **Equipment** – computers with internet, printer, telephone with a dedicated number (toll-free for PIC), fax/e-mail.
5. **Documentation** – query receipt and response forms, register or database of all queries, follow-up records.
6. **Policies and SOPs** – who may ask, response times, confidentiality, quality checks.
7. **Funding and administrative support** for subscriptions and staff.

## 11.8 Information resources

Drug information sources are grouped into three levels.

### Primary sources

**Original research and reports** published for the first time – clinical trials, case reports, cohort studies – mainly in **journals**.

Examples: The Lancet, New England Journal of Medicine, BMJ, Indian Journal of Pharmacology, Indian Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Annals of Pharmacotherapy.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Most current and detailed information | Huge volume; hard to search |
| Original data – reader can judge the method | Needs skill to evaluate study quality |
| Basis of all other sources | Single studies may be biased or wrong |
| | Costly subscriptions |

### Secondary sources

**Indexing and abstracting services** that help find primary literature.

Examples: **PubMed/MEDLINE**, Embase, International Pharmaceutical Abstracts (IPA), Cochrane Library (systematic reviews), Google Scholar.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Quick way to find relevant primary articles | Abstracts alone may mislead – full text still needed |
| Wide coverage of journals; search by keywords | Time lag before indexing |
| Many are free online (PubMed) | Needs training to search well; some are costly |

### Tertiary sources

**Compiled, summarised information** based on primary and secondary literature – textbooks, compendia, handbooks, databases, review articles.

Examples:

- Official compendia – **Indian Pharmacopoeia**, **National Formulary of India**, British National Formulary (BNF)
- Reference books – **Martindale: The Complete Drug Reference**, Goodman & Gilman's The Pharmacological Basis of Therapeutics, AHFS Drug Information, Drug Facts and Comparisons, Stockley's Drug Interactions, Trissel's Handbook on Injectable Drugs, Briggs' Drugs in Pregnancy and Lactation
- Indian drug indexes – **CIMS**, **IDR (Indian Drug Review)**, Drug Today
- Electronic databases – Micromedex, Lexicomp, UpToDate, Medscape
- Toxicology references – Goldfrank's Toxicologic Emergencies

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Easy to use, quick answers | Information may be out of date (time lag) |
| Compact; summarised by experts | Limited detail; author bias possible |
| Good starting point for most queries | May not cover new drugs or rare questions |
| Many trusted by regulators/professionals | Commercial indexes may lack independent evaluation |

**Search order:** start with **tertiary** sources for a quick, reliable answer → use **secondary** sources to find recent **primary** articles if more detail or newer data is needed.

## 11.9 Systematic approach to answering a query (outline)

```text
1. Receive query – record requester's name, profession, contact, urgency
2. Get background information – patient details, diagnosis, drugs, why
   the question is asked (find the "real" question)
3. Classify the query (dose, ADR, interaction, pregnancy, poisoning…)
4. Search – tertiary → secondary → primary
5. Evaluate and put together the information
6. Formulate and give the answer – clear, referenced, within the time needed
7. Follow up and document
```

The practical guide "Drug information queries" works through two cases using this approach.

## Remember

- Poisoning types: accidental, suicidal, homicidal, therapeutic; acute and chronic; by route and by agent.
- General management: ABC → history and toxidrome → decontamination (charcoal within about 1 h; no lavage for corrosives/kerosene) → enhanced elimination → antidote → supportive care.
- Key antidotes: paracetamol–NAC; OP–atropine + pralidoxime; opioid–naloxone; benzodiazepine–flumazenil; heparin–protamine; warfarin–vitamin K; iron–desferrioxamine; methanol–fomepizole/ethanol; cyanide–hydroxocobalamin or nitrite + thiosulfate; digoxin–Fab; lead–EDTA/DMSA; arsenic–BAL; methaemoglobinaemia–methylene blue; isoniazid–pyridoxine; snake bite–polyvalent ASV; scorpion–prazosin.
- DIC = evaluated, unbiased drug information; PIC = 24-hour poisoning advice (National Poisons Information Centre, AIIMS).
- Requirements: space, trained staff, resources, equipment, documentation, SOPs, funds.
- Sources: primary (journals), secondary (PubMed, Embase, IPA), tertiary (Martindale, BNF, CIMS, IDR, NFI, Micromedex) – know an advantage and a disadvantage of each.
