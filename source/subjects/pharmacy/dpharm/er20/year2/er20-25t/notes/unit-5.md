# Unit 5: Drug distribution in the hospital

This unit covers syllabus chapter 5: drug distribution to in-patients and out-patients (definition, advantages and disadvantages of the individual prescription order method, floor stock method, unit dose drug distribution method and drug basket method), distribution to ICCU/ICU/NICU and emergency wards, automated drug dispensing systems and devices, and the distribution and storage of narcotic and psychotropic substances. Worked examples of floor-stock top-up and a unit-dose fill are included.

## 5.1 Meaning of drug distribution

**Drug distribution** is the system by which medicines move from the hospital pharmacy to the patient – either handed directly to out-patients, or sent to the wards for nurses to give to in-patients. A good system makes sure that:

1. the right drug reaches the right patient, in the right dose, by the right route, at the right time;
2. errors, wastage and pilferage are kept low;
3. the pharmacist can check every order before the patient receives it;
4. nurses spend less time handling medicines;
5. charges to patients are correct.

## 5.2 Out-patient dispensing

Out-patients bring a prescription from the OPD to the pharmacy counter.

```text
Patient with OPD prescription → token / queue → pharmacist checks
prescription (legality, dose, interactions, allergy) → pick, count,
label → second check → hand over with counselling → record (register
or software), bill or free-supply entry
```

Good OPD practice: separate counters for new and repeat prescriptions, waiting area with seating, privacy for counselling, clear labels in local language, pictograms for patients who cannot read, and a system for refills of chronic medicines.

## 5.3 In-patient distribution systems

### 5.3.1 Individual prescription order (IPO) system

The physician writes an order for each patient; the order (or a copy) goes to the pharmacy; the pharmacist dispenses that patient's medicines in a labelled container (usually for several days) which goes to the ward.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacist reviews every order for each patient | Delay in getting drugs to the ward |
| Close pharmacist–doctor–nurse–patient contact | Needs more pharmacy staff and time |
| Correct billing for each patient | Many days' supply on the ward → wastage, returns, pilferage |
| Better inventory control than floor stock | Nurses still prepare individual doses → errors possible |
| Suits small and private hospitals | Unused medicines when therapy changes |

### 5.3.2 Floor stock (ward stock) system

A stock of commonly used drugs is kept on each ward; nurses take doses from it as ordered. It may be:

- **Charged floor stock** – each dose is charged to the patient by the nurse.
- **Non-charged (free) floor stock** – cheap, commonly used items (paracetamol, antacids, IV fluids, antiseptics) are not charged individually; the cost is part of the bed charge.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Drugs immediately available | Pharmacist does not review each order → **more medication errors** |
| Fewer drug orders to the pharmacy | Large stock on wards → money blocked, expiry, pilferage |
| Fewer pharmacy staff needed | Nurse workload increased (preparing, recording) |
| Fewer returns of drugs | Poor storage conditions on wards |
| | Difficult to charge correctly |

Floor stock is suitable for emergency departments, operation theatres and for low-cost items. A **par level** (maximum quantity) is fixed for each item and the pharmacy tops the stock up to that level at fixed intervals.

**Worked example 1 – floor stock top-up.** A surgical ward uses on average 20 bottles of normal saline (500 mL) a day. The pharmacy restocks every 2 days and keeps a buffer of 10 bottles.

```text
Par level = (average daily use × days between restocking) + buffer
          = (20 × 2) + 10 = 50 bottles
On the restocking day the ward count shows 18 bottles.
Quantity to supply = par level − present stock = 50 − 18 = 32 bottles
```

### 5.3.3 Combination system

Most Indian hospitals use a **combination**: individual prescription orders for costly and restricted drugs (antibiotics, oncology drugs) and floor stock for cheap, common items and emergency drugs.

### 5.3.4 Unit dose drug distribution system (UDDDS)

**Unit dose** = a single dose of a drug, packed and labelled ready to give to the patient (for example one tablet in a labelled blister strip, a pre-filled syringe, a labelled oral syringe).

**Unit dose drug distribution** is a pharmacist-coordinated system in which medicines are dispensed **in unit-dose packages, ready to administer**, and **not more than a 24-hour supply** is sent to or kept on the ward at any time.

**Procedure (centralised):**

```text
Doctor's order → copy / electronic order reaches pharmacy
     ↓
Pharmacist enters order in patient medication profile; checks dose,
allergy, interactions, duplicates
     ↓
Patient-specific drawer of a medication cart (cassette) filled with
each dose for 24 h; second person checks
     ↓
Cart exchanged on the ward at a fixed time each day; old cart (with
returned doses) comes back to pharmacy → credit given
     ↓
Nurse gives each dose from the patient's drawer; records on medication
administration record (MAR)
     ↓
New / changed orders → supplied in between by the pharmacy
```

It may be **centralised** (all doses from the main pharmacy) or **decentralised** (from satellite pharmacies on each floor).

**Worked example 2 – a 24-hour cassette.** Bed 12 has these orders: amoxicillin 500 mg capsule three times a day, pantoprazole 40 mg tablet once daily before breakfast, paracetamol 650 mg tablet four times a day, and enoxaparin 40 mg SC once daily.

```text
Item                         Doses in 24 h   Units placed in drawer
Amoxicillin 500 mg cap       3               3 labelled unit-dose capsules
Pantoprazole 40 mg tab       1               1 labelled unit-dose tablet
Paracetamol 650 mg tab       4               4 labelled unit-dose tablets
Enoxaparin 40 mg/0.4 mL      1               1 pre-filled syringe
Total                        9 doses         9 units, each labelled with
                                             patient name, bed, drug,
                                             strength, time
```

If the paracetamol is stopped at midday, only the remaining doses are returned and credited, not a whole strip or bottle.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| **Fewer medication errors** (each dose checked by pharmacist, labelled until given) | High initial cost (packing machines, carts, software) |
| Less wastage and pilferage; credit for unused doses | Needs more pharmacists and longer pharmacy hours |
| Less drug stock on wards; better inventory control | Unit-dose packs not available for all drugs; repackaging needed |
| Accurate charging per dose | Needs good communication between ward and pharmacy |
| More nursing time for patient care | |
| Pharmacist more involved in therapy (patient profiles) | |

### 5.3.5 Drug basket method

In the **drug basket (patient basket/tray) method**, the pharmacy prepares a separate labelled **basket or tray for each patient** (bed number and name) containing that patient's medicines for a fixed period, commonly **24 hours**, based on the current orders. The baskets are carried to the ward in a trolley; the nurse gives doses from the patient's basket, and the basket returns to the pharmacy to be refilled the next day.

It is a low-cost step between the individual prescription system and the full unit-dose system and is used in many Indian hospitals.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Each patient's medicines kept separate → fewer mix-ups | Medicines may not be in individually labelled unit doses |
| Pharmacist checks orders daily | Needs daily filling and transport |
| Less stock on wards; returns easy to credit | Changes in orders during the day need extra supply |
| Cheaper than a full unit-dose system | Baskets need cleaning and space |

### Comparison of in-patient systems

| Feature | Individual order | Floor stock | Unit dose | Drug basket |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pharmacist reviews each order | Yes | No | Yes | Yes |
| Supply on ward | Several days | Large, common stock | ≤24 h, unit doses | Usually 24 h per patient |
| Medication errors | Moderate | Highest | Lowest | Low |
| Wastage / pilferage | Moderate | High | Low | Low |
| Cost to set up | Low | Low | High | Low–moderate |
| Pharmacy staff needed | Moderate | Few | Many | Moderate |

## 5.4 Distribution to ICCU, ICU, NICU and emergency wards

These areas need **immediate** access to drugs and use many high-alert injectables, so a mixed system is used:

1. **Emergency (crash cart) stock** – a fixed list in a sealed trolley, checked every day (seal number recorded) and after each use; refilled at once from the pharmacy.
2. **Floor stock with par levels** for common IV fluids, injections, sedatives and inotropes, often held in an **automated dispensing cabinet** or a locked satellite stock.
3. **Satellite (decentralised) pharmacy** in large ICUs, open 24 hours, preparing IV infusions and checking orders on rounds.
4. **Standard concentrations** for infusions (for example inotropes, insulin, heparin) prepared in the pharmacy or with ready-to-use labels, to reduce calculation errors.
5. **NICU** – very small doses: oral syringes, dilution charts, single-use small vials, preservative-free products (for example benzyl alcohol is avoided in neonates); dose checks by weight in kg.
6. **Emergency ward** – rapid supply of antidotes, anti-snake venom, analgesics, antibiotics; afterwards the patient's file is charged and orders are written up.
7. Temperature-sensitive drugs (insulin, some antibiotics, vaccines) kept in a ward refrigerator with a temperature log.
8. Narcotics in a double-locked cabinet with a ward register (section 5.6).

## 5.5 Automated drug dispensing systems and devices

Automation uses machines and software to store, count, pack, dispense and track medicines with fewer errors.

| System | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Automated dispensing cabinet (ADC)** | Computer-controlled locked cabinet on the ward; the nurse logs in (password or fingerprint), selects the patient from the pharmacy-verified profile, and only the right drawer opens; every removal is recorded; stock alerts go to the pharmacy |
| **Central pharmacy robots** | Pick unit doses for patient carts automatically using barcodes |
| **Automated counting machines** | Count tablets and capsules for out-patient prescriptions |
| **Unit-dose packaging machines** | Strip-pack individual doses with printed label and barcode |
| **Carousels** | Rotating shelves bring the right bin to the operator |
| **Barcode medication administration (BCMA)** | Nurse scans the patient wrist-band and the dose barcode before giving; mismatch gives an alert |
| **Smart infusion pumps** | Built-in drug library with dose limits stop gross programming errors |
| **Computerised prescriber order entry (CPOE)** | Doctor enters orders electronically; dose and interaction checks; goes straight to the pharmacy |

**Advantages:** fewer dispensing and administration errors, better stock control and security of controlled drugs, instant records, saves staff time.

**Disadvantages:** high cost, power and software failure, need for training, risk of over-reliance (staff override alerts), and a pharmacist must still check every order.

## 5.6 Distribution and storage of narcotic and psychotropic substances

Narcotic drugs and psychotropic substances are controlled under the **NDPS Act, 1985** and its rules (and state rules). Within a hospital:

**Storage**

1. Kept in a **steel cupboard with two separate locks** (double lock), fixed to the wall, in the pharmacy and on wards that keep them.
2. Keys held by the pharmacist in charge (pharmacy) and the sister-in-charge (ward); keys handed over at shift change with a count.
3. Separate shelf for each drug and strength; no other items in the cupboard.

**Distribution**

1. Issued only on a **written order/special indent** signed by a registered medical practitioner, stating patient name, drug, dose, quantity.
2. Ward stock limited to a small fixed quantity; replaced only when **empty ampoules** and the record of administration are returned ("one empty for one full").
3. **Narcotic register** in the pharmacy and on each ward: date, patient's name, bed or file number, drug and dose, quantity issued/given, balance, signature of the person giving and a witness.
4. Unused part of an ampoule is discarded in front of a witness; both sign.
5. **Counting** of stock at every shift change; any discrepancy reported immediately to the medical superintendent.
6. Expired or damaged NDPS drugs are kept in the cupboard until destroyed as per procedure (see Unit 4).
7. Records are kept for the period required by the rules and shown to inspectors.

## Remember

- Systems: individual prescription order, floor stock (charged/non-charged), combination, unit dose, drug basket.
- Floor stock: quickest but most errors; par level = daily use × restock interval + buffer.
- Unit dose: single labelled ready-to-give doses, ≤24 h supply on ward, pharmacist checks every order → fewest errors.
- Drug basket: one labelled basket per patient for 24 h; cheap step towards unit dose.
- ICU/NICU/emergency: crash cart (sealed, checked daily), par-level stock or ADC, satellite pharmacy, standard infusion concentrations, weight-based NICU doses.
- Automation: ADC, robots, counting and unit-dose packing machines, carousels, BCMA, smart pumps, CPOE.
- NDPS on wards: double lock, written order, register with witness, empty ampoule returned for refill, count at every shift.
