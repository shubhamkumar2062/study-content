# Unit 1: Community pharmacy practice, professional responsibilities, GPP and SOPs

This unit covers syllabus chapters 1 and 2: what community pharmacy is, how it grew in the world and in India, what a community pharmacist is responsible for, the idea of Good Pharmacy Practice (GPP), and Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs).

## 1.1 Definitions

**Community pharmacy** is a pharmacy that serves the public directly from a shop in the community (a chemist shop, medical store or retail pharmacy). It supplies medicines on prescription and without prescription, and gives advice on their safe use and on health.

**Community pharmacist** is a pharmacist registered under the Pharmacy Act, 1948 who works in such a pharmacy. He or she is often the health professional people meet first and most easily, without an appointment and without a fee.

**Community pharmacy practice** is the whole set of professional services given from a community pharmacy: dispensing, patient counselling, advice on minor ailments, health screening, health education, medicine record keeping and referral to doctors.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Retail pharmacy / chemist and druggist | Common Indian names for a community pharmacy |
| Pharmaceutical care | Responsible provision of drug therapy to achieve definite outcomes that improve the patient's quality of life |
| Patient-centred practice | The patient, not the product, is the focus of the service |
| Model community pharmacy | A training pharmacy set up in a college for practical work (required under ER-2020) |

## 1.2 History and development of community pharmacy

### International scenario

```text
Physician makes and gives medicine  →  Apothecary (maker and seller of medicines)
      →  Separation of pharmacy from medicine  →  Chemist and druggist (product focus)
      →  Professional pharmacist (dispensing + advice)  →  Pharmaceutical care (patient focus)
```

- **Early period:** in ancient civilisations the healer collected, prepared and gave medicines. Pharmacy was not a separate job.
- **Arab world:** some of the earliest known shops selling prepared medicines opened in Baghdad in the 8th century. Arab pharmacists wrote formularies and regulated drug quality.
- **Europe:** in the 13th century a decree of Emperor Frederick II separated the work of the pharmacist from that of the physician in his kingdom. Apothecary shops became common in European towns.
- **Professional bodies and laws:** the Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain was founded in 1841 and the American Pharmaceutical Association in 1852. Laws then made registration of pharmacists compulsory.
- **Industrial age (20th century):** factories began making most medicines as tablets and capsules. Compounding in the shop declined, and the pharmacist became mainly a supplier of ready-made products.
- **Change to patient care (from the 1960s–1990s):** clinical pharmacy, and in 1990 the concept of **pharmaceutical care** (Hepler and Strand), moved the focus back to the patient. In many developed countries community pharmacists now give vaccinations, review medicines, run smoking-cessation and screening services, and in some countries prescribe for minor ailments.
- **International bodies:** the International Pharmaceutical Federation (**FIP**) and the World Health Organization (**WHO**) set guidelines such as Good Pharmacy Practice.

| Country | Typical features of community pharmacy |
| --- | --- |
| United Kingdom | Pharmacist must be present; services like medicine use review, minor ailment schemes, vaccinations, emergency contraception |
| United States | Large chains; pharmacists give immunisations and medication therapy management |
| Canada, Australia | Medication reviews; limited prescribing for minor ailments in some states/provinces |
| Many developing countries | Mainly supply-focused; shortage of qualified pharmacists at the counter |

### Indian scenario

- **Traditional systems:** in Ayurveda and Unani, the vaidya or hakim prepared and gave the medicine.
- **British period:** imported medicines were sold from chemist shops in port cities (Bathgate and Co., Calcutta, 1811, is often quoted as an early one). Compounders were trained on the job.
- **Laws:** the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and its Rules, 1945 brought licensing of drug sale. The **Pharmacy Act, 1948** created the Pharmacy Council of India and State Pharmacy Councils and allowed only registered pharmacists to dispense.
- **After independence:** the number of retail chemist shops grew very fast. Many shops were run by traders, and the pharmacist was often absent – a problem still seen in some places.
- **Recent developments:**
  - PCI **Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015** – duties and standards of registered pharmacists.
  - **Education Regulations, 2020** – D.Pharm made practice-oriented, with Community Pharmacy and Management as a full subject and a model community pharmacy in every college.
  - **Jan Aushadhi Kendras** (Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana) – quality generic medicines at low prices.
  - Chain pharmacies, online (e-) pharmacies and pharmacy software.
  - Involvement in national programmes – TB, pharmacovigilance (PvPI), family planning, immunisation awareness.

### Problems of community pharmacy in India

| Problem | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacist not present at many shops | Medicines sold by untrained staff |
| Business focus, low counselling | Patients get no advice |
| Sale of prescription drugs without prescription (e.g. antibiotics) | Self-medication, antimicrobial resistance |
| No clear legal OTC list | Confusion about what may be sold freely |
| Low public recognition of pharmacists | Pharmacist seen as a shopkeeper |
| Crowded counters, no counselling area | No privacy |

## 1.3 Professional responsibilities of community pharmacists

A community pharmacist is responsible to the patient, to society, to the profession and to the law.

### Main responsibilities

1. **Dispensing:** receive, check and interpret prescriptions; supply the correct medicine, dose and quantity with a proper label.
2. **Patient counselling:** explain how, when and how long to take medicines, side effects, storage and what to do if a dose is missed.
3. **Responding to symptoms and OTC supply:** advise on minor ailments, supply suitable non-prescription medicines, and refer when needed.
4. **Prevention of drug-related problems:** check for interactions, duplications, wrong doses, allergies and contraindications; contact the prescriber when needed.
5. **Health screening:** blood pressure, blood glucose, BMI, peak flow, pulse oximetry; refer abnormal results.
6. **Health promotion and education:** advice on diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, family planning, hygiene; awareness campaigns.
7. **Medication adherence support:** reminders, pill organisers, simple regimens.
8. **Pharmacovigilance:** recognise and report adverse drug reactions under the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI).
9. **Drug information:** answer queries from patients and doctors.
10. **Legal duties:** keep licences, records and registers (for example Schedule H1 and X records), store drugs correctly, sell only from licensed sources.
11. **Rational use and antimicrobial stewardship:** do not supply antibiotics without a prescription; explain completion of the course.
12. **Safe disposal of expired and unused medicines.**
13. **Management:** purchase, stock control, staff supervision and finances.
14. **Professional development:** keep knowledge up to date and follow the code of ethics.

### Duties under the Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015 (in brief)

- Maintain proper patient medication records and dispensing records.
- Counsel patients and provide information on medicines.
- Dispense only on a valid prescription where the law requires one.
- Display the registration certificate and work personally in the pharmacy during working hours.
- Maintain dignity of the profession and confidentiality of patients.

### Code of ethics (key points)

| Towards | Duty |
| --- | --- |
| Patient | Put the patient's welfare first, keep information confidential, respect dignity |
| Prescriber | Cooperate; do not change a prescription without consulting the prescriber |
| Profession | Keep high standards; do not run down colleagues |
| Society | Promote rational use, take part in public health programmes |

## 1.4 Good Pharmacy Practice (GPP)

### Definition

**Good Pharmacy Practice** is practice that meets the needs of the people who use pharmacy services by giving the best, evidence-based care. GPP guidelines were first adopted by FIP in 1993 and revised jointly by FIP and WHO in 2011 (*Joint FIP/WHO guidelines on GPP: standards for quality of pharmacy services*). In India, the Indian Pharmaceutical Association and drug regulators have promoted GPP training for community pharmacists.

### Requirements of GPP

1. The first concern of the pharmacist is the welfare of the patient.
2. The core activity is supplying medicines and health products of assured quality, with information and advice, and monitoring their effects.
3. The pharmacist contributes to rational and economic prescribing and appropriate use of medicines.
4. Each element of the service is relevant to the individual, clearly defined and effectively communicated.

### Four main roles in the FIP/WHO GPP guidelines

| Role | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Prepare, obtain, store, secure, distribute, administer, dispense and dispose of medical products | Procurement from licensed wholesalers, cold chain, dispensing, disposal |
| 2. Provide effective medication therapy management | Checking prescriptions, counselling, monitoring outcomes |
| 3. Maintain and improve professional performance | Continuing education, SOPs, audits |
| 4. Contribute to improving the effectiveness of the health care system and public health | Health promotion, screening, pharmacovigilance, AMR awareness |

### Elements of GPP in a community pharmacy

- **Premises:** clean, well lit, ventilated, adequate area, separate counselling space, temperature control and refrigerator.
- **Personnel:** registered pharmacist present; trained support staff; clean uniform and name badge.
- **Procurement and storage:** only from licensed sources; FEFO stock rotation; correct temperature; separate area for expired and returned goods.
- **Dispensing:** prescription checking, accurate selection, labelling and counselling.
- **Documentation:** records of purchases, sales, prescriptions, registers and patient records.
- **Quality management:** SOPs, self-inspection and audit, handling of complaints and errors.

## 1.5 Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)

### Definition

A **Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)** is a written, approved, step-by-step instruction that tells staff exactly how to do a routine task, so that it is done the same correct way every time, by every person.

### Why SOPs are needed

- Uniform quality of service whoever is on duty.
- Fewer errors (dispensing errors, wrong storage).
- Clear responsibility for each task.
- Training tool for new staff.
- Evidence during inspection or audit that the pharmacy follows rules.
- Base for continuous improvement.

### Format of an SOP

```text
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Name of pharmacy                 SOP No.: CP/007   │
│ Title: Receiving and checking a prescription        │
│ Version: 02      Effective date:    Review date:    │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Purpose                                          │
│ 2. Scope (where and to whom it applies)             │
│ 3. Responsibility (who does it, who checks)         │
│ 4. Definitions / abbreviations                      │
│ 5. Procedure (numbered steps)                       │
│ 6. Records / formats to fill                        │
│ 7. References                                       │
│ 8. Prepared by / Reviewed by / Approved by (signed) │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Examples of SOPs in a community pharmacy

| Area | SOP titles |
| --- | --- |
| Dispensing | Receiving and checking prescriptions; dispensing and labelling; handling Schedule H1 and X drugs; patient counselling |
| Stock | Ordering and receiving goods; storage and cold chain; stock rotation (FEFO); handling expired and damaged stock |
| Services | Blood pressure measurement; blood glucose testing; responding to symptoms |
| Housekeeping | Cleaning of premises and refrigerator; pest control; temperature recording |
| Safety | Handling dispensing errors and complaints; reporting adverse drug reactions; disposal of waste |
| Administration | Opening and closing the pharmacy; cash handling; record keeping |

### Writing a good SOP

1. Write in short, clear, numbered steps using simple language.
2. Describe what is actually done, by whom and with what.
3. Get it reviewed and approved by the pharmacist in charge.
4. Train staff and keep a signed training record.
5. Review it at fixed intervals (for example yearly) or when practice changes; withdraw old versions.

## Remember

- Community pharmacy = public-facing pharmacy giving medicines **and** advice; the pharmacist is the most accessible health professional.
- History: healer → apothecary → separation of pharmacy (13th century Europe) → chemist and druggist → pharmaceutical care (1990).
- India: Pharmacy Act 1948, Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940, Pharmacy Practice Regulations 2015, ER-2020; problems include absent pharmacists and sale without prescription.
- Responsibilities: dispensing, counselling, OTC advice, screening, health promotion, pharmacovigilance, legal records, AMR prevention, disposal.
- GPP: FIP 1993, FIP/WHO 2011; four roles – supply, medication therapy management, professional performance, public health.
- SOP: written, approved, step-by-step instruction; parts – purpose, scope, responsibility, procedure, records, approval, review.
