# Unit 2: Prescription and prescription handling

This unit covers syllabus chapter 3: what a prescription is, its parts, when it is legally valid, how the pharmacist handles it, how dispensed medicines are labelled (main label, ancillary labels, pictograms), the dispensing process, Good Dispensing Practices, dispensing errors and how to prevent them.

## 2.1 Definition of prescription

A **prescription** is a written (or electronic) order from a registered medical practitioner (RMP), dentist or veterinarian to a pharmacist to supply a particular medicine, in a stated dose and quantity, to a named patient, with directions for use.

The word comes from Latin *prae* (before) and *scribere* (to write). The symbol **Rx** is read as "recipe" – "take thou".

- **Prescriber:** the RMP, dentist or veterinary surgeon who writes it.
- **Dispenser:** the registered pharmacist who reads, checks and supplies it.

## 2.2 Parts of a prescription

```text
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Dr A. Sharma, MBBS, MD          Reg. No. 12345 │  1. Prescriber details
│ City Clinic, Main Road, Shimla  Ph: ………       │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Date: 12/06/20xx                               │  2. Date
│ Name: Ram Kumar   Age: 54 y   Sex: M           │  3. Patient details
│ Address: ………                                   │
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Rx                                             │  4. Superscription
│ Tab. Amlodipine 5 mg                           │  5. Inscription
│ Dispense 30 tablets                            │  6. Subscription
│ Sig: one tablet once daily in the morning      │  7. Signa (transcription)
├───────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Refill: nil                                    │  8. Renewal instructions
│                       (signature) Dr A. Sharma │  9. Signature
└───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Part | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Prescriber details | Name, qualification, registration number, address, phone |
| Date | Date of writing; helps check validity and stops misuse |
| Patient details | Name, age (very important for children and elderly), sex, address, weight for children |
| Superscription | The symbol Rx |
| Inscription | Main part: name of medicine, dosage form and strength |
| Subscription | Directions to the pharmacist: how much to dispense or how to prepare |
| Signa / transcription | Directions to the patient (written on the label) – dose, frequency, route, timing, duration |
| Renewal / refill | Whether and how many times it may be repeated |
| Signature | Prescriber's signature; essential for legality |

### Common Latin abbreviations

| Abbreviation | Meaning | Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a.c. | before meals | p.c. | after meals |
| o.d. | once a day | b.d. / b.i.d. | twice a day |
| t.d.s. / t.i.d. | three times a day | q.i.d. / q.d.s. | four times a day |
| h.s. | at bedtime | stat | immediately |
| s.o.s. | if necessary (once) | p.r.n. | when required |
| q.4h | every 4 hours | Tab. / Cap. | tablet / capsule |
| Syr. | syrup | Inj. | injection |
| gtt. | drops | Ung. | ointment |
| p.o. | by mouth | i.m. / i.v. / s.c. | intramuscular / intravenous / subcutaneous |
| aq. | water | mitte | send (quantity) |

Abbreviations can be misread (for example "od" taken as "right eye", "u" for units read as zero). Safe practice is to write words in full; the pharmacist should confirm any doubtful abbreviation.

## 2.3 Legality of prescriptions

A prescription is legally valid when:

1. It is written by a **registered medical practitioner** (or dentist or veterinarian for their fields) and carries the prescriber's name, address and registration number.
2. It is **signed and dated** by the prescriber.
3. It carries the **name and address of the patient** (and of the owner for animals).
4. The medicine, strength, dose and total quantity are stated clearly.
5. It is used within a reasonable time and has not already been fully dispensed.

### Special legal points in India (Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945)

| Category | Prescription requirement | Records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Schedule H drugs | Sold by retail only on prescription of an RMP; label carries the Rx symbol and warning | Sale record (cash memo / bill) with patient and prescriber names |
| Schedule H1 drugs (some antibiotics, anti-TB drugs, habit-forming drugs) | Only on prescription; Rx in red with a boxed warning on the label | Separate H1 register: patient and prescriber name and address, drug and quantity; kept for 3 years and open to inspection |
| Schedule X drugs (strong habit-forming and psychotropic drugs) | Prescription in duplicate; one copy kept by the pharmacy; label marked XRx | Copy of prescription kept 2 years; special register; stored under lock and key |
| Narcotic drugs | Also controlled by the NDPS Act, 1985 and its rules | Special records under NDPS rules |

When a Schedule H, H1 or X drug is supplied, the prescription is stamped with the name and address of the pharmacy and the date, so that it cannot be used again.

A pharmacist may **refuse** to dispense a prescription that looks forged, altered, unsigned, illegible or dangerous, and should contact the prescriber.

## 2.4 Prescription handling

```text
Receive → Read and check legality → Check completeness → Interpret
   → Clinical check (dose, interactions, allergy, duplication) → Clarify doubts with prescriber
   → Select and prepare medicine → Label → Final check → Hand over with counselling
   → Record and file
```

1. **Receive the prescription** politely; confirm patient identity and whether the medicine is for the person present.
2. **Read the whole prescription** carefully; never guess an unclear word.
3. **Check legality:** prescriber's details, signature, date, patient details.
4. **Check completeness:** drug, strength, dosage form, dose, frequency, duration, quantity.
5. **Check clinically:** dose suitable for age and weight; drug–drug interactions; allergy; pregnancy or breast-feeding; duplicate therapy; contraindications.
6. **Clarify problems** with the prescriber by phone; record what was agreed. Do not change the prescription on your own.
7. **Prepare:** select the right product, check name, strength, batch and expiry; count or measure accurately.
8. **Label** each container.
9. **Final check** of drug, strength, quantity and label against the prescription, preferably by a second person.
10. **Hand over and counsel** the patient.
11. **Record:** enter in registers and the patient record; stamp scheduled-drug prescriptions; return the prescription or keep a copy as required.

## 2.5 Labelling of dispensed medications

A label tells the patient how to use the medicine safely after leaving the pharmacy.

### Main (primary) label – contents

1. Name and address of the pharmacy.
2. Name of the patient.
3. Name and strength of the medicine (and dosage form).
4. Directions for use in simple words: dose, frequency, timing, route.
5. Quantity dispensed.
6. Date of dispensing and prescription / serial number.
7. Name of prescriber.
8. Batch number and expiry date (on the pack or label).
9. Special storage instruction where needed.
10. "Keep out of reach of children."

Example:

```text
┌────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ ABC Pharmacy, Mall Road, Shimla          │
│ Rx No. 2345             Date: 12/06/20xx │
│ Ram Kumar                                │
│ AMLODIPINE 5 mg TABLETS        Qty: 30   │
│ Take ONE tablet ONCE a day in the        │
│ MORNING by mouth.                        │
│ Dr A. Sharma                             │
│ Keep out of reach of children.           │
└────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Ancillary (auxiliary) labels

Small extra labels (often coloured) that give an important warning or instruction.

| Ancillary label | Typical use |
| --- | --- |
| Shake well before use | Suspensions, emulsions, lotions |
| For external use only / Not to be taken by mouth | Lotions, liniments, antiseptics |
| May cause drowsiness – do not drive or use machines | Sedating antihistamines, benzodiazepines |
| Avoid alcohol | Metronidazole, sedatives |
| Take with food / after meals | NSAIDs, metformin |
| Take on an empty stomach | Some antibiotics, levothyroxine |
| Swallow whole – do not crush or chew | Enteric-coated and sustained-release tablets |
| Complete the full course | Antibiotics, anti-TB drugs |
| Store in a refrigerator (2–8 °C), do not freeze | Insulin, some eye drops and vaccines |
| Avoid exposure to sunlight | Tetracyclines, some fluoroquinolones |
| Use within … days of opening | Eye drops, reconstituted antibiotics |

### Pictograms

**Pictograms** are simple standard pictures that show how to take a medicine – for example a sun for morning, a moon for night, a plate for "with food", a crossed-out glass of alcohol, a drop falling into an eye. Sets of pictograms have been published by the United States Pharmacopeia and FIP.

Uses: help patients who cannot read or do not know the language, children and the elderly; improve understanding and adherence. The pharmacist should explain each pictogram, because a picture alone can be misunderstood.

```text
 Morning   Afternoon   Night
  (sun)     (sun high)  (moon)
   [1]         [0]       [1]      → one tablet morning and night
```

### Brief instructions on medication usage

| Dosage form | Key instructions |
| --- | --- |
| Tablets / capsules | Swallow with a glass of water, sitting or standing; do not crush modified-release forms |
| Sublingual tablet | Place under the tongue; do not swallow |
| Oral liquids | Shake if needed; measure with the cup, spoon or syringe provided, not a kitchen spoon |
| Eye drops | Wash hands, tilt head, pull lower lid, instil, close eye and press inner corner for a minute; do not touch the tip |
| Ear drops | Warm to body temperature in the hand; tilt head; pull ear back (up and back in adults) |
| Nasal spray | Blow nose, tilt head slightly forward, spray away from the septum |
| Inhaler | Shake, breathe out, seal lips, press and breathe in slowly, hold breath about 10 seconds; rinse mouth after steroid inhalers |
| Suppository | Moisten, insert pointed end first while lying on the side |
| Transdermal patch | Apply to clean, dry, hairless skin; rotate sites |

## 2.6 Dispensing process

**Dispensing** is the preparation and supply of a medicine to a patient on a prescription, with the information needed for its correct use. It is the most visible job of the community pharmacist.

```text
1 Receive and validate  →  2 Interpret  →  3 Prepare and select
        →  4 Label  →  5 Final check  →  6 Issue with counselling  →  7 Record
```

## 2.7 Good Dispensing Practices (GDP)

Good Dispensing Practice means the right patient gets the right drug, in the right dose and quantity, with clear instructions and suitable packaging, every time.

| Area | Good practice |
| --- | --- |
| Dispensing area | Clean, uncluttered, well lit, free from noise; separate counselling space |
| Staff | Registered pharmacist supervises; trained helpers; clean dress and name badge |
| Stock | Arranged so that similar-looking items are kept apart; FEFO; regular expiry checks |
| Process | Follow SOPs; one prescription at a time; never dispense from memory |
| Counting and measuring | Use counting trays and calibrated measures; avoid touching tablets with hands |
| Packaging | Clean, suitable containers; light-resistant for light-sensitive drugs; child-resistant where possible |
| Labelling | Clear, typed labels in simple language and the local language where possible |
| Checking | Final check, ideally by a second person (double check) |
| Counselling | Every patient, especially new medicines |
| Records | Accurate dispensing records and registers |

## 2.8 Dispensing errors

A **dispensing error** is any difference between the medicine supplied and what the prescription intended, or any failure in labelling or information, whether or not it harms the patient.

### Types

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Wrong drug | Look-alike sound-alike names (for example chlorpromazine and chlorpropamide) |
| Wrong strength | 5 mg instead of 2.5 mg |
| Wrong dosage form | Sustained-release instead of plain tablet |
| Wrong dose or directions on label | "three times daily" written for "once daily" |
| Wrong quantity | Fewer or more tablets than ordered |
| Wrong patient | Bags exchanged between two patients |
| Expired or damaged medicine | Supplying a product past expiry |
| Omission | One item of the prescription left out |
| Missing counselling or warning label | No "shake well" on a suspension |

### Causes

- Illegible handwriting and unclear abbreviations.
- Look-alike sound-alike (LASA) drug names and similar packaging.
- Heavy workload, interruptions, fatigue and hurry.
- Poor shelf arrangement and lighting.
- Lack of knowledge or training; no SOPs.
- Not checking patient identity.
- Calculation mistakes (children's doses, dilutions).

### Strategies to minimise dispensing errors

1. Ask for clarification of any unclear prescription; never guess.
2. Store LASA drugs apart and use "tall man" lettering on shelf labels (for example chlorproMAZINE, chlorproPAMIDE).
3. Read the label three times – when picking the product, when preparing and when returning it to the shelf.
4. Use a final independent double check.
5. Confirm patient identity with two details (name and address or age).
6. Use computer-printed labels and barcode scanning where available.
7. Limit interruptions; keep a calm, well-lit dispensing area.
8. Counsel every patient – counselling often catches errors before harm.
9. Keep up-to-date references and drug-interaction software.
10. Record and review every error or near-miss, without blame, and change the system to stop a repeat.

## Remember

- Prescription = order from an RMP to a pharmacist; parts: prescriber details, date, patient details, superscription (Rx), inscription, subscription, signa, refill, signature.
- Legal: RMP's signature, date, registration number, patient name and address; Schedule H1 register kept 3 years; Schedule X duplicate prescription copy kept 2 years.
- Handling: receive → check legality and completeness → clinical check → clarify → prepare → label → final check → counsel → record.
- Label: pharmacy name, patient, drug and strength, directions, quantity, date, prescriber, "keep out of reach of children"; ancillary labels add warnings; pictograms help people with low literacy.
- Dispensing errors: wrong drug, strength, form, dose, quantity, patient; prevent by LASA separation, tall-man lettering, double check, counselling, no-blame error reporting.
