# Unit 5: Medication adherence and health screening services

This unit joins two short syllabus chapters: chapter 6, **medication adherence** (definition, factors behind non-adherence, strategies to overcome it), and chapter 7, **health screening services in community pharmacy** (introduction, scope, importance for routine monitoring, early detection and referral).

## Part A: Medication adherence

## 5.1 Definitions

**Medication adherence** is the extent to which a patient's behaviour – taking medicines, following a diet or making lifestyle changes – matches the recommendations agreed with the health care provider (WHO, 2003).

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Adherence | Patient actively agrees with and follows the plan (preferred word) |
| Compliance | Older word; suggests the patient simply obeys orders |
| Concordance | Agreement reached between patient and prescriber after discussion |
| Persistence | Continuing the treatment for the full prescribed time |
| Non-adherence | Not taking medicines as agreed |

Non-adherence is very common in long-term diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, asthma, epilepsy, TB and HIV. It leads to treatment failure, complications, hospital admission, drug resistance (antibiotics, anti-TB drugs, ART), wasted medicines and money, and wrong dose increases by doctors who think the drug is not working.

## 5.2 Types of non-adherence

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Unintentional** (patient wants to take but cannot) | Forgets, does not understand the instructions, cannot open the pack, cannot use the inhaler, cannot afford, cannot read the label |
| **Intentional** (patient decides not to take) | Feels better and stops; fears side effects; believes medicine is not needed or is harmful; prefers home remedies |

Patterns seen: not collecting the prescription at all (primary non-adherence), missing doses, taking extra doses, wrong timing, "drug holidays", stopping early, and "white-coat adherence" (taking medicines properly only just before a clinic visit).

## 5.3 Factors influencing non-adherence

WHO groups the causes into five dimensions:

| Dimension | Factors |
| --- | --- |
| Social and economic | Poverty, cost of medicines, illiteracy, distance from pharmacy, lack of family support, unemployment |
| Health-care system | Short consultations, poor doctor–patient relationship, no counselling, medicines not available, no follow-up |
| Condition-related | No symptoms (hypertension, high cholesterol), chronic disease, depression, memory loss |
| Therapy-related | Many medicines (polypharmacy), several doses a day, long duration, side effects, difficult devices (injections, inhalers), slow benefit |
| Patient-related | Forgetfulness, poor knowledge, wrong beliefs, fear of dependence, low motivation, alcohol or drug misuse, hearing or vision problems |

### Measuring adherence (brief)

- **Direct:** drug or marker level in blood or urine; directly observed therapy (as in TB).
- **Indirect:** asking the patient (self-report questionnaires such as the Morisky scale), pill count, refill records, electronic pill-bottle caps, checking clinical outcome (BP, blood glucose).

## 5.4 Strategies to overcome non-adherence

| Strategy | How |
| --- | --- |
| Counselling and education | Explain the disease and why each medicine matters; check understanding (teach-back) |
| Simplify the regimen | Once-daily or long-acting products; fixed-dose combinations; fewer medicines (with the prescriber) |
| Reminders | Mobile alarms and apps, SMS refill reminders, calendars, linking doses to daily habits (brushing, meals) |
| Adherence aids | Pill organisers (weekly boxes), blister/dose packs, medication charts |
| Manage side effects | Explain which are minor and temporary; suggest timing changes; refer if serious |
| Address cost | Generic medicines, Jan Aushadhi Kendras, government schemes (free TB and ART medicines) |
| Device training | Demonstrate and recheck inhaler, insulin pen, eye drop technique |
| Family involvement | Carer supervises doses for elderly or children |
| Follow-up | Refill tracking, phone calls, regular BP/glucose checks to show progress |
| Motivational talk | Respect beliefs, set small goals, praise progress, avoid blaming |
| Directly observed therapy | Used in TB programmes |
| Clear labels and pictograms | Large print, local language, pictures |

**Role of the community pharmacist:** the pharmacist sees the patient at every refill, so is best placed to notice late refills, ask about missed doses, find the reason and act.

## Part B: Health screening services

## 5.5 Introduction

**Screening** is testing apparently healthy people to find a disease or risk factor early, before symptoms appear, so that treatment can start early. **Monitoring** is regular testing of a patient already diagnosed, to check control.

Community pharmacies are suitable for screening because they are easy to reach, open long hours, need no appointment and are visited by both healthy and sick people.

## 5.6 Scope – screening services that a pharmacy can offer

| Service | Equipment | Detects / monitors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | Validated digital or aneroid sphygmomanometer | Hypertension, BP control |
| Capillary blood glucose | Glucometer, test strips, lancets | Diabetes, glucose control |
| Body Mass Index and waist circumference | Weighing scale, height scale (stadiometer), measuring tape | Overweight, obesity, under-nutrition |
| Peak expiratory flow | Peak flow meter | Asthma control |
| Pulse oximetry | Pulse oximeter | Low oxygen saturation |
| Cholesterol (point-of-care) | Cholesterol meter | Hyperlipidaemia |
| Body temperature | Digital thermometer | Fever |
| Others | Pregnancy test kits, HbA1c point-of-care tests, simple eye and hearing checks, smoking-status assessment | Various |

### Typical reference values used for referral (adults)

| Test | Normal / target | Refer when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blood pressure | Below 120/80 mmHg is normal | 140/90 mmHg or more on repeated readings (sooner if very high, e.g. 180/110 or above, or with symptoms) |
| Fasting plasma glucose | Below 100 mg/dL | 100–125 mg/dL (prediabetes) or 126 mg/dL or more |
| Random glucose | – | 200 mg/dL or more, especially with symptoms |
| HbA1c | Below 5.7% | 5.7–6.4% prediabetes; 6.5% or more suggests diabetes |
| BMI (Asian Indian cut-offs) | 18.5–22.9 kg/m² | 23–24.9 overweight; 25 or more obese; below 18.5 underweight |
| Waist circumference (Indian adults) | Men below 90 cm, women below 80 cm | At or above these values (central obesity) |
| SpO2 (pulse oximetry) | 95–100% | Below 95% needs attention; below 90% is urgent |
| Peak flow | 80% or more of personal best or predicted | 50–80% (warning zone), below 50% (danger – urgent care) |
| Total cholesterol | Below 200 mg/dL desirable | 200 mg/dL or more |

**BMI = weight (kg) ÷ [height (m)]²**. Example: 70 kg, 1.65 m → 70 ÷ 2.72 = 25.7 kg/m² → obese by Asian cut-offs.

A single reading in a pharmacy is a **screen, not a diagnosis**. The pharmacist must never label a patient as diabetic or hypertensive – only advise referral to a doctor for confirmation.

## 5.7 Importance of health screening in pharmacies

### a) Routine monitoring of patients

- Shows whether treatment is working (BP, glucose, peak flow targets).
- Detects side effects early (for example low glucose with sulfonylureas).
- Encourages adherence – patients see their own numbers improve.
- Results can be recorded and shown to the doctor at the next visit.

### b) Early detection

- Many diseases (hypertension, type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol) have no symptoms for years.
- Early diagnosis prevents complications such as stroke, heart attack, kidney failure, blindness and amputation.
- Reaches people who rarely visit doctors.

### c) Referral of undiagnosed cases

- Abnormal results → clear written referral to a doctor with the reading, date and time.
- Urgent referral for dangerous values or symptoms (chest pain, very high BP, very low SpO2, confusion).
- Links the pharmacy with the health system, including national programmes for non-communicable diseases.

## 5.8 Running a screening service properly

1. **Space:** private area with a chair and table; clean and well lit.
2. **Equipment:** validated devices, calibrated and maintained as per the maker's instructions; spare batteries; correct cuff sizes.
3. **SOPs:** a written method for each test; staff trained and assessed.
4. **Consent:** explain the test and get the patient's agreement.
5. **Infection control:** single-use lancets, gloves, hand hygiene, sharps container, biomedical waste disposal as per rules.
6. **Correct technique:** for example rest 5 minutes before BP, arm at heart level; wipe away the first drop if alcohol was used before glucose testing.
7. **Interpretation and advice:** explain results simply; lifestyle advice; refer when needed.
8. **Records:** date, time, reading, advice given and referral; give the patient a copy.
9. **Confidentiality:** results are private health information.
10. **Quality control:** control solutions for glucometers; regular checking of devices.

## Remember

- Adherence (WHO 2003) = behaviour matches agreed recommendations; better word than "compliance".
- Non-adherence: unintentional (forgetting, not understanding) or intentional (fear, feeling better).
- WHO five factors: social/economic, health system, condition, therapy, patient.
- Fix with counselling, simple regimens, FDCs, reminders, pill boxes, cheaper generics, follow-up, family support, DOT.
- Screening finds hidden disease; monitoring checks control; pharmacy screening is a screen, not a diagnosis → refer.
- Key cut-offs: BP 140/90; fasting glucose 126 mg/dL; HbA1c 6.5%; BMI 23 and 25 (Asian); SpO2 95%; peak flow 80% and 50% zones.
