# Unit 6: OTC medications, self-medication and responding to symptoms

Syllabus chapter 9 (Over The Counter medications, 15 hours) is split into two units in this guide. This unit covers the definition and need of OTC medicines, the pharmacist's role, OTC medicines in India and their counselling, self-medication, and the general method of responding to symptoms. Unit 7 covers advice for each minor ailment.

## 6.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **OTC medicine** (non-prescription medicine) | A medicine considered safe and effective for use by the public without a prescription, for conditions people can recognise themselves, when used as directed on the label |
| **Prescription medicine** | A medicine that may be supplied only on the order of a registered medical practitioner |
| **Self-medication** | Use of medicines by people to treat self-recognised illness or symptoms without consulting a doctor |
| **Self-care** | What people do for themselves to stay healthy and manage minor illness (diet, rest, hygiene, home remedies, OTC medicines) |
| **Responsible self-medication** | Self-medication with approved, safe, quality OTC medicines, with correct information, for minor conditions, usually with a pharmacist's advice |
| **Minor ailment** | A short, self-limiting, non-serious condition that can be managed safely without a doctor (common cold, mild headache, simple diarrhoea) |
| **Responding to symptoms** | The pharmacist's process of assessing a patient's symptoms and deciding whether to advise, supply an OTC product, or refer to a doctor |

### Features of a good OTC medicine

- Wide margin of safety; low risk of misuse or dependence.
- Used for symptoms people can recognise easily.
- Little need for a doctor's diagnosis or monitoring.
- Clear, simple label directions.
- Few serious interactions or side effects.

## 6.2 Need for OTC medicines

- People can get quick relief for minor problems without waiting for a doctor.
- Saves time and money for patients and the health system.
- Doctors' time is kept for serious illness.
- Useful in remote areas with few doctors.
- Increases people's responsibility for their own health.

**Risks** if not handled well: wrong self-diagnosis, delay in treatment of serious disease, wrong dose, interactions, masking symptoms, misuse (laxatives, codeine cough syrups), allergic reactions, antibiotic misuse and resistance.

## 6.3 OTC medications in India

- Indian law does **not** have a separate legal schedule called "OTC". The Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945 list drugs that need a prescription (**Schedule H, H1 and X**). Drugs not in these lists can in practice be sold without a prescription, but still only from a licensed pharmacy (unless specially exempted).
- **Schedule K** lists certain classes of drugs that are exempted from some provisions; it includes household remedies that may be sold by ordinary shops in villages where there is no licensed chemist, under conditions.
- A formal OTC policy and list has been discussed by the regulators for several years.
- Many products are sold freely as "general sale" items (for example antacids, ORS, some analgesics, cough lozenges, antiseptic creams). Some ayurvedic products are sold as OTC.
- Problem: prescription drugs, especially antibiotics, are often sold without a prescription in India, which is illegal and adds to antimicrobial resistance.

### Common OTC categories and examples

| Category | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Analgesics / antipyretics | Paracetamol, low-dose ibuprofen, topical diclofenac gel |
| Antacids and anti-flatulents | Aluminium hydroxide + magnesium hydroxide, simethicone |
| Oral rehydration and antidiarrhoeal | ORS, zinc tablets for children |
| Laxatives | Ispaghula husk, lactulose |
| Cough and cold | Lozenges, steam inhalation products, saline nasal drops, cetirizine |
| Antiseptics | Povidone-iodine, chlorhexidine, cetrimide creams and solutions |
| Skin products | Calamine lotion, clotrimazole cream, moisturisers, sunscreens |
| Vitamins and minerals | Multivitamins, iron and folic acid, calcium |
| Oral care | Medicated toothpastes, mouthwashes, choline salicylate gel |
| Others | Nicotine replacement gum, contraceptive condoms |

## 6.4 Role of the pharmacist in OTC dispensing

1. **Assess** the person and symptoms (WWHAM / ASMETHOD) before selecting a product.
2. **Decide**: advice only, OTC product, or referral to a doctor.
3. **Select** a suitable, evidence-based product – right drug, dose and dosage form for age, pregnancy, other diseases and medicines.
4. **Counsel** on correct use (see 6.5).
5. **Warn** about danger signs and when to see a doctor.
6. **Avoid misuse** – refuse or limit supply of products likely to be abused (laxatives, codeine cough syrups, sleep aids) and never sell prescription drugs without a prescription.
7. **Record** supply of significant products and monitor repeat purchases.
8. **Educate** on non-drug measures and healthy habits.
9. **Report** adverse reactions to OTC products.

## 6.5 Counselling for OTC products

Every OTC supply should include:

| Point | Example (paracetamol 500 mg tablets for fever) |
| --- | --- |
| What it is for | Lowers fever and relieves pain |
| Dose and frequency | 1–2 tablets every 4–6 hours as needed |
| Maximum dose | Do not exceed the daily maximum on the pack (for adults usually 4 g a day; lower for heavy drinkers or liver disease) |
| Duration | Do not use for fever for more than about 3 days without seeing a doctor |
| Duplication | Many cold and pain products also contain paracetamol – do not take two together |
| Side effects / cautions | Liver damage in overdose; avoid alcohol |
| When to see a doctor | Fever not settling, rash, stiff neck, breathing difficulty, fits |
| Storage | Cool, dry place, away from children |

Special groups need extra care: babies and young children, the elderly, pregnant and breast-feeding women, patients with kidney or liver disease, and people already taking many medicines.

## 6.6 Self-medication

### Reasons people self-medicate

- Minor illness that people think is not worth a doctor's visit.
- Cost and time of consultation; distance from a doctor.
- Previous experience with the same illness or medicine.
- Easy availability of medicines and advice from family or media.
- Advertising of OTC products.

### Advantages

- Quick relief; convenient.
- Saves money and doctor time.
- Encourages people to take part in their own health.

### Disadvantages / risks

- Wrong diagnosis; serious disease hidden or delayed (for example chest pain taken as "gas").
- Wrong choice or dose of medicine.
- Adverse effects and interactions.
- Drug dependence and misuse.
- Antibiotic misuse → antimicrobial resistance.
- Use of old prescriptions or leftover medicines.

### Role of pharmacists in promoting safe self-medication

1. Be available and approachable at the counter.
2. Use a structured approach (WWHAM / ASMETHOD) for every symptom request.
3. Supply only suitable OTC medicines; never supply prescription-only medicines (especially antibiotics and steroids) without a prescription.
4. Give clear advice on dose, duration, side effects and when to stop.
5. Tell the patient the "red flag" signs that need a doctor.
6. Check for interactions with existing medicines.
7. Discourage the use of leftover medicines and sharing of medicines.
8. Promote non-drug measures (fluids, rest, diet, hygiene).
9. Educate the public on reading labels and expiry dates.
10. Keep records and follow up where possible.

## 6.7 Responding to symptoms – the general method

```text
Patient asks for advice or a product
          │
          ▼
Gather information (WWHAM / ASMETHOD)
          │
          ▼
Any danger (red-flag) features? ── yes ──► Refer to doctor (urgently if needed)
          │ no
          ▼
Minor, self-limiting condition?
          │ yes
          ▼
Advice ± suitable OTC product + counselling
          │
          ▼
Safety net: "If not better in X days or if Y happens, see a doctor"
```

### Reasons to refer (general red flags)

- Symptoms that are severe, unusual or getting worse.
- Symptoms lasting longer than a set time (for example cough more than 2–3 weeks, diarrhoea more than 2–3 days in adults, fever more than 3 days).
- Very young babies, frail elderly, pregnancy (for many products).
- Blood in sputum, vomit, stool or urine; unexplained weight loss.
- Chest pain, breathlessness, confusion, fits, severe headache with stiff neck.
- Signs of dehydration (very little urine, sunken eyes, drowsiness).
- Treatment already tried has failed.
- Suspected adverse drug reaction.
- Patient has a serious long-term disease (diabetes, heart, kidney or liver disease) that may change the choice.

### Communicating the outcome

- Explain the likely cause in simple words.
- Agree on the plan; give written advice if possible.
- For referral, say clearly why and how soon the patient should see a doctor; give a referral note if useful.

## Remember

- OTC = safe without prescription for self-recognised conditions; India has no legal OTC schedule – prescription drugs are Schedule H, H1, X; Schedule K covers exemptions including household remedies.
- Pharmacist's OTC role: assess (WWHAM/ASMETHOD) → decide (advise / supply / refer) → select → counsel → safety-net → record.
- Counsel on use, dose, maximum dose, duration, duplication (paracetamol), side effects and red flags.
- Self-medication: quick and cheap but risks wrong diagnosis, misuse and antibiotic resistance; pharmacist promotes **responsible** self-medication.
- Refer when symptoms are severe, prolonged, unusual, in high-risk groups, or show red-flag features.
