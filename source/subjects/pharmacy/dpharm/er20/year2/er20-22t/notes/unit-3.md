# Unit 3: Communication skills

This unit covers syllabus chapter 4: what communication is, its types, how a pharmacist talks with other professionals and with patients, verbal communication (face to face and on the telephone), written communication, body language and patient interview techniques.

## 3.1 Definition and process of communication

**Communication** is the process of sending and receiving information, ideas and feelings between two or more people so that they reach a shared understanding. In a pharmacy, good communication decides whether the patient leaves knowing how to use the medicine safely.

**Communication skills** are the abilities that make this exchange clear and effective: speaking, listening, writing, questioning, observing body language and showing empathy.

### The communication process

```text
 Sender ──► encodes message ──► channel (speech, writing, gesture)
   ▲                                       │
   │                                       ▼
 Feedback ◄── decodes and responds ◄── Receiver
            (noise / barriers can distort the message at any stage)
```

| Element | Meaning | Pharmacy example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sender | Person who starts the message | Pharmacist |
| Message | Information or idea | "Take this tablet after breakfast" |
| Channel | Medium used | Spoken words, label, leaflet, phone |
| Receiver | Person who gets it | Patient |
| Feedback | Response showing understanding | Patient repeats the instructions |
| Noise | Anything that disturbs | Crowd, poor hearing, jargon |

## 3.2 Types of communication

| Type | Description | Examples in pharmacy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbal (oral) | Spoken words | Counselling, phone call to doctor |
| Non-verbal | Body language, facial expression, tone, touch, space | Smile, eye contact, nodding |
| Written | Words on paper or screen | Labels, leaflets, notes to prescriber, records |
| Visual | Pictures, charts, demonstrations | Pictograms, inhaler demonstration, posters |
| Electronic | Email, SMS, apps, video calls | Refill reminders, teleconsultation |

Other ways of classifying:

- **Formal vs informal** – an official letter to a doctor vs friendly talk with a regular customer.
- **One-way vs two-way** – a poster gives information only; counselling allows questions and feedback.
- **Interpersonal, group and mass** – one patient; a diabetes group talk; a public health campaign.

## 3.3 Interactions with professionals and patients

### With other health professionals (doctors, nurses, other pharmacists)

- Be polite, brief and specific; identify yourself and the patient.
- State the problem and suggest an option, for example: "The prescription has warfarin and aspirin together; would you like to change to paracetamol for pain?"
- Use correct medical terms; have the facts (dose, patient details) ready.
- Never criticise the prescriber in front of the patient.
- Record the outcome (what was agreed, with whom, time).
- The **SBAR** format helps: **S**ituation, **B**ackground, **A**ssessment, **R**ecommendation.

### With patients and carers

- Greet warmly, introduce yourself and use the patient's name.
- Use simple language and the patient's own language where possible; avoid jargon ("high blood pressure", not "hypertension").
- Listen more than you speak; show empathy.
- Check understanding (teach-back).
- Respect privacy and confidentiality; use the counselling area for sensitive topics.
- Adapt to special groups: children (talk to the child as well as the parent), elderly (speak slowly, check hearing and vision), people with disabilities, people who cannot read.

## 3.4 Verbal communication skills

### One-to-one (face to face)

Good verbal communication needs:

1. **Clarity** – short sentences, one idea at a time.
2. **Correct pace and volume** – not too fast or loud.
3. **Tone** – friendly and confident; tone carries feeling.
4. **Active listening** – full attention, nodding, short encouraging words ("I see", "go on"), no interruption.
5. **Paraphrasing and summarising** – "So the cough started three days ago and is worse at night?"
6. **Empathy** – show you understand the patient's feelings ("I can see this pain is troubling you").
7. **Questioning** – open and closed questions used well (see 3.7).

### Over the telephone

The other person cannot see you, so voice and words do all the work.

| Do | Do not |
| --- | --- |
| Answer promptly; greet and name the pharmacy and yourself | Keep the caller waiting without explanation |
| Speak clearly, slowly, and smile while talking (it is heard in the voice) | Eat or talk to others while on the phone |
| Confirm who is calling and identity before giving patient information | Give confidential details to an unknown caller |
| Spell drug names and repeat numbers (doses, strengths) back | Rely on memory; write notes |
| Summarise what was agreed and end politely | Hang up abruptly |
| Record the call (time, caller, advice given) | Give advice outside your competence – refer instead |

## 3.5 Written communication skills

Written messages last longer and can be read again, so they must be accurate.

**Forms in pharmacy:** labels, patient information leaflets, letters or referral notes to doctors, emails, records and registers, SOPs, notices and display material.

Principles (the "7 Cs"): clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete and courteous.

Tips for patient materials:

- Large font, short sentences, headings and bullet points.
- Everyday words; local language where possible.
- Pictures and pictograms for people with low literacy.
- Include what the medicine is for, how to take it, side effects, what to avoid, storage.
- Test the leaflet on a few patients before use.

**Referral note to a doctor** should contain: patient name and age, reason for referral, symptoms and duration, medicines taken, results of any screening (for example BP 170/104 mmHg), pharmacist's name, pharmacy and contact number, date.

## 3.6 Body language (non-verbal communication)

Much of what people understand comes from how a thing is said and shown, not only the words.

| Element | Positive | Negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eye contact | Regular, friendly | Avoiding the eyes, staring |
| Facial expression | Smile, interested look | Frown, bored look |
| Posture | Upright, leaning slightly forward, open arms | Slouching, crossed arms, turning away |
| Gestures | Nodding, open palms | Pointing finger, fidgeting, looking at the phone |
| Proximity (space) | Comfortable distance, at the same level | Too close, talking over a high counter |
| Touch | Light touch where culturally acceptable | Unwanted touch |
| Voice (paralanguage) | Calm, warm, varied tone | Harsh, monotonous, sarcastic |
| Appearance | Clean apron or coat, name badge | Untidy dress |

**SOLER** is a simple guide to attentive body language: **S**it (or stand) squarely facing the patient, **O**pen posture, **L**ean slightly forward, **E**ye contact, **R**elaxed manner.

The pharmacist should also **read the patient's body language** – a worried face, pain, confusion or hesitation shows that more explanation or privacy is needed.

## 3.7 Patient interview techniques

A **patient interview** is a planned conversation to collect information (symptoms, medical and medication history) and to give advice.

### Types of questions

| Type | Use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open-ended | Get the patient talking, more detail | "How are you taking these tablets?" |
| Closed | Specific facts, yes/no | "Are you allergic to penicillin?" |
| Probing | Explore further | "What do you mean by dizzy?" |
| Leading (avoid) | Suggests the answer | "You take it every day, don't you?" |
| Reflective | Shows listening | "You feel the tablets make you tired?" |

### Stages of an interview

1. **Opening:** greet, introduce yourself, explain the purpose and time needed, ensure privacy, get consent.
2. **Information gathering:** start with open questions, then closed ones; listen actively.
3. **Assessment:** sort out the problem; decide whether to treat, advise or refer.
4. **Advice / plan:** explain clearly; agree a plan with the patient.
5. **Closing:** summarise, check understanding, invite questions, explain when to come back or see a doctor.

### Mnemonics used when responding to symptoms

| Mnemonic | Stands for |
| --- | --- |
| **WWHAM** | **W**ho is the patient? **W**hat are the symptoms? **H**ow long have they had them? **A**ction already taken? **M**edicines being taken? |
| **ASMETHOD** | **A**ge / appearance, **S**elf or someone else, **M**edication, **E**xtra medicines, **T**ime persisting, **H**istory, **O**ther symptoms, **D**anger symptoms |

### Medication history interview

Ask about: current prescription medicines, OTC products, herbal and home remedies, allergies and past reactions, how the patient actually takes each medicine, missed doses, side effects, smoking and alcohol, pregnancy or breast-feeding.

### Good interview habits

- Sit or stand in a private, quiet place; give full attention.
- Use the patient's language; avoid medical jargon.
- Do not judge; respect beliefs.
- Take brief notes and tell the patient why.
- End by summarising and checking understanding (teach-back: "Can you tell me how you will take this?").

## 3.8 Barriers to good communication (overview)

- **Physical:** noise, crowd, high counters, no privacy.
- **Language and literacy:** different language, poor reading ability.
- **Sensory:** poor hearing or vision.
- **Emotional:** fear, anger, anxiety, embarrassment about the illness.
- **Cultural:** beliefs about illness and medicines.
- **Pharmacist factors:** lack of time, jargon, poor attitude.

These are dealt with in detail under patient counselling (Unit 4).

## Remember

- Communication = sender → message → channel → receiver → feedback; noise disturbs it.
- Types: verbal, non-verbal, written, visual, electronic.
- With professionals: brief, factual, polite, use SBAR, record the outcome; with patients: simple words, empathy, privacy, teach-back.
- Telephone: identify caller, spell names, repeat numbers back, record the call.
- Written: 7 Cs; patient leaflets in simple language with pictures.
- Body language: SOLER; eye contact, open posture, nodding; read the patient's signals too.
- Interview: open → closed questions, avoid leading questions; WWHAM and ASMETHOD for symptoms; close with a summary and teach-back.
