# Unit 4: Patient counselling

This unit covers syllabus chapter 5 (10 hours): definition and benefits of patient counselling, the stages of a counselling session, barriers and how to overcome them, counselling points for hypertension, diabetes, asthma, tuberculosis, COPD and HIV/AIDS, Patient Package Inserts (PPIs) and Patient Information Leaflets (PILs).

## 4.1 Definition

**Patient counselling** is a two-way, face-to-face discussion in which the pharmacist gives the patient (or carer) information about the illness, medicines and lifestyle, in a way the patient can understand, and checks that it has been understood, so that the patient uses the medicines safely and effectively.

It is not just "telling" – it is **listening, educating, motivating and checking understanding**.

## 4.2 Benefits of patient counselling

| To the patient | To the pharmacist and health system |
| --- | --- |
| Understands the illness and why the medicine is needed | Builds trust and professional image |
| Takes medicines correctly → better results | Detects and prevents drug-related problems and dispensing errors |
| Better adherence | Fewer hospital admissions and lower costs |
| Knows side effects and what to do about them | Patient loyalty to the pharmacy |
| Fewer adverse reactions and interactions | Legal and ethical duty fulfilled |
| Knows how to use devices (inhaler, insulin pen) | Data for pharmacovigilance |
| Takes part in decisions; less anxiety | Supports rational use of medicines |

## 4.3 Stages of patient counselling

```text
1. Introduction  →  2. Counselling content  →  3. Counselling process  →  4. Closing
 (prepare, greet,      (what to tell)           (how to tell: two-way,     (summary,
  identify, explain                              check understanding)       teach-back,
  purpose, privacy)                                                         follow-up)
```

### Stage 1: Introduction (opening the session)

- **Prepare:** read the prescription and patient record; know the drug, dose, side effects; have leaflets and demonstration devices ready.
- Greet the patient, introduce yourself and confirm the patient's identity.
- Explain the purpose and how long it will take ("I'd like to spend five minutes explaining your new medicines").
- Choose a private, quiet place; sit at the same level.
- Ask for the patient's consent and find out what the patient already knows.

### Stage 2: Counselling content (what to discuss)

1. Name of the medicine and what it is for.
2. Dose, how often, at what time, and for how long.
3. How to take it (with or without food, swallow whole, route, device technique).
4. What to expect – when it starts working, how the patient will know.
5. Common side effects and what to do; serious effects that need a doctor.
6. Precautions – foods, alcohol, driving, other medicines (including OTC and herbal) to avoid.
7. What to do if a dose is missed.
8. Storage (temperature, light, away from children) and disposal.
9. Refills and follow-up visits, monitoring tests.
10. Lifestyle advice related to the disease.

### Stage 3: Counselling process (how to discuss)

- Use **open-ended prime questions**, a well-known approach:
  1. "What did your doctor tell you the medicine is for?"
  2. "How did your doctor tell you to take it?"
  3. "What did your doctor tell you to expect?"
- Fill in only the gaps; do not repeat what the patient already knows.
- Use simple words, demonstrations and written material; show the actual product.
- Listen to worries and beliefs; respond with empathy.
- Adjust to the patient – elderly, children, low literacy, language.

### Stage 4: Closing the session

- **Summarise** the key points (no more than 3–4).
- **Verify understanding** by teach-back: "Can you show me how you will use this inhaler?"
- Invite final questions.
- Give written information and the pharmacy phone number.
- Plan follow-up (next refill, BP check).
- Document the counselling in the patient record.

## 4.4 Barriers to effective counselling

| Type | Barriers | Strategies to overcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Patient-related** | Language difference; low literacy; poor hearing or vision; memory problems; anxiety, anger, denial; cultural beliefs; lack of interest or time | Use the patient's language or an interpreter; pictograms and demonstration; speak slowly, face the patient, large-print material; involve a carer; empathy and reassurance; respect beliefs |
| **Pharmacist-related** | Lack of time; poor knowledge; poor communication skills; negative attitude; jargon | Plan and prioritise; training and updated references; practise skills; simple words; positive attitude |
| **Environment-related** | Noise, crowd, no privacy, high counter, interruptions | Separate counselling area or quiet corner; appointment for long sessions; reduce interruptions |
| **System / organisational** | Too few pharmacists; no payment for counselling; no patient records | Trained support staff for non-professional tasks; counselling as a standard service; patient medication records |
| **Emotional / sensitive topics** | Embarrassment (HIV, sexual health, mental illness) | Private area, non-judgemental attitude, confidentiality |

## 4.5 Counselling points for chronic diseases

### Hypertension

| Area | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Disease | High BP often has no symptoms but damages heart, brain, kidneys and eyes; treatment is usually lifelong |
| Medicines | Take every day at the same time, even when feeling well; do not stop suddenly (especially beta-blockers and clonidine) |
| Side effects | Amlodipine – ankle swelling, flushing; ACE inhibitors – dry cough; diuretics – more urine (take in the morning), low potassium; beta-blockers – tiredness, cold hands; dizziness on standing – rise slowly |
| Monitoring | Regular BP checks; home BP monitor technique; kidney function and potassium as advised |
| Lifestyle | Less salt; fruits and vegetables; regular exercise (about 30 minutes most days); weight control; stop smoking; limit alcohol; manage stress |
| Avoid | Self-medication with NSAIDs and decongestants that can raise BP; ask before taking OTC products |

### Diabetes mellitus

| Area | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Disease | High blood sugar; lifelong control prevents damage to eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and feet |
| Oral drugs | Metformin with or after meals (stomach upset common early); sulfonylureas (glimepiride, gliclazide) before or with breakfast – risk of low sugar if meals are missed |
| Insulin | Correct type and dose; inject subcutaneously into abdomen or thigh; rotate sites; pen technique (prime, dial, inject, hold about 10 seconds); store unopened insulin at 2–8 °C, do not freeze; in-use pen at room temperature for the period on the leaflet |
| Hypoglycaemia | Signs – sweating, shaking, hunger, confusion; take 15–20 g quick sugar (glucose, sugar, juice), recheck; carry sugar and an identity card |
| Monitoring | Home glucose meter; HbA1c every 3–6 months; eyes, kidneys, feet yearly |
| Lifestyle | Regular meals, diet low in sugar and refined starch, regular exercise, weight control, no smoking |
| Foot care | Inspect feet daily, wear proper footwear, treat cuts early |
| Sick days | Do not stop insulin; drink fluids; check sugar more often; see doctor if vomiting |

### Asthma

| Area | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Disease | Airway inflammation and narrowing; triggers – dust, smoke, pollen, cold air, exercise, infections |
| Reliever | Short-acting β2 agonist (salbutamol) – use during an attack; needing it often means poor control |
| Controller (preventer) | Inhaled corticosteroid (budesonide, beclometasone, fluticasone) – use every day even when well; rinse mouth and spit after use to prevent thrush and hoarseness |
| Inhaler technique | Shake; breathe out; seal lips; press and breathe in slowly and deeply; hold breath about 10 seconds; wait before a second puff; spacer for children and those with poor coordination; dry powder inhalers need a quick deep breath and should not be shaken or breathed into |
| Monitoring | Peak flow diary; written asthma action plan (green, yellow, red zones) |
| Emergency signs | Reliever not helping, cannot speak in sentences, blue lips – seek emergency care |
| Lifestyle | Avoid triggers and smoking; influenza vaccination as advised |
| Avoid | Some patients react to aspirin/NSAIDs and to non-selective beta-blockers – tell the pharmacist and doctor |

### Tuberculosis

| Area | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Disease | Bacterial infection, usually of the lungs, spread by coughing; curable with full treatment |
| Medicines | Fixed-dose combination of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol (intensive phase), then fewer drugs (continuation phase); free under the National TB Elimination Programme |
| Adherence | Take daily, on an empty stomach as advised, for the full course (at least 6 months for drug-sensitive TB); stopping early causes relapse and drug-resistant TB |
| Side effects | Orange-red urine, tears and sweat from rifampicin (harmless); report yellow eyes or skin, vomiting, abdominal pain (liver damage); tingling of feet (isoniazid – pyridoxine is given); blurred vision or colour changes (ethambutol); joint pain (pyrazinamide) |
| Interactions | Rifampicin reduces the effect of oral contraceptives and many other drugs – use another method of contraception |
| Infection control | Cover mouth when coughing, use a mask, good ventilation; household contacts to be screened |
| Other | Good nutrition; avoid alcohol; follow-up sputum tests; Ni-kshay registration and support |

### Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

| Area | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Disease | Long-term airflow limitation, mainly due to smoking and indoor smoke (chulha); breathlessness, cough, sputum; progressive |
| Most important step | Stop smoking – the only measure that slows decline; offer cessation support |
| Medicines | Bronchodilators – long-acting antimuscarinic (tiotropium) and long-acting β2 agonists; inhaled steroids in some; short-acting reliever |
| Device | Correct inhaler or nebuliser use; clean the nebuliser parts after use |
| Exacerbations | More breathlessness or change in sputum colour – see doctor early |
| Lifestyle | Breathing exercises and pulmonary rehabilitation; good nutrition; influenza and pneumococcal vaccination as advised; avoid smoke and dust |
| Oxygen | If prescribed, use as directed; no smoking or flames near oxygen |

### HIV / AIDS

| Area | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Disease | HIV attacks immune cells; AIDS is the late stage; treatment controls the virus but does not cure it |
| Medicines | Antiretroviral therapy (ART), often a once-daily fixed-dose combination, free at ART centres under the National AIDS Control Programme |
| Adherence | Very high adherence needed – missed doses allow resistance; same time daily; reminders |
| Side effects | Nausea, headache, sleep disturbances, rash; report serious rash, yellow eyes, severe tiredness |
| Interactions | Many – check with pharmacist before any new medicine, including herbal products |
| Prevention | Safe sex with condoms; no sharing of needles; prevention of mother-to-child transmission |
| Opportunistic infections | Take prophylaxis as prescribed (e.g. co-trimoxazole); report fever, cough, diarrhoea |
| Support | Confidentiality, non-judgemental attitude, nutrition, counselling and support groups |

## 4.6 Patient Package Inserts (PPIs)

### Definition

A **Patient Package Insert (PPI)** is printed information for the patient, placed inside (or attached to) the pack of a medicine by the manufacturer, written in plain language and approved by the drug regulator. It explains what the medicine is, how to use it safely and what to watch for.

It differs from the **package insert for doctors** (prescribing information), which is technical.

### Contents of a PPI

1. Name of the medicine (brand and generic) and strength.
2. What it is used for.
3. Who should not use it (contraindications) and precautions.
4. How to take it – dose, timing, duration, missed dose.
5. Possible side effects and what to do.
6. Interactions with other medicines, food and alcohol.
7. Use in pregnancy and breast-feeding.
8. Storage and disposal.
9. Manufacturer's name and date of revision.

### Importance and benefits

- Reinforces verbal counselling; patient can read it again at home.
- Improves knowledge, safety and adherence.
- Helps the patient recognise side effects early.
- Supports informed decisions and the patient's right to information.
- Gives the same standard information to every patient.

### Limitations

- Useless to people who cannot read or do not know the language.
- Long lists of side effects may frighten patients and reduce adherence.
- Strip-dispensing (cutting tablets from a strip) means patients often do not get the insert.

### Scenarios of PPI use in India and other countries

| Country / region | Situation |
| --- | --- |
| United States | FDA requires patient labelling for certain products (for example oral contraceptives and oestrogens) and "Medication Guides" for medicines with serious risks |
| European Union | A patient information leaflet must be supplied in the pack of every medicine, written so that patients can understand it |
| United Kingdom, Australia | Consumer leaflets in every pack; consumer medicine information widely available |
| India | Most packs carry prescribing information meant for doctors; patient-friendly leaflets are not required for most medicines; many are in English only; medicines are often dispensed as cut strips without any leaflet. Pharmacists can fill this gap with their own leaflets in local languages |

## 4.7 Patient Information Leaflets (PILs)

### Definition

A **Patient Information Leaflet** is a short written leaflet, prepared by a pharmacist, hospital, health organisation or manufacturer, that gives patients information about a disease, a medicine, a device or a healthy lifestyle in simple language.

(In some countries "PIL" is the name for the leaflet in the medicine pack; in this subject PIL means any patient leaflet, often made by the pharmacist.)

### Uses

- Support and remember counselling points.
- Educate on disease, diet and lifestyle (for example "Living with diabetes").
- Explain device use with pictures (inhaler, insulin pen, eye drops).
- Health promotion – smoking cessation, AMR, safe disposal.
- Reach carers and family members.
- Promote the pharmacy's services.

### How to prepare a good PIL

1. Decide the purpose and the target readers.
2. Use simple words, short sentences, large font and the local language.
3. Use headings, bullet points and pictures.
4. Give correct, up-to-date, evidence-based information.
5. Include: what it is, why it matters, what to do, what to avoid, when to see a doctor, contact details.
6. Keep it to one or two pages; test it with a few patients.
7. Review and update it regularly.

## Remember

- Counselling = two-way, face-to-face education with a check of understanding.
- Stages: introduction → content → process → closing (summary + teach-back + follow-up + documentation).
- Prime questions: what is it for, how to take it, what to expect.
- Barriers: patient, pharmacist, environment, system; overcome with privacy, simple language, pictures, interpreters, time planning and training.
- Key counselling points: hypertension (daily, lifestyle, salt), diabetes (hypoglycaemia, insulin technique, foot care), asthma (reliever vs preventer, inhaler technique, rinse mouth), TB (full course, rifampicin orange urine and contraceptive interaction), COPD (stop smoking, inhalers), AIDS (very high ART adherence, interactions, prevention).
- PPI = manufacturer's plain-language leaflet in the pack; required in EU, partly in USA; not generally required in India.
- PIL = simple leaflet on disease or medicine, often prepared by the pharmacist.
