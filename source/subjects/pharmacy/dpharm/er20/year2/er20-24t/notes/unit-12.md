# Unit 12: Ophthalmic disorders and antimicrobial resistance

This unit joins two short syllabus parts of 2 hours each: chapter 2(k) **ophthalmology** – conjunctivitis (bacterial and viral) and glaucoma – and chapter 2(l) **antimicrobial resistance (AMR)**. They are kept together because both are short; the conjunctivitis section is also a good example of where antibiotics are often used when they are not needed.

## Part A: Ophthalmology

## 12.1 Conjunctivitis

### Definition

**Conjunctivitis** ("pink eye", "eye flu") is **inflammation of the conjunctiva** – the thin transparent membrane covering the white of the eye and the inner surface of the eyelids – causing redness, discharge and discomfort. It may be **infective** (bacterial, viral) or **non-infective** (allergic, irritant).

### Etiopathogenesis

| Type | Causes | Spread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bacterial** | *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*; in newborns *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and *Chlamydia trachomatis* (ophthalmia neonatorum) | Contact with hands, towels, cosmetics |
| **Viral** (most common infective type in adults) | **Adenovirus** (epidemic keratoconjunctivitis), enterovirus (acute haemorrhagic conjunctivitis outbreaks), herpes simplex, varicella-zoster | Highly contagious – hands, towels, droplets, swimming pools; outbreaks in monsoon |
| Allergic | Pollen, dust, animal dander; vernal keratoconjunctivitis in children | Not contagious |

### Clinical manifestations

| Feature | Bacterial | Viral | Allergic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discharge | **Thick, purulent (yellow-green)**; lids stuck together in the morning | **Watery** | Watery, stringy |
| Itching | Mild | Mild | **Marked** |
| Eyes | Often one eye first, then both | Often starts in one eye, spreads to the other; recent cold or sore throat | Both eyes |
| Lymph node | Usually none | **Tender pre-auricular node** | None |
| Other | — | Grittiness, light sensitivity, may last 1–3 weeks | Sneezing, hay fever, seasonal |

**Red flags (refer to an eye specialist):** **reduced vision**, severe eye pain, marked light sensitivity, a cloudy cornea, unequal pupils, contact-lens wearer with red painful eye, newborn with discharge, injury or chemical splash – these may be keratitis, uveitis or acute glaucoma, not simple conjunctivitis.

### Management

**General / non-drug (all infective types):**
- **Hand washing**; do not touch or rub eyes; **separate towel, pillow and handkerchief**; avoid crowded places, school or work until discharge settles.
- Clean the eyelids with boiled-and-cooled water and clean cotton, wiping from inside to outside, one swab per wipe.
- Cold compresses for comfort; stop contact lenses until fully recovered and discard the old lenses/case; avoid eye make-up.
- **Do not use steroid eye drops without an eye specialist** – steroids can worsen herpes infection and cause glaucoma and cataract.

**Bacterial conjunctivitis:**
- Often settles in a week even without treatment; **topical antibiotics** shorten the illness: **moxifloxacin, ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, gatifloxacin, chloramphenicol, tobramycin** eye drops (ointment at night or for children), usually 5–7 days.
- **Ophthalmia neonatorum** – systemic treatment (for example ceftriaxone for gonococcal, oral azithromycin/erythromycin for chlamydial) + eye care; urgent.

**Viral conjunctivitis:**
- **No antibiotic is needed** – it is self-limiting; supportive care: cold compresses, **lubricating eye drops (artificial tears)**, paracetamol for discomfort, strict hygiene.
- **Herpes simplex** conjunctivitis/keratitis: topical **acyclovir** or ganciclovir gel under specialist care; no steroids.

**Allergic:** avoid the allergen, cold compresses, **antihistamine/mast-cell stabiliser drops** (olopatadine, ketotifen, sodium cromoglicate), oral antihistamines.

### Counselling on using eye drops

1. Wash hands; tilt head back; pull down the lower lid to make a pocket.
2. Instil **one drop**; do not let the dropper tip touch the eye or lashes.
3. Close the eye gently for 1–2 minutes and press the inner corner (**nasolacrimal occlusion**) – keeps the drug in the eye and reduces absorption into the body.
4. Wait **5 minutes between different drops**; apply ointment last.
5. **Discard opened eye drops after 4 weeks** (or as labelled); never share.

## 12.2 Glaucoma

### Definition

**Glaucoma** is a group of eye diseases characterised by **progressive damage to the optic nerve (optic neuropathy)** with **characteristic loss of the visual field**, usually associated with **raised intraocular pressure (IOP)**. Normal IOP is about **10–21 mmHg**. Lost vision **cannot be restored**, so early detection is vital – glaucoma is a leading cause of irreversible blindness ("silent thief of sight").

### Physiology and pathogenesis

```text
Aqueous humour made by ciliary body
        ↓
Flows through pupil into the anterior chamber
        ↓
Drains through trabecular meshwork → canal of Schlemm (main route)
          and uveoscleral route (second route)

Drainage blocked or reduced → aqueous builds up → ↑ IOP
        ↓
Pressure + poor blood supply damage optic nerve → cupping of the optic disc
        ↓
Peripheral vision lost first (tunnel vision) → blindness
```

### Types

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)** – most common | Angle is open but drainage through the meshwork is poor; **slow, painless**, no symptoms until late; gradual loss of side vision |
| **Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)** – **acute attack is an emergency** | Iris blocks the drainage angle (narrow angles, more common in Asians, hypermetropes, elderly women); **sudden severe eye pain, headache, red eye, blurred vision, haloes around lights, nausea and vomiting**, hazy cornea, mid-dilated fixed pupil |
| Normal-tension glaucoma | Optic nerve damage with IOP in normal range |
| **Secondary glaucoma** | Due to **long-term corticosteroids** (eye drops, oral, inhaled), uveitis, injury, cataract, diabetes (neovascular) |
| Congenital glaucoma | In infants – large, cloudy eyes, watering |

**Risk factors:** age above 40, family history, high myopia (open-angle) or hypermetropia (angle-closure), diabetes, hypertension, long-term steroid use, African or Asian descent.

**Drugs that can precipitate angle-closure** in people with narrow angles: **anticholinergics** (atropine, ipratropium nebulisation, TCAs), **antihistamines**, **adrenergic** drugs, topiramate, mydriatic eye drops.

**Diagnosis:** tonometry (IOP), gonioscopy (angle), ophthalmoscopy (optic disc cupping), perimetry (visual fields), OCT.

### Management

**Goal:** lower IOP to a "target" level that stops further optic nerve damage.

**Drugs for open-angle glaucoma (eye drops):**

| Group | Examples | How it lowers IOP | Adverse effects / cautions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Prostaglandin analogues** – usually first choice | **Latanoprost**, travoprost, bimatoprost | ↑ uveoscleral outflow; **once daily at night** | Red eye, **darkening of the iris (brown colour)**, longer darker eyelashes; avoid in pregnancy |
| **Beta-blockers** | **Timolol**, betaxolol | ↓ aqueous production | **Systemic absorption → bronchospasm and slow heart rate; avoid in asthma, COPD, heart block** |
| Alpha-2 agonists | Brimonidine | ↓ production, ↑ outflow | Allergy, dry mouth, fatigue; avoid in infants |
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | Dorzolamide, brinzolamide (topical); **acetazolamide** (oral/IV) | ↓ aqueous production | Topical: stinging, bitter taste; oral: tingling, frequent urination, low potassium, kidney stones; sulfonamide allergy |
| Cholinergic (miotics) | **Pilocarpine** | Constricts pupil, opens the angle → ↑ outflow | Small pupil, dim vision, brow ache; mainly in angle-closure |
| Rho-kinase inhibitor | Netarsudil | ↑ trabecular outflow | Red eye |

Fixed combinations (for example timolol + dorzolamide, timolol + brimonidine) reduce the number of drops.

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma (emergency):** lie the patient flat, refer at once; **IV acetazolamide**, **IV mannitol** or oral glycerol (osmotic agents), topical **timolol**, **pilocarpine** (after IOP starts to fall), topical steroid, analgesics and anti-emetics; then **laser peripheral iridotomy** (both eyes usually).

**Surgery and laser:** laser trabeculoplasty (open-angle), trabeculectomy, drainage devices.

**Counselling:** glaucoma needs **lifelong treatment** even without symptoms – stopping drops lets pressure rise silently; use drops at the same time daily; **nasolacrimal occlusion** reduces systemic side effects (important with timolol); wait 5 minutes between drops; tell every doctor you have glaucoma (avoid anticholinergic drugs if angle-closure); regular IOP and visual field checks; screen family members over 40.

## Part B: Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)

## 12.3 Definitions

- **Antimicrobial resistance:** the ability of a microorganism (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites) to **survive and grow in the presence of a drug that would normally kill it or stop its growth**, so the drug no longer works.
- **Multidrug-resistant (MDR):** resistant to at least one drug in three or more antimicrobial classes. **Extensively drug-resistant (XDR):** susceptible to only one or two classes. **Pan-drug resistant (PDR):** resistant to all available drugs.
- **Intrinsic resistance:** natural (for example *Pseudomonas* to many drugs, Gram-negatives to vancomycin). **Acquired resistance:** develops by mutation or by gaining resistance genes.

### Important resistant organisms

MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*), VRE (vancomycin-resistant enterococci), **ESBL**-producing *E. coli* and *Klebsiella*, **carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales** (for example **NDM-1**, New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase), MDR *Acinetobacter* and *Pseudomonas*, **MDR- and XDR-TB**, drug-resistant gonorrhoea, artemisinin-resistant malaria parasites, resistant *Candida auris*, resistant HIV.

## 12.4 Mechanisms of resistance

| Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Enzymatic destruction of the drug** | Beta-lactamases (penicillinase, ESBL, carbapenemases) break beta-lactam antibiotics; aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes |
| **Alteration of the drug target** | Altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a) in MRSA; altered DNA gyrase (fluoroquinolones); altered ribosome (macrolides); *rpoB* mutation (rifampicin in TB) |
| **Reduced entry (permeability)** | Loss of porins in Gram-negatives (carbapenems) |
| **Efflux pumps** | Pump the drug out (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones) |
| **Bypass pathway** | Alternative enzyme not inhibited (sulfonamides, trimethoprim) |

**Spread of resistance genes:** mutation and selection (vertical), and **horizontal gene transfer** between bacteria – **conjugation** (plasmids), transformation (free DNA), transduction (bacteriophage).

```text
Antibiotic use → susceptible bacteria killed
        ↓
Resistant bacteria survive and multiply (selection pressure)
        ↓
Spread: person to person, hospitals, food animals, water, environment
```

## 12.5 Causes of AMR (especially in India)

1. **Overuse and misuse** – antibiotics for viral infections (colds, viral fever, most diarrhoea).
2. **Self-medication and over-the-counter sale** of antibiotics without prescription.
3. Wrong drug, dose or **incomplete courses**; poor-quality or substandard drugs.
4. Irrational fixed-dose combinations and unnecessary broad-spectrum use.
5. **Poor infection control** in hospitals; poor sanitation and hand hygiene in the community.
6. **Antibiotic use in animals and agriculture** (growth promoters, poultry, aquaculture).
7. Pharmaceutical effluents and hospital waste reaching water.
8. Lack of rapid diagnostic tests; few new antibiotics being developed.

## 12.6 Consequences

Longer illness and hospital stay, treatment failure and **more deaths**, need for costlier and more toxic drugs (colistin, carbapenems), risk to surgery, cancer chemotherapy, transplants and neonatal care, and higher health costs – a **global public health threat**.

## 12.7 Prevention and control

### Global and national actions

| Action | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| WHO **Global Action Plan on AMR** | 2015 |
| **One Health approach** | Humans, animals and environment together |
| WHO **AWaRe** classification | **Access** (first-choice, narrow), **Watch** (higher resistance risk – use carefully), **Reserve** (last resort) |
| **Chennai Declaration** | 2012 – Indian medical societies' road map on AMR |
| **Schedule H1** (Drugs Rules) | Introduced 2013 – third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, anti-TB drugs and some others; sold only on prescription, with a **separate register** of prescriber, patient, drug and quantity, kept for 3 years |
| **"Red Line" campaign** | 2016 – a red vertical line on packs of prescription-only antibiotics: "do not use without a prescription" |
| **National Action Plan on AMR (NAP-AMR)** | **2017**, India – awareness, surveillance, infection prevention, rational use, research |
| Surveillance | ICMR AMR surveillance network; national networks |
| Animal sector | India banned colistin for growth promotion in food animals (2019) |

### Antimicrobial stewardship

**Antimicrobial stewardship** is a coordinated programme in a hospital or community that promotes the **appropriate use of antimicrobials** – right drug, dose, route and duration – to improve outcomes and limit resistance. Elements: hospital antibiotic policy, restricted (Reserve) antibiotics needing approval, **culture before antibiotics**, de-escalation to a narrow drug when culture results come, IV-to-oral switch, shortest effective duration, audit and feedback, infection-control team, education.

### Role of the pharmacist

1. **No sale of antibiotics without a valid prescription**; maintain the Schedule H1 register.
2. Counsel to **complete the prescribed course**, take at the right times, and **never share or keep leftover antibiotics**.
3. Explain that antibiotics **do not work on viral infections** (cold, flu, most sore throats).
4. Check dose, duration, interactions and allergies; suggest narrower alternatives where appropriate.
5. Take part in stewardship, prescription audits and adverse drug reaction reporting.
6. Promote **vaccination** (fewer infections → fewer antibiotics), hand hygiene and safe disposal of unused antibiotics.
7. Public awareness – World AMR Awareness Week (November).

### Role of the public

Use antibiotics only when prescribed by a qualified doctor; complete the course; do not demand antibiotics; wash hands; keep vaccinations up to date; prepare food hygienically.

## Remember

- Conjunctivitis: bacterial = purulent, sticky lids → topical antibiotic; viral = watery, pre-auricular node, very contagious → **no antibiotic**, hygiene, lubricants; allergic = itch → antihistamine drops; never self-use steroid drops; red flags = pain, reduced vision.
- Glaucoma: optic nerve damage, IOP normally 10–21 mmHg; POAG silent; acute angle-closure = painful red eye, haloes, vomiting – emergency (acetazolamide, mannitol, pilocarpine, iridotomy).
- Glaucoma drops: latanoprost (iris darkening), timolol (avoid in asthma), brimonidine, dorzolamide, pilocarpine; nasolacrimal occlusion; lifelong use.
- AMR: mechanisms – enzymes, target change, reduced entry, efflux; causes – misuse, OTC sale, animals, poor infection control.
- India: Chennai Declaration 2012, Schedule H1 2013, Red Line 2016, NAP-AMR 2017; WHO GAP 2015; AWaRe; stewardship; pharmacist must not sell antibiotics without prescription.
