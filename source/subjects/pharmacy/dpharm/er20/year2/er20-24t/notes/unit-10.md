# Unit 10: Musculoskeletal and dermatological disorders

This unit joins two short syllabus parts: chapter 2(h) **musculoskeletal disorders** – rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis (3 hours) – and chapter 2(i) **dermatology** – psoriasis, scabies and eczema (3 hours). Comparing rheumatoid arthritis with osteoarthritis, and the three skin conditions with each other, is a favourite exam question.

## Part A: Musculoskeletal disorders

## 10.1 Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)

### Definition

**Rheumatoid arthritis** is a **chronic, systemic, autoimmune inflammatory disease** that mainly affects the **synovial lining of joints**, causing **symmetrical polyarthritis** of small joints, which can lead to joint destruction, deformity and disability; it can also affect other organs.

### Etiopathogenesis

- **Cause:** not fully known – **genetic** tendency (HLA-DR4), **female sex** (about 2–3 times more common in women), **smoking**, possibly infections; usual onset 30–50 years.
- **Mechanism:**

```text
Trigger in a genetically prone person
        ↓
Autoimmune response (T cells, B cells → rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP antibodies)
        ↓
Cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) → synovial inflammation and thickening (pannus)
        ↓
Pannus erodes cartilage and bone → joint deformity
```

### Clinical manifestations

- **Symmetrical** pain, swelling and tenderness of **small joints of hands and feet** (MCP, PIP, wrists, MTP); **distal interphalangeal (DIP) joints are usually spared**.
- **Morning stiffness lasting more than 1 hour**, better with activity.
- Fatigue, low-grade fever, weight loss, anaemia.
- Late deformities: **ulnar deviation**, **swan-neck** and **boutonnière** deformities.
- Extra-articular: rheumatoid nodules, dry eyes, lung fibrosis, vasculitis, higher risk of heart disease.

**Investigations:** raised ESR and CRP; **rheumatoid factor** and **anti-CCP antibodies** (anti-CCP is more specific); X-ray (erosions, narrowing); anaemia of chronic disease.

### Goals

Relieve pain and inflammation, **stop joint damage**, maintain function and quality of life – aiming for **remission or low disease activity ("treat to target")**.

### Non-drug management

Patient education; rest during flares and **regular exercise and physiotherapy** between flares; splints; occupational therapy; weight control; **stop smoking**; balanced diet; surgery (joint replacement) for damaged joints.

### Drug management

**Key principle:** start a **disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug (DMARD) early** – within the first few months – because joint damage happens early. NSAIDs and steroids relieve symptoms but **do not stop damage**.

| Group | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Conventional DMARDs** | **Methotrexate** (anchor drug, weekly), hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine, leflunomide | Take weeks to months to act; can be combined |
| **Biologic DMARDs** | Anti-TNF: **infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept**; tocilizumab (anti-IL-6); rituximab; abatacept | When conventional DMARDs fail; screen for **TB and hepatitis B** first; infection risk |
| Targeted synthetic DMARDs | Tofacitinib (JAK inhibitor) | Oral |
| **NSAIDs** | Ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, etoricoxib | Symptom relief only; GI, kidney, CV risks |
| **Corticosteroids** | Prednisolone (low dose, short "bridge"), intra-articular injection | Rapid relief while DMARDs start working |

**Methotrexate counselling and monitoring:**
- Taken **once a week on the same day – never daily** (daily dosing by mistake has caused deaths).
- **Folic acid** is given on another day to reduce side effects (mouth ulcers, nausea).
- Regular **blood counts, liver and kidney tests**; avoid alcohol.
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy** (teratogenic) – use contraception (men and women).
- Avoid cotrimoxazole and high-dose NSAIDs without advice (raise toxicity).
- Report sore throat, fever, mouth ulcers, breathlessness or bruising.

Hydroxychloroquine needs regular **eye examinations** (retinal toxicity). Sulfasalazine may colour urine orange.

## 10.2 Osteoarthritis (OA)

### Definition

**Osteoarthritis** is a **chronic degenerative joint disease** characterised by **progressive loss of articular cartilage**, with new bone formation (osteophytes) at the joint margins and changes in the underlying bone, leading to pain and stiffness, mainly in **weight-bearing joints**. It is the **most common form of arthritis**.

### Etiopathogenesis

- **Risk factors:** **increasing age**, **obesity** (major, especially for knees), female sex (after menopause), previous joint injury, repeated joint stress (occupation, squatting), joint deformity, family history.
- **Mechanism:** wear and repair imbalance – cartilage chondrocytes cannot keep up with damage → cartilage thins and cracks → bone rubs on bone → bone hardens (sclerosis), forms osteophytes and cysts → secondary mild inflammation.

### Clinical manifestations

- Joint **pain that increases with use and is relieved by rest**; worse at the end of the day.
- **Short morning stiffness (less than 30 minutes)** and stiffness after rest ("gelling").
- **Crepitus** (grating sound), reduced movement, bony swelling, deformity (bow legs).
- Commonly affected: **knees, hips**, spine, **DIP joints** (Heberden's nodes), PIP joints (Bouchard's nodes), base of thumb.
- No systemic features; ESR, CRP and rheumatoid factor normal. X-ray: joint-space narrowing, osteophytes.

### Rheumatoid arthritis vs osteoarthritis

| Feature | Rheumatoid arthritis | Osteoarthritis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Autoimmune, inflammatory | Degenerative ("wear and tear") |
| Age | Any, often 30–50 years | Usually above 50 years |
| Joints | Small joints of hands/feet, symmetrical; DIP spared | Large weight-bearing joints (knee, hip); DIP involved |
| Morning stiffness | More than 1 hour | Less than 30 minutes |
| Systemic features | Fever, fatigue, anaemia, nodules | Absent |
| Blood tests | Raised ESR/CRP; RF and anti-CCP positive | Normal |
| X-ray | Erosions, periarticular osteopenia | Joint-space narrowing, osteophytes |
| Main treatment | DMARDs (methotrexate) | Exercise, weight loss, analgesics |

### Management of OA

**Non-drug (the foundation):**
1. **Weight reduction** – reduces knee load and pain.
2. **Exercise** – quadriceps strengthening, low-impact aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming); physiotherapy.
3. Walking stick (in the hand opposite the painful knee or hip), knee braces, proper footwear.
4. Avoid squatting, sitting cross-legged and climbing too many stairs; hot or cold packs.
5. **Total joint replacement** for severe disease.

**Drugs:**

| Drug | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Topical NSAIDs** (diclofenac gel) | First choice for knee and hand OA, especially in the elderly – fewer systemic side effects |
| Paracetamol | Safe and cheap, but modest effect; respect the maximum daily dose |
| Oral NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) or COX-2 inhibitors (etoricoxib, celecoxib) | Lowest dose, shortest time; with PPI if GI risk; avoid in kidney disease, heart failure |
| Duloxetine | For chronic pain in some patients |
| Topical capsaicin | Burning at first; helps some |
| **Intra-articular corticosteroid** | Short-term relief of a painful flare |
| Intra-articular hyaluronic acid | Modest, variable benefit |
| Glucosamine, chondroitin | Evidence weak and inconsistent |
| Tramadol / opioids | Only when other measures fail; risk of falls, dependence |

**Counselling:** lose weight and keep exercising – these work better than many drugs; use NSAIDs only as advised with food; wash hands after applying gels; report black stools or swelling of legs.

## Part B: Dermatological disorders

## 10.3 Psoriasis

### Definition

**Psoriasis** is a **chronic, immune-mediated, inflammatory skin disease** characterised by **well-defined red plaques covered with silvery-white scales**, due to rapid skin-cell turnover; it runs a course of remissions and relapses and is **not contagious**.

### Etiopathogenesis

- **Genetic** tendency (family history); **T-cell–mediated** inflammation (TNF-α, IL-17, IL-23) → skin cells mature in a few days instead of about 4 weeks → thick scaly plaques.
- **Triggers:** stress, streptococcal throat infection (guttate psoriasis), skin injury (**Koebner phenomenon** – lesions appear at injured sites), cold dry weather, smoking, alcohol, obesity; **drugs – lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials (chloroquine), NSAIDs, sudden withdrawal of systemic steroids**.

### Clinical manifestations

- **Plaque psoriasis** (most common): raised red plaques with silvery scales on **elbows, knees, scalp, lower back**; may itch.
- **Auspitz sign** – pinpoint bleeding when scales are scraped off.
- Nail changes: **pitting**, thickening, separation (onycholysis).
- Other forms: guttate (drop-like), pustular, erythrodermic (whole body – emergency), flexural.
- **Psoriatic arthritis** in some patients; higher risk of obesity, diabetes, heart disease and depression.

### Management

**Non-drug:** **emollients and moisturisers** daily; avoid triggers; gentle bathing; reduce stress, alcohol and smoking; weight loss; controlled sunlight.

**Topical (mild–moderate disease):**

| Drug | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Topical corticosteroids** | Mainstay; potency by site (mild on face and folds); limit long-term use (skin thinning) |
| **Vitamin D analogues** (calcipotriol) | Often combined with a steroid |
| Coal tar | Messy, smelly; stains clothes |
| **Salicylic acid** | Removes scales (keratolytic), helps other drugs penetrate |
| Dithranol | Short-contact therapy; stains and irritates normal skin |
| Tacrolimus | Face and flexures |

**Phototherapy:** narrow-band UVB; PUVA (psoralen + UVA).

**Systemic (moderate–severe):** **methotrexate**, ciclosporin, acitretin (strongly **teratogenic** – avoid pregnancy for years after stopping), apremilast, and **biologics** (adalimumab, etanercept, secukinumab, ustekinumab). **Systemic corticosteroids should be avoided** – stopping them can cause severe pustular or erythrodermic flares.

**Counselling:** psoriasis is not infectious; use moisturiser daily; apply topical steroids thinly as directed and do not overuse; coal tar and dithranol stain; expect long-term control rather than cure.

## 10.4 Scabies

### Definition

**Scabies** is a **contagious, intensely itchy skin infestation** caused by the mite ***Sarcoptes scabiei* var. *hominis***, which burrows into the upper layer of the skin.

### Etiopathogenesis

- **Spread by close, prolonged skin-to-skin contact** (family members, hostels, overcrowded homes, sexual contact); less often through shared clothes and bedding.
- The female mite burrows and lays eggs; itching is due to an **allergic reaction** to mites, eggs and faeces, which takes 2–6 weeks to develop in a first infection (so contacts may be infected without itching yet).
- **Crusted (Norwegian) scabies:** thousands of mites in elderly, immunocompromised or disabled people – very contagious.

### Clinical manifestations

- **Severe itching, worse at night**; often several family members itch.
- **Burrows** (short wavy lines) and small papules, vesicles in **finger webs, wrists, elbows, armpits, around the waist and navel, genitals, buttocks, around nipples**; in infants also palms, soles and scalp.
- Secondary bacterial infection (impetigo) from scratching.

### Management

**Treat the patient and ALL household and close contacts at the same time**, even without symptoms.

| Drug | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Permethrin 5% cream** | **First choice**; apply to the **whole body from the neck down** (include head and face in infants and the elderly), leave for **8–12 hours** (overnight), then wash off; **repeat after 7 days** |
| **Ivermectin (oral)** | Two doses about a week apart; useful for crusted scabies, institutions, or when topical treatment is difficult; not in young children or pregnancy |
| Benzyl benzoate 25% lotion | Alternative; irritant; diluted for children |
| Crotamiton, sulphur ointment | Alternatives (sulphur ointment is safe in infants and pregnancy) |
| Lindane | Not preferred – neurotoxicity |

**Supportive:** antihistamines (for example cetirizine) or calamine for itch; antibiotics if infected.

**Counselling:**
1. Apply to cool, dry skin after a bath; cover all areas including under nails, between fingers and toes; re-apply to hands if washed.
2. **Wash clothes, towels and bedding in hot water** and dry in the sun on the day of treatment; items that cannot be washed should be kept sealed in a bag for about 3 days.
3. **Itching may continue for 2–4 weeks** after successful treatment – do not keep re-applying permethrin; use antihistamines.
4. Return if new burrows appear after the second application.

## 10.5 Eczema (dermatitis)

### Definition

**Eczema** (used here interchangeably with dermatitis) is an **inflammatory skin condition** with itching, redness, scaling and, in acute stages, oozing vesicles; **atopic eczema (atopic dermatitis)** is the most common type, a chronic relapsing condition often starting in childhood.

### Types

| Type | Cause / features |
| --- | --- |
| **Atopic dermatitis** | Genetic **skin-barrier defect (filaggrin)** + immune tendency; family or personal history of asthma, hay fever; flexures of elbows and knees, face in infants |
| Contact dermatitis – irritant | Repeated detergents, soaps, water (hands of housewives, cleaners) |
| Contact dermatitis – allergic | Type IV allergy to nickel (jewellery), hair dye, rubber, cosmetics, topical medicines |
| Seborrhoeic dermatitis | Scalp (dandruff), face, chest; *Malassezia* yeast |
| Others | Nummular (coin-shaped), dyshidrotic (vesicles on palms), stasis (legs with varicose veins) |

### Clinical manifestations

**Intense itching** (the itch–scratch cycle), dry skin, red patches; acute – oozing, crusting; chronic – thick, darkened, leathery skin (**lichenification**). Infection with *Staphylococcus aureus* or herpes simplex can complicate it.

### Management

**Non-drug (most important):**
- **Emollients** liberally and often (after bathing, on damp skin) – the foundation of treatment.
- **Soap substitutes**, lukewarm short baths; avoid harsh soaps, wool, tight synthetic clothes, overheating.
- **Avoid irritants and identified allergens** (patch testing for contact dermatitis); wear gloves.
- Keep nails short; manage stress.

**Drugs:**

| Drug | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Topical corticosteroids** | Mainstay for flares; choose potency for site and age (mild on face and in children), short courses |
| **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) | Face, eyelids, flexures; steroid-sparing |
| Oral antihistamines | Sedating ones (for example hydroxyzine) at night help itch and sleep |
| Antibiotics / antivirals | For infected eczema |
| Systemic therapy (severe) | Short oral steroids (rarely), ciclosporin, methotrexate, azathioprine, **dupilumab** (biologic), phototherapy |

**Fingertip unit (FTU):** the amount of cream squeezed from a standard tube along the last segment of an adult index finger – enough for an area about two adult palms; helps patients apply the right amount of steroid.

**Counselling:** use emollients every day, even when the skin is clear; apply the steroid thinly and only as long as advised (overuse thins skin; stopping suddenly after long use can cause flares); avoid triggers; it is not contagious.

### Psoriasis vs eczema vs scabies

| Feature | Psoriasis | Eczema | Scabies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Immune, rapid cell turnover | Barrier defect, allergy, irritants | Mite infestation |
| Contagious | No | No | **Yes** |
| Lesion | Sharp red plaques, silvery scale | Ill-defined red, dry, oozing or thick patches | Burrows, papules |
| Sites | Elbows, knees, scalp | Flexures, hands, face | Finger webs, wrists, waist, genitals |
| Itch | Mild–moderate | Severe | Severe, worse at night |
| Key treatment | Topical steroid + calcipotriol; methotrexate, biologics | Emollients + topical steroid | Permethrin 5% for patient and contacts |

## Remember

- RA: autoimmune, symmetrical small joints, morning stiffness more than 1 hour, RF and anti-CCP; start methotrexate early (weekly, with folic acid, not in pregnancy).
- OA: degenerative, weight-bearing joints, pain on use, stiffness less than 30 minutes; weight loss + exercise first; topical NSAIDs; paracetamol.
- Psoriasis: silvery plaques, Auspitz sign, Koebner phenomenon; topical steroids + calcipotriol; avoid systemic steroids; lithium and beta-blockers can trigger it.
- Scabies: *Sarcoptes scabiei*; itch worse at night; permethrin 5% neck down, 8–12 hours, repeat after 7 days; treat all contacts; hot-wash bedding.
- Eczema: emollients daily are the foundation; topical steroids for flares; tacrolimus for face.
