# Unit 13: Women's health – polycystic ovary syndrome, dysmenorrhoea and premenstrual syndrome

Syllabus chapter 2(m), 4 hours. All three conditions are common in young women, are often managed first in the community, and need sensitive, private counselling. Before studying them, recall the normal menstrual cycle.

## 13.1 The normal menstrual cycle (background)

```text
Hypothalamus → GnRH → Pituitary → FSH and LH → Ovary → oestrogen, progesterone

Day 1–5     Menstruation (shedding of endometrium)
Day 1–14    Follicular phase: FSH → follicle grows → oestrogen rises → endometrium thickens
Day ~14     LH surge → ovulation
Day 15–28   Luteal phase: corpus luteum → progesterone → endometrium ready for implantation
            No pregnancy → corpus luteum regresses → hormones fall → next period
```

A cycle is normally about **21–35 days**; bleeding lasts about 2–7 days.

## 13.2 Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)

### Definition

**Polycystic ovary syndrome** is a **common endocrine and metabolic disorder of women of reproductive age**, characterised by a combination of **irregular or absent ovulation**, **excess androgens (male hormones)** and **polycystic-appearing ovaries** on ultrasound, often with **insulin resistance**. It is a leading cause of irregular periods and infertility due to anovulation.

### Diagnostic criteria (Rotterdam criteria – any 2 of 3, after excluding other causes)

1. **Oligo-ovulation or anovulation** – infrequent or absent periods.
2. **Hyperandrogenism** – clinical (excess hair growth, acne, scalp hair thinning) or biochemical (raised testosterone).
3. **Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound** – many small follicles and/or increased ovarian volume.

Other causes to exclude: thyroid disease, high prolactin, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome, androgen-secreting tumours.

### Etiopathogenesis

Exact cause unknown; **genetic tendency** + **insulin resistance** + **obesity** and lifestyle.

```text
Insulin resistance → high insulin
        ↓                         ↓
Ovary makes more androgens   Liver makes less SHBG → more free testosterone
        ↓
↑ LH relative to FSH (LH:FSH ratio often raised)
        ↓
Follicles start growing but do not mature → no ovulation → many small cysts
        ↓
Irregular periods, infertility, hirsutism, acne, weight gain
```

### Clinical manifestations

- **Menstrual:** irregular, infrequent or absent periods (oligomenorrhoea, amenorrhoea); sometimes heavy bleeding.
- **Androgen excess:** **hirsutism** (hair on face, chin, chest, abdomen), **acne**, oily skin, scalp hair thinning.
- **Metabolic:** **weight gain / central obesity**, **acanthosis nigricans** (dark velvety skin on the neck and armpits – a sign of insulin resistance), prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia.
- **Reproductive:** **infertility** (anovulation), higher risk of pregnancy complications (gestational diabetes).
- **Long-term risks:** type 2 diabetes, heart disease, **endometrial hyperplasia and cancer** (unopposed oestrogen from lack of ovulation), sleep apnoea, depression and anxiety.

### Management

Treatment depends on the woman's **main concern** – irregular periods, hirsutism/acne, weight, or wish to conceive.

**Non-drug (first line for everyone, especially overweight women):**
- **Weight loss of even 5–10%** restores ovulation and regular cycles in many.
- Healthy diet (less refined carbohydrate and sugar, more fibre and protein), **regular exercise**, sleep, stress management.
- Cosmetic hair removal (threading, waxing, laser).
- Screening for diabetes, lipids, BP, depression.

**Drug management:**

| Aim | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| **Regular periods, protect endometrium, reduce androgens** (not trying to conceive) | **Combined oral contraceptive pills (COCs)**, for example containing ethinylestradiol with a less androgenic progestin (cyproterone acetate, drospirenone, desogestrel); or **cyclic progestin** (medroxyprogesterone, dydrogesterone) to cause regular withdrawal bleeds |
| **Hirsutism, acne** | COCs first; **anti-androgens** – **spironolactone**, cyproterone, finasteride – **always with reliable contraception** (can feminise a male fetus); topical **eflornithine** for facial hair; acne treatment |
| **Insulin resistance, weight, metabolic risk** | **Metformin** (improves insulin sensitivity, may help cycles); weight-loss medicines in selected patients |
| **Infertility (ovulation induction)** | **Letrozole** (now often first choice) or **clomiphene citrate**; metformin as an add-on; gonadotropins, laparoscopic ovarian drilling or IVF under specialists |

**Counselling:** PCOS is common and manageable, not a cancer; lifestyle change is the most powerful treatment; take the pill daily and know the rule for missed pills; results on hair growth take **6 months or more**; metformin with meals (GI upset at first); pregnancy is possible with treatment; regular check-ups for sugar and lipids.

## 13.3 Dysmenorrhoea

### Definition

**Dysmenorrhoea** is **painful menstruation** – crampy lower abdominal pain occurring just before or during menstrual periods, severe enough to interfere with daily activities.

### Types

| Feature | **Primary dysmenorrhoea** | **Secondary dysmenorrhoea** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | **No pelvic disease**; excess prostaglandins | **Underlying pelvic disease** – endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease, copper IUD |
| Age of onset | Within 6–12 months of menarche (when ovulatory cycles begin); teens and early twenties | Usually later (after 25), or a change in the pattern of pain |
| Timing of pain | Starts a few hours before or with bleeding; lasts 1–3 days | May start days before the period and continue after; may have pain with intercourse, heavy or irregular bleeding |
| Examination | Normal | Abnormal findings may be present |

### Etiopathogenesis (primary)

```text
Progesterone falls before menstruation
        ↓
Endometrium releases prostaglandins (PGF2α, PGE2) and leukotrienes
        ↓
Strong, frequent uterine contractions + vasoconstriction → uterine ischaemia
        ↓
Cramping pain; prostaglandins also reach the blood →
nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, headache, dizziness
```

**Risk factors:** young age, early menarche, heavy or long periods, smoking, family history, stress; less common after childbirth.

### Clinical manifestations

Cramping, spasmodic pain in the **lower abdomen**, may spread to the **lower back and thighs**, beginning with the period; nausea, vomiting, loose stools, tiredness, headache, fainting in severe cases.

**Refer** if pain is severe or worsening, not relieved by NSAIDs, started after age 25, is associated with heavy or irregular bleeding, fever, abnormal discharge or pain on intercourse (possible secondary cause).

### Management

**Non-drug:**
- **Local heat** (hot water bottle or heat pad on the lower abdomen) – as effective as some pain-killers.
- Regular **exercise**, yoga and stretching; adequate rest and sleep.
- Stop smoking; balanced diet; reduce caffeine and salt.
- TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation) in some.

**Drugs (primary dysmenorrhoea):**

| Drug | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **NSAIDs** – first choice | **Mefenamic acid**, ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac – reduce prostaglandin production; **start at the first sign of pain or bleeding (or a day before if periods are predictable) and take regularly for 2–3 days**; take with food; avoid in peptic ulcer, kidney disease, aspirin-sensitive asthma |
| Paracetamol | If NSAIDs are not suitable; less effective |
| Antispasmodics | Drotaverine, dicyclomine (often combined with NSAIDs in India) |
| **Hormonal contraceptives** | **Combined oral contraceptive pill**, progestin-only methods or levonorgestrel IUD – suppress ovulation and thin the endometrium → less prostaglandin; useful when contraception is also wanted or NSAIDs fail |

**Secondary dysmenorrhoea:** treat the cause (endometriosis – NSAIDs, hormonal therapy, surgery; PID – antibiotics; fibroids – medical or surgical treatment).

**Counselling:** take the NSAID early and regularly for the first days rather than waiting for severe pain; take with food; heat and exercise help; see a doctor if pain is unusual or not relieved.

## 13.4 Premenstrual syndrome (PMS)

### Definition

**Premenstrual syndrome** is a group of **physical, emotional and behavioural symptoms** that occur **regularly in the luteal phase** (the 1–2 weeks before menstruation) and **resolve within a few days after the period starts**, with a symptom-free interval, severe enough to affect daily life. A severe form with marked mood symptoms is **premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD)**.

### Etiopathogenesis

Not fully understood. It is linked to **ovulation**: normal cyclical changes in oestrogen and progesterone trigger an **abnormal response** in sensitive women, especially through altered **serotonin** activity and GABA sensitivity to progesterone metabolites; stress, family history, smoking, lack of exercise and diet may contribute. (Symptoms disappear when ovulation is suppressed, in pregnancy and after menopause.)

### Clinical manifestations

| Emotional / behavioural | Physical |
| --- | --- |
| Irritability, anger, mood swings | **Breast tenderness and swelling** |
| Depressed mood, tearfulness | **Bloating**, fluid retention, weight gain |
| Anxiety, tension | Headache, backache, joint and muscle pain |
| Poor concentration, forgetfulness | Fatigue, sleep change |
| Food cravings, appetite change | Acne flares |
| Social withdrawal | — |

**Diagnosis:** a **prospective symptom diary for at least two cycles** showing symptoms limited to the luteal phase; exclude depression, anxiety disorders and thyroid disease (which do not clear after the period).

### Management

**Non-drug (first line for mild to moderate PMS):**
- **Symptom diary**; education and reassurance.
- **Regular aerobic exercise**; adequate sleep; stress reduction (yoga, relaxation, CBT).
- Diet: small frequent meals, complex carbohydrates, less salt (bloating), less caffeine, alcohol and sugar.
- **Calcium** supplementation has evidence for reducing symptoms; vitamin B6 and magnesium are sometimes used (avoid high-dose B6 – nerve damage).

**Drug management (moderate–severe PMS and PMDD):**

| Drug | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine) | **First-line drugs for PMDD and severe emotional symptoms**; can be taken **daily or only during the luteal phase** (they work faster here than in depression) |
| **Combined oral contraceptives** – especially those containing **drospirenone** | Suppress ovulation → reduce cyclical symptoms |
| **NSAIDs** (mefenamic acid, naproxen) | Pain, headache, breast pain |
| **Spironolactone** | Bloating and breast tenderness (fluid retention) in the luteal phase |
| GnRH analogues (with add-back hormone therapy) | Severe resistant cases; specialist |
| Anxiolytics | Benzodiazepines are generally avoided (dependence) |

**Counselling:** keep a diary to plan ahead; exercise regularly and reduce salt and caffeine before periods; SSRIs may be used only in the second half of the cycle if advised; the pill should be taken daily; seek help if mood symptoms are severe or include thoughts of self-harm.

## 13.5 Comparison of the three conditions

| Feature | PCOS | Primary dysmenorrhoea | PMS / PMDD |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Main problem | Anovulation + androgen excess + insulin resistance | Painful periods | Cyclical mood and physical symptoms before periods |
| Key mediator | Insulin, androgens, LH | **Prostaglandins** | Hormone-sensitive serotonin activity |
| Timing | Persistent | During first 1–3 days of period | Luteal phase, clears after period starts |
| First-line measure | Weight loss, exercise | NSAIDs (mefenamic acid) + heat | Lifestyle; SSRIs for severe |
| Hormonal option | COC, cyclic progestin | COC | COC with drospirenone |
| Other key drugs | Metformin, spironolactone, letrozole/clomiphene | Antispasmodics | Calcium, NSAIDs, spironolactone |

## Remember

- PCOS: Rotterdam – any 2 of anovulation, hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovaries; insulin resistance; lifestyle first; COCs for cycles and hirsutism; metformin; letrozole/clomiphene for fertility; anti-androgens only with contraception.
- Dysmenorrhoea: primary = prostaglandins, no pelvic disease; NSAIDs started early + heat; COCs; investigate secondary causes (endometriosis, fibroids, PID).
- PMS: luteal-phase symptoms that clear after menses; diary for 2 cycles; exercise, diet, calcium; SSRIs (continuous or luteal) for PMDD; drospirenone COC.
