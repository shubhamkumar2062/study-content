# Unit 5: Central nervous system disorders

Syllabus chapter 2(d), 8 hours: epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, stroke and migraine. These are long-term diseases, so **adherence and counselling** are as important as choosing the drug.

## 5.1 Epilepsy

### Definition

A **seizure** is a sudden, brief episode of abnormal, excessive and synchronous electrical discharge of brain neurons, causing changes in movement, sensation, behaviour or consciousness. **Epilepsy** is a brain disorder with an enduring tendency to have **recurrent, unprovoked seizures** (in practice, usually two or more unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart). **Status epilepticus** is a seizure lasting more than about 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery in between – a medical emergency.

### Causes

- **Idiopathic / genetic** (no cause found) – common.
- **Structural:** head injury, birth injury, brain tumour, stroke, brain infections – in India **neurocysticercosis** (pork tapeworm larvae in the brain) is an important cause, also tuberculosis and meningitis.
- **Metabolic:** low sugar, low sodium, low calcium.
- **Triggers in a known patient:** missed doses, lack of sleep, alcohol or its withdrawal, flashing lights, fever (in children), stress, some drugs (tramadol, high-dose antipsychotics, bupropion).

### Pathogenesis

Seizures result from an **imbalance between excitation (glutamate) and inhibition (GABA)**, or abnormal ion channels (Na+, Ca2+), which lets a group of neurons fire together and spread the activity.

### Classification and features

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Focal (partial)** – starts in one area | With awareness (simple partial): jerking of one limb, odd sensation; with impaired awareness (complex partial): staring, lip-smacking, automatic movements; may spread to both sides |
| **Generalised tonic–clonic** (grand mal) | Sudden loss of consciousness, stiffening (tonic), then rhythmic jerking (clonic), tongue bite, incontinence, confusion and sleepiness after |
| **Absence** (petit mal) | Children; brief blank stare for a few seconds, many times a day |
| **Myoclonic** | Sudden brief jerks, often in the morning |
| **Atonic** | Sudden loss of muscle tone → fall ("drop attacks") |

**Diagnosis:** eye-witness description, EEG, MRI/CT, blood tests.

### Non-drug management

Regular sleep, avoid alcohol and known triggers, adherence; **safety advice** (no swimming alone, no driving until seizure-free as per law, avoid heights and fire); ketogenic diet in some children; surgery or vagus nerve stimulation in drug-resistant cases.

**First aid during a seizure:** keep calm, protect the head, loosen tight clothing, **turn the person on the side** (recovery position) after jerking stops; **do not put anything in the mouth**, do not restrain; call for help if it lasts more than 5 minutes or another follows.

### Drug management

Principles: start **one drug (monotherapy)** at a low dose and increase slowly; choose by seizure type, age, sex and side effects; add or switch only if needed; never stop suddenly; consider withdrawal only after a long seizure-free period, slowly, under a doctor.

| Seizure type | First choice (examples) | Alternatives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focal | Carbamazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, oxcarbazepine | Phenytoin, valproate, lacosamide |
| Generalised tonic–clonic | **Sodium valproate**, lamotrigine, levetiracetam | Phenytoin, carbamazepine |
| Absence | **Ethosuximide**, sodium valproate | Lamotrigine |
| Myoclonic | Sodium valproate, levetiracetam | Clonazepam |

**Carbamazepine and phenytoin can worsen absence and myoclonic seizures.**

| Drug | Important adverse effects |
| --- | --- |
| Phenytoin | Gum overgrowth, hairiness, acne, coarse face, unsteadiness and double vision at high levels, nystagmus; zero-order kinetics – small dose change gives large level change; enzyme inducer |
| Carbamazepine | Dizziness, low sodium, rash including **Stevens–Johnson syndrome** (more risk with HLA-B*1502, common in some Asian groups), enzyme inducer (reduces effect of oral contraceptives) |
| Sodium valproate | Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, liver injury, pancreatitis; **highly teratogenic** (neural tube defects, developmental problems) – avoid in girls and women who can become pregnant unless no other option |
| Lamotrigine | Rash (start very slowly) |
| Levetiracetam | Irritability, mood changes |
| Phenobarbitone | Sedation, behaviour problems in children |

**Status epilepticus:** secure airway, oxygen, check glucose (give IV dextrose with thiamine if low); **IV lorazepam or diazepam** (or buccal/intranasal midazolam outside hospital) → if seizures continue, IV **phenytoin/fosphenytoin, valproate or levetiracetam** → if still continuing, anaesthesia in ICU.

**Pregnancy:** plan pregnancy; folic acid supplements before and during pregnancy; avoid valproate; continue effective treatment (uncontrolled seizures also harm mother and baby).

**Counselling:** take every dose at the same time; never stop suddenly (risk of status epilepticus); keep a seizure diary; carry an ID card; check interactions before any new medicine (many antiepileptics interact with oral contraceptives and other drugs); report rash, fever, yellow eyes, unusual bruising.

## 5.2 Parkinson's disease

### Definition

**Parkinson's disease** is a **chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder** caused by **loss of dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra**, leading to a deficiency of dopamine (and relative excess of acetylcholine) in the striatum, which produces a movement disorder.

### Causes and risk factors

Mostly unknown (idiopathic); age (usually above 60), genetic forms, possibly toxins (pesticides). **Drug-induced parkinsonism:** antipsychotics (haloperidol), metoclopramide, reserpine – usually reversible on stopping.

```text
Loss of dopaminergic neurons (substantia nigra) + Lewy bodies
        ↓
↓ Dopamine in striatum  vs  normal acetylcholine
        ↓
Imbalance → tremor, rigidity, slowness of movement
```

### Clinical manifestations ("TRAP")

- **T**remor at rest – "pill-rolling", reduces with movement.
- **R**igidity – "lead-pipe" or "cog-wheel" stiffness.
- **A**kinesia / bradykinesia – slow movement, difficulty starting movement, mask-like face, small handwriting, soft voice.
- **P**ostural instability – stooped posture, shuffling festinating gait, falls.

Non-motor: constipation, loss of smell, depression, sleep problems, orthostatic hypotension, dementia later.

### Non-drug management

Physiotherapy and exercise, speech therapy, occupational therapy, fall prevention, high-fibre diet and fluids for constipation; deep brain stimulation in selected patients.

### Drug management

| Group | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Levodopa + carbidopa** (or benserazide) | — | **Most effective**; carbidopa blocks peripheral breakdown → less nausea, lower dose. Long-term: "wearing-off", "on–off" fluctuations, dyskinesias. Protein meals can reduce absorption |
| Dopamine agonists | Pramipexole, ropinirole | Used early in younger patients; sleepiness, sudden sleep attacks, hallucinations, impulse control problems (gambling) |
| MAO-B inhibitors | Selegiline, rasagiline | Mild effect; added to levodopa |
| COMT inhibitors | Entacapone | Prolong levodopa effect; orange urine |
| Amantadine | — | Reduces dyskinesias |
| Anticholinergics | Trihexyphenidyl (benzhexol) | For tremor in younger patients; avoid in elderly (confusion, constipation, urinary retention); also used for drug-induced parkinsonism |

**Counselling:** take doses at regular times; do not stop suddenly; rise slowly (postural hypotension); avoid driving if sleepy; report hallucinations or compulsive behaviour; avoid metoclopramide and older antipsychotics (domperidone is preferred for nausea).

## 5.3 Alzheimer's disease

### Definition

**Alzheimer's disease** is the **most common cause of dementia**: a **progressive neurodegenerative disease** causing gradual loss of memory, thinking, language and ability to carry out daily activities.

### Etiopathogenesis

Age is the strongest risk factor; family history, ApoE4 gene, Down syndrome, head injury, hypertension, diabetes, low education, inactivity.

```text
β-amyloid plaques outside neurons + neurofibrillary tangles (tau) inside
        ↓
Loss of cholinergic neurons → ↓ acetylcholine
        ↓
Brain shrinkage (hippocampus first) → memory loss → dementia
```

### Clinical manifestations

Early: forgetting recent events, repeating questions, misplacing things. Middle: confusion about time and place, difficulty with language and money, personality change, wandering. Late: unable to recognise family, dependent for eating and toileting, incontinence, bedridden.

### Management

**Non-drug (most important):** safe home, routine, memory aids, reminders, social activity, exercise, caregiver support and education, control of BP and sugar.

**Drugs (improve symptoms; do not stop the disease):**

| Group | Examples | Use / adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cholinesterase inhibitors** | Donepezil, rivastigmine (also patch), galantamine | Mild to moderate disease; nausea, diarrhoea, slow pulse, vivid dreams |
| **NMDA antagonist** | Memantine | Moderate to severe disease; dizziness, confusion |

Treat depression (SSRIs) and sleep problems carefully; avoid anticholinergic drugs (worsen memory); antipsychotics only for severe distressing behaviour and short term (raise stroke and death risk in elderly with dementia).

**Counselling (often to the caregiver):** use a pill organiser and supervise doses; benefits may be modest; report fainting or slow pulse; keep the patient safe and active.

## 5.4 Stroke (cerebrovascular accident)

### Definition

**Stroke** is a **sudden focal (sometimes global) loss of brain function lasting more than 24 hours or causing death**, due to a vascular cause. A **transient ischaemic attack (TIA)** is a short episode of similar symptoms that resolves (usually within an hour) without infarction – a warning sign.

### Types and causes

| Type | Share | Cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ischaemic** | About 80–85% | Thrombosis on atherosclerotic plaque, or embolism (often from the heart in atrial fibrillation) |
| **Haemorrhagic** | About 15–20% | Bleeding into the brain (often hypertension) or subarachnoid space (ruptured aneurysm) |

**Risk factors:** **hypertension (most important)**, diabetes, smoking, dyslipidaemia, atrial fibrillation, previous TIA, obesity, alcohol, age.

### Clinical manifestations – BE FAST

```text
B – Balance loss       E – Eyes: sudden vision loss
F – Face drooping      A – Arm (or leg) weakness on one side
S – Speech difficulty  T – Time to call emergency services
```

Also sudden severe headache (especially in haemorrhage), vomiting, confusion. A **CT scan** is done first to tell ischaemic from haemorrhagic stroke – essential before any clot-busting drug.

### Management

**General:** airway, breathing, swallowing check (nothing by mouth until safe), control fever and blood sugar, prevent pressure sores and clots, early rehabilitation (physiotherapy, speech therapy).

**Ischaemic stroke:**
1. **Thrombolysis** with IV **alteplase** (or tenecteplase) within about **4.5 hours** of symptom onset in eligible patients, after CT excludes bleeding; mechanical thrombectomy for large-vessel blocks.
2. **Aspirin** after 24 hours of thrombolysis, or early if no thrombolysis; short-term aspirin + clopidogrel for minor stroke or TIA.
3. **Secondary prevention:** antiplatelet (aspirin or clopidogrel) long term; **anticoagulant** (warfarin or newer oral anticoagulants – apixaban, dabigatran, rivaroxaban) if atrial fibrillation; **statin**; BP control; diabetes control; stop smoking.

**Haemorrhagic stroke:** no antiplatelets or anticoagulants; reverse anticoagulants; careful BP lowering; neurosurgery for some bleeds; nimodipine in subarachnoid haemorrhage.

**Counselling:** take antiplatelet/anticoagulant regularly; report bleeding; warfarin needs INR tests and consistent diet; control BP; recognise BE FAST signs and reach hospital at once.

## 5.5 Migraine

### Definition

**Migraine** is a **primary headache disorder** with recurrent attacks of moderate to severe, usually **one-sided, throbbing headache**, lasting about 4–72 hours, aggravated by routine activity, with **nausea/vomiting and sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia)**.

| Type | Feature |
| --- | --- |
| Migraine without aura (common) | Headache alone |
| Migraine with aura (classical) | Preceded by reversible visual zig-zag lines, flashing lights, or numbness, lasting minutes |

### Etiopathogenesis

Genetic tendency; women more affected (hormonal). Triggers: stress, lack of or excess sleep, skipped meals, bright light, strong smells, menstruation, oral contraceptives, certain foods (cheese, chocolate, alcohol), weather change. Mechanism: abnormal brain excitability (cortical spreading depression – aura) and activation of the **trigeminovascular system** with release of CGRP and serotonin changes → inflamed, dilated meningeal vessels → pain.

### Management

**Non-drug:** headache diary to find triggers; regular sleep and meals; stress management; rest in a dark quiet room during attacks.

**Acute attack (take early):**

| Severity | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Mild–moderate | Paracetamol, aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, ± anti-emetic (domperidone, metoclopramide) |
| Moderate–severe | **Triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, zolmitriptan) – 5-HT1B/1D agonists; avoid in coronary disease, uncontrolled hypertension, stroke |
| Older option | Ergotamine (rarely used now) |

**Medication-overuse headache:** using pain-killers or triptans on many days each month can worsen headaches – limit their use.

**Prophylaxis** (frequent or disabling attacks): **propranolol**, **flunarizine**, **amitriptyline**, **topiramate**, sodium valproate (not in women who may become pregnant), newer anti-CGRP antibodies. Continue for some months and review.

**Counselling:** take acute medicine at the start of the headache; do not exceed the stated number of doses; keep a diary; prophylaxis works over weeks.

## Remember

- Epilepsy: monotherapy; valproate for generalised, carbamazepine for focal, ethosuximide/valproate for absence; never stop suddenly; avoid valproate in women of child-bearing age; lorazepam/diazepam first in status epilepticus.
- Parkinson's: dopamine loss in substantia nigra; TRAP; levodopa + carbidopa most effective; avoid metoclopramide and typical antipsychotics.
- Alzheimer's: plaques + tangles + ↓ acetylcholine; donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine; memantine.
- Stroke: CT first; alteplase within 4.5 h for ischaemic stroke; antiplatelets, statin, BP control; anticoagulant in atrial fibrillation.
- Migraine: NSAIDs/triptans for attacks; propranolol, flunarizine, amitriptyline, topiramate for prevention; beware medication-overuse headache.
