# Unit 6: Gastrointestinal disorders

Syllabus chapter 2(e), 8 hours: gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), alcoholic liver disease and inflammatory bowel diseases (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis). Acid-related diseases are among the most common reasons people come to a community pharmacy.

## 6.1 Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD)

### Definition

**GERD** is a condition in which **stomach contents (acid, pepsin, bile) flow back (reflux) into the oesophagus** often enough to cause troublesome symptoms and/or damage to the oesophageal lining (oesophagitis).

### Etiopathogenesis

The **lower oesophageal sphincter (LES)** normally stops reflux. GERD develops when:

- the LES is weak or relaxes too often (transient LES relaxations);
- there is a **hiatus hernia**;
- pressure in the abdomen is high (obesity, pregnancy, tight clothes);
- the stomach empties slowly.

**Things that lower LES pressure or irritate:** fatty and spicy food, large late-night meals, chocolate, peppermint, coffee, alcohol, smoking; drugs such as **calcium channel blockers, nitrates, anticholinergics, theophylline, benzodiazepines**; irritant drugs (NSAIDs, doxycycline, bisphosphonates, potassium chloride, iron tablets).

### Clinical manifestations

- **Heartburn** – burning feeling behind the breastbone, often after meals or on lying down or bending.
- **Regurgitation** of sour fluid into the mouth; water brash.
- Others: difficulty or pain on swallowing, chronic cough, hoarseness, asthma-like symptoms, dental erosion, chest pain.
- **Alarm features** (refer for endoscopy): difficulty swallowing, weight loss, vomiting blood, black stools, anaemia, age above about 50–55 with new symptoms.

**Complications:** oesophagitis, stricture, **Barrett's oesophagus** (risk of adenocarcinoma), bleeding.

### Non-drug management

| Advice | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Raise the head end of the bed by 15–20 cm | Gravity keeps acid down at night |
| Avoid meals 2–3 hours before lying down | Empty stomach at bedtime |
| Small, frequent meals; avoid trigger foods | Less reflux |
| Lose weight; avoid tight clothes | Lower abdominal pressure |
| Stop smoking and alcohol | Both weaken the LES |
| Review drugs that worsen reflux | Remove the cause |

### Drug management

| Group | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antacids, alginates | Aluminium/magnesium hydroxide, sodium alginate | Quick relief of mild, occasional symptoms |
| **H2-receptor antagonists** | Famotidine (ranitidine was withdrawn in many countries because of an impurity) | Mild symptoms; at night |
| **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** | **Omeprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole** | **Most effective**; heal oesophagitis; usually 4–8 weeks, then lowest effective dose or on-demand |
| Prokinetics | Domperidone, itopride, metoclopramide | Limited role; add-on |

**Step-down approach:** start with a PPI to control symptoms quickly, then reduce to the lowest effective dose.

**Long-term PPI concerns:** low magnesium and vitamin B12, fractures, *Clostridioides difficile* diarrhoea, pneumonia; use the lowest dose for the shortest needed time.

**Counselling:** take the PPI **30–60 minutes before breakfast**; swallow whole (enteric-coated); antacids should be kept about 2 hours apart from other medicines (they reduce absorption of iron, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones); follow life-style measures.

## 6.2 Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)

### Definition

A **peptic ulcer** is a **break in the lining of the stomach (gastric ulcer) or duodenum (duodenal ulcer)** that extends through the muscularis mucosae, caused by the action of acid and pepsin when the mucosal defence is weakened.

### Etiopathogenesis – imbalance of aggressive and defensive factors

```text
Aggressive factors                    Defensive factors
- Acid and pepsin                     - Mucus and bicarbonate layer
- H. pylori infection                 - Prostaglandins
- NSAIDs, aspirin                     - Mucosal blood flow
- Smoking, stress, alcohol            - Cell renewal
          \                               /
           → Aggression > defence → ULCER ←
```

**Main causes:**
1. ***Helicobacter pylori*** – a Gram-negative spiral bacterium that survives in acid (urease makes ammonia); causes most duodenal ulcers and many gastric ulcers.
2. **NSAIDs and aspirin** – block prostaglandin synthesis (COX-1) → less mucus and bicarbonate, less blood flow.
3. Others: smoking, alcohol, severe physical stress (burns, ICU – stress ulcers), Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (gastrin-secreting tumour), steroids with NSAIDs.

### Clinical manifestations

| Feature | Duodenal ulcer | Gastric ulcer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pain timing | 2–3 hours after meals, at night; **relieved by food** | Soon after meals; **worsened by food** |
| Weight | Often maintained | Loss (fear of eating) |
| Cancer risk | Very low | Must be biopsied to exclude cancer |

Burning epigastric pain, bloating, nausea. **Complications:** **bleeding** (vomiting blood, black tarry stools – melaena), **perforation** (sudden severe pain, rigid abdomen), gastric outlet obstruction (vomiting).

**Diagnosis:** endoscopy; tests for *H. pylori* – **urea breath test**, stool antigen test, rapid urease test on biopsy, serology.

### Non-drug management

Stop smoking, avoid alcohol, avoid NSAIDs (use paracetamol for pain), small regular meals, avoid foods that worsen symptoms, stress reduction.

### Drug management

**(a) *H. pylori*-positive ulcer – eradication therapy (usually 14 days):**

| Regimen | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| **Standard triple therapy** | **PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin** (metronidazole instead of amoxicillin in penicillin allergy) |
| **Bismuth quadruple therapy** | PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole – used where clarithromycin resistance is high or after failure |
| Others | Sequential or levofloxacin-based regimens for resistant cases |

After eradication, the PPI is continued for some weeks to heal the ulcer; a test of cure (breath or stool test) is done later.

**(b) NSAID-induced ulcer:** stop the NSAID if possible; PPI for 4–8 weeks; if NSAID must continue, use a PPI with it (or misoprostol), or a COX-2 selective drug in suitable patients.

**(c) Other drugs:** H2-blockers; sucralfate (coats the ulcer; give on an empty stomach, separate from other drugs); misoprostol (prostaglandin analogue – **contraindicated in pregnancy**, causes uterine contractions); antacids for symptom relief.

**Bleeding ulcer:** hospital care, IV PPI, endoscopic therapy, blood transfusion if needed.

**Counselling:** complete the full eradication course even if pain stops (resistance and failure otherwise); metronidazole – **no alcohol** (disulfiram-like reaction) and metallic taste; bismuth may darken stools and tongue; avoid self-medication with NSAIDs; report black stools or vomiting of blood.

## 6.3 Alcoholic liver disease (ALD)

### Definition

**Alcoholic liver disease** is liver damage caused by **long-term excessive alcohol consumption**. It is a spectrum:

```text
Alcoholic fatty liver (steatosis)  →  Alcoholic hepatitis  →  Alcoholic cirrhosis
   (reversible on stopping)          (inflammation, can be       (irreversible scarring,
                                      severe and fatal)          liver failure, cancer risk)
```

### Etiopathogenesis

Alcohol is metabolised in the liver by alcohol dehydrogenase and CYP2E1 to **acetaldehyde**, which is toxic. This causes fat build-up, oxidative stress, inflammation and fibrosis. Risk increases with **amount and duration of drinking**; women, obesity, malnutrition, hepatitis B or C infection and genetic factors raise the risk.

### Clinical manifestations

- **Fatty liver:** usually no symptoms; enlarged liver; mildly raised enzymes.
- **Alcoholic hepatitis:** jaundice, fever, tender enlarged liver, loss of appetite; AST typically higher than ALT (ratio often about 2:1); raised GGT.
- **Cirrhosis and its complications:** jaundice, **ascites** (fluid in the abdomen), leg oedema, **oesophageal varices** with vomiting of blood, **hepatic encephalopathy** (confusion, flapping tremor, coma), spider naevi, gynaecomastia, easy bruising, low albumin, prolonged prothrombin time, liver cancer.

### Management

**Non-drug (most important):**
1. **Complete and permanent abstinence from alcohol** – the single most effective treatment at every stage.
2. **Nutrition:** adequate calories and protein (do not restrict protein routinely), vitamins; small frequent meals and a late-evening snack in cirrhosis.
3. Salt restriction if ascites.
4. Vaccination against hepatitis A and B; avoid liver-toxic drugs; liver transplant in selected patients who have stopped drinking.

**Drug management:**

| Problem | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| **Alcohol withdrawal** | **Benzodiazepines** (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam; lorazepam or oxazepam preferred in severe liver disease) |
| Prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy | **Thiamine (vitamin B1)** – give before glucose |
| Maintaining abstinence | Acamprosate, naltrexone (avoid in severe liver disease), disulfiram (with care); counselling, support groups |
| Severe alcoholic hepatitis | **Prednisolone** in selected patients |
| Ascites | **Spironolactone ± furosemide**, salt restriction, paracentesis |
| Varices | Non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol, carvedilol) to prevent bleeding; endoscopic banding; octreotide/terlipressin in acute bleeding |
| Hepatic encephalopathy | **Lactulose** (2–3 soft stools a day), **rifaximin** |

**Drug use in liver disease:** avoid NSAIDs (bleeding, kidney failure, fluid retention); limit paracetamol to a reduced dose; avoid sedatives (can precipitate encephalopathy); reduce doses of drugs cleared by the liver.

**Counselling:** stop alcohol completely; take lactulose regularly; report confusion, black stools, vomiting of blood or swelling of abdomen; no over-the-counter pain-killers or herbal remedies without advice.

## 6.4 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

### Definition

**Inflammatory bowel disease** means **chronic, relapsing inflammation of the gut** of uncertain cause. Its two main forms are **ulcerative colitis (UC)** and **Crohn's disease (CD)**.

### Etiopathogenesis

An abnormal immune response to gut bacteria in a genetically susceptible person; environmental factors (diet, infections, antibiotics). **Smoking worsens Crohn's disease** but appears protective in UC. Common in young adults (15–35 years).

### Ulcerative colitis vs Crohn's disease

| Feature | Ulcerative colitis | Crohn's disease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | **Colon and rectum only**; starts in rectum and spreads continuously | **Any part from mouth to anus**; most often terminal ileum |
| Pattern | Continuous | **Skip lesions** (patches with normal gut between) |
| Depth | Mucosa only | **Full thickness (transmural)** |
| Main symptoms | **Bloody diarrhoea**, urgency, tenesmus | Abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weight loss, malabsorption |
| Fistulas, strictures | Rare | Common |
| Granulomas | Absent | Present |
| Smoking | Not a risk (may protect) | Makes it worse |
| Surgery | Removal of colon can cure | Not curative; disease recurs |

**Extra-intestinal features (both):** arthritis, eye inflammation (uveitis), skin lesions, liver disease, anaemia. **Complications:** toxic megacolon (UC), colon cancer (long-standing), strictures and fistulas (CD), malnutrition.

### Non-drug management

Balanced nutrition, enough protein and calories, correct iron, B12, calcium and vitamin D deficiency; low-residue diet during flares if there are strictures; **stop smoking (Crohn's)**; stress management; surgery when needed.

### Drug management

| Group | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aminosalicylates (5-ASA)** | **Mesalamine (mesalazine)**, sulfasalazine, balsalazide | Mild–moderate UC – induction and maintenance; oral, enema or suppository. Sulfasalazine can cause headache, low sperm count (reversible), folate deficiency, orange urine |
| **Corticosteroids** | Prednisolone, budesonide (less systemic effect), IV hydrocortisone in severe attacks | **Induce remission** only; **not for maintenance** |
| **Immunomodulators** | Azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, methotrexate (Crohn's) | Maintain remission, steroid-sparing; monitor blood counts and liver |
| **Biologics** | Infliximab, adalimumab (anti-TNF); vedolizumab; ustekinumab | Moderate–severe or resistant disease; screen for TB and hepatitis B first |
| Others | Antibiotics (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin) for fistulas and abscesses in Crohn's; loperamide carefully for diarrhoea | Avoid antidiarrhoeals in severe UC (toxic megacolon) |

**Counselling:** take maintenance drugs even when well; do not stop steroids suddenly; report fever, sore throat or infections on immunosuppressants; avoid NSAIDs (can trigger flares); get recommended vaccines (avoid live vaccines on immunosuppressants).

## Remember

- GERD: weak LES; heartburn and regurgitation; life-style + PPI before breakfast; alarm features need endoscopy.
- PUD: *H. pylori* and NSAIDs are the two main causes; triple therapy PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin for 14 days; bismuth quadruple as alternative; no alcohol with metronidazole.
- ALD: fatty liver → hepatitis → cirrhosis; abstinence is the key; thiamine; benzodiazepines for withdrawal; spironolactone for ascites; lactulose and rifaximin for encephalopathy.
- IBD: UC = colon, continuous, mucosal, bloody diarrhoea; Crohn's = anywhere, skip, transmural, fistulas; 5-ASA, steroids for flares only, azathioprine and biologics for maintenance.
