# Unit 7: Haematological disorders – iron deficiency anaemia and megaloblastic anaemia

Syllabus chapter 2(f), 4 hours. Anaemia is extremely common in India, especially in women, adolescent girls, pregnant women and young children, and is the target of the national **Anaemia Mukt Bharat** programme.

## 7.1 Anaemia – general points

**Definition:** **anaemia** is a reduction in the **haemoglobin (Hb) concentration** or red cell mass below the normal for age and sex, so that the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood is reduced.

| Group | Anaemia if Hb is below (WHO cut-offs, g/dL) |
| --- | --- |
| Adult men | 13 |
| Adult non-pregnant women | 12 |
| Pregnant women | 11 |
| Children 6–59 months | 11 |

### Classification by red-cell size (MCV)

| Type | MCV | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Microcytic, hypochromic** (small, pale cells) | Low (below about 80 fL) | **Iron deficiency**, thalassaemia, anaemia of chronic disease (some) |
| **Normocytic, normochromic** | Normal | Acute blood loss, chronic disease, kidney failure, haemolysis |
| **Macrocytic** (large cells) | High (above about 100 fL) | **Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency (megaloblastic)**, alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism |

### General symptoms of any anaemia

Tiredness, weakness, breathlessness on exertion, palpitations, dizziness, headache, poor concentration, **pallor** (of skin, conjunctiva, nails, palms). Severe anaemia can cause heart failure.

## 7.2 Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA)

### Definition

**Iron deficiency anaemia** is a **microcytic hypochromic anaemia** resulting from a lack of iron, so that the body cannot make enough haemoglobin. It is the **most common anaemia worldwide and in India**.

### Iron basics

- Iron is absorbed mainly in the **duodenum and upper jejunum**, in the **ferrous (Fe2+)** form.
- **Haem iron** (meat, fish) is absorbed better than **non-haem iron** (cereals, pulses, green leafy vegetables, jaggery).
- **Vitamin C** and acid improve absorption; **tea, coffee, calcium, antacids, PPIs, phytates (bran) and tetracyclines** reduce it.
- The body has no active way to excrete iron; balance is controlled at absorption.

### Causes

| Group | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| **Increased need** | Pregnancy, lactation, infancy, adolescence (growth spurts) |
| **Blood loss** (most common in adults) | Heavy menstrual bleeding; GI bleeding – **hookworm infestation**, peptic ulcer, NSAIDs, piles, cancer of stomach or colon; frequent blood donation |
| **Poor intake** | Vegetarian diet poor in iron, poverty, faulty infant feeding |
| **Poor absorption** | Coeliac disease, gastrectomy, atrophic gastritis, *H. pylori*, drugs that reduce acid |

### Pathogenesis

```text
Iron loss or need > iron intake
        ↓
Iron stores (ferritin) fall first  →  serum iron falls, TIBC rises
        ↓
Less haem made  →  small, pale red cells (microcytic hypochromic)
        ↓
Low Hb → anaemia symptoms
```

### Clinical manifestations

General anaemia symptoms + features of iron lack:

- **Koilonychia** (spoon-shaped, brittle nails).
- **Angular stomatitis** (cracks at the corners of the mouth), **glossitis** (smooth sore tongue).
- **Pica** – craving for non-food items such as clay, ice, chalk.
- Difficulty in swallowing in long-standing cases (oesophageal web).
- Hair loss; in children – poor growth, poor school performance.

### Laboratory findings

| Test | Iron deficiency |
| --- | --- |
| Hb | Low |
| MCV, MCH | Low (microcytic, hypochromic) |
| **Serum ferritin** | **Low – the best single test for iron stores** (can be falsely normal in infection or inflammation) |
| Serum iron | Low |
| Total iron-binding capacity (TIBC) | **High** |
| Transferrin saturation | Low |
| Peripheral smear | Small pale cells, pencil cells |

Always find the **cause** (stool examination for hookworm and occult blood, menstrual history, endoscopy in older adults).

### Non-drug management

- Iron-rich diet: green leafy vegetables, pulses, jaggery, dates, millets (ragi, bajra), eggs, meat, fish; cook in iron vessels.
- Eat vitamin C sources (lemon, amla, guava, orange) with meals.
- **Avoid tea or coffee with or just after meals.**
- Treat the cause: **deworming** (albendazole), treat heavy periods, ulcers, piles; stop unnecessary NSAIDs.
- Delayed cord clamping in newborns; exclusive breastfeeding and iron-rich complementary food.

### Drug management

**Oral iron (first choice):**

- **Ferrous salts** – ferrous sulfate (most widely used), ferrous fumarate, ferrous gluconate; also ferrous ascorbate and carbonyl iron.
- Usual adult treatment gives roughly **100–200 mg of elemental iron per day** (ferrous sulfate 200 mg tablet contains about 60–65 mg elemental iron).
- Hb should rise by about **1–2 g/dL in 3–4 weeks**; continue for about **3 months after Hb becomes normal** to refill stores.
- **Adverse effects:** nausea, epigastric pain, constipation or diarrhoea, **black stools** (harmless); liquid iron can stain teeth (use a straw). Taking with food reduces upset but also absorption.
- **Iron poisoning** in children who swallow tablets is dangerous; antidote: **desferrioxamine**.

**Parenteral iron** – when oral iron is not tolerated or absorbed, continuing blood loss, late pregnancy with severe anaemia, or a need to raise stores quickly:

| Preparation | Note |
| --- | --- |
| **Iron sucrose** (IV) | Widely used; given in several doses |
| **Ferric carboxymaltose** (IV) | Large dose in one or two sittings; used in the national programme for pregnant women with moderate–severe anaemia |
| Iron dextran (IV/IM) | Risk of anaphylaxis – test dose; less used |

Hypersensitivity can occur with IV iron – give where resuscitation facilities exist. **Blood transfusion** only for very severe anaemia or heart failure.

**National programme (Anaemia Mukt Bharat):** iron and folic acid (IFA) supplementation for children, adolescents (weekly blue tablet), pregnant and lactating women (daily red tablet), biannual deworming, testing and treatment, and behaviour change communication.

### Counselling points

1. Take iron preferably on an **empty stomach** or between meals with water or juice; if stomach upset, take with food.
2. **Do not take with tea, coffee, milk, calcium, antacids**; separate from tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones and levothyroxine by about 2 hours or more.
3. Stools will be **black** – this is expected.
4. Continue for the full period (about 3 months after Hb is normal) even if you feel better.
5. Keep iron tablets out of reach of children.

## 7.3 Megaloblastic anaemia

### Definition

**Megaloblastic anaemia** is a **macrocytic anaemia** caused by **impaired DNA synthesis**, usually due to deficiency of **vitamin B12 (cobalamin)** or **folic acid**. Red-cell precursors in the bone marrow become large, immature **megaloblasts** because the nucleus matures more slowly than the cytoplasm.

### Causes

| Vitamin B12 deficiency | Folate deficiency |
| --- | --- |
| **Strict vegetarian / vegan diet** (B12 comes only from animal foods) – common in India | Poor diet (lack of green leafy vegetables, overcooking destroys folate) |
| **Pernicious anaemia** – autoimmune loss of **intrinsic factor** from gastric parietal cells | **Increased need:** pregnancy, lactation, haemolytic anaemia, cancer |
| Gastrectomy; disease or removal of terminal ileum (Crohn's) | Alcoholism |
| Fish tapeworm; bacterial overgrowth | Malabsorption (coeliac disease, tropical sprue) |
| Drugs: **metformin** (long-term), PPIs | Drugs: **methotrexate, trimethoprim, phenytoin, phenobarbitone, pyrimethamine** |

Body stores of B12 last several years; folate stores last only a few months – so folate deficiency appears faster.

### Pathogenesis

```text
B12 needed to convert methyl-folate → tetrahydrofolate
        ↓  (either vitamin lacking)
↓ Thymidine → ↓ DNA synthesis
        ↓
Nucleus matures slowly, cytoplasm normally → large megaloblasts
        ↓
Ineffective erythropoiesis → macrocytic anaemia (also low WBC, platelets)

B12 also needed for myelin → deficiency damages nerves
```

### Clinical manifestations

- General anaemia symptoms; pale **lemon-yellow** tinge (mild jaundice from destroyed cells).
- **Glossitis** (red, smooth, sore tongue), angular stomatitis, loss of appetite, diarrhoea.
- **Only in B12 deficiency – neurological features:** tingling and numbness of hands and feet, loss of vibration and position sense, unsteady gait (**subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord**), memory loss, depression.
- Folate deficiency in early pregnancy → **neural tube defects** in the baby.

### Laboratory findings

High MCV (macrocytes, oval macrocytes), **hypersegmented neutrophils** on smear, low serum B12 or low folate (serum or red-cell folate), raised homocysteine (both) and methylmalonic acid (B12 only), raised LDH and bilirubin; megaloblastic bone marrow.

### Management

**Non-drug:** diet rich in folate (green leafy vegetables, pulses, citrus fruits; avoid overcooking) and B12 (milk, curd, eggs, meat, fish); for strict vegetarians – fortified foods or regular B12 supplements; reduce alcohol.

**Drug management:**

| Deficiency | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| **Vitamin B12** | **Hydroxocobalamin or cyanocobalamin by intramuscular injection** – a course of frequent loading injections, then maintenance injections (lifelong in pernicious anaemia, which cannot absorb oral B12 well). High-dose **oral B12** can be used for diet-related deficiency. Methylcobalamin is also used in India |
| **Folic acid** | **Oral folic acid 5 mg daily**, usually for about 4 months (longer if the cause continues) |
| Prevention in pregnancy | Folic acid **before conception and in the first trimester** (low-dose daily for all; higher dose for women at high risk, such as those on antiepileptics or with a previous affected baby) |

**Very important rule:** **never give folic acid alone to a patient with B12 deficiency** – it can correct the blood picture but **allow the nerve damage to progress**. Check B12 (or give both) before treating with folic acid.

**Response to treatment:** patients feel better within days; **reticulocyte count rises within about a week**; Hb normal in about 6–8 weeks. **Low potassium** can occur early in treatment of severe anaemia (rapid new cell production). Neurological damage improves partly; long-standing damage may be permanent.

### Counselling points

- B12 injections in pernicious anaemia are needed **for life** – do not stop when you feel well.
- Vegetarians should include milk and curd and consider supplements.
- Folic acid is safe; take daily for the full course; women planning pregnancy should start folic acid before conceiving.
- Patients on long-term metformin or PPIs may need B12 checks.

## 7.4 Iron deficiency vs megaloblastic anaemia

| Feature | Iron deficiency | Megaloblastic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell size | Microcytic, hypochromic | Macrocytic |
| Cause | Iron loss or lack | B12 or folate lack |
| Special signs | Koilonychia, pica | Glossitis, nerve damage (B12) |
| Key test | Low ferritin, high TIBC | Low B12/folate, hypersegmented neutrophils |
| Treatment | Oral ferrous salts; IV iron if needed | IM hydroxocobalamin; oral folic acid 5 mg |
| Common in India due to | Hookworm, heavy periods, poor diet, pregnancy | Vegetarian diet, pregnancy, alcohol |

## Remember

- Anaemia: Hb below 13 (men), 12 (women), 11 (pregnancy) g/dL.
- IDA: microcytic hypochromic; low ferritin is the key test; ferrous sulfate orally, continue 3 months after Hb normalises; black stools; avoid tea and calcium with iron; deworm; IV iron sucrose or ferric carboxymaltose if needed.
- Megaloblastic: macrocytic, hypersegmented neutrophils; B12 deficiency causes nerve damage; IM hydroxocobalamin; folic acid 5 mg daily; never folic acid alone in B12 deficiency.
- Folic acid before and in early pregnancy prevents neural tube defects.
