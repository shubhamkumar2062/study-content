# Unit 3: Respiratory disorders – asthma and COPD

Syllabus chapter 2(b), 4 hours. Both diseases block the airways, both are treated mainly with **inhalers**, and both need careful **inhaler-technique counselling** – which is exactly where the pharmacist helps most.

## 3.1 Bronchial asthma

### Definition

**Asthma** is a **chronic inflammatory disease of the airways** in which the airways are **hyper-responsive** to many triggers, producing **recurrent episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough**, with airflow obstruction that is **variable and usually reversible** (on its own or with treatment).

### Types

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Extrinsic (allergic, atopic)** | Starts in childhood; personal or family history of eczema, hay fever; IgE-mediated; triggered by allergens |
| **Intrinsic (non-allergic)** | Starts in adult life; triggered by infection, exercise, cold air, drugs; IgE not raised |
| Special forms | Exercise-induced, aspirin-induced (NSAID-sensitive), occupational asthma |

### Causes and triggers

- **Allergens:** house dust mite, pollen, mould, animal dander, cockroach.
- **Irritants:** tobacco smoke, smoke from cooking fuels, air pollution, strong smells.
- **Infections:** viral respiratory infections (most common trigger of attacks in children).
- **Exercise, cold air, emotional stress.**
- **Drugs:** aspirin and other NSAIDs, **non-selective beta-blockers** (propranolol, timolol eye drops).
- **Others:** gastro-oesophageal reflux, obesity, occupational chemicals.

### Pathogenesis

```text
Allergen enters airway
        ↓
Binds IgE on mast cells → release of histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins
        ↓                                   (early phase: minutes)
Bronchospasm + mucosal oedema + mucus
        ↓
Eosinophils, T-lymphocytes arrive → inflammation (late phase: hours)
        ↓
Long-term: airway remodelling (thick wall, smooth muscle growth)
```

Three things narrow the airway: **smooth-muscle spasm, swelling of the lining, and thick mucus plugs**.

### Clinical manifestations

Episodic wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, typically **worse at night or early morning**, and after exposure to triggers. Between attacks the patient may be normal.

**Severe attack (acute severe asthma):** cannot complete sentences, respiratory rate and pulse high, use of accessory muscles, low oxygen saturation; a **silent chest**, cyanosis, confusion or exhaustion are signs of a life-threatening attack.

### Diagnosis

- **Spirometry:** reduced FEV1/FVC that improves by **12% or more (and at least 200 mL)** after a bronchodilator.
- **Peak expiratory flow (PEF):** variable from day to day; useful for home monitoring.
- Raised blood eosinophils or IgE, skin-prick tests for allergy.

### Goals

Good symptom control, normal activity and sleep, minimal reliever use, prevent attacks, lowest effective dose of medicines, few side effects.

### Non-drug management

- Identify and **avoid triggers** (dust-proof bedding, hot-wash bedding, no smoking at home, avoid smoke from wood or kerosene stoves).
- Correct **inhaler technique** and a written **asthma action plan**.
- Weight control, regular exercise (with pre-exercise reliever if needed), influenza vaccination.
- Treat reflux and allergic rhinitis.

### Drug management

**Two kinds of medicines:**

| Kind | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Relievers (rescue)** | Quick relief of symptoms | Short-acting beta-2 agonists (SABA): **salbutamol**, terbutaline; or **low-dose ICS–formoterol** used as needed |
| **Controllers (preventers)** | Reduce airway inflammation, prevent attacks | **Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)**: budesonide, beclomethasone, fluticasone; ICS + long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA: formoterol, salmeterol); leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast); long-acting antimuscarinic (tiotropium) as add-on; theophylline (rarely now) |
| Add-on for severe asthma | Specialist use | Oral steroids (short courses), biologic drugs (omalizumab – anti-IgE; mepolizumab – anti-IL-5) |

**Key modern principle (GINA guidance):** every adult and adolescent with asthma should receive an **inhaled corticosteroid**; treatment with a SABA **alone** is no longer recommended, because it controls symptoms but not inflammation and is linked to severe attacks. Treatment is **stepped up** when control is poor (after checking technique and adherence) and **stepped down** after about three months of good control.

```text
Step 1–2: as-needed low-dose ICS–formoterol (or low-dose daily ICS + SABA)
Step 3:   low-dose ICS–LABA maintenance
Step 4:   medium-dose ICS–LABA
Step 5:   add LAMA, refer for biologics / specialist care
```

**LABA must never be used alone in asthma** – always with an ICS.

**Acute attack (emergency):** oxygen to keep saturation around 93–95%; repeated inhaled **salbutamol** by spacer or nebuliser; **ipratropium** added in severe attack; **systemic corticosteroid** (oral prednisolone or IV hydrocortisone); IV magnesium sulphate in severe cases; avoid sedatives.

### Adverse effects to explain

| Drug | Adverse effects |
| --- | --- |
| Salbutamol | Tremor, palpitations, low potassium |
| ICS | **Oral thrush**, hoarse voice (prevent by rinsing mouth); high doses: bone loss, cataract |
| Theophylline | Narrow therapeutic range: nausea, arrhythmias, seizures; many interactions |
| Montelukast | Mood changes, sleep disturbance (rare) |
| Oral steroids (long use) | High sugar, weight gain, bone loss, infections |

### Counselling points

1. Know which inhaler is the **reliever** and which is the **preventer**; use the preventer every day even when well.
2. Correct technique: metered-dose inhaler with **slow, deep breath and 10-second breath-hold**; dry powder inhaler with **fast, deep breath**; a **spacer** helps children and elderly.
3. Rinse mouth and spit after ICS.
4. Signs of poor control: using the reliever more than twice a week, waking at night, limited activity → see the doctor.
5. Follow the action plan; go to hospital if the reliever does not help.
6. Avoid aspirin and beta-blockers unless advised.

## 3.2 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

### Definition

**COPD** is a common, preventable and treatable **chronic lung disease** with persistent respiratory symptoms and **airflow limitation that is not fully reversible**, usually progressive, caused by long exposure to noxious particles or gases. It includes:

- **Chronic bronchitis** – cough with sputum on most days for at least **3 months a year for 2 consecutive years**.
- **Emphysema** – permanent enlargement of air spaces beyond the terminal bronchioles with destruction of their walls.

### Causes and risk factors

- **Tobacco smoking** (cigarettes, beedi, hookah) – the main cause.
- **Indoor air pollution** from burning biomass (wood, dung, crop waste) in poorly ventilated kitchens – an important cause in Indian women.
- Outdoor air pollution, occupational dusts and fumes.
- **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** (genetic; emphysema at a young age).
- Repeated childhood chest infections, past tuberculosis, low birth weight.

### Pathogenesis

```text
Smoke / biomass particles
        ↓
Chronic inflammation (neutrophils, macrophages)
        ↓                                   ↓
Mucus gland enlargement,           Protease (elastase) > antiprotease
airway narrowing and fibrosis              ↓
(chronic bronchitis)            Destruction of alveolar walls (emphysema)
        ↓                                   ↓
        └──────→  Air trapping, poor gas exchange  ←──────┘
                           ↓
           Low O2, high CO2 → cor pulmonale (right heart failure)
```

### Clinical manifestations

Usually over 40 years with a history of smoking or biomass exposure. **Progressive breathlessness**, chronic cough with sputum, wheeze, frequent chest infections. Signs: barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing, use of accessory muscles. Two classical pictures: "pink puffer" (mainly emphysema, thin and breathless) and "blue bloater" (mainly bronchitis, cyanosed and oedematous). **Exacerbations** – sudden worsening of breathlessness, cough or sputum, often due to infection.

### Diagnosis

**Spirometry:** post-bronchodilator **FEV1/FVC below 0.70** confirms persistent airflow limitation. Severity (GOLD grades 1–4) is based on FEV1 % predicted. Chest X-ray, oxygen saturation, blood gases in severe disease.

### Asthma vs COPD

| Feature | Asthma | COPD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age at onset | Often childhood | Usually above 40 years |
| Main cause | Allergy, triggers | Smoking, biomass smoke |
| Symptoms | Episodic, variable | Persistent, progressive |
| Reversibility | Largely reversible | Not fully reversible |
| Inflammatory cells | Eosinophils, mast cells | Neutrophils, macrophages |
| Mainstay of drug therapy | Inhaled corticosteroids | Long-acting bronchodilators (LAMA, LABA) |

### Non-drug management

1. **Stop smoking** – the single most effective step; slows the decline of lung function (use counselling and nicotine replacement, bupropion or varenicline).
2. Reduce biomass exposure – clean fuel (LPG), ventilated kitchen.
3. **Vaccination:** influenza every year, pneumococcal vaccine.
4. **Pulmonary rehabilitation:** exercise training, breathing techniques (pursed-lip breathing), nutrition.
5. **Long-term oxygen therapy** for chronic low oxygen (improves survival).
6. Surgery (lung volume reduction, transplant) in selected severe cases.

### Drug management (stable COPD)

| Group | Examples | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SABA / SAMA (as needed) | Salbutamol; ipratropium | Short relief |
| **LAMA** | **Tiotropium**, glycopyrronium | First-choice maintenance in many patients; dry mouth, urinary retention |
| **LABA** | Salmeterol, formoterol, indacaterol | Maintenance |
| LAMA + LABA | Combination inhalers | When symptoms persist |
| ICS (added) | Budesonide, fluticasone | Only in frequent exacerbators, especially with high blood eosinophils; ICS raise **pneumonia** risk |
| Others | Roflumilast, theophylline, azithromycin (long-term, selected), mucolytics (N-acetylcysteine) | Specialist choice |

### Acute exacerbation

Increase short-acting bronchodilators (nebulised salbutamol ± ipratropium); **oral prednisolone for about 5 days**; **antibiotics** when sputum becomes purulent or more in amount (for example amoxicillin–clavulanate, doxycycline or a macrolide); **controlled oxygen** (target saturation about 88–92%, because high oxygen can raise CO2 in some patients); non-invasive ventilation if needed.

### Counselling points

- Stop smoking and avoid smoke from wood or dung fires.
- Use inhalers correctly every day; a LAMA capsule inhaler must be pierced and inhaled, **not swallowed**.
- Know the early signs of an exacerbation (more breathlessness, change in sputum colour) and seek care early.
- Take yearly influenza vaccine.
- Use oxygen safely: no smoking or open flame near it.

## Remember

- Asthma: reversible, variable, inflammatory; triggers; ICS is the cornerstone; never SABA alone or LABA alone; rinse mouth after ICS.
- Spirometry: asthma – reversibility of 12% and 200 mL; COPD – post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70.
- COPD: smoking and biomass smoke; not fully reversible; LAMA/LABA maintenance; ICS only for frequent exacerbations.
- Smoking cessation is the most important COPD measure; vaccinate against influenza and pneumococcus.
- Exacerbation of COPD: bronchodilators + short oral steroid + antibiotic if sputum is purulent + controlled oxygen (88–92%).
