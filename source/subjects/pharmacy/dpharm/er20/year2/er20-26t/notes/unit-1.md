# Unit 1: General principles of law, history and Acts related to drugs and pharmacy

This unit covers syllabus chapter 1. It explains what law is, how an Act is made in India, the difference between an Act, Rules and Regulations, and how the laws that control medicines and the pharmacy profession grew over time. Read it first: every later unit uses these words.

## 1.1 What law is and why pharmacy needs it

**Law** is a set of rules made by a recognised authority (Parliament, a State Legislature or a body given power by them) that everyone must follow. Breaking it leads to a penalty imposed by a court.

Medicines are different from ordinary goods:

- The buyer (the patient) cannot judge their quality by looking at them.
- A wrong, fake or poor-quality medicine can cause harm or death.
- Some medicines (narcotics, sedatives) can be misused and cause addiction.
- Prices and supply of essential medicines affect public health.

So the State controls who may make, import, store, sell and dispense medicines, and who may practise as a pharmacist. **Pharmaceutical jurisprudence** is the branch of law that deals with these controls.

## 1.2 Sources of law in India

| Source | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Constitution of India | The supreme law; every other law must agree with it | Division of law-making power between Union and States |
| Legislation (statutes) | Acts passed by Parliament or a State Legislature | Pharmacy Act, 1948 |
| Delegated (subordinate) legislation | Rules, regulations, orders and notifications made by the Government or a body under the power given by an Act | Drugs Rules, 1945; Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015 |
| Judicial precedent | Earlier decisions of the Supreme Court and High Courts that later courts follow | Interpretation of "drug" or "patent" in a case |
| Custom | Long-followed practice accepted as binding | Rarely important in drug law |

### The Constitution and drug law

The Seventh Schedule of the Constitution divides subjects into three lists:

- **Union List** – only Parliament makes laws (for example, import and export across customs frontiers, patents).
- **State List** – only States make laws (for example, public health, hospitals and dispensaries).
- **Concurrent List** – both may make laws; if they conflict, the Union law usually prevails. **"Drugs and poisons"** and **"legal, medical and other professions"** are in the Concurrent List.

This is why the main drug laws are Central Acts, but their day-to-day enforcement (sale licences, drug inspectors at district level, pharmacy councils) is done by the States.

## 1.3 Act, Rules, Regulations and Orders

| Term | Who makes it | What it contains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Act** (statute) | Parliament or State Legislature | The main law: objectives, definitions, authorities, prohibitions, offences and penalties |
| **Rules** | Central or State Government, under a section of the Act | Detailed procedures: forms, fees, licence conditions, labelling, schedules |
| **Regulations** | A statutory body (for example, PCI) with the Government's approval | Standards for its own area, such as education or practice |
| **Order** | Government under an Act | A specific control, such as the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013 under the Essential Commodities Act |
| **Notification** | Government, published in the Official Gazette | Brings a rule into force, bans a drug, adds a drug to a schedule |

**Schedules** are lists attached to an Act or to Rules (for example, Schedule H of the Drugs Rules). They can be changed by notification without passing a new Act, so they change often.

## 1.4 How an Act is made

```text
Draft Bill (Ministry)
      |
      v
Introduced in one House of Parliament
      |
First reading -> Second reading (clause-by-clause, may go to a Committee) -> Third reading (passed)
      |
      v
Same process in the other House
      |
      v
President's assent  -->  Act
      |
      v
Published in the Gazette of India; comes into force on the date notified
```

- A **Bill** becomes an **Act** only after both Houses pass it and the President gives assent.
- An Act may come into force at once or on a date the Government notifies. Different sections can start on different dates.
- An **amendment Act** changes an existing Act. A **repeal** removes an Act; usually a newer Act replaces it.

## 1.5 Some basic legal terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Offence | An act or omission that the law punishes |
| Cognizable offence | Police may arrest without a warrant |
| Bailable / non-bailable | Whether bail is a right or at the court's discretion |
| Licence | Written permission from an authority to do something that is otherwise prohibited (manufacture, sale) |
| Licensee | The holder of a licence |
| Penalty | Punishment: fine, imprisonment or both, and sometimes confiscation |
| Compounding | Settling certain minor offences by paying an amount instead of trial |
| Ex officio | Holding a post because of another office (for example, the Drugs Controller General as a member of a board) |
| Gazette notification | Official publication that gives legal effect to rules and orders |

**Civil law** deals with disputes between persons (contracts, compensation). **Criminal law** deals with offences against society, such as selling spurious drugs. Most drug laws create criminal offences.

## 1.6 History of drug and pharmacy law in India

### Before the main Acts

- In the 19th and early 20th centuries most modern medicines were imported. There was no control over their quality, and many sub-standard and adulterated products were sold.
- Opium was controlled mainly for revenue, through the **Opium Acts of 1857 and 1878**.
- The **Poisons Act, 1919** regulated the possession and sale of poisons.
- The **Dangerous Drugs Act, 1930** controlled opium, coca leaf, cannabis and their products.

### Drugs Enquiry Committee (Chopra Committee), 1930–31

The Government of India appointed this committee under **Col. R. N. Chopra** to study the sale of drugs and the state of pharmacy in India. Its main findings and advice:

1. Poor-quality and adulterated drugs were widely sold; a central law was needed to control the import, manufacture, distribution and sale of drugs.
2. A central drugs laboratory should test drugs.
3. India should have its own pharmacopoeia.
4. Pharmacists should be properly trained and registered; a law should regulate the profession.

Its report led to the **Drugs Act, 1940** and later the **Pharmacy Act, 1948**.

### Other important committees

- **Hathi Committee (Committee on Drugs and Pharmaceutical Industry), 1974–75** – recommended a list of essential drugs, use of generic names and a larger role for Indian companies.
- These ideas later appeared in the **National List of Essential Medicines** and in pricing policies (Unit 7).

## 1.8 Why a pharmacist must know the law

1. Only a **registered pharmacist** may dispense medicines; registration itself is a legal status.
2. Every pharmacy works under a **drug licence** with conditions that must be followed.
3. The pharmacist decides which medicines need a prescription and which records to keep.
4. Breaking these rules can lead to cancellation of the licence, removal from the register, fine or imprisonment.
5. The law protects the patient; knowing it helps the pharmacist protect the public.

## Remember

- Drugs and poisons, and the medical and other professions, are in the **Concurrent List**: both Parliament and States can make laws.
- An **Act** gives the main law; **Rules** give the detailed procedure; **Regulations** are made by statutory bodies; **Schedules** are lists that can be changed by notification.
- A Bill becomes an Act after passing both Houses and receiving the **President's assent**.
- **Chopra Committee (1930–31)** → Drugs Act 1940 and Pharmacy Act 1948.
- The **NDPS Act, 1985** replaced the Opium Acts and the Dangerous Drugs Act, 1930.
- Laws change often: always check the latest amendment and notification.
