# Unit 7: National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority, DPCO 2013, Pharmaceutical Policy 2002 and NLEM

This unit covers syllabus chapter 9 (5 hours). It explains how the Government keeps essential medicines affordable: the National List of Essential Medicines decides **which** medicines are price-controlled, the Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013 decides **how** their prices are fixed, and the NPPA **enforces** it.

## 7.1 Why medicine prices are controlled

- Patients cannot shop around in an illness; the doctor chooses the medicine.
- Many people pay for medicines from their own pocket.
- Without control, prices of essential medicines can rise beyond reach.

India has issued Drugs (Prices Control) Orders since **1970** (later orders in 1979, 1987, 1995 and **2013**). They are made under section 3 of the **Essential Commodities Act, 1955**, which lets the Government control the production, supply, distribution and price of essential goods.

## 7.2 National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA)

- Set up by the Government of India in **1997** as an independent body of experts, now under the **Department of Pharmaceuticals, Ministry of Chemicals and Fertilizers**.
- It is the **implementing and enforcing authority** for the DPCO.

### Functions of NPPA

1. **Fix and revise the ceiling prices** of scheduled formulations and the **retail prices** of new drugs under the DPCO.
2. **Monitor prices** of non-scheduled (decontrolled) formulations and make sure annual increases stay within the allowed limit.
3. **Enforce** the order: detect **overcharging** and **recover the overcharged amount** with interest from companies.
4. Monitor the **availability** of medicines and act on shortages.
5. Collect and maintain data on production, imports, exports and market share.
6. Advise the Government on pricing policy, and deal with legal cases about prices.
7. Publish price notifications and run a consumer helpline/app for price complaints.

## 7.3 Drugs (Prices Control) Order, 2013

### Background and objectives

DPCO 2013 was notified in **May 2013**, based on the **National Pharmaceutical Pricing Policy, 2012**. Its principles are:

1. **Essentiality** – only medicines in the **National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)** are price-controlled.
2. **Control of formulation prices only** – the price of the finished formulation is controlled, not the bulk drug.
3. **Market-based pricing** – the ceiling price is worked out from the prices already in the market, not from the cost of production (which was the basis of earlier orders).

Objective: to make essential medicines available at **reasonable prices** while giving industry room to grow and innovate.

### Important definitions

| Term | Meaning (simplified) |
| --- | --- |
| Active pharmaceutical ingredient (bulk drug) | Any pharmaceutical, chemical, biological or plant product, including its salts, esters and derivatives, used as an active ingredient in a formulation |
| Formulation | A medicine processed from one or more bulk drugs, with or without pharmaceutical aids, for internal or external use in humans or animals |
| Scheduled formulation | A formulation included in the **First Schedule** of the order (the NLEM medicines, in the listed dosage form and strength), branded or generic |
| Non-scheduled formulation | A formulation not in the First Schedule |
| Ceiling price | The price fixed by the Government for a scheduled formulation; no one may sell above it (plus applicable taxes) |
| Retail price | The price fixed by the Government for a **new drug** under the order |
| Maximum retail price (MRP) | The maximum price at which a drug can be sold to the consumer, including all taxes |
| Price to retailer (PTR) | The price at which a drug is sold to a retailer, including taxes |
| Market share | Share of a brand or generic in the total sales of that formulation |
| New drug (for DPCO) | A formulation launched by an existing manufacturer of an NLEM drug by combining it with another drug, or by changing its strength or dosage form |
| Wholesale price index (WPI) | The annual change in the wholesale price index announced by the Government, used for yearly revision |

### Ceiling price of scheduled formulations

```text
Step 1  List all brands and generics of the formulation with at least 1% market share
Step 2  Take the simple average of their price to retailer (PTR)
Step 3  Add a 16% margin for the retailer
        Ceiling price = average PTR x (1 + 16/100)
Step 4  Notify the ceiling price; companies selling above it must reduce their MRP to it
        (companies selling below it must keep their lower price)
```

- Ceiling prices are **revised every year** (from 1 April) according to the **wholesale price index** of the previous calendar year; companies may raise prices up to the revised ceiling.
- If the NLEM is revised, the ceiling prices of newly added medicines are fixed afresh.

### Retail price of a new drug

For a "new drug" as defined in the order, the manufacturer must apply to the NPPA, which fixes a **retail price** using the prices of the existing similar formulations, before the product is launched.

### Non-scheduled formulations

The manufacturer fixes the MRP, but **may not increase it by more than 10%** over the previous twelve months. NPPA monitors this.

### Sale prices of bulk drugs

Under DPCO 2013 the prices of bulk drugs (APIs) are **generally not fixed**. However, to keep formulations available, the Government may **direct a bulk drug manufacturer** to increase production and to sell the bulk drug to formulation makers at the terms it decides.

### Other provisions

1. **Price list** – manufacturers must issue a price list in the prescribed form to dealers and the Government; retailers must **display** it so customers can see it.
2. **MRP printed on the label** – a drug may not be sold above the printed MRP or the notified ceiling price.
3. **Overcharging** – the amount charged above the notified price, with interest, is recovered from the company, and penalties can follow under the Essential Commodities Act.
4. **Discontinuation** – a manufacturer intending to stop making a scheduled formulation must give advance notice (six months) to the Government, which may direct him to continue for a period in public interest.
5. **Exemptions** – some new drugs developed through Indian research, patented in India, and some medicines for rare diseases are exempted for a period under specific provisions; check the current text.
6. In special situations, the Government may fix prices of any drug in public interest, even if it is not scheduled (for example, coronary stents and knee implants have been price-capped this way).

## 7.4 Pharmaceutical Policy, 2002

A national policy announced in **2002** to guide the pharmaceutical sector.

### Objectives

1. Ensure **abundant availability**, at **reasonable prices**, of good quality **essential pharmaceuticals** of mass consumption in India.
2. **Strengthen the indigenous capability** for cost-effective, quality production and exports.
3. **Strengthen the quality control** system for drug production and distribution.
4. Encourage **research and development (R&D)** and create an environment for it, including in Indian systems of medicine.
5. Reduce trade barriers and encourage **efficient, cost-effective** industry.
6. Rationalise **price control**: control prices of drugs based on their market turnover and competition, rather than covering a long list.

### Main features

- Price control was to cover drugs with **large sales and little competition**, through criteria based on turnover and market share.
- Encouraged R&D through incentives (for example, tax benefits on R&D spending).
- Supported **quality control** improvements, including strengthening of drug control organisations.
- Industrial licensing for most bulk drugs and formulations was removed; foreign investment was allowed more freely.

The price-control part of this policy was challenged in court and did not fully take effect; the 1995 order continued until it was replaced by the **2012 policy and DPCO 2013**, which moved to the **essentiality (NLEM) basis**.

## 7.5 National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)

### Essential medicines

The WHO describes **essential medicines** as those that satisfy the **priority health care needs** of the population. They are selected for public health relevance, evidence of efficacy and safety, and comparative cost-effectiveness, and should be available at all times, in adequate amounts, in appropriate dosage forms and of assured quality, at a price the community can afford. WHO published its first Model List in **1977**.

### NLEM in India

| Edition | Note |
| --- | --- |
| 1996 | First NLEM |
| 2003, 2011, 2015 | Revisions |
| **2022** | Current list with **384 medicines** (check whether a newer edition has been issued) |

- Prepared by an **expert committee** of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (the Standing National Committee on Medicines).
- Medicines are grouped by therapeutic category and marked for the **level of health care**: **P** (primary), **S** (secondary), **T** (tertiary).
- Selection criteria: disease burden in India, proven efficacy and safety, cost-effectiveness, availability, and use in national health programmes. Medicines that are banned, no longer safe or have better alternatives are removed.

### Uses of the NLEM

1. Basis of **price control** – NLEM medicines become **scheduled formulations** under DPCO 2013.
2. Guides **procurement** and supply in public hospitals and health programmes.
3. Promotes **rational use** of medicines and **generic prescribing**.
4. Guides **education and training** of doctors and pharmacists.
5. Helps States prepare their own essential medicine lists.

## 7.6 How the pieces fit together

```text
Essential Commodities Act, 1955
          |
          v
National Pharmaceutical Pricing Policy 2012  --->  DPCO 2013 (the legal order)
          |                                           |
NLEM (which medicines)  ----------------->  First Schedule = scheduled formulations
                                                      |
                                       NPPA fixes ceiling prices, revises them yearly by WPI,
                                       monitors non-scheduled drugs (max 10%/year),
                                       recovers overcharged amounts
```

## Remember

- DPCO **2013** is made under the **Essential Commodities Act, 1955**; enforced by **NPPA** (set up **1997**).
- Price control is based on **essentiality**: NLEM medicines = **scheduled formulations**.
- **Ceiling price** = simple average PTR of brands with **≥1% market share** + **16%** retailer margin.
- Yearly revision by **WPI**; non-scheduled drugs may rise by **at most 10%** a year.
- Pharmaceutical Policy **2002**: availability at reasonable prices, quality, R&D, indigenous capability.
- NLEM: first **1996**; **2022** edition has **384** medicines; levels **P, S, T**.
