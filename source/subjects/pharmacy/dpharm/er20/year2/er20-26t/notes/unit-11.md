# Unit 11: New drug development, clinical trials, BCS, ANDA/NDA, brands and generics, patents and emergency use authorisation

This unit covers syllabus chapter 14 (7 hours) – the largest of the "new" topics in ER-2020. It explains how a new medicine reaches the market, how its trials are controlled in India, how generics are approved, and how patents and trade names protect innovation.

## 11.1 New drug development – the journey

Developing a new medicine usually takes **10–15 years** and very large costs; only a small fraction of compounds that enter testing are finally approved.

```text
Drug discovery (target, lead compound, optimisation)
        |
Preclinical studies (laboratory and animal: pharmacology, toxicology, pharmacokinetics,
                     formulation, stability)
        |
Application to start human trials (IND in the USA; clinical trial permission in India)
        |
Phase I  -> Phase II -> Phase III  (clinical trials)
        |
Marketing application (NDA in the USA; new drug permission in India)
        |
Approval and launch
        |
Phase IV (post-marketing surveillance, pharmacovigilance)
```

### Preclinical studies

- **Pharmacology** – what the drug does (efficacy in models) and how (mechanism).
- **Toxicology** – single-dose (acute) and repeated-dose toxicity, reproductive toxicity, genotoxicity (mutagenicity), carcinogenicity where needed, local tolerance.
- **Pharmacokinetics** – absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion in animals.
- Done under **Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)**, with animal experiments approved by an IAEC (Unit 6).

## 11.2 Basic concepts of clinical trials

A **clinical trial** is a systematic study of a new drug in human participants to find out its **safety, efficacy, pharmacokinetics and dose**, carried out according to an approved protocol.

### Phases

| Phase | Participants | Main purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phase 0 (optional) | Very few, tiny "micro" doses | Early pharmacokinetic information |
| **Phase I** | Small number of **healthy volunteers** (patients for very toxic drugs such as anticancer agents) | Safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, safe dose range |
| **Phase II** | Small number of **patients** with the disease | Preliminary efficacy, best dose, short-term safety |
| **Phase III** | Large number of patients, often in many centres | Confirm efficacy, compare with standard treatment, detect less common side-effects; basis for approval |
| **Phase IV** | Patients after marketing | Long-term safety, rare adverse effects, new uses (post-marketing surveillance) |

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Protocol | Written plan of the trial: objectives, design, participants, dose, procedures, statistics |
| Sponsor | The company or institution that starts and funds the trial |
| Investigator | The qualified doctor who conducts the trial at a site |
| Informed consent | Voluntary agreement of a participant after being told the purpose, procedures, risks, benefits and rights (including the right to withdraw), documented in writing (audio-video recording in some cases as the rules require) |
| Ethics committee | Independent committee that approves and monitors trials to protect participants |
| Randomisation and blinding | Methods to avoid bias (participants or investigators do not know who gets which treatment) |
| Placebo | An inactive look-alike used for comparison |
| Serious adverse event (SAE) | Death, life-threatening event, hospitalisation, disability or birth defect during the trial; must be reported within set timelines |
| Good Clinical Practice (GCP) | International and Indian standard for design, conduct, recording and reporting of trials (ICH-GCP and Indian GCP guidelines) |

Trials in India must be **registered with the Clinical Trials Registry – India (CTRI)** (run by ICMR) **before** enrolling the first participant.

## 11.3 New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019

These rules (made under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act) replaced **Part XA and Schedule Y** of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules for new drugs and clinical trials.

### Main features

1. **Central Licensing Authority (CLA)** = the DCGI, who grants permission for clinical trials, bioavailability/bioequivalence studies, import and manufacture of new drugs.
2. **Ethics committees** must be **registered** with the CLA (for clinical trials and BA/BE studies) and follow set composition and procedures.
3. **Time-bound decisions** – an application to conduct a clinical trial is decided within a set number of working days; a shorter timeline applies to drugs **discovered and developed in India**. Check the current rules for the exact days.
4. **New drug** definition covers new substances, new indications, dosage forms, routes and combinations; an approved new drug **remains a "new drug" for four years** from its approval.
5. **Compensation** – participants are entitled to **free medical management** for injury and to **financial compensation** for trial-related injury or death, calculated by a set formula.
6. **Academic clinical trials** (by institutions, of already approved drugs for new uses, not for commercial purposes) need ethics committee approval and do not need CLA permission if conditions are met.
7. **Waiver of local trials** is possible for drugs already approved in specified countries, in situations such as unmet medical need, orphan drugs or national emergencies.
8. **Post-marketing obligations** – periodic safety update reports (PSURs) after approval, at set intervals.
9. **Accelerated approval** for drugs for serious or life-threatening conditions or unmet needs, based on early evidence, with confirmatory trials afterwards.

## 11.4 NDA and ANDA

These terms come from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) but are used worldwide.

| Point | NDA (New Drug Application) | ANDA (Abbreviated New Drug Application) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| For | A **new drug** (innovator product) | A **generic** copy of an approved drug |
| Data needed | Full **preclinical and clinical** (Phase I–III) data, chemistry, manufacturing and controls, labelling | **Pharmaceutical equivalence** (same active ingredient, strength, dosage form, route) and **bioequivalence** with the reference listed drug; chemistry and manufacturing data |
| Clinical trials | Required | Not repeated – safety and efficacy rely on the innovator's data |
| Cost and time | Very high | Much lower |
| Result | Brand-name innovator product | Generic product, usually cheaper |

The **IND (Investigational New Drug application)** is filed before human trials begin. In India the equivalent steps are clinical trial permission and new drug permission under the NDCT Rules, and a generic is approved by the State licensing authority with **bioequivalence** data where required.

## 11.5 BCS – Biopharmaceutics Classification System

The BCS (proposed by Amidon and co-workers in **1995**) classifies drug substances by their **aqueous solubility** and **intestinal permeability**, the two factors that mainly control oral absorption.

| Class | Solubility | Permeability | Absorption limited by | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I** | High | High | Gastric emptying (absorption usually good) | Metoprolol, propranolol |
| **II** | Low | High | **Dissolution** | Carbamazepine, ketoconazole, glibenclamide, phenytoin |
| **III** | High | Low | **Permeability** | Atenolol, metformin, cimetidine |
| **IV** | Low | Low | Both; poor, variable absorption | Furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide |

### Definitions used

- **Highly soluble** – the highest dose (or strength) dissolves in **250 mL or less** of aqueous media over the **pH range 1.2–6.8** at 37 °C.
- **Highly permeable** – the extent of absorption in humans is **85% or more** (older guidance used 90%).
- **Rapidly dissolving** – at least 85% of the labelled amount dissolves within 30 minutes.

### Uses of BCS

1. **Biowaiver** – for immediate-release oral products of Class I (and Class III under strict conditions), regulators may accept dissolution data instead of an in-vivo bioequivalence study.
2. Guides **formulation**: Class II drugs need solubility enhancement (salt forms, particle size reduction, solid dispersions).
3. Predicts **in vitro–in vivo correlation** and the effect of food.

## 11.6 Brand versus generic; trade name concept

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic (non-proprietary) name | The official scientific name of the drug, usually the **International Non-proprietary Name (INN)** given by WHO; used in pharmacopoeias | Paracetamol |
| Brand (trade, proprietary) name | A name given by a company to its product; protected as a **trademark** | Various company brands of paracetamol |
| Generic medicine | A medicine equivalent to the innovator product in active ingredient, strength, dosage form, route, quality and performance, sold after patent expiry | Paracetamol tablets sold by the generic name |
| Branded generic | A generic medicine sold under a company's brand name – very common in India | |

### Brand vs generic

| Point | Branded product | Generic product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Trade name | Generic (INN) name |
| Price | Usually higher (promotion, brand value) | Usually lower |
| Quality | Must meet the same standards | Must meet the same standards (IP) and show bioequivalence where required |
| Appearance | Company's own colour/shape | May differ in shape, colour, excipients |

### Trade name concept

- A **trademark** is a word, symbol or design that distinguishes one company's goods from another's; protected under the **Trade Marks Act, 1999**. ® means registered; ™ means claimed.
- **Look-alike, sound-alike** brand names can cause dispensing errors; pharmacists should read prescriptions carefully and confirm doubtful names.
- To lower costs, the Government promotes **generic prescribing** and sells quality generics through **Jan Aushadhi** stores (Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana).

## 11.7 Introduction to patent law and intellectual property rights

### Intellectual property rights (IPR)

**IPR** are legal rights given to creators over the products of their minds, for a limited time.

| Type | Protects | Example in pharmacy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Patent** | Inventions (products, processes) | A new molecule or new process |
| **Trademark** | Brand names, logos | Brand name of a tablet |
| **Copyright** | Literary and artistic works, software | Textbooks, package insert text |
| **Industrial design** | Shape and appearance | Shape of an inhaler |
| **Geographical indication** | Products linked to a place | Regional herbal or food products |
| **Trade secret** | Confidential business information | Manufacturing know-how |

The **WTO TRIPS Agreement (1995)** set minimum standards of IPR protection for member countries, including India.

### The Patents Act, 1970

- A **patent** is an exclusive right granted by the Government to an inventor to **make, use, sell or import** the invention for a limited period, in exchange for **full public disclosure** of the invention.
- **Term:** **20 years from the date of filing** the application.
- **Criteria for patentability:**
  1. **Novelty** – new, not published or used before.
  2. **Inventive step** – not obvious to a person skilled in the field.
  3. **Industrial application** – can be made or used in industry.
  4. Not in the list of **non-patentable** inventions.
- Originally the 1970 Act allowed only **process patents** for medicines, which let Indian companies make cheaper generics. To meet TRIPS, the **2005 amendment** brought back **product patents** for medicines.
- **Section 3(d)** – a new form of a known substance (salt, ester, polymorph and the like) is **not patentable unless it shows significantly enhanced efficacy**. This prevents "**evergreening**". The Supreme Court upheld it in 2013 when it refused a patent for a new form of imatinib.
- **Compulsory licence** – after a set period from grant, the Controller may let another company make a patented product if it is not available to the public at a reasonably affordable price or not worked in India. India's first compulsory licence was granted in **2012** for an anticancer drug (sorafenib).
- The **Bolar exception** allows generic companies to use a patented invention for developing data for regulatory approval before the patent expires.
- Patents are granted by the **Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks** through the Indian Patent Office, after application, publication, examination and opposition stages.

## 11.8 Emergency use authorisation (EUA)

- **Emergency use authorisation** allows the use of a medicine, vaccine or diagnostic that is **not yet fully approved**, during a **public health emergency**, when:
  1. the disease is serious or life-threatening;
  2. available evidence suggests the product **may be effective** and its known benefits outweigh known risks; and
  3. there is **no adequate approved alternative**.
- It is **temporary** and can be revoked; trials must continue, safety is closely monitored, and recipients are given information about the product.
- In the USA it is granted by the FDA; WHO has a similar **Emergency Use Listing**.
- In India, the CLA used the NDCT Rules to grant **restricted use in an emergency situation** to COVID-19 vaccines (in **January 2021**) and several medicines during the pandemic.

## Remember

- Drug development: discovery → **preclinical** → **Phase I** (healthy volunteers, safety) → **II** (patients, dose) → **III** (large, confirm) → approval → **IV** (post-marketing).
- **NDCT Rules 2019** replaced Part XA and **Schedule Y**; CLA = DCGI; registered ethics committees; compensation; new drug status for **four years**; trials registered in **CTRI**.
- **NDA** = full data for a new drug; **ANDA** = generic with **bioequivalence**.
- **BCS**: I high/high, II low sol/high perm, III high sol/low perm, IV low/low; high solubility = dose in **≤250 mL**, pH **1.2–6.8**.
- Patent term **20 years**; criteria **novelty, inventive step, industrial application**; **Section 3(d)** stops evergreening; product patents since **2005**.
- **EUA** = temporary approval in emergencies when benefits outweigh risks and no alternative exists.
