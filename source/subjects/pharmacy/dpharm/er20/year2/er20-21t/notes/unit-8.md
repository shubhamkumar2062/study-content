# Unit 8: Drugs acting on the gastrointestinal tract

This unit covers syllabus chapter 8: anti-ulcer drugs, antiemetics, laxatives and purgatives, and antidiarrhoeal drugs.

## 8.1 Anti-ulcer drugs

### Peptic ulcer

A **peptic ulcer** is a break in the lining of the stomach (gastric ulcer) or duodenum (duodenal ulcer) caused by acid and pepsin. It occurs when **aggressive factors** outweigh **protective factors**.

```text
 Aggressive factors                      Protective factors
  • Acid (HCl) and pepsin                 • Mucus and bicarbonate layer
  • Helicobacter pylori infection         • Prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2)
  • NSAIDs (block protective PGs)         • Good mucosal blood flow
  • Smoking, alcohol, stress              • Rapid cell renewal
          ULCER when aggressive > protective
```

**Symptoms:** burning epigastric pain (gastric ulcer – worse after food; duodenal ulcer – relieved by food, pain at night), nausea, bloating. **Complications:** bleeding (black stools, vomiting blood), perforation, obstruction.

**Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD):** acid flows back into the oesophagus → heartburn; treated with the same acid-reducing drugs (PPIs first).

### Control of acid secretion

```text
 Parietal cell receptors:  H2 (histamine), M3 (ACh), CCK2 (gastrin)
        ↓
 Final step: H+/K+ ATPase ("proton pump") pushes H+ into the stomach
 PPIs block the pump (final common pathway) → most powerful acid suppression
```

### Classification

```text
 1. Drugs that reduce acid secretion
    a. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs): omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole,
       lansoprazole, rabeprazole
    b. H2 receptor antagonists: ranitidine (withdrawn in many countries), famotidine,
       cimetidine, roxatidine
    c. Anticholinergics: pirenzepine (little used)
    d. Prostaglandin analogue: misoprostol
 2. Drugs that neutralise acid (antacids)
    Systemic: sodium bicarbonate
    Non-systemic: magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, magnesium trisilicate,
                  calcium carbonate
 3. Ulcer protectives (mucosal protective agents)
    Sucralfate, colloidal bismuth subcitrate
 4. Anti-Helicobacter pylori drugs
    Amoxicillin, clarithromycin, metronidazole, tinidazole, tetracycline, bismuth
 5. Potassium-competitive acid blocker: vonoprazan (newer)
```

### Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)

**Mechanism:** prodrugs that become active in the acidic canaliculus of the parietal cell and **irreversibly block H+/K+ ATPase** → profound fall in acid (all stimuli).
**Use:** take **30–60 minutes before breakfast** (pumps are active after a meal). Omeprazole 20 mg or pantoprazole 40 mg once daily are common doses.
**Uses:** **peptic ulcer** (drug of choice), **GERD**, **H. pylori eradication** (with antibiotics), prevention of NSAID ulcers, stress ulcers in ICU, **Zollinger–Ellison syndrome** (gastrin-secreting tumour), upper GI bleeding (IV).
**Adverse effects:** usually safe; headache, diarrhoea; long-term – vitamin B12 deficiency, low magnesium, fractures, risk of gut infections (*C. difficile*), pneumonia. Omeprazole may reduce the effect of clopidogrel.

### H2 receptor antagonists

**Mechanism:** competitive blockade of **H2 receptors** on parietal cells → ↓ acid (especially night-time secretion).
**Drugs:** famotidine (common now), cimetidine.
**Uses:** peptic ulcer, GERD (milder), stress ulcer prevention, pre-anaesthetic (acid aspiration).
**Adverse effects:** headache, dizziness. **Cimetidine:** enzyme inhibitor (raises levels of warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline), antiandrogenic effects (gynaecomastia, impotence) – largely replaced.

### Antacids

**Definition:** weak bases that neutralise gastric acid and give quick symptom relief.

| Antacid | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium bicarbonate | Fast, systemic; may cause **alkalosis**, sodium load (avoid in heart failure, hypertension), CO2 belching and rebound acid |
| **Magnesium hydroxide** | Fast; **diarrhoea** (laxative) |
| **Aluminium hydroxide** | Slow; **constipation**; binds phosphate (hypophosphataemia) |
| Calcium carbonate | Effective; constipation, hypercalcaemia, rebound acid |

**Combinations** of magnesium and aluminium hydroxide balance diarrhoea and constipation (with simethicone for gas).
**Interactions:** antacids reduce absorption of **tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, iron, digoxin** – give these 2 hours apart.
**Uses:** occasional heartburn, dyspepsia. Avoid magnesium salts in kidney failure.

### Ulcer protectives

- **Sucralfate:** in acid it forms a sticky gel that coats the ulcer base; also stimulates PGs. Take on an empty stomach; **do not give with antacids** (needs acid); constipation; binds other drugs.
- **Colloidal bismuth subcitrate:** coats the ulcer, kills H. pylori; **black tongue and stools**.
- **Misoprostol:** PGE1 analogue – ↓ acid and ↑ mucus; prevents **NSAID-induced ulcers**; diarrhoea, abdominal cramps; **contraindicated in pregnancy** (uterine contraction, abortion).

### Helicobacter pylori eradication

H. pylori is a spiral bacterium living under the gastric mucus; it causes most duodenal ulcers and many gastric ulcers. Eradication heals the ulcer and prevents recurrence.

**Standard triple therapy (usually for 14 days):** **PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin**; metronidazole replaces amoxicillin in penicillin allergy. Bismuth-based quadruple therapy (PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole) where resistance is common.

## 8.2 Antiemetics

**Vomiting (emesis)** is the forceful expulsion of stomach contents through the mouth. It is coordinated by the **vomiting centre** in the medulla, which receives inputs from several areas.

```text
 Chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) – D2, 5-HT3 receptors (drugs, toxins, uraemia)
 Gut (vagus) – 5-HT3 receptors (chemotherapy, gastritis)
 Vestibular apparatus – H1, M1 receptors (motion sickness)
 Higher centres – smells, sights, anxiety
              ↓
         VOMITING CENTRE (medulla) – M, H1, NK1 receptors → vomiting
```

### Classification

```text
 1. Anticholinergics: hyoscine (scopolamine)
 2. H1 antihistamines: promethazine, dimenhydrinate, meclizine (meclozine),
    cyclizine, doxylamine (with pyridoxine)
 3. Dopamine D2 antagonists
    a. Prokinetics: metoclopramide, domperidone
    b. Neuroleptics: prochlorperazine, chlorpromazine, haloperidol
 4. 5-HT3 antagonists: ondansetron, granisetron, palonosetron
 5. NK1 receptor antagonists: aprepitant, fosaprepitant
 6. Adjuvants: dexamethasone, lorazepam, cannabinoids (dronabinol)
```

| Drug | Mechanism | Main use | Important adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hyoscine** | Muscarinic block | **Motion sickness** (best prophylactic, patch behind ear) | Dry mouth, drowsiness, blurred vision |
| **Promethazine, dimenhydrinate, meclizine** | H1 + anticholinergic | Motion sickness, morning sickness (doxylamine–pyridoxine), postoperative vomiting | Sedation, dry mouth |
| **Metoclopramide** | D2 block in CTZ + **prokinetic** (↑ gastric emptying, 5-HT4) | Vomiting due to drugs, gastritis, migraine, gastroparesis, GERD | **Extrapyramidal effects** (dystonia, especially young people), raised prolactin, drowsiness |
| **Domperidone** | Peripheral D2 block (does not cross BBB) | Nausea, vomiting, gastroparesis; vomiting with levodopa | Raised prolactin, **QT prolongation** – low dose, short course |
| Prochlorperazine | D2 block | Vertigo, vomiting | EPS, sedation |
| **Ondansetron** | **5-HT3 antagonist** (gut vagal and CTZ) | **Chemotherapy-induced** and radiotherapy and postoperative vomiting; drug of choice for these | Headache, constipation, QT prolongation |
| Aprepitant | NK1 block | With ondansetron + dexamethasone for highly emetogenic chemotherapy | Fatigue, hiccups |

**Choosing an antiemetic:** motion sickness – hyoscine or H1 antihistamine (take before travel); chemotherapy – ondansetron + dexamethasone ± aprepitant; pregnancy – doxylamine–pyridoxine, then others under advice; postoperative – ondansetron.

**Contraindications/cautions:** metoclopramide and domperidone in GI obstruction, bleeding or perforation; metoclopramide in epilepsy and with other drugs causing EPS; anticholinergics in glaucoma and prostate enlargement.

## 8.3 Laxatives and purgatives

**Definitions:** a **laxative** produces a softer, formed stool (mild action); a **purgative (cathartic)** produces more fluid evacuation (stronger action). The same drug can act as either depending on dose.

**Constipation:** infrequent, hard or difficult passage of stools. First treatment: **more fibre, fluids and exercise**.

### Classification

```text
 1. Bulk-forming: ispaghula (psyllium husk), methylcellulose, bran, sterculia
 2. Stool softeners (emollients/surfactants): docusate sodium, liquid paraffin (lubricant)
 3. Stimulant (irritant) purgatives
    Diphenylmethanes: bisacodyl, sodium picosulfate
    Anthraquinones: senna, cascara, aloes
    Castor oil
 4. Osmotic purgatives
    Saline: magnesium sulphate, magnesium hydroxide, sodium phosphate
    Non-absorbable sugars: lactulose, lactitol
    Macrogols: polyethylene glycol (PEG)
 5. Others: glycerine suppository, enema; lubiprostone, prucalopride
```

| Group | How it works | Onset | Uses | Adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bulk-forming** | Absorbs water, swells → bulky soft stool, stimulates peristalsis | 1–3 days | Chronic constipation, IBS, haemorrhoids, pregnancy (safe) | Bloating; must take with plenty of water (obstruction risk) |
| **Stool softeners** | Lower surface tension, let water into stool | 1–3 days | Avoid straining (after MI, hernia, haemorrhoids) | Mild |
| Liquid paraffin | Lubricates and softens | 1–3 days | Rarely now | Lipid pneumonia if aspirated, ↓ fat-soluble vitamins, anal leakage |
| **Stimulants** (bisacodyl, senna) | Irritate mucosa and stimulate nerves → ↑ motility and secretion | 6–12 h orally (bedtime dose works by morning); bisacodyl suppository 15–60 min | Short-term constipation, bowel preparation | Cramps; long use – "cathartic colon", electrolyte loss; senna colours urine |
| **Osmotic** | Retain water in the gut by osmosis | Saline: 1–3 h (purgative); lactulose 1–2 days | Bowel cleansing before colonoscopy (PEG), food/drug poisoning; **lactulose in hepatic encephalopathy** | Dehydration, electrolyte imbalance; lactulose flatulence; magnesium salts avoid in kidney failure |
| Castor oil | Hydrolysed to ricinoleic acid (irritant) | 1–3 h | Rarely used | Cramps |

**Lactulose in hepatic encephalopathy:** colon bacteria break it into acids → ammonia changes to non-absorbable ammonium (NH4+) and is removed → less ammonia reaches the brain.

**Contraindications of laxatives:** **undiagnosed abdominal pain**, suspected **appendicitis or intestinal obstruction**, faecal impaction (bulk agents), inflammatory bowel disease (stimulants); stimulants in pregnancy (with care) and long-term use; magnesium in kidney failure.

**Laxative abuse** (often for weight loss) causes loss of potassium, weakness and dependence.

## 8.4 Antidiarrhoeal drugs

**Diarrhoea** is the passage of three or more loose or watery stools a day. Most cases are due to infection (viruses, bacteria such as *E. coli*, *Vibrio cholerae*, Shigella; amoebae). The main danger is **dehydration**, especially in children.

### Management

1. **Oral rehydration solution (ORS)** – the mainstay of treatment.
2. **Zinc** for children (WHO: 10–20 mg a day for 10–14 days) – shortens and reduces diarrhoea.
3. Continue feeding and breastfeeding.
4. Antimicrobials only when indicated (cholera, dysentery with blood, amoebiasis, giardiasis).
5. Symptomatic antidiarrhoeal drugs in adults only, and not in bloody diarrhoea.

### Oral rehydration solution

**WHO low-osmolarity ORS** (per litre of water): sodium chloride 2.6 g, glucose (anhydrous) 13.5 g, potassium chloride 1.5 g, trisodium citrate dihydrate 2.9 g; total osmolarity **245 mOsm/L**.
**Principle:** **glucose-coupled sodium absorption** in the small intestine continues even in diarrhoea; water follows the sodium. Citrate corrects acidosis; potassium replaces losses.

### Classification of antidiarrhoeal drugs

```text
 1. Antimotility drugs (opioids): loperamide, diphenoxylate (with atropine), codeine
 2. Antisecretory drugs: racecadotril (enkephalinase inhibitor), bismuth subsalicylate,
    octreotide (in special cases)
 3. Adsorbents / bulk agents: kaolin, pectin, activated charcoal, ispaghula
 4. Antimicrobials: metronidazole, tinidazole (amoebiasis, giardiasis), ciprofloxacin,
    azithromycin (shigella, cholera), doxycycline (cholera), cotrimoxazole
 5. Probiotics: Lactobacillus, Saccharomyces boulardii
 6. Zinc (children)
```

| Drug | Mechanism and points |
| --- | --- |
| **Loperamide** | Opioid µ agonist acting on gut; does not enter brain at normal doses (no dependence); ↓ motility and secretion; adults – traveller's diarrhoea, chronic diarrhoea. **Avoid in young children, and not in bloody diarrhoea or high fever** (may prolong infection, toxic megacolon) |
| Diphenoxylate + atropine | Atropine added in small amount to discourage abuse |
| **Racecadotril** | Prevents breakdown of enkephalins in the gut → ↓ secretion without slowing motility; used in children and adults with ORS |
| Kaolin, pectin | Adsorb toxins and water; improve stool consistency but do not reduce fluid loss; may bind other drugs |
| Probiotics | Restore normal flora; antibiotic-associated diarrhoea |

**Contraindications of antimotility drugs:** bloody diarrhoea (dysentery), infection with fever, ulcerative colitis flare, children; can mask fluid loss.

## Remember

- PPIs (omeprazole) irreversibly block H+/K+ ATPase – most powerful; take before breakfast.
- H2 blockers reduce acid; cimetidine is an enzyme inhibitor with antiandrogen effects.
- Magnesium antacids cause diarrhoea, aluminium antacids cause constipation; antacids reduce absorption of tetracyclines and iron.
- Sucralfate needs acid – not with antacids; misoprostol prevents NSAID ulcers but is banned in pregnancy.
- H. pylori triple therapy: PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin (14 days).
- Ondansetron (5-HT3) for chemotherapy vomiting; hyoscine for motion sickness; metoclopramide → EPS; domperidone → QT.
- Bulk laxatives are safest; lactulose for hepatic encephalopathy; laxatives contraindicated in undiagnosed abdominal pain and obstruction.
- ORS is the mainstay of diarrhoea treatment (245 mOsm/L; glucose-coupled sodium absorption); zinc for children; loperamide not in bloody diarrhoea or young children.
