# Unit 6: Drugs acting on blood and blood-forming organs

This unit covers syllabus chapter 6: haematinic agents, anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents and thrombolytic (fibrinolytic) drugs.

## 6.1 Haematinic agents

**Haematinics** are drugs needed for the formation of blood (haemoglobin and red cells), used to prevent and treat **anaemia**. The main ones are **iron, vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin) and folic acid**; erythropoietin stimulates red cell production.

**Anaemia** means a fall in haemoglobin below normal for age and sex (WHO: below 13 g/dl in men, 12 g/dl in non-pregnant women, 11 g/dl in pregnancy). Symptoms: tiredness, breathlessness, pallor, palpitation.

| Type of anaemia | Red cells | Common causes | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Iron-deficiency** | Small and pale (microcytic, hypochromic) | Blood loss (menstrual, hookworm, ulcers), poor diet, pregnancy, growth | **Iron** |
| **Megaloblastic** | Large (macrocytic) | Deficiency of **vitamin B12** or **folic acid** | B12 and/or folic acid |
| Anaemia of chronic kidney disease | Normocytic | Low erythropoietin | **Erythropoietin** (epoetin, darbepoetin) |
| Haemolytic, aplastic | Varies | Destruction or marrow failure | Treat cause; transfusion |

### Iron

**Absorption:** in the duodenum and upper jejunum as **ferrous (Fe2+)** iron. Absorption is increased by **vitamin C** (ascorbic acid), acid and body need; reduced by antacids, tea (tannins), phosphates, phytates, calcium and tetracyclines. Iron is carried in blood by **transferrin** and stored as **ferritin** and haemosiderin. The body has no active way to excrete iron.

**Oral preparations (preferred):** ferrous sulphate, ferrous fumarate, ferrous gluconate, ferrous ascorbate, carbonyl iron, iron polysaccharide complex. Dose is expressed as **elemental iron** – usually about **100–200 mg elemental iron a day** for treating deficiency in adults. Hb rises by about 1 g/dl per week (roughly), and treatment continues for **about 3 months after Hb becomes normal** to refill stores.

Under the national anaemia programme, pregnant women receive iron with folic acid tablets for prevention.

**Adverse effects (oral):** epigastric pain, nausea, **constipation** or diarrhoea, **black stools** (harmless – tell the patient), metallic taste, staining of teeth (liquid – use a straw).

**Tips:** take on an empty stomach for best absorption, or after food if it upsets the stomach; avoid tea and antacids near the dose.

**Parenteral iron:** **iron sucrose** (IV), **ferric carboxymaltose** (IV, large single doses), iron dextran (IM/IV; risk of anaphylaxis – test dose). Used when oral iron fails, is not tolerated, in malabsorption, or when rapid repletion is needed (late pregnancy, chronic kidney disease). Adverse effects: allergic reactions, pain and staining at IM site, fever, joint pain.

**Acute iron poisoning (children):** vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, shock, liver damage. Antidote: **desferrioxamine** (deferoxamine) chelator.

**Contraindications of iron therapy:** anaemia not due to iron deficiency (thalassaemia, haemolytic anaemia), haemochromatosis (iron overload).

### Vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin, hydroxocobalamin, methylcobalamin)

**Role:** needed for DNA synthesis (with folate) and for the myelin of nerves.
**Absorption:** combines with **intrinsic factor** from the stomach and is absorbed in the **terminal ileum**.
**Causes of deficiency:** **pernicious anaemia** (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor), stomach or ileum surgery, strict vegetarian diet, fish tapeworm, long-term metformin or PPIs.
**Features:** megaloblastic anaemia, glossitis, and **neurological damage** (numbness, tingling, subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord).
**Treatment:** **hydroxocobalamin or cyanocobalamin injections (IM)**, especially in pernicious anaemia (lifelong); oral methylcobalamin for dietary deficiency and neuropathy. Very safe.

### Folic acid

**Role:** DNA synthesis. Deficiency → megaloblastic anaemia; in early pregnancy → **neural tube defects** in the baby.
**Causes of deficiency:** poor diet, alcoholism, pregnancy, malabsorption, drugs (**phenytoin, methotrexate, trimethoprim**, pyrimethamine).
**Uses:** megaloblastic anaemia due to folate deficiency; **prevention of neural tube defects** (before conception and in early pregnancy); with antiepileptics; **folinic acid (leucovorin)** to rescue normal cells after high-dose methotrexate.
**Caution:** never give folic acid alone in B12 deficiency – it corrects the blood picture but the **nerve damage continues and may worsen**.

### Erythropoietin

Hormone made by the kidney; recombinant **epoetin alfa, darbepoetin** stimulate red cell formation. Uses: anaemia of chronic kidney disease, cancer chemotherapy, HIV (zidovudine). Adverse effects: hypertension, thrombosis; iron stores must be adequate.

## 6.2 Blood coagulation in brief

```text
 Injury → platelets stick (adhesion) and clump (aggregation) → platelet plug
        → clotting cascade (factors, mostly made in liver; II, VII, IX, X need vitamin K)
        → Prothrombin (II) ──(Xa)──► Thrombin (IIa)
        → Fibrinogen ──(thrombin)──► Fibrin → stable clot
 Later: Plasminogen ──(tissue plasminogen activator)──► Plasmin → dissolves fibrin
 Natural anticoagulant: antithrombin (inactivates thrombin and Xa)
```

| Group | Prevents/treats | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anticoagulants | Formation and growth of clots, mainly in **veins** (DVT, pulmonary embolism, AF stroke) | Heparin, warfarin, rivaroxaban |
| Antiplatelets | Platelet clumping in **arteries** (heart attack, stroke) | Aspirin, clopidogrel |
| Thrombolytics | **Dissolve** an existing clot | Streptokinase, alteplase |

## 6.3 Anticoagulants

**Definition:** drugs that reduce the coagulability of blood and prevent the formation or extension of clots.

### Classification

```text
 A. Parenteral
    1. Unfractionated heparin (UFH)
    2. Low molecular weight heparins (LMWH): enoxaparin, dalteparin
    3. Fondaparinux (synthetic pentasaccharide)
    4. Direct thrombin inhibitors: bivalirudin, argatroban
 B. Oral
    1. Vitamin K antagonists (coumarins): warfarin, acenocoumarol (nicoumalone)
    2. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs/NOACs):
       Factor Xa inhibitors: rivaroxaban, apixaban
       Direct thrombin inhibitor: dabigatran
 C. Used outside the body (in vitro): sodium citrate, EDTA, oxalate, heparin
```

### Heparin

**Source:** mucosa of pig intestine or ox lung; a strongly acidic (negatively charged) polysaccharide.
**Mechanism:** binds **antithrombin III** and speeds up its inactivation of **thrombin (IIa) and factor Xa** about 1000 times. LMWH acts mainly on factor Xa.
**Features:** acts **immediately**; not absorbed orally – given **IV or SC** (never IM – haematoma); monitored by **aPTT** (UFH). LMWH: given SC once or twice daily, more predictable, usually no routine monitoring.
**Uses:** treatment and prevention of **deep vein thrombosis (DVT)** and pulmonary embolism; acute coronary syndrome; during heart surgery, dialysis and to keep IV lines open; **anticoagulant of choice in pregnancy** (does not cross placenta).
**Adverse effects:** **bleeding**, **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)**, osteoporosis (long use), allergy, alopecia.
**Antidote:** **protamine sulphate** (strongly basic; neutralises heparin).

### Warfarin

**Mechanism:** blocks the enzyme **vitamin K epoxide reductase**, so the liver cannot make active clotting factors **II, VII, IX and X** (and proteins C and S).
**Features:** **oral**; effect starts only after **2–4 days** (existing factors must be used up), so heparin is started together and overlapped; long action. Monitored by **prothrombin time expressed as INR** (target usually 2–3).
**Uses:** long-term prevention of DVT/pulmonary embolism, stroke prevention in **atrial fibrillation**, after mechanical heart valve replacement.
**Adverse effects:** **bleeding** (gums, urine, bruises, GI), skin necrosis (rare), **teratogenic** (bone and facial defects) – **contraindicated in pregnancy**.
**Antidote:** **vitamin K1 (phytomenadione)**; fresh frozen plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate for serious bleeding.
**Interactions (many):** effect increased by aspirin and NSAIDs, cotrimoxazole, metronidazole, fluconazole, erythromycin, amiodarone, cimetidine; reduced by rifampicin, phenobarbitone, carbamazepine (enzyme inducers), vitamin K-rich food changes, oral contraceptives.

### Comparison

| Feature | Heparin | Warfarin |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Route | IV, SC | Oral |
| Onset | Immediate | 2–4 days |
| Acts in vitro? | Yes | No |
| Mechanism | Activates antithrombin | Blocks vitamin K-dependent factor synthesis |
| Monitoring | aPTT | PT/INR |
| Antidote | Protamine sulphate | Vitamin K1 |
| Pregnancy | Safe | Contraindicated |

**DOACs (rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran):** fixed oral doses, no routine INR monitoring, fewer food interactions; used in non-valvular AF and DVT; bleeding is the main risk; avoid in severe kidney disease and with mechanical valves.

### Contraindications of anticoagulants

Active bleeding, bleeding disorders, recent brain, eye or spinal surgery, haemorrhagic stroke, severe uncontrolled hypertension, peptic ulcer, severe liver or kidney disease, threatened abortion; warfarin in pregnancy.

## 6.4 Antiplatelet agents

**Definition:** drugs that prevent platelets from sticking together (aggregation), mainly used to prevent clots in **arteries**.

### Classification

```text
 1. COX inhibitor: aspirin (low dose)
 2. P2Y12 (ADP) receptor blockers: clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor, ticlopidine
 3. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor blockers (IV): abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban
 4. Phosphodiesterase inhibitors: dipyridamole, cilostazol
```

| Drug | Mechanism | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aspirin** (75–150 mg daily) | **Irreversibly** inhibits platelet COX-1 → ↓ thromboxane A2 for the whole life of the platelet (7–10 days) | Gastric irritation, bleeding; stop about a week before major surgery if advised |
| **Clopidogrel** | Prodrug; irreversibly blocks platelet **ADP (P2Y12) receptor** | Combined with aspirin ("dual antiplatelet therapy") after stents and acute coronary syndrome; bleeding; less effective with omeprazole (prefer pantoprazole) |
| Ticagrelor, prasugrel | P2Y12 block, faster and stronger | More bleeding; ticagrelor – breathlessness |
| Abciximab, tirofiban | Block final common pathway (GP IIb/IIIa) | IV, during angioplasty |
| Dipyridamole | ↑ cAMP in platelets | With aspirin in stroke prevention; headache |
| Cilostazol | PDE-3 inhibitor; vasodilator | Intermittent claudication; avoid in heart failure |

**Uses:** prevention of heart attack and stroke in people at risk (secondary prevention), acute MI and unstable angina, after coronary stenting and bypass surgery, transient ischaemic attacks, peripheral artery disease.

**Contraindications:** active bleeding, peptic ulcer, bleeding disorders, aspirin allergy/asthma (aspirin), severe liver disease.

## 6.5 Thrombolytic (fibrinolytic) drugs

**Definition:** drugs that **dissolve** a clot that has already formed, by converting plasminogen to **plasmin**, which breaks down fibrin.

### Classification

```text
 1. Streptokinase (from β-haemolytic streptococci) – non-fibrin specific, antigenic
 2. Urokinase (from human urine / kidney cells)
 3. Tissue plasminogen activators (recombinant): alteplase, reteplase, tenecteplase
    – relatively fibrin-specific (act more on clot-bound plasminogen)
```

**Uses:**
1. **Acute myocardial infarction (STEMI)** – given as early as possible, best within the first hours ("time is muscle") when angioplasty is not available.
2. **Acute ischaemic stroke** – alteplase within a few hours of onset, only after a CT scan excludes bleeding.
3. Massive pulmonary embolism, extensive DVT, blocked catheters and shunts.

**Adverse effects:** **bleeding**, including **intracranial haemorrhage** (most serious); streptokinase: **allergy, fever, hypotension**, and antibodies after first use (should not be repeated within months).

**Contraindications:** active internal bleeding, history of haemorrhagic stroke, recent stroke, recent major surgery or trauma, severe uncontrolled hypertension, brain tumour or aneurysm, bleeding disorders, pregnancy (relative).

**Antifibrinolytic (to stop excessive bleeding):** **tranexamic acid** and epsilon-aminocaproic acid block plasminogen activation; used in heavy menstrual bleeding, after dental extraction in haemophilia, trauma and thrombolytic overdose.

### Haemostatics (related)

Vitamin K (phytomenadione) for bleeding due to warfarin or vitamin K deficiency and to prevent **haemorrhagic disease of the newborn** (vitamin K injection at birth); thrombin and fibrin foam as local haemostatics; ethamsylate.

## Remember

- Iron is absorbed as Fe2+ in the duodenum; vitamin C helps, tea and antacids reduce; black stools are harmless; continue 3 months after Hb normalises; poisoning → desferrioxamine.
- B12 needs intrinsic factor and is absorbed in the terminal ileum; pernicious anaemia needs injections.
- Never give folic acid alone in B12 deficiency; folic acid before conception prevents neural tube defects.
- Heparin: immediate, IV/SC, activates antithrombin, aPTT, antidote protamine; safe in pregnancy.
- Warfarin: oral, slow onset, blocks vitamin K factors II, VII, IX, X, INR 2–3, antidote vitamin K; teratogenic; many interactions.
- Aspirin low dose irreversibly blocks platelet thromboxane A2; clopidogrel blocks ADP receptor.
- Thrombolytics (streptokinase, alteplase) dissolve clots via plasmin; main danger is bleeding; tranexamic acid opposes them.
