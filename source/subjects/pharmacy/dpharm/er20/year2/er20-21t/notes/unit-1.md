# Unit 1: General pharmacology

This unit covers syllabus chapter 1: scope of pharmacology, routes of drug administration, absorption, bioavailability, distribution, biotransformation, excretion, mechanisms of drug action and factors that modify drug action.

## 1.1 Introduction and scope of pharmacology

**Pharmacology** is the science that studies drugs and how they interact with living systems. It covers where drugs come from, their properties, what they do to the body, what the body does to them, and their uses and harmful effects.

**Drug** (WHO): any substance or product that is used or meant to be used to change or explore physiological systems or disease states, for the benefit of the person receiving it. Drugs are used to prevent, diagnose or treat disease.

### Main branches

| Branch | What it studies |
| --- | --- |
| **Pharmacokinetics (PK)** | What the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion (ADME) |
| **Pharmacodynamics (PD)** | What the drug does to the body: actions, mechanism of action, effects |
| Pharmacotherapeutics | Use of drugs to prevent and treat disease |
| Chemotherapy | Drugs that kill or stop the growth of microbes, parasites or cancer cells |
| Toxicology | Harmful (poisonous) effects of drugs and chemicals, and their treatment |
| Clinical pharmacology | Study of drugs in human beings, including clinical trials |
| Pharmacy | Preparation, compounding, dispensing and supply of medicines |
| Pharmacovigilance | Detection, assessment and prevention of adverse drug reactions |
| Pharmacogenetics | How genetic differences change the response to drugs |

### Some basic terms

- **Dose:** the amount of drug given at one time to produce the desired effect.
- **Therapeutic index (TI):** LD50 ÷ ED50 (in animals). A large TI means a safe drug; a small TI (digoxin, lithium, warfarin, theophylline) means the drug needs careful dosing and monitoring.
- **Essential medicines:** medicines that meet the priority health needs of the population (WHO model list; India's National List of Essential Medicines).
- **Orphan drugs:** drugs for rare diseases, which companies have little commercial reason to develop.

### Scope for a pharmacist

A pharmacist uses pharmacology to dispense safely, advise on dose and timing, spot interactions and adverse effects, counsel patients, and choose suitable over-the-counter medicines.

## 1.2 Routes of drug administration

The route depends on the drug's properties (acid stability, irritancy, absorption), the speed of action needed, the patient's condition and the site of action.

```text
 Routes
  ├── Local ── topical (skin, eye, ear, nose), deep tissue (intra-articular), intrathecal (some)
  └── Systemic
        ├── Enteral ── oral, sublingual/buccal, rectal
        └── Parenteral ── IV, IM, SC, intradermal, inhalation, transdermal, others
```

### Enteral routes

| Route | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oral** | Safe, cheap, convenient, self-use, no sterility needed | Slow onset; not usable in vomiting or unconscious patients; destroyed by gastric acid or enzymes (insulin, penicillin G); first-pass metabolism; irritant drugs cause nausea; variable absorption with food |
| **Sublingual / buccal** | Fast onset; avoids first-pass metabolism; tablet can be spat out if effect is excessive | Only for small doses of lipid-soluble, non-irritant drugs (glyceryl trinitrate, buprenorphine); bad taste |
| **Rectal** | Useful in vomiting, unconscious patients and children; partly avoids first-pass | Irregular absorption; inconvenient; rectal irritation |

### Parenteral routes (not through the gut)

| Route | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Intravenous (IV)** | Fastest, 100% bioavailability; exact dose control; large volumes and irritant drugs can be given (diluted) | Needs sterility and skill; cannot be withdrawn once given; risk of thrombophlebitis, infection, air embolism, severe reactions |
| **Intramuscular (IM)** | Fairly fast; depot (oily) preparations give long action; moderately irritant drugs tolerated | Painful; risk of nerve injury (sciatic) and abscess; volume up to about 5 ml (deltoid less) |
| **Subcutaneous (SC)** | Slow, steady absorption; self-injection possible (insulin); implants and pellets | Only small volumes of non-irritant drugs; slow in shock |
| Intradermal | Used for BCG vaccine and skin tests | Very small volumes only |
| **Inhalation** | Volatile anaesthetics and aerosols act fast on the lungs (salbutamol) with few systemic effects | Needs proper technique; irritant gases cause cough |
| Transdermal | Steady levels for days, avoids first pass (nitroglycerin, nicotine, fentanyl patches) | Only potent, lipid-soluble drugs; skin irritation |
| Intrathecal / epidural | Spinal anaesthesia, some antibiotics into CSF | Requires expert; risk of infection and headache |
| Intra-arterial, intra-articular, intracardiac | Special situations | Specialist use only |

## 1.3 Drug absorption

**Absorption** is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the bloodstream. IV injection needs no absorption.

### Mechanisms (types) of absorption

| Mechanism | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Passive diffusion** (lipid diffusion) | Down the concentration gradient; no energy, no carrier; needs lipid solubility; most common | Most drugs |
| Filtration (aqueous diffusion) | Through water-filled pores; small water-soluble molecules | Urea, small ions |
| **Carrier-mediated: facilitated diffusion** | Carrier, no energy, down the gradient; can be saturated | Glucose entry into cells |
| **Carrier-mediated: active transport** | Carrier and energy (ATP); against the gradient; saturable, can be inhibited | Levodopa, iron, methyldopa |
| Pinocytosis / endocytosis | Cell membrane engulfs large molecules | Vitamin B12 with intrinsic factor, some proteins |

### Ionisation and pH partition

Only the **unionised (non-ionised)** form of a drug is lipid-soluble and crosses membranes easily. Weak acids (aspirin, barbiturates) are mostly unionised in acidic gastric juice; weak bases (morphine, quinine) are mostly unionised in alkaline intestinal fluid. But because the small intestine has a huge surface area, **most drugs, acids included, are absorbed mainly from the small intestine.**

### Factors affecting absorption

1. **Drug factors:** lipid/water solubility, particle size (smaller → faster), degree of ionisation (pKa), dosage form (solution > suspension > capsule > tablet > coated tablet), molecular size, formulation excipients.
2. **Physiological factors:** area of absorbing surface, blood flow at the site, gastric emptying rate and gut motility, pH at the site, presence of food (food delays many drugs; increases griseofulvin absorption with fatty food), disease (diarrhoea, malabsorption), first-pass metabolism.
3. **Interactions:** tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones bind calcium, iron, magnesium and aluminium (antacids, milk) → poor absorption.

## 1.4 Bioavailability

**Bioavailability (F)** is the fraction (or percentage) of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. By definition IV bioavailability is 100%.

```text
            AUC (oral)
  F (%) = ─────────────── × 100        (same dose by both routes)
            AUC (IV)
```

AUC = area under the plasma concentration–time curve.

**Bioequivalence:** two products of the same drug (for example two brands) are bioequivalent when their rate and extent of absorption are not significantly different. Generic products must show this.

### Factors affecting bioavailability

- All the factors that affect absorption (above).
- **First-pass (presystemic) metabolism:** drug absorbed from the gut goes by the portal vein to the liver, where part is metabolised before it reaches the general circulation. High first-pass drugs: glyceryl trinitrate, propranolol, lidocaine (not given orally), morphine, verapamil. Sublingual, transdermal and parenteral routes avoid it.
- Formulation and manufacturing (binder, disintegrant, coating, particle size).
- Drug destruction in the gut (penicillin G by acid, insulin by enzymes).
- Patient factors: age, liver disease, enzyme induction or inhibition.

## 1.5 Drug distribution

**Distribution** is the movement of a drug from blood into tissues and body fluids.

**Apparent volume of distribution (Vd)** = amount of drug in the body ÷ plasma concentration. A large Vd (chloroquine, digoxin) means the drug is concentrated in tissues; a small Vd (warfarin, heparin) means it stays mostly in plasma.

### Factors affecting distribution

1. **Lipid solubility and ionisation:** lipid-soluble drugs enter tissues and the brain.
2. **Plasma protein binding:** acidic drugs bind mainly to **albumin**, basic drugs to α1-acid glycoprotein. Bound drug is inactive and stays in plasma; only free drug acts and is eliminated. One drug can **displace** another from binding sites (aspirin or sulphonamides displacing warfarin → bleeding; sulphonamides displacing bilirubin in newborns → kernicterus).
3. **Blood flow:** brain, heart, liver and kidney get drug first; then muscle; fat and bone last.
4. **Tissue binding:** chloroquine in liver and retina, tetracyclines in bones and teeth, iodine in thyroid, digoxin in heart muscle.
5. **Barriers:**
   - **Blood–brain barrier:** tight junctions and glial cells; only lipid-soluble drugs pass. Inflammation (meningitis) increases entry of penicillin.
   - **Placental barrier:** lipid-soluble drugs cross and may harm the foetus.
6. **Redistribution:** thiopentone first reaches the brain (rapid sleep), then moves to muscle and fat, so the patient wakes quickly.

## 1.6 Biotransformation (drug metabolism)

**Biotransformation** is the chemical change of a drug inside the body. It mostly converts lipid-soluble drugs into more water-soluble (polar) compounds that the kidney can excrete. The **liver** is the main site; others are gut wall, kidney, lungs, plasma and skin.

Results of metabolism:
- Active drug → inactive metabolite (most drugs, e.g. phenobarbitone, propranolol).
- Active drug → active metabolite (diazepam → oxazepam; codeine → morphine).
- Inactive **prodrug** → active drug (levodopa → dopamine; enalapril → enalaprilat; prednisone → prednisolone).
- Drug → toxic metabolite (paracetamol in overdose → NAPQI, which damages the liver).

### Types of reactions

| Phase | Reactions | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phase I (non-synthetic)** | **Oxidation** (most common, by cytochrome P450 enzymes), **reduction**, **hydrolysis** (esters such as procaine, succinylcholine; amides) | Adds or exposes a reactive group; product may be active or inactive |
| **Phase II (synthetic / conjugation)** | **Glucuronide conjugation** (most common), acetylation (isoniazid, sulphonamides), sulphate conjugation, glycine conjugation, methylation, glutathione conjugation | Joins drug with an endogenous molecule; product usually inactive and water-soluble |

**Microsomal enzymes** (cytochrome P450, glucuronyl transferase) are in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum of liver cells and can be induced or inhibited. **Non-microsomal enzymes** (plasma esterases, MAO, acetyltransferase) are not easily induced.

### Factors influencing drug metabolism

1. **Age:** newborns have immature enzymes (chloramphenicol → grey baby syndrome); the elderly metabolise slowly.
2. **Genetics:** slow and fast **acetylators** of isoniazid; pseudocholinesterase deficiency (prolonged succinylcholine apnoea); G6PD deficiency (haemolysis with primaquine).
3. **Enzyme induction:** rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone, chronic alcohol and smoking increase P450 activity → other drugs (oral contraceptives, warfarin) metabolised faster → failure of therapy.
4. **Enzyme inhibition:** erythromycin, ketoconazole, cimetidine, ciprofloxacin, grapefruit juice → other drugs build up → toxicity.
5. **Liver disease, nutrition, sex, pregnancy, and other diseases.**

## 1.7 Excretion of drugs

**Excretion** is the removal of a drug or its metabolites from the body.

### Routes

| Route | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Kidney (urine)** – main route | Glomerular filtration of free drug + active tubular secretion (penicillin, probenecid compete) − passive tubular reabsorption of lipid-soluble unionised drug |
| Bile and faeces | Large conjugated molecules; **enterohepatic circulation** (glucuronide split in gut → drug reabsorbed) prolongs action of oral contraceptives, morphine |
| Lungs | Volatile anaesthetics, alcohol |
| Saliva, sweat | Minor; rifampicin colours sweat orange; phenytoin in saliva |
| **Milk** | May affect the breast-fed baby (avoid many drugs during lactation) |

**Urinary pH:** making urine **alkaline** (sodium bicarbonate) speeds excretion of **weak acids** (aspirin, phenobarbitone poisoning); making it **acidic** speeds excretion of weak bases (amphetamine).

**Half-life (t½):** time taken for the plasma concentration to fall to half. About 4–5 half-lives are needed to reach steady state on regular dosing, and to remove a drug almost completely after stopping.

**Kinetics:** most drugs follow **first-order** kinetics (a constant fraction is eliminated per unit time). A few follow **zero-order** kinetics at higher doses (a constant amount per unit time: alcohol, phenytoin, high-dose aspirin), so small dose increases can cause toxicity.

## 1.8 General mechanisms of drug action

Drugs act in four main ways:

1. **Through receptors** (most drugs).
2. **Through enzymes:** inhibit them (neostigmine inhibits cholinesterase; aspirin inhibits COX; captopril inhibits ACE; allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase) or occasionally stimulate them.
3. **Through ion channels:** block them (local anaesthetics block sodium channels; nifedipine blocks calcium channels) or open them (minoxidil opens potassium channels).
4. **Through transporters (pumps):** omeprazole blocks the H+/K+ ATPase; digoxin blocks Na+/K+ ATPase; furosemide blocks the Na+-K+-2Cl− co-transporter; fluoxetine blocks serotonin reuptake.
5. **Physical and chemical actions (non-receptor):** antacids neutralise acid; osmotic diuretics (mannitol) and osmotic purgatives (magnesium sulphate) act by osmosis; chelating agents (dimercaprol, EDTA) bind metals; bulk laxatives; activated charcoal adsorbs poisons.

### Receptors

A **receptor** is a specific protein (on the cell membrane or inside the cell) with which a drug or body chemical combines to produce its effect.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Agonist** | Binds the receptor and activates it (affinity + intrinsic activity) | Adrenaline at α and β receptors |
| **Antagonist** | Binds but does not activate; blocks the agonist (affinity only) | Atropine at muscarinic receptors |
| **Partial agonist** | Activates submaximally; can block a full agonist | Buprenorphine at µ opioid receptors |
| **Inverse agonist** | Produces the opposite effect to an agonist | β-carbolines at benzodiazepine receptor |
| Competitive antagonism | Antagonist competes for the same site; overcome by more agonist | Atropine vs acetylcholine |
| Non-competitive antagonism | Cannot be overcome by more agonist | Phenoxybenzamine at α receptors |

### Main receptor families

```text
 1. Ligand-gated ion channels  → milliseconds   (nicotinic, GABA-A, NMDA)
 2. G-protein-coupled receptors→ seconds        (muscarinic, adrenergic, opioid, histamine)
 3. Enzyme-linked (kinase)     → minutes        (insulin receptor, growth factors)
 4. Intracellular (nuclear)    → hours to days  (steroid, thyroid hormone, vitamin D)
```

### Dose–response relationship

As the dose rises, the response increases until a maximum. **Potency** is the amount of drug needed for a given effect (the lower the dose, the more potent). **Efficacy** is the maximum effect a drug can produce. Furosemide has higher efficacy than thiazides; bumetanide is more potent than furosemide.

## 1.9 Factors modifying drug action

| Factor | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Body weight and size** | Doses for children worked out on body weight or surface area |
| **Age** | Newborn: immature liver and kidney (grey baby syndrome); elderly: reduced kidney function, more sensitive brain |
| **Sex** | Pregnancy: avoid teratogens (thalidomide, warfarin, ACE inhibitors, tetracyclines) |
| **Genetics** | Acetylator status, G6PD deficiency, malignant hyperthermia with halothane + succinylcholine |
| **Route and dosage form** | Magnesium sulphate: purgative orally, anticonvulsant IV, reduces swelling as a local application |
| **Disease** | Liver and kidney disease reduce elimination; hypothyroid patients sensitive to morphine |
| **Psychological factors / placebo** | A dummy medicine can relieve symptoms through belief |
| **Tolerance** | Larger dose needed for the same effect (morphine, nitrates, alcohol); **cross-tolerance** between related drugs; **tachyphylaxis** = rapid tolerance (ephedrine) |
| **Repeated dosing** | Cumulation (digoxin), dependence |
| **Drug interactions** | **Synergism:** sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim; **potentiation:** levodopa + carbidopa; **antagonism:** naloxone reverses morphine |
| **Environment, time of day, diet** | Food reduces absorption of tetracyclines; MAO inhibitors + cheese (tyramine) → hypertensive crisis |

### Types of drug effects

- **Side effect:** an unwanted but expected effect at the usual dose (dry mouth with atropine).
- **Adverse drug reaction (ADR):** any harmful, unintended response at normal doses.
- **Toxic effect:** from overdose or prolonged use.
- **Idiosyncrasy:** genetically determined abnormal response.
- **Allergy (hypersensitivity):** immune reaction (penicillin anaphylaxis).
- **Teratogenicity:** birth defects (thalidomide – phocomelia).
- **Iatrogenic disease:** disease caused by treatment.

## Remember

- PK = ADME (what the body does to the drug); PD = what the drug does to the body.
- IV route has 100% bioavailability and fastest onset; sublingual and transdermal avoid first-pass metabolism.
- Only unionised, lipid-soluble drug crosses membranes; most absorption happens in the small intestine.
- Bioavailability = AUC oral ÷ AUC IV × 100.
- Phase I = oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis; Phase II = conjugation (glucuronide commonest).
- Inducers: rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone; inhibitors: erythromycin, ketoconazole, cimetidine.
- Kidney is the main excretion route; alkaline urine removes weak acids faster.
- Agonist = affinity + intrinsic activity; antagonist = affinity only.
- Steady state is reached after about 4–5 half-lives.
