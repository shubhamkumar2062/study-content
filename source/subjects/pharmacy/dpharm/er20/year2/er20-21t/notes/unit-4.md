# Unit 4: Drugs acting on the central nervous system

This unit covers syllabus chapter 4: general anaesthetics, hypnotics and sedatives, anticonvulsants, anti-anxiety drugs, antidepressants, antipsychotics, nootropic agents, centrally acting muscle relaxants and opioid analgesics.

## 4.1 Important CNS neurotransmitters

| Transmitter | Main role | Drugs that act through it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GABA** | Main inhibitory transmitter | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, valproate, baclofen |
| Glutamate | Main excitatory transmitter | Memantine, ketamine (NMDA blockers) |
| **Dopamine** | Movement, mood, reward, vomiting centre | Antipsychotics (block D2), levodopa |
| **Serotonin (5-HT)** | Mood, sleep, appetite | SSRIs, ondansetron, triptans |
| Noradrenaline | Alertness, mood | Antidepressants (TCAs, SNRIs) |
| Acetylcholine | Memory, movement | Donepezil, anticholinergic antiparkinsonian drugs |
| Opioid peptides | Pain control | Morphine and other opioids |

## 4.2 General anaesthetics (GA)

**Definition:** drugs that cause reversible loss of consciousness and of all sensation, so that surgery can be done without pain. A good anaesthetic state includes unconsciousness, analgesia, amnesia, muscle relaxation and loss of reflexes.

### Stages of anaesthesia (Guedel's stages with ether)

```text
 Stage I   Analgesia      – from start to loss of consciousness
 Stage II  Delirium/excitement – unconscious, irregular breathing, struggling,
                                  vomiting risk (pass through quickly)
 Stage III Surgical anaesthesia – 4 planes; regular breathing, muscles relax,
                                  reflexes lost; surgery done here
 Stage IV  Medullary paralysis – respiratory and vasomotor failure → death
```

Modern fast-acting drugs pass these stages so quickly that they are not clearly seen.

### Classification

```text
 A. Inhalational
    Gas: nitrous oxide
    Volatile liquids: halothane, isoflurane, sevoflurane, desflurane, (ether – historical)
 B. Intravenous
    Fast-acting inducing agents: thiopentone sodium, propofol, etomidate
    Slower acting: benzodiazepines (midazolam, diazepam)
    Dissociative anaesthesia: ketamine
    Opioid analgesia: fentanyl
```

### Important features

| Drug | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **Nitrous oxide** | "Laughing gas"; good analgesic, weak anaesthetic; used with oxygen and other agents; quick recovery; long use → megaloblastic anaemia |
| **Halothane** | Pleasant, non-irritant; poor analgesic and muscle relaxant; sensitises heart to adrenaline (arrhythmias); rare **hepatitis**; malignant hyperthermia |
| **Isoflurane, sevoflurane** | Widely used now; sevoflurane is pleasant – good for induction in children; quick recovery |
| **Thiopentone** | Ultra-short-acting barbiturate; induction in 10–20 s; short action due to **redistribution**; no analgesia; laryngospasm; extravascular injection causes tissue damage; contraindicated in porphyria |
| **Propofol** | Most widely used IV inducing agent; smooth, rapid recovery with little hangover; antiemetic; pain on injection, hypotension |
| **Ketamine** | **Dissociative anaesthesia** (patient seems awake but feels no pain); good analgesic; raises BP and heart rate; bronchodilator; useful in shock and asthma, short procedures, burns dressing; emergence delirium and hallucinations |
| Midazolam | Sedation for endoscopy, premedication; amnesia |

### Pre-anaesthetic medication

Drugs given before anaesthesia to make it safer and smoother: anxiolytics/sedatives (diazepam, midazolam), opioids (morphine, fentanyl) for analgesia, anticholinergics (atropine, glycopyrrolate) to reduce secretions and vagal reflexes, antiemetics (ondansetron, metoclopramide), H2 blockers or PPIs (to reduce acid aspiration risk).

## 4.3 Sedatives and hypnotics

**Sedative:** reduces anxiety and excitement and calms without inducing sleep. **Hypnotic:** induces sleep resembling natural sleep. The same drug is often sedative at a small dose and hypnotic at a larger dose.

### Classification

```text
 1. Benzodiazepines (BZDs)
    Hypnotic use: nitrazepam, flurazepam, temazepam, triazolam
    Anxiolytic use: diazepam, alprazolam, lorazepam, chlordiazepoxide, clonazepam
 2. Barbiturates
    Long-acting: phenobarbitone; Short-acting: pentobarbitone;
    Ultra-short-acting: thiopentone
 3. Newer non-benzodiazepine hypnotics ("Z drugs"): zolpidem, zaleplon, zopiclone
 4. Melatonin receptor agonist: ramelteon; melatonin
 5. Others: chloral hydrate, some antihistamines (promethazine, diphenhydramine)
```

### Benzodiazepines

**Mechanism:** bind the **benzodiazepine site on the GABA-A receptor** and increase the **frequency** of chloride channel opening caused by GABA → more inhibition. They do not open the channel without GABA, so they are much safer in overdose than barbiturates.

**Actions and uses:** sedation and anxiety relief, sleep, **anticonvulsant** (diazepam/lorazepam IV in status epilepticus, clonazepam), centrally acting muscle relaxation, amnesia (premedication, endoscopy), **alcohol withdrawal** (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam), panic disorder (alprazolam).

**Adverse effects:** drowsiness, hangover, impaired driving, confusion and falls in the elderly, anterograde amnesia, tolerance and **dependence** with withdrawal symptoms (anxiety, insomnia, fits) on sudden stopping; respiratory depression mainly with alcohol or other depressants.

**Antidote:** **flumazenil** (BZD receptor antagonist).

### Barbiturates

**Mechanism:** increase the **duration** of GABA-A chloride channel opening; at high doses open the channel directly and block glutamate → low safety margin.
**Uses now limited:** phenobarbitone in epilepsy and neonatal seizures; thiopentone for induction.
**Disadvantages:** respiratory depression and death in overdose, strong enzyme induction (drug interactions), dependence; contraindicated in **acute intermittent porphyria**. Barbiturate poisoning is treated with supportive care and **alkalinisation of urine** for phenobarbitone.

### Z drugs

Zolpidem acts at the BZD site (BZ1 subtype); short-term treatment of insomnia, less hangover; may cause complex sleep behaviours (sleep-walking).

## 4.4 Anticonvulsant (antiepileptic) drugs

**Epilepsy** is a chronic disorder with recurrent seizures due to sudden, abnormal, excessive discharge of brain neurons.

### Types of seizures

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Generalised tonic–clonic (grand mal) | Loss of consciousness, stiffening then jerking, tongue bite, incontinence |
| Absence (petit mal) | Brief loss of awareness (seconds), staring; mostly children |
| Myoclonic | Brief shock-like jerks |
| Focal (partial) | Start in one area; simple (awareness kept) or complex (impaired awareness) |
| **Status epilepticus** | Seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery – emergency |

### Classification

```text
 1. Hydantoin: phenytoin, fosphenytoin
 2. Barbiturates: phenobarbitone, primidone
 3. Iminostilbenes: carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine
 4. Succinimide: ethosuximide
 5. Aliphatic carboxylic acid: sodium valproate
 6. Benzodiazepines: diazepam, lorazepam, clonazepam, clobazam, midazolam
 7. Newer drugs: lamotrigine, levetiracetam, topiramate, gabapentin, pregabalin,
    lacosamide, zonisamide, vigabatrin
```

### Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Block voltage-gated sodium channels (prolong inactivation) | Phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, valproate, topiramate |
| Enhance GABA action | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, valproate, vigabatrin (inhibits GABA breakdown) |
| Block T-type calcium channels (thalamus) | **Ethosuximide**, valproate |
| Bind SV2A synaptic vesicle protein | Levetiracetam |

### Drugs of choice

| Seizure type | Preferred drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Generalised tonic–clonic | Valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, phenytoin |
| Focal | Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam |
| Absence | **Ethosuximide**, valproate (carbamazepine and phenytoin can worsen absence) |
| Myoclonic | Valproate, levetiracetam, clonazepam |
| **Status epilepticus** | **Lorazepam or diazepam IV** (midazolam buccal/IM), then phenytoin/fosphenytoin, valproate or levetiracetam IV; anaesthesia if refractory |
| Febrile seizures | Treat fever; diazepam rectal for prolonged fit |

### Important adverse effects

| Drug | Adverse effects |
| --- | --- |
| **Phenytoin** | **Gum hypertrophy**, hirsutism, coarse facial features, ataxia, nystagmus, megaloblastic anaemia (folate), osteomalacia, enzyme induction; **teratogenic** (foetal hydantoin syndrome); zero-order kinetics at higher doses – monitor levels |
| **Carbamazepine** | Dizziness, diplopia, hyponatraemia, rashes (Stevens–Johnson syndrome, more with HLA-B*1502 in some Asians), blood dyscrasias, enzyme inducer (reduces effect of oral contraceptives); also used in **trigeminal neuralgia** and bipolar disorder |
| **Valproate** | Nausea, weight gain, hair loss, tremor, **liver toxicity** (young children), pancreatitis; **highly teratogenic (neural tube defects)** – avoid in women who may become pregnant unless no alternative |
| Ethosuximide | Nausea, drowsiness |
| Phenobarbitone | Sedation, behaviour problems in children, enzyme induction, dependence |
| Lamotrigine | Serious skin rash (slow dose increase) |

**General rules:** start with one drug at low dose and increase slowly; never stop suddenly (risk of status epilepticus); folic acid before and during pregnancy; check interactions.

## 4.5 Anti-anxiety (anxiolytic) drugs

**Anxiety** is excessive fear and worry with physical symptoms (palpitation, sweating, tremor).

```text
 1. Benzodiazepines: alprazolam, diazepam, lorazepam, chlordiazepoxide, clonazepam
 2. Azapirone: buspirone (5-HT1A partial agonist; slow onset, no sedation or dependence)
 3. Antidepressants: SSRIs (escitalopram, sertraline, paroxetine), SNRIs (venlafaxine)
    – first-line for long-term anxiety disorders
 4. β-blockers: propranolol – controls palpitation and tremor (performance anxiety)
 5. Others: hydroxyzine (antihistamine), pregabalin
```

Benzodiazepines act fast and are used short-term (2–4 weeks) because of dependence; SSRIs take 2–4 weeks to work and are preferred for chronic anxiety, panic and obsessive–compulsive disorder.

## 4.6 Antidepressant drugs

**Depression** is a mood disorder with persistent sadness, loss of interest, poor sleep and appetite, low energy, guilt and sometimes suicidal thoughts. The **monoamine theory** links it with low brain noradrenaline and serotonin.

### Classification

```text
 1. Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs): imipramine, amitriptyline, nortriptyline,
    clomipramine, dothiepin
 2. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs): fluoxetine, sertraline,
    paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram, fluvoxamine
 3. Serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs): venlafaxine, duloxetine
 4. Monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs): phenelzine, tranylcypromine (non-selective);
    moclobemide (reversible MAO-A)
 5. Atypical antidepressants: mirtazapine, trazodone, bupropion, agomelatine
```

### Mechanism

TCAs, SSRIs and SNRIs block **reuptake** of serotonin and/or noradrenaline into nerve endings; MAOIs prevent their breakdown. The level rises at once, but the mood benefit appears only after **2–4 weeks** (receptor adaptation). Treatment continues for at least 6 months after recovery.

### Comparison of TCAs and SSRIs

| Feature | TCAs | SSRIs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Side effects | **Anticholinergic** (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, blurred vision), sedation, weight gain, **postural hypotension** (α block), **cardiac arrhythmias** | Nausea, insomnia or drowsiness, headache, **sexual dysfunction**, anxiety early on |
| Safety in overdose | **Dangerous** – arrhythmias, convulsions, coma | Much safer |
| Use today | Second-line; amitriptyline in low doses for neuropathic pain, migraine prophylaxis; imipramine for bed-wetting | **First-line** for depression, anxiety, OCD, panic |

**MAOIs:** cause **hypertensive crisis ("cheese reaction")** with tyramine-rich foods (cheese, wine, pickled fish) and with sympathomimetics; little used.

**Serotonin syndrome:** when serotonergic drugs are combined (SSRI + MAOI, SSRI + tramadol): agitation, fever, sweating, tremor, muscle twitching. Avoid such combinations.

**Other uses:** anxiety disorders, OCD (clomipramine, fluvoxamine), neuropathic pain (amitriptyline, duloxetine), smoking cessation (bupropion), premature ejaculation (dapoxetine).

**Mood stabiliser:** **lithium carbonate** for bipolar disorder (mania); narrow therapeutic range – monitor serum level; toxicity: tremor, vomiting, diarrhoea, confusion; long-term hypothyroidism and diabetes insipidus.

## 4.7 Antipsychotic (neuroleptic) drugs

**Psychosis** is a severe mental illness with loss of contact with reality: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking (schizophrenia, mania). The **dopamine theory** links positive symptoms with overactivity of dopamine.

### Classification

```text
 A. Typical (first-generation)
    Phenothiazines: chlorpromazine, thioridazine, trifluoperazine, fluphenazine
    Butyrophenones: haloperidol
    Thioxanthenes: flupenthixol
 B. Atypical (second-generation)
    Clozapine, risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, ziprasidone,
    paliperidone, amisulpride
```

**Mechanism:** typicals block **dopamine D2** receptors in the mesolimbic pathway; atypicals block both **D2 and 5-HT2A** receptors and cause fewer movement disorders.

**Uses:** schizophrenia, acute mania and bipolar disorder, severe agitation and delirium, **nausea and vomiting** (chlorpromazine, prochlorperazine – block CTZ dopamine receptors), intractable hiccups (chlorpromazine), Tourette's syndrome (haloperidol). **Depot injections** (fluphenazine decanoate, haloperidol decanoate) for poor adherence.

### Adverse effects

| Problem | Details |
| --- | --- |
| **Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS)** – mostly typicals | Acute dystonia (treated with anticholinergics), **parkinsonism**, akathisia (restlessness), **tardive dyskinesia** (late, involuntary mouth movements – may be permanent) |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Rare, serious: fever, rigidity, confusion, unstable BP – stop drug; dantrolene, bromocriptine |
| Hormonal | Raised prolactin → galactorrhoea, gynaecomastia, menstrual problems |
| Autonomic | Anticholinergic effects, postural hypotension (α block), sedation |
| Metabolic (atypicals) | **Weight gain, diabetes, high lipids** (olanzapine, clozapine) |
| **Clozapine** | **Agranulocytosis** – regular blood counts; seizures; reserved for resistant schizophrenia |
| Others | QT prolongation, photosensitivity (chlorpromazine), lowered seizure threshold |

## 4.8 Nootropic agents (cognition enhancers)

**Definition:** drugs claimed to improve memory, learning and other brain functions, especially in dementia and cognitive decline.

| Group | Drugs | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cholinesterase inhibitors | **Donepezil**, rivastigmine, galantamine | Mild to moderate **Alzheimer's disease** (modest benefit) |
| NMDA receptor antagonist | **Memantine** | Moderate to severe Alzheimer's disease |
| "Racetams" | Piracetam | Memory problems, cerebrovascular disorders (evidence limited) |
| Others | Citicoline, ginkgo biloba extract | Stroke recovery, memory (benefit uncertain) |

Adverse effects of cholinesterase inhibitors: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, bradycardia, muscle cramps; caution in asthma and heart block.

## 4.9 Centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxants

**Definition:** drugs that reduce skeletal muscle tone by acting in the brain and spinal cord (on polysynaptic reflexes), without affecting the neuromuscular junction or consciousness much.

```text
 1. GABA-B agonist: baclofen
 2. Benzodiazepines: diazepam
 3. Central α2 agonist: tizanidine
 4. Mephenesin-type: chlorzoxazone, methocarbamol, carisoprodol
 5. Others: thiocolchicoside, tolperisone
 (Direct-acting peripheral: dantrolene – acts on muscle itself, see Unit 2)
```

**Uses:** painful muscle spasm (backache, sprain, neck pain – often combined with an NSAID), spasticity in cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis and spinal injury (baclofen, tizanidine, dantrolene), tetanus (diazepam).
**Adverse effects:** drowsiness, dizziness, weakness, dependence (carisoprodol, diazepam); tizanidine – hypotension, liver toxicity. Avoid driving and alcohol.

## 4.10 Opioid analgesics

**Opioids** are drugs with morphine-like actions that act on opioid receptors. **Opium** is the dried latex of the unripe capsule of *Papaver somniferum*.

### Opioid receptors

| Receptor | Effects of stimulation |
| --- | --- |
| **µ (mu)** | Supraspinal analgesia, respiratory depression, euphoria, miosis, constipation, physical dependence |
| κ (kappa) | Spinal analgesia, sedation, dysphoria, miosis |
| δ (delta) | Analgesia, affective behaviour |

### Classification

```text
 1. Natural opium alkaloids: morphine, codeine
 2. Semisynthetic: heroin (diacetylmorphine), pholcodine, hydromorphone
 3. Synthetic: pethidine (meperidine), fentanyl, methadone, tramadol, tapentadol
 4. Agonist–antagonists / partial agonists: pentazocine, butorphanol, buprenorphine
 5. Pure antagonists: naloxone, naltrexone
```

### Morphine – actions

- **CNS:** strong analgesia (raises pain threshold and reduces suffering), euphoria, sedation, **respiratory depression** (main cause of death in poisoning), cough suppression, **miosis (pin-point pupils)**, nausea and vomiting (stimulates CTZ), ADH release.
- **GIT:** **constipation** (spasm of sphincters, reduced propulsion), biliary spasm.
- **Others:** urinary retention, histamine release (itching, bronchospasm), mild bradycardia and hypotension.

### Uses of opioids

1. **Severe pain:** myocardial infarction, cancer pain, post-operative pain, burns, trauma, renal and biliary colic (pethidine or with antispasmodic).
2. **Acute left ventricular failure (cardiac asthma):** morphine IV reduces breathlessness and anxiety.
3. Pre-anaesthetic medication and balanced anaesthesia (fentanyl).
4. **Cough** (codeine, pholcodine).
5. **Diarrhoea** (codeine; synthetic loperamide, diphenoxylate – see Unit 8).
6. **Opioid dependence** (methadone, buprenorphine substitution therapy).

**Tramadol:** weak µ agonist that also inhibits noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake; moderate pain; may cause seizures and serotonin syndrome.

### Adverse effects

Nausea, vomiting, constipation, drowsiness, respiratory depression, urinary retention, itching, hypotension; **tolerance**, **physical and psychological dependence**. **Withdrawal syndrome:** yawning, lacrimation, running nose, sweating, gooseflesh, diarrhoea, muscle aches, anxiety.

### Contraindications and cautions

Head injury (raised intracranial pressure, masks signs), **bronchial asthma** and respiratory insufficiency, hypothyroidism, liver failure, elderly and infants (more sensitive), undiagnosed acute abdomen, during labour near delivery (neonatal respiratory depression), with MAOIs (pethidine).

### Acute morphine poisoning

Triad: **coma, pin-point pupils and respiratory depression**. Treatment: airway and ventilation; **naloxone IV**, repeated because its action is shorter than that of morphine.

## Remember

- GA stages: analgesia → delirium → surgical anaesthesia → medullary paralysis.
- Thiopentone acts briefly due to redistribution; ketamine causes dissociative anaesthesia and raises BP.
- BZDs ↑ frequency of GABA-A Cl− channel opening (antidote flumazenil); barbiturates ↑ duration.
- Status epilepticus: lorazepam/diazepam IV first; absence seizures: ethosuximide or valproate.
- Phenytoin → gum hypertrophy; valproate → neural tube defects and liver damage.
- SSRIs are first-line antidepressants; effect takes 2–4 weeks; MAOIs + cheese → hypertensive crisis.
- Typical antipsychotics → extrapyramidal effects; clozapine → agranulocytosis; atypicals → weight gain.
- Donepezil and memantine for Alzheimer's disease.
- Morphine: analgesia, respiratory depression, miosis, constipation; antidote naloxone.
