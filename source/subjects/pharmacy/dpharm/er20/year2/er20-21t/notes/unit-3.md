# Unit 3: Drugs acting on the eye

This unit covers syllabus chapter 3: miotics, mydriatics and drugs used in glaucoma. It builds on the cholinergic and adrenergic drugs of Unit 2.

## 3.1 How the eye is controlled

Two muscles in the **iris** decide pupil size, and the **ciliary muscle** controls focusing (accommodation) and drainage of aqueous humour.

| Muscle | Nerve supply | Receptor | Contraction causes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sphincter pupillae** (circular) | Parasympathetic (oculomotor nerve) | M3 | **Miosis** (small pupil) |
| **Dilator pupillae** (radial) | Sympathetic | α1 | **Mydriasis** (large pupil) |
| **Ciliary muscle** | Parasympathetic | M3 | Accommodation for near vision; opens the trabecular meshwork, improving aqueous outflow |

```text
                 Cornea
            ┌──────────────┐
  Anterior  │   aqueous    │   ← aqueous made by ciliary body (β receptors,
  chamber   │   humour     │      carbonic anhydrase help production)
            └──┬────────┬──┘
     iris ─────┤ pupil  ├───── iris
  angle ►  trabecular meshwork → canal of Schlemm → veins   (main outflow)
               ciliary body          lens
```

**Aqueous humour** is formed by the ciliary body, flows through the pupil into the anterior chamber and drains through the **trabecular meshwork and canal of Schlemm** at the angle of the anterior chamber, and partly through the uveoscleral route. A balance between formation and drainage keeps **intraocular pressure (IOP)** normal (about 10–21 mmHg).

**Cycloplegia** = paralysis of the ciliary muscle → loss of accommodation (cannot focus on near objects).

## 3.2 Miotics

**Definition:** drugs that **constrict the pupil** (miosis).

### Classification

```text
 1. Cholinergic agonists (direct): pilocarpine, carbachol, acetylcholine (intraocular)
 2. Anticholinesterases (indirect): physostigmine, echothiophate, demecarium
 3. α-adrenergic blocker: dapiprazole (reverses phenylephrine mydriasis; little used in India)
 4. Others producing miosis: morphine and other opioids (central action)
```

### Pharmacological actions

- Contraction of sphincter pupillae → **miosis**.
- Contraction of ciliary muscle → **spasm of accommodation** (eye fixed for near vision; blurred distant vision; brow ache).
- Pull on the iris and ciliary body opens the angle and trabecular meshwork → **better aqueous drainage → fall in IOP**.

### Pilocarpine

A natural alkaloid (from *Pilocarpus* leaves), direct muscarinic agonist. Eye drops act within minutes and last 4–8 hours, so they are put in several times a day.

**Uses:**
1. **Glaucoma:** acute angle-closure glaucoma (to pull the iris away from the angle, before laser or surgery); occasionally open-angle glaucoma.
2. To **reverse mydriasis** after eye examination with sympathomimetic mydriatics.
3. Alternating with mydriatics to **break adhesions** between iris and lens (synechiae) in iritis.
4. Orally for dry mouth (xerostomia) in Sjögren's syndrome.

**Adverse effects:** brow ache and headache, blurred and dim vision (especially at night), myopia from accommodation spasm; long use – iris cysts and retinal detachment (rare); systemic absorption → sweating, salivation, diarrhoea, bronchospasm.

**Contraindications:** iritis and uveitis (miosis worsens synechiae), retinal detachment risk, asthma (systemic absorption).

### Physostigmine and long-acting anticholinesterases

Physostigmine eye drops produce miosis and lower IOP; echothiophate is long-acting but causes cataract and is rarely used now.

## 3.3 Mydriatics

**Definition:** drugs that **dilate the pupil** (mydriasis). Some also cause **cycloplegia**.

### Classification

```text
 A. Anticholinergics (mydriasis + cycloplegia) — "mydriatic-cycloplegics"
    Atropine (longest, about 7–10 days), homatropine (1–3 days),
    cyclopentolate (about 1 day), tropicamide (shortest, 4–6 hours)
 B. Sympathomimetics (mydriasis without cycloplegia)
    Phenylephrine (10%, 2.5%), ephedrine, adrenaline
```

### Mechanism

- **Anticholinergics** block M3 receptors in the sphincter pupillae (mydriasis) and ciliary muscle (cycloplegia). The pupil no longer reacts to light.
- **Sympathomimetics** stimulate α1 receptors in the dilator pupillae; accommodation and light reflex are kept.

### Comparison

| Drug | Duration of mydriasis | Cycloplegia | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Atropine (1% drops/ointment) | 7–10 days | Strong, long | Refraction in young children (strong ciliary tone), iritis, iridocyclitis |
| Homatropine | 1–3 days | Moderate | Refraction, uveitis |
| Cyclopentolate | About 1 day | Good | Refraction in older children and adults |
| **Tropicamide** | 4–6 hours | Weak, short | **Fundus examination** (ophthalmoscopy) in adults |
| Phenylephrine | A few hours | None | Fundus examination (with tropicamide), before surgery |

### Uses of mydriatics

1. **Fundoscopy** (examination of the retina) – tropicamide ± phenylephrine.
2. **Refraction testing** (measuring the power of glasses) – cycloplegia needed, especially in children (atropine, cyclopentolate).
3. **Iritis, iridocyclitis and keratitis** – atropine rests the inflamed iris and ciliary muscle (relieves pain) and prevents or breaks synechiae (alternating with a miotic).
4. Before and after cataract and other eye surgery.

### Adverse effects

Blurred vision, photophobia (glare), difficulty in reading; rise in IOP and **may precipitate acute angle-closure glaucoma** in people with a narrow angle; allergic conjunctivitis; systemic atropine toxicity in children (flushing, fever, dry skin, tachycardia, restlessness). Phenylephrine 10% can raise BP.

**Contraindications:** **angle-closure glaucoma** or a shallow anterior chamber; caution in the elderly (narrow angles), infants (systemic toxicity), and in hypertension and heart disease (phenylephrine 10%).

**Patient advice:** do not drive until vision clears; wear dark glasses; press on the inner corner of the eye (nasolacrimal occlusion) for 1–2 minutes after putting in drops to reduce systemic absorption.

## 3.4 Glaucoma

**Glaucoma** is a group of eye diseases in which the **optic nerve is damaged, usually with raised intraocular pressure**, leading to a gradual loss of the field of vision and blindness if untreated.

### Types

| Feature | Open-angle (chronic simple) glaucoma | Angle-closure (acute congestive) glaucoma |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Angle | Open; drainage through trabecular meshwork is reduced | Iris blocks the angle (narrow angle) |
| Onset | Slow, painless; most common type | Sudden; painful red eye, headache, haloes, vomiting, blurred vision |
| Treatment | Long-term drugs; laser or surgery if not controlled | **Emergency**: drugs to lower IOP fast, then **laser iridotomy** or surgery |

Secondary glaucoma can follow injury, uveitis or long-term **corticosteroid eye drops**. Congenital glaucoma needs surgery.

### Ways to lower intraocular pressure

```text
 ↓ Aqueous formation:   β-blockers, α2 agonists, carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
 ↑ Aqueous outflow:     prostaglandin analogues (uveoscleral),
                        miotics (trabecular), α2 agonists (some)
 Withdraw fluid from eye: osmotic agents (mannitol, glycerol)
```

### Drugs used in glaucoma

| Class | Drugs | How they act | Notes and adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Prostaglandin F2α analogues** | **Latanoprost**, travoprost, bimatoprost | ↑ uveoscleral outflow | First-line for open-angle; **once daily at night**; darkening of the iris, longer eyelashes, red eye |
| **β-blockers** | **Timolol**, betaxolol (β1-selective) | ↓ aqueous production | Common first-line; twice daily; may cause bradycardia and **bronchospasm** (avoid in asthma and heart block) |
| α2 agonists | Brimonidine, apraclonidine | ↓ production, ↑ uveoscleral outflow | Dry mouth, allergy, drowsiness; avoid brimonidine in infants |
| **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** | Topical: **dorzolamide**, brinzolamide; oral/IV: **acetazolamide** | ↓ bicarbonate and aqueous formation | Acetazolamide (oral) in acute glaucoma; tingling, kidney stones, metabolic acidosis, low potassium; topical – bitter taste, stinging |
| **Miotics** | **Pilocarpine** | ↑ trabecular outflow | Acute angle-closure (pulls iris from angle); brow ache, dim vision |
| Sympathomimetics | Dipivefrine (prodrug of adrenaline), adrenaline | ↑ outflow | Older drugs; red eye, not for angle-closure |
| **Osmotic agents** | **Mannitol** (IV), glycerol (oral) | Draw water out of the eye | Emergency in acute glaucoma; fluid overload, headache |

### Treatment of acute angle-closure glaucoma (emergency)

1. **Acetazolamide** IV or orally – reduces aqueous formation.
2. **Mannitol 20% IV** (or oral glycerol) – rapidly lowers IOP.
3. **Pilocarpine 2% drops** – once IOP starts falling, pulls the iris away from the angle.
4. **Timolol** drops ± other topical drugs.
5. Analgesics and antiemetics for pain and vomiting.
6. Definitive: **laser peripheral iridotomy** (or surgical iridectomy), and the other eye treated to prevent an attack.
7. **Avoid** atropine and other mydriatics.

### Treatment of open-angle glaucoma

Start with a **prostaglandin analogue** or **timolol**; add a second drug (dorzolamide, brimonidine) if IOP is not controlled; fixed-dose combination drops improve adherence. Laser trabeculoplasty or trabeculectomy if drugs fail.

### Counselling points for eye drops

- Wash hands; tilt head back, pull lower lid down and put one drop in the pocket; do not touch the dropper to the eye.
- Close the eye and press the inner corner for 1–2 minutes.
- Leave at least 5 minutes between two different drops.
- Most multi-dose eye drops should be discarded about 4 weeks after opening (follow the label).
- Glaucoma treatment is lifelong; regular eye pressure checks are needed even when there are no symptoms.

## Remember

- Sphincter pupillae (M3) → miosis; dilator pupillae (α1) → mydriasis; ciliary muscle (M3) → accommodation and outflow.
- Miotic: pilocarpine – lowers IOP, used in acute angle-closure glaucoma and to reverse mydriasis.
- Anticholinergic mydriatics also cause cycloplegia; phenylephrine dilates without cycloplegia.
- Duration: atropine longest (7–10 days), tropicamide shortest (4–6 h).
- Mydriatics are contraindicated in angle-closure glaucoma.
- Glaucoma drugs: latanoprost (↑ outflow), timolol (↓ formation), dorzolamide/acetazolamide (↓ formation), brimonidine, pilocarpine, mannitol (emergency).
- Timolol drops can cause bronchospasm and bradycardia – avoid in asthma.
