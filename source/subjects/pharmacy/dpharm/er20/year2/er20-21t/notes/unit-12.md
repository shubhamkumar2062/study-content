# Unit 12: Chemotherapy I – principles and antibacterial drugs

This unit covers the first half of syllabus chapter 12: introduction and basic principles of chemotherapy, and the antibacterial classes – penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, tetracyclines, sulphonamides and anti-tubercular drugs. The rest of chapter 12 (antifungal, antiviral, antiamoebic, anthelmintic, antimalarial and anticancer drugs) is in Unit 13.

## 12.1 Introduction and basic principles of chemotherapy

**Chemotherapy** is the use of chemicals that kill or stop the growth of **microorganisms, parasites or cancer cells** with little harm to the host. Paul Ehrlich, who coined the word, aimed for a "magic bullet" that hits only the invader.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Antibiotic** | Substance produced by a microorganism that kills or inhibits other microbes (penicillin from *Penicillium*, streptomycin from *Streptomyces*) |
| **Antimicrobial agent** | Any drug (natural or synthetic) that acts against microbes |
| **Bactericidal** | Kills bacteria (penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, rifampicin, isoniazid) |
| **Bacteriostatic** | Stops multiplication; the immune system clears the infection (tetracyclines, macrolides, sulphonamides, chloramphenicol) |
| Narrow spectrum | Acts on a limited group (penicillin G – mainly gram-positive) |
| Broad spectrum | Acts on gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria and others (tetracyclines, chloramphenicol) |
| MIC | Minimum inhibitory concentration – lowest concentration that stops visible growth |

### Basic principles (selective toxicity)

A good chemotherapeutic agent acts on a target present in the microbe but absent or different in human cells.

```text
 Bacterial target                    Drugs acting there
 Cell wall (peptidoglycan)    ──►  Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, vancomycin
 Cell membrane                ──►  Polymyxins; (antifungals: amphotericin B, azoles)
 Protein synthesis (70S)      ──►  30S: aminoglycosides, tetracyclines
                                    50S: macrolides, chloramphenicol, clindamycin, linezolid
 Nucleic acid synthesis       ──►  DNA gyrase: fluoroquinolones; RNA polymerase: rifampicin
 Folate synthesis             ──►  Sulphonamides, trimethoprim
```

### Principles of rational antimicrobial therapy

1. **Confirm the infection and identify the organism** (clinical signs, Gram stain, culture and sensitivity) – start empirical therapy in serious infections after taking samples.
2. **Choose the right drug:** spectrum, sensitivity pattern, site of infection (drug must reach it – e.g. CSF in meningitis), patient factors (age, pregnancy, kidney and liver function, allergy), cost.
3. **Right dose, route, frequency and duration**; complete the course.
4. **Combinations** only when needed: to prevent resistance (TB, HIV), for synergy (penicillin + aminoglycoside in endocarditis), for mixed infections, or in severe infection before the organism is known.
5. **Prophylaxis** only where proven (surgery, rheumatic fever, contacts of meningitis).
6. Monitor for response and toxicity; **de-escalate** to a narrower drug when results come.

### Antimicrobial resistance (AMR)

Bacteria become resistant by: producing **enzymes** that destroy the drug (β-lactamases), changing the **target** (altered penicillin-binding proteins in MRSA), reducing **entry** or increasing **efflux**, or using an alternative pathway. Resistance spreads by mutation and transfer of genes (plasmids – conjugation, transduction, transformation). Causes: overuse, misuse (viral infections), incomplete courses, use in animals. **Pharmacists help by dispensing antibiotics only on prescription (Schedule H1 records) and counselling on completing the course.**

### Problems with antimicrobials

Toxicity, hypersensitivity, **superinfection** (overgrowth of resistant organisms such as *Candida* or *Clostridioides difficile* after broad-spectrum drugs), drug resistance, masking of infections (partially treated TB, meningitis).

## 12.2 Penicillins

**Source:** *Penicillium* moulds (penicillin G discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928). Penicillins are **β-lactam antibiotics**.

**Mechanism:** bind **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)** and inhibit **transpeptidation** (cross-linking) of peptidoglycan → weak cell wall → bacterial lysis. **Bactericidal**, most active on dividing bacteria.

### Classification

```text
 1. Natural: benzylpenicillin (penicillin G); long-acting benzathine penicillin, procaine penicillin
 2. Semi-synthetic
    a. Acid-resistant: phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V)
    b. Penicillinase-resistant: cloxacillin, dicloxacillin, flucloxacillin, (methicillin)
    c. Extended (broad) spectrum – aminopenicillins: ampicillin, amoxicillin
    d. Antipseudomonal: piperacillin, carbenicillin, ticarcillin
 3. β-lactamase inhibitor combinations:
    amoxicillin + clavulanic acid, ampicillin + sulbactam, piperacillin + tazobactam
```

### Uses

| Drug | Main uses |
| --- | --- |
| Penicillin G | Streptococcal infections (sore throat), **syphilis**, gas gangrene, tetanus, diphtheria (with antitoxin), meningococcal meningitis; **benzathine penicillin monthly for rheumatic fever prophylaxis** |
| **Amoxicillin** | Upper and lower respiratory infections, otitis media, sinusitis, urinary infections, typhoid (in sensitive strains), **H. pylori eradication**; better oral absorption than ampicillin |
| **Amoxicillin + clavulanate** | β-lactamase-producing organisms: sinusitis, bites, skin, respiratory and urinary infections |
| Cloxacillin, flucloxacillin | Staphylococcal (penicillinase-producing) skin and bone infections |
| Piperacillin + tazobactam | Serious hospital infections incl. *Pseudomonas* |

### Adverse effects

- **Hypersensitivity** – most important: rashes, fever, serum sickness, angioedema, **anaphylaxis** (rare but can be fatal). Ask about allergy before giving; a person allergic to one penicillin is allergic to all; possible cross-reaction with cephalosporins.
- Diarrhoea, superinfection; ampicillin rash (especially in infectious mononucleosis).
- Pain at IM site; very high doses – convulsions; Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction in syphilis.

**Contraindication:** history of penicillin allergy.
**Interaction:** probenecid delays tubular secretion of penicillin → higher, longer levels.

## 12.3 Cephalosporins

β-lactam antibiotics originally from the fungus *Cephalosporium*; same mechanism as penicillins (cell-wall synthesis inhibition), more resistant to many β-lactamases. Bactericidal.

### Classification (generations)

| Generation | Drugs | Spectrum |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First** | Cefazolin (inj.), cephalexin, cefadroxil (oral) | Mainly gram-positive (staph, strep); surgical prophylaxis (cefazolin) |
| **Second** | Cefuroxime, cefaclor, cefoxitin | Wider gram-negative; respiratory infections |
| **Third** | **Ceftriaxone**, cefotaxime, **ceftazidime** (anti-*Pseudomonas*), cefoperazone; oral – **cefixime**, cefpodoxime | Strong gram-negative; cross into CSF |
| **Fourth** | Cefepime | Broad, incl. *Pseudomonas*; hospital infections |
| Fifth | Ceftaroline | Active against **MRSA** |

### Uses

**Meningitis** (ceftriaxone, cefotaxime), **typhoid fever** (ceftriaxone, cefixime), **gonorrhoea** (ceftriaxone), pneumonia, urinary and skin infections, hospital infections, surgical prophylaxis (cefazolin), infections in penicillin-allergic patients (only if the allergy was not anaphylaxis).

### Adverse effects

Hypersensitivity (cross-reaction with penicillins in a small fraction), pain/thrombophlebitis at injection site, diarrhoea and *C. difficile* colitis, bleeding (some – cefoperazone), **disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol** (cefoperazone), kidney toxicity (older ones, with aminoglycosides); ceftriaxone with calcium-containing IV fluids in newborns (precipitates) – avoid.

## 12.4 Aminoglycosides

**Drugs:** **streptomycin**, **gentamicin**, **amikacin**, tobramycin, kanamycin, neomycin (topical/oral only), netilmicin.

**Mechanism:** bind the **30S ribosomal subunit** → misreading of mRNA and faulty proteins → **bactericidal**. Entry into bacteria needs oxygen-dependent transport, so they do **not act on anaerobes**. Concentration-dependent killing with a post-antibiotic effect → **once-daily dosing** is common.

**Pharmacokinetics:** highly polar – **not absorbed orally** (given IM/IV; neomycin orally for gut action), do not enter CSF well, excreted unchanged by the **kidney** (dose reduced in kidney disease).

**Uses:** serious **gram-negative** infections (with a β-lactam) – septicaemia, hospital pneumonia, complicated urinary infections, *Pseudomonas* (tobramycin, amikacin); **infective endocarditis** with penicillin (synergy); **tuberculosis** (streptomycin, amikacin); plague, tularaemia (streptomycin); topical eye and skin infections (gentamicin, tobramycin, neomycin); neomycin orally in hepatic coma (reduces ammonia-forming gut bacteria) and bowel preparation.

**Adverse effects (narrow therapeutic index):**
1. **Ototoxicity** – vestibular (giddiness; streptomycin, gentamicin) and cochlear (deafness; amikacin, neomycin), may be **permanent**.
2. **Nephrotoxicity** – usually reversible.
3. **Neuromuscular blockade** – respiratory paralysis, worse in myasthenia gravis and with NM blockers (reversed by IV calcium).
Monitor blood levels and kidney function; avoid with loop diuretics (furosemide) and other nephrotoxic drugs.

**Contraindications:** **pregnancy** (foetal deafness), myasthenia gravis, kidney impairment (adjust dose), existing hearing loss.

## 12.5 Fluoroquinolones

**Drugs:** first-generation quinolone – nalidixic acid (urinary only). Fluoroquinolones: **ciprofloxacin**, **ofloxacin**, **levofloxacin**, norfloxacin, **moxifloxacin**, gatifloxacin (eye drops).

**Mechanism:** inhibit bacterial **DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II)** and **topoisomerase IV** → DNA cannot supercoil/separate → **bactericidal**.

**Uses:** **urinary tract infections**, gonorrhoea (resistance now common), **typhoid** (resistance increasing), bacterial diarrhoea (shigella, cholera), bone and joint infections, respiratory infections (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin – "respiratory quinolones"), **multidrug-resistant tuberculosis** (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), anthrax, eye and ear infections (drops).

**Adverse effects:** nausea, diarrhoea; headache, dizziness, insomnia, **seizures** (more with NSAIDs); **tendinitis and tendon rupture** (Achilles; elderly, with steroids); **damage to growing cartilage** (joint problems in young animals); **QT prolongation** (moxifloxacin); photosensitivity; blood sugar disturbances; peripheral neuropathy.

**Interactions:** **antacids, iron, calcium, zinc, milk** reduce absorption (give 2 hours before or 6 hours after); ciprofloxacin **raises theophylline levels** (enzyme inhibition).

**Contraindications:** **pregnancy, breastfeeding and children** (cartilage damage; used in children only when no alternative), epilepsy, history of tendon disorders, QT prolongation.

## 12.6 Macrolides

**Drugs:** **erythromycin**, **azithromycin**, **clarithromycin**, roxithromycin.

**Mechanism:** bind the **50S ribosomal subunit** → block translocation and protein synthesis → **bacteriostatic** (bactericidal at high levels).

**Uses:**
- Alternative to penicillin in **penicillin-allergic** patients (streptococcal pharyngitis, rheumatic fever prophylaxis).
- **Atypical pneumonia** (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia*, *Legionella*), community-acquired pneumonia.
- **Whooping cough (pertussis)**, **diphtheria** (carriers).
- **Chlamydia** and non-gonococcal urethritis (azithromycin single dose), trachoma.
- **Typhoid** (azithromycin), cholera, Campylobacter diarrhoea.
- **H. pylori eradication** (clarithromycin), **Mycobacterium avium complex** and leprosy (clarithromycin).
- Erythromycin as a **prokinetic** (motilin receptor agonist).

**Adverse effects:** **GI upset** (erythromycin – epigastric pain, vomiting), **cholestatic jaundice** (erythromycin estolate), **QT prolongation**, hearing loss (high doses), allergy.
**Interactions:** erythromycin and clarithromycin are **enzyme (CYP3A4) inhibitors** → toxicity of theophylline, warfarin, carbamazepine, some statins; azithromycin interacts much less.
Azithromycin has a long half-life → once daily, short courses (3–5 days).

## 12.7 Tetracyclines

**Drugs:** tetracycline, oxytetracycline, **doxycycline**, **minocycline**, demeclocycline; related – tigecycline.

**Mechanism:** bind the **30S ribosome** and block attachment of aminoacyl-tRNA → **bacteriostatic**; **broad spectrum** (gram-positive, gram-negative, rickettsiae, chlamydiae, mycoplasma, some protozoa).

**Uses:**
- **Rickettsial infections** (scrub typhus – doxycycline is the drug of choice), **cholera** (doxycycline single dose), brucellosis (with rifampicin or streptomycin).
- **Chlamydial infections** (trachoma, lymphogranuloma venereum, urethritis), **atypical pneumonia**.
- **Acne** (doxycycline, minocycline – long-term low doses).
- **Leptospirosis**, Lyme disease, plague.
- **Malaria** – doxycycline for prophylaxis and with quinine/artesunate in resistant falciparum.
- Demeclocycline in SIADH (blocks ADH action).

**Adverse effects:**
- GI irritation, nausea, diarrhoea; **superinfection** (candidiasis, *C. difficile*); **oesophageal ulceration** (take with plenty of water, not lying down).
- **Discolouration and hypoplasia of teeth** and **deposition in growing bones** (binds calcium) – children and foetus.
- **Photosensitivity** (demeclocycline, doxycycline).
- Liver toxicity (high IV doses, pregnancy), kidney: outdated tetracycline → Fanconi syndrome; raised intracranial pressure; vestibular toxicity (minocycline).

**Interactions:** **chelate with calcium, iron, magnesium, aluminium, zinc** (milk, antacids, iron tablets) → poor absorption; doxycycline is affected less and can be taken with food.

**Contraindications:** **pregnancy, breastfeeding and children under 8 years** (teeth and bones), kidney failure (except doxycycline).

## 12.8 Sulphonamides

**Drugs:** sulfadiazine, sulfamethoxazole, **silver sulfadiazine** (topical – burns), sulfacetamide (eye drops), sulfadoxine (with pyrimethamine – malaria), sulfasalazine (ulcerative colitis, rheumatoid arthritis).

**Mechanism:** structural analogues of **para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)**; competitively inhibit **dihydropteroate synthase** → bacteria cannot make **folic acid** → **bacteriostatic**. Humans use dietary folate, so are not affected.

**Cotrimoxazole** = **sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim** (5:1). Trimethoprim inhibits **dihydrofolate reductase** – a **sequential block** of the same pathway → **synergistic and bactericidal** effect, less resistance.

```text
 PABA ──(dihydropteroate synthase)──► Dihydrofolic acid ──(dihydrofolate reductase)──► Tetrahydrofolic acid → DNA
             ▲ blocked by sulphonamides                    ▲ blocked by trimethoprim
```

**Uses of cotrimoxazole:** urinary tract infections, respiratory infections, **Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia** (treatment and prophylaxis in HIV), toxoplasmosis prophylaxis, shigellosis, nocardiosis. **Silver sulfadiazine** cream prevents infection of **burns**; **sulfacetamide** eye drops in conjunctivitis.

**Adverse effects:** **hypersensitivity** – rashes, fever, **Stevens–Johnson syndrome** (serious); **crystalluria** (drink plenty of water); **haemolysis in G6PD deficiency**; bone-marrow suppression (megaloblastic anaemia from trimethoprim), **kernicterus** in newborns (displace bilirubin); nausea; hyperkalaemia (trimethoprim).

**Contraindications:** sulpha allergy, **late pregnancy and newborns** (kernicterus), G6PD deficiency, severe kidney or liver disease.

## 12.9 Anti-tubercular drugs

**Tuberculosis (TB)** is a chronic infection caused by ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis***, usually of the lungs (cough for more than 2 weeks, fever, night sweats, weight loss, blood in sputum). It spreads by droplets. The bacilli grow slowly, live inside cells and readily become resistant, so TB needs a **combination of drugs for a long time** (at least 6 months).

### Classification

```text
 First-line (essential) drugs:
   Isoniazid (H), Rifampicin (R), Pyrazinamide (Z), Ethambutol (E), (Streptomycin (S))
 Second-line drugs (for drug-resistant TB):
   Fluoroquinolones: levofloxacin, moxifloxacin
   Bedaquiline, linezolid, delamanid, pretomanid
   Clofazimine, cycloserine, ethionamide, PAS, injectable amikacin
```

### First-line drugs

| Drug | Mechanism | Action | Important adverse effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Isoniazid (H)** | Inhibits **mycolic acid** synthesis (cell wall) | Bactericidal (dividing bacilli) | **Peripheral neuropathy** (prevented by **pyridoxine** 10 mg daily), **hepatitis**, slow vs fast acetylators |
| **Rifampicin (R)** | Inhibits bacterial **DNA-dependent RNA polymerase** | Bactericidal, sterilising | **Hepatitis**, **orange-red urine, sweat, tears** (stains contact lenses), flu-like syndrome, **powerful enzyme inducer** (oral contraceptives fail; reduces effect of warfarin, antiretrovirals, steroids) |
| **Pyrazinamide (Z)** | Active in acidic environment (inside macrophages) | Bactericidal | **Hepatotoxicity** (most liver toxic), **hyperuricaemia** and gout, joint pain |
| **Ethambutol (E)** | Inhibits arabinosyl transferase (cell wall) | Bacteriostatic; prevents resistance | **Optic neuritis** – reduced vision and **red–green colour blindness** (check vision; caution in young children who cannot report) |
| **Streptomycin (S)** | 30S ribosome | Bactericidal (extracellular) | Ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity; IM; avoid in pregnancy |

### Treatment under the National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP)

Daily fixed-dose combination tablets, doses by body weight:

```text
 Drug-sensitive TB (new and previously treated):
   Intensive phase  – 2 months  HRZE  (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol)
   Continuation     – 4 months  HRE
   = 6 months total (may be extended in some forms, e.g. TB of brain or bones)
 Directly observed / supported treatment; 99DOTS and other adherence support;
 Nikshay Poshan Yojana gives nutritional support.
```

**Drug-resistant TB:** **MDR-TB** = resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; **XDR-TB** = MDR plus resistance to a fluoroquinolone and at least one more key drug (bedaquiline or linezolid). Treated with longer, all-oral regimens containing bedaquiline, linezolid, fluoroquinolones and others according to national guidelines.

**Preventive therapy:** isoniazid (or rifapentine-based regimens) for household contacts and people living with HIV.

**Counselling:** take every dose, preferably on an empty stomach; do not stop when you feel better; report yellow eyes, dark urine, vomiting (liver), tingling (isoniazid), blurred or colour vision change (ethambutol); orange urine from rifampicin is harmless; use a barrier method of contraception (rifampicin reduces pill effect).

**Pregnancy:** HRZE are considered safe; **streptomycin is avoided** (foetal deafness).

## Remember

- Bactericidal: β-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, rifampicin, isoniazid; bacteriostatic: tetracyclines, macrolides, sulphonamides.
- Penicillins and cephalosporins inhibit cell-wall cross-linking; main danger of penicillins is anaphylaxis.
- Ceftriaxone: meningitis, typhoid, gonorrhoea; benzathine penicillin: rheumatic fever prophylaxis; amoxicillin + clavulanate beats β-lactamase.
- Aminoglycosides (30S): ototoxic, nephrotoxic, neuromuscular block; not oral; avoid in pregnancy.
- Fluoroquinolones inhibit DNA gyrase; tendon rupture, cartilage damage, seizures; avoid in children and pregnancy; antacids reduce absorption.
- Macrolides (50S): penicillin alternative, atypical pneumonia, chlamydia; erythromycin is an enzyme inhibitor.
- Tetracyclines (30S): rickettsia (doxycycline), cholera, acne; teeth staining – avoid under 8 years and in pregnancy; chelate with calcium and iron.
- Sulphonamides block folate synthesis; cotrimoxazole = sequential block; Stevens–Johnson syndrome, crystalluria, kernicterus.
- TB: 2HRZE + 4HRE; isoniazid → neuropathy (give pyridoxine); rifampicin → orange urine, enzyme induction; pyrazinamide → hepatotoxicity, gout; ethambutol → optic neuritis.
