# Unit 2: Preventive Healthcare — Role of Pharmacists

Prevention is cheaper and kinder than cure. This unit covers population and family planning, mother and child health, vaccines, the environment, and drug abuse — and what a pharmacist does in each.

**Levels of prevention (useful opening for any long answer)**

| Level | When | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primordial | Before risk factors appear | Healthy lifestyle habits in children |
| Primary | Before disease starts | Vaccination, safe water, condoms, health education |
| Secondary | Early disease | Screening for BP, diabetes, TB; early treatment |
| Tertiary | Established disease | Rehabilitation, limiting disability (e.g. leprosy care) |

## 2.1 Demography and family planning

### Demography

**Demography** is the scientific study of human population: its **size, composition (age, sex), distribution, and changes** caused by births, deaths and migration.

**Sources of data:** Census (every 10 years in India), Sample Registration System (SRS), National Family Health Survey (NFHS), civil registration of births and deaths.

**Demographic cycle (five stages)**

```text
Stage 1 High stationary   High birth rate, high death rate     -> slow growth
Stage 2 Early expanding   Death rate falls, birth rate high    -> fast growth
Stage 3 Late expanding    Birth rate starts falling            -> growth slows
Stage 4 Low stationary    Low birth rate, low death rate       -> stable
Stage 5 Declining         Birth rate below death rate          -> population falls
```

India is generally placed in the **late expanding** stage.

**Demographic indicators**

| Indicator | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Crude birth rate | Live births per 1000 mid-year population per year |
| Crude death rate | Deaths per 1000 mid-year population per year |
| Growth rate | Birth rate minus death rate (plus migration) |
| Total fertility rate (TFR) | Average children a woman would have in her reproductive life; 2.1 = replacement level |
| Sex ratio (India) | Females per 1000 males |
| Dependency ratio | Children (0–14) + elderly (65+) per 100 working-age people (15–64) |

**Problems of a fast-growing population:** pressure on food, water, housing, schools and jobs; unemployment; poor health services; environmental damage; poverty.

### Family planning

WHO describes family planning as a way of thinking and living adopted voluntarily, based on knowledge and responsible decisions, to promote the health of the family. It lets couples decide **how many children to have and when**.

**Benefits:** spacing between births (healthier mother and child), fewer unsafe abortions, lower maternal and infant deaths, better nutrition and education for children, control of population growth.

**Classification of contraceptive methods**

```text
CONTRACEPTIVES
├── Spacing (temporary)
│   ├── Barrier: condom (male/female), diaphragm, spermicides
│   ├── Intra-uterine devices (IUD): Cu-T 380A, Cu-T 375, LNG-IUS
│   ├── Hormonal: oral pills, injectables, implants, emergency pill
│   ├── Non-hormonal pill: centchroman
│   └── Natural: rhythm/calendar, withdrawal, LAM (breastfeeding)
└── Terminal (permanent)
    ├── Male: vasectomy (no-scalpel vasectomy)
    └── Female: tubectomy (laparoscopic / minilap)
```

| Method | How it works | Points for counselling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Male condom | Barrier to sperm | Also protects against STDs/HIV; new one each time |
| Combined oral pill (e.g. Mala-N) | Oestrogen + progestin stop ovulation | One pill daily; 21 hormone + 7 iron tablets; missed pill rules |
| Centchroman (Chhaya) | Non-steroidal; prevents implantation | Twice a week for 3 months, then once a week |
| Injectable DMPA (Antara) | Progestin; stops ovulation | One injection every 3 months; irregular bleeding |
| Cu-T 380A | Copper is toxic to sperm, prevents implantation | Effective for about 10 years; Cu-T 375 about 5 years |
| Emergency pill (levonorgestrel 1.5 mg) | Delays ovulation | Within 72 hours of unprotected sex; not a regular method |
| LAM | Exclusive breastfeeding suppresses ovulation | Only if baby < 6 months, exclusive feeding, no periods |
| Vasectomy / tubectomy | Vas deferens / fallopian tubes cut and tied | Permanent; vasectomy is simpler and safer |

**Role of pharmacist:** stock and explain methods; explain missed-pill rules; tell clients that the emergency pill is not for regular use; promote condoms for dual protection; maintain privacy; refer for IUD or sterilisation; support national campaigns.

## 2.2 Mother and child health (MCH)

Mothers and children form a large, vulnerable group. Most maternal and infant deaths are preventable.

**Antenatal care (ANC)**

- Early registration, at least four ANC visits.
- Tetanus–diphtheria (Td) vaccine, two doses (or one booster).
- Iron–folic acid tablets daily from the second trimester; calcium; deworming after the first trimester.
- Check weight, BP, haemoglobin, urine, blood sugar; identify high-risk pregnancy (anaemia, high BP, previous caesarean, twins).
- Advice on diet, rest, danger signs (bleeding, fits, swelling, reduced foetal movement).

**Intranatal care:** institutional delivery by skilled birth attendant; "five cleans" if delivered at home (clean hands, surface, blade, cord tie, cord stump).
**Postnatal care:** check mother and baby, breastfeeding support, family planning advice, newborn immunisation.

### Importance of breastfeeding

**Recommendations:** start within **one hour** of birth; give **colostrum** (first thick yellow milk); **exclusive** breastfeeding for the first **6 months** (no water, honey, ghutti); then add complementary foods and continue breastfeeding up to **2 years** or beyond.

| Benefits to baby | Benefits to mother | Benefits to family/society |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect nutrition, easy to digest | Less bleeding after delivery (uterus contracts) | Free, always ready, no preparation |
| Antibodies (IgA in colostrum) protect against diarrhoea and pneumonia | Natural spacing (LAM) | No fuel, bottles or water needed |
| Lower risk of allergy, obesity, diabetes later | Lower risk of breast and ovarian cancer | Fewer illnesses, lower health costs |
| Better brain development, bonding | Faster return to pre-pregnancy weight | Environment-friendly |

### Ill effects of infant milk substitutes and bottle feeding

- **Infections:** bottles and teats are hard to clean → diarrhoea, thrush.
- **Over-dilution** (to save money) → malnutrition, marasmus; **under-dilution** → dehydration, kidney load, obesity.
- No antibodies → more pneumonia and ear infections.
- Allergy to cow's milk protein; poorer bonding.
- Costly; needs safe water and fuel.
- Mother loses natural contraception.

India's **Infant Milk Substitutes, Feeding Bottles and Infant Foods Act (IMS Act), 1992**, amended in 2003, bans advertising and promotion of infant milk substitutes and feeding bottles, so that breastfeeding is protected.

**Role of pharmacist:** do not promote formula; counsel mothers on breastfeeding and correct expressing/storage of breast milk; explain safe preparation if formula is medically needed; supply ORS and zinc; check immunisation card.

## 2.3 Vaccines, immunity and immunisation

### Immunity

**Immunity** is the body's ability to resist infection.

```text
IMMUNITY
├── Innate (non-specific): skin, mucus, stomach acid, phagocytes, fever
└── Acquired (specific)
    ├── Active (body makes its own antibodies; slow onset, long-lasting)
    │   ├── Natural: after an infection (e.g. measles)
    │   └── Artificial: after a vaccine
    └── Passive (ready-made antibodies; quick, short-lived)
        ├── Natural: mother → baby via placenta and breast milk
        └── Artificial: immunoglobulins, antitoxins, antisera (e.g. anti-rabies Ig, ASV)
```

**Herd immunity:** when most of a community is immune, the infection cannot spread easily, so even unvaccinated people are protected.

### Types of vaccines

| Type | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live attenuated | Weakened live organism; strong, long immunity; avoid in pregnancy and immunodeficiency | BCG, OPV, measles–rubella (MR), rotavirus, varicella, yellow fever |
| Inactivated (killed) | Whole killed organism; needs boosters | IPV, rabies cell-culture vaccine, hepatitis A, whole-cell pertussis |
| Toxoid | Inactivated toxin | Tetanus, diphtheria |
| Subunit / recombinant | Only a part (protein) of the microbe | Hepatitis B, HPV, acellular pertussis |
| Conjugate | Sugar coat joined to a protein for better response in infants | Hib, pneumococcal (PCV), typhoid conjugate |
| mRNA / viral vector (newer) | Carry instructions to make an antigen | Some COVID-19 vaccines |

### Immunisation in India

The **Universal Immunization Programme (UIP)** gives free vaccines to children and pregnant women. **Mission Indradhanush** catches up children and women who missed doses. The full schedule is in the practical "National immunization schedule".

**Cold chain** — the system of keeping vaccines at the right temperature from maker to child.

- Most vaccines: **+2 °C to +8 °C**; never freeze Td, pentavalent, hepatitis B, PCV.
- Equipment: walk-in coolers, ice-lined refrigerators (ILR), deep freezers, cold boxes, vaccine carriers with ice packs.
- **Vaccine vial monitor (VVM):** a square inside a circle on the label; if the square becomes as dark as or darker than the circle, the vaccine has had too much heat — discard.
- **Shake test:** checks whether a freeze-sensitive vaccine has been frozen.
- OPV is the most heat-sensitive; BCG and MR must be protected from light.

**Adverse events following immunisation (AEFI):** mild (fever, pain, swelling) to rare serious ones (anaphylaxis). Every AEFI must be reported.

**Role of pharmacist:** explain the schedule and remind about due doses; maintain the cold chain where vaccines are stocked; counsel about mild side effects (paracetamol for fever); dispel myths; report AEFI; promote adult vaccines (influenza, hepatitis B).

## 2.4 Effect of environment on health

### Water pollution and safe drinking water

**Safe and wholesome water** is free from pathogens and harmful chemicals, pleasant to taste, and usable for domestic purposes.

**Sources of pollution:** sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural run-off (fertilisers, pesticides), animal waste, hospital waste.

**Water-borne and water-related diseases**

| Group | Diseases |
| --- | --- |
| Viral | Hepatitis A and E, poliomyelitis, rotavirus diarrhoea |
| Bacterial | Cholera, typhoid, bacillary dysentery, E. coli diarrhoea |
| Protozoal | Amoebiasis, giardiasis |
| Helminthic | Roundworm, threadworm, guinea worm (now eradicated in India) |
| Chemical | Fluorosis (excess fluoride), arsenic poisoning, nitrate excess (blue-baby), lead |

**Purification of water**

- **Large scale:** storage → filtration (slow sand or rapid sand filters) → chlorination.
- **Small scale (household):** boiling (rolling boil for 5–10 minutes), chlorination (bleaching powder, chlorine tablets), filtration (ceramic filters), ultraviolet and reverse-osmosis units.
- **Chlorination rule:** after a contact period of 1 hour, a **free residual chlorine of 0.5 mg/L** should remain. Bleaching powder contains about **33% available chlorine**.

### Air pollution

**Main pollutants:** particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5), carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, ozone, lead, smoke. **Indoor pollution:** smoke from wood, cow-dung cakes and kerosene in poorly ventilated kitchens; tobacco smoke.

**Health effects:** asthma, chronic bronchitis, COPD, lung cancer, heart disease, eye irritation; CO binds haemoglobin and reduces oxygen delivery.

**Control:** clean fuels (LPG), better stoves and ventilation, vehicle emission norms, industrial filters, tree planting, masks on high-pollution days.

### Noise pollution

Unwanted sound, measured in **decibels (dB)**. Long exposure above about **85 dB** damages hearing.
**Effects:** noise-induced hearing loss, annoyance, poor sleep, high BP, poor concentration.
**Control:** silencers, sound barriers, ear plugs and muffs, limits on loudspeakers and horns.

### Sewage and solid waste disposal

**Sewage** = waste water from houses and industries. Treatment: primary (screening, grit removal, sedimentation) → secondary (biological: activated sludge, trickling filters, oxidation ponds) → (tertiary in some plants) → safe discharge. Rural options: sanitary latrines (pour-flush, two-pit), septic tanks. **Swachh Bharat Mission** promotes toilets and ending open defecation.

**Solid waste (refuse):** methods — composting, sanitary landfill, incineration, recycling; segregate at source into wet (green bin) and dry (blue bin).

**Bio-medical waste (BMW Management Rules, 2016) colour coding**

| Colour | Waste | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow | Anatomical waste, soiled dressings, expired or discarded medicines, chemical waste | Incineration / deep burial |
| Red | Contaminated recyclable plastics: tubing, catheters, IV sets, syringes without needles, gloves | Autoclave/microwave, then recycling |
| White (translucent, puncture-proof) | Sharps: needles, blades, scalpels | Autoclave, shredding |
| Blue | Glassware, broken or discarded glass, metal implants | Disinfection, then recycling |

### Occupational illnesses

Diseases caused by the work environment.

| Hazard | Disease / example |
| --- | --- |
| Dusts | Pneumoconioses: silicosis (silica, mining, stone-cutting), asbestosis, anthracosis (coal), byssinosis (cotton), bagassosis (sugarcane), farmer's lung |
| Chemicals | Lead poisoning, mercury poisoning, benzene (leukaemia), occupational dermatitis |
| Physical | Noise-induced deafness, heat stroke, radiation injury |
| Biological | Anthrax, brucellosis, hepatitis B and needle-stick injuries in health workers |
| Mechanical/ergonomic | Back pain, accidents, repetitive strain |
| Psychosocial | Stress, shift-work problems |

**Prevention:** engineering controls (ventilation, enclosure, wet drilling), protective equipment, pre-placement and periodic medical examination, health education. Laws: **Factories Act 1948**, **ESI Act 1948**.

### Environmental pollution due to pharmaceuticals

Medicines enter the environment from **factory effluents**, **hospital waste**, excretion by people and animals, and **wrong disposal** (flushing or throwing in garbage).

**Problems:**

- Antibiotic residues → **antimicrobial resistance** in environmental bacteria.
- Hormones such as ethinyl-oestradiol → feminisation of fish.
- Veterinary **diclofenac** in cattle carcasses caused a steep fall in India's vulture population; its veterinary use was banned.
- Contamination of drinking water and soil.

**Prevention and role of pharmacist:** effluent treatment plants in industry; green chemistry; take-back of unused and expired medicines; proper BMW segregation; never flush medicines; prescribe and dispense only needed quantities; educate the public.

## 2.5 Psychosocial pharmacy: drugs of misuse and abuse

**Definitions**

- **Drug misuse:** use of a medicine for a purpose or in a way not intended (e.g. extra sleeping pills).
- **Drug abuse:** self-administration of a drug, usually for pleasure, in a way that harms health or social life.
- **Tolerance:** larger doses needed for the same effect.
- **Dependence:** psychological (craving) and/or physical (withdrawal symptoms when stopped).
- **Addiction:** compulsive use despite harm.

| Class | Examples | Main dangers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opioids (narcotics) | Morphine, heroin (smack), codeine syrups, tramadol | Respiratory depression, overdose death, HIV/hepatitis by shared needles |
| CNS depressants | Alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates | Accidents, dependence, dangerous withdrawal |
| Stimulants | Cocaine, amphetamines | Psychosis, heart attack, stroke |
| Cannabis | Bhang, ganja, charas | Impaired memory, psychosis |
| Hallucinogens | LSD | Bad trips, accidents |
| Inhalants | Glue, solvents, whitener | Brain and liver damage, sudden death |
| Tobacco | Cigarettes, bidi, gutkha, khaini | Cancer, heart and lung disease |

**Law:** Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act, 1985.

**Alcohol:** acute — intoxication, accidents, violence; chronic — fatty liver, cirrhosis, pancreatitis, gastritis, thiamine deficiency (Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome), depression, family breakdown. Withdrawal — tremors, sweating, fits, delirium tremens. Treatment — detoxification with benzodiazepines plus thiamine; relapse prevention with disulfiram, naltrexone or acamprosate; counselling, self-help groups.

**Tobacco:** nicotine is the addictive substance. Causes oral, lung and throat cancers, heart attacks, stroke, COPD, low birth weight. Smokeless tobacco is common in India. **COTPA, 2003** bans smoking in public places, sale to minors and sale within 100 yards of educational institutions, and requires pictorial warnings. Help to quit: counselling (5 As and 5 Rs), nicotine replacement (gum, patch, lozenge), bupropion, varenicline.

**Social impact of these habits:** poor school and work performance, absenteeism and loss of productivity, poverty from spending on drugs, crime and violence, road accidents, family disputes and domestic violence, spread of HIV and hepatitis, stigma.

**Suicidal behaviour:** substance abuse, depression, chronic illness, debt, exam stress and family problems raise the risk. Warning signs: talking about death, giving away belongings, withdrawal from friends, buying large amounts of tablets or pesticides. Pharmacist role: notice repeated purchase of sedatives, listen without judging, refer to a doctor or the national tele-mental health helpline (**Tele-MANAS, 14416**), support safe storage of pesticides and medicines.

**Means to avoid substance abuse (role of pharmacist)**

1. Sell schedule H, H1 and X drugs and codeine syrups only on valid prescription; keep records.
2. Watch for repeated purchases and "doctor shopping".
3. Educate schools and youth about harms; use posters and talks.
4. Counsel smokers and drinkers to quit; supply nicotine replacement.
5. Refer to de-addiction centres; support families.
6. Report suspicious activity; follow the NDPS Act.
