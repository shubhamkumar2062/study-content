# Unit 1: Introduction to Social Pharmacy

Social Pharmacy looks at medicines and pharmacists from the side of **society**: how people get, use and misuse medicines, what keeps a community healthy, and how a pharmacist can help the public stay well, not only dispense tablets.

## 1.1 Definition and scope of Social Pharmacy

**Definition.** Social Pharmacy is the branch of pharmacy that studies the social, behavioural, economic and cultural factors that affect the use of medicines and the health of people, and the role of the pharmacist in improving public health.

In simple words: pharmaceutics asks *"how is the tablet made?"*; social pharmacy asks *"will the patient actually get it, take it correctly, afford it, and stay healthy?"*

**Social Pharmacy as a discipline** borrows ideas from several subjects:

| Subject it borrows from | What it adds |
| --- | --- |
| Sociology and psychology | Beliefs, habits, self-medication, compliance |
| Public health and epidemiology | Disease patterns, prevention, health programmes |
| Economics | Cost of medicines, affordability, pharmacoeconomics |
| Communication sciences | Counselling, health education, IEC materials |
| Law and ethics | Drug laws, rational use, patient rights |

**Scope of Social Pharmacy in improving public health**

1. **Rational use of medicines** — right drug, right dose, right duration, right price; fighting irrational fixed-dose combinations and antibiotic misuse.
2. **Health education** — teaching the public about hygiene, nutrition, vaccines, family planning and danger signs of diseases.
3. **Disease prevention** — immunisation awareness, screening camps (blood pressure, blood sugar), tobacco and alcohol cessation.
4. **Access to medicines** — generic medicines, Jan Aushadhi stores, free-drug schemes of government hospitals.
5. **Pharmacovigilance** — reporting adverse drug reactions and medicine quality problems.
6. **Support to national health programmes** — DOTS for TB, ORS and zinc for diarrhoea, iron–folic acid, condoms and oral pills.
7. **Safe disposal of medicines** and reducing environmental pollution from pharmaceuticals.
8. **Research** on medicine-use behaviour, adherence and community needs.

## 1.2 Role of pharmacists in public health

The community pharmacist is often the **most accessible health professional** — no appointment, no fee, open long hours, present even in small towns. This makes the pharmacy a natural "first point of contact".

**Key public-health roles**

- **Health promoter and educator:** advice on diet, exercise, hygiene, safe water, breastfeeding.
- **Counsellor:** explains dose, timing, side effects, storage; improves adherence in TB, HIV, diabetes, hypertension.
- **Screener and referrer:** checks BP, blood glucose, BMI; refers suspected TB (cough > 2 weeks), malaria, high-risk pregnancy to a doctor or PHC.
- **Supplier of preventive products:** ORS, zinc, condoms, sanitary pads, mosquito repellents, iodised salt advice.
- **Partner in national programmes:** DOTS provider, family planning distributor, vaccination awareness.
- **Pharmacovigilance and drug information:** reports adverse reactions, answers drug queries.
- **Responding in emergencies and disasters:** first aid, emergency supply of medicines.
- **Fighting misuse:** refuses to sell habit-forming drugs without prescription, counsels on tobacco and alcohol.

**The "seven-star pharmacist" (WHO idea, extended by FIP)**

```text
          Care-giver
   Leader             Decision-maker
 Manager      PHARMACIST      Communicator
   Teacher            Life-long learner
       (+ Researcher = "eight-star" pharmacist)
```

## 1.3 Concept of health

### WHO definition (1948)

> Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.

Points to note:

- It is **positive** — health is not just "no disease".
- It is **broad** — body, mind and social life all count.
- Criticism: "complete" well-being is an ideal that few people ever reach; it is hard to measure. Later, the idea of a socially and economically productive life was added to health goals ("Health for All").

### Dimensions of health

| Dimension | Meaning | Example of good health |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | Body works normally | Normal pulse, BP, weight; good sleep and appetite |
| Mental | Ability to think, learn, handle stress | Free from anxiety disorders; good self-esteem |
| Social | Getting on well with others | Good family and community relations |
| Spiritual | Sense of purpose, values | Inner peace, meaning in life |
| Emotional | Feelings handled well | Can express and control emotions |
| Vocational | Satisfaction from work | Job matches ability; work brings achievement |

Other dimensions sometimes listed: philosophical, cultural, educational, environmental, nutritional and preventive.

### Determinants of health (what decides how healthy a person is)

```text
              Biological (genes, age, sex)
                        |
 Behaviour/lifestyle --- HEALTH --- Environment (water, air, housing)
                        |
   Socio-economic (income, education, job) + Health services
```

1. **Biological/genetic** — inherited diseases (thalassaemia, haemophilia), sex, age.
2. **Behavioural and lifestyle** — smoking, alcohol, diet, exercise, sexual behaviour.
3. **Environmental** — physical (water, air, noise, housing), biological (vectors, microbes), psychosocial (stress, family).
4. **Socio-economic** — income, education (especially female literacy), occupation, political system.
5. **Health services** — availability, cost and quality of hospitals, medicines and vaccines.
6. **Ageing, gender, and others** — e.g. industrial growth, mass media, cultural beliefs.

### Health indicators

An **indicator** is a variable that helps measure the health of a community and to compare places or years.

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Mortality | Crude death rate, infant mortality rate (IMR), under-5 mortality, maternal mortality ratio (MMR), life expectancy |
| Morbidity | Incidence and prevalence of diseases, hospital admissions |
| Disability | Days of restricted activity, DALY (disability-adjusted life years) |
| Nutritional status | Low birth weight, stunting, wasting, underweight, anaemia |
| Health-care delivery | Doctor–population ratio, beds per population, PHC coverage |
| Utilisation | Immunisation coverage, institutional deliveries, contraceptive use |
| Socio-economic | Literacy, per-capita income, housing, safe water access |
| Composite | Physical Quality of Life Index (PQLI), Human Development Index (HDI) |

**Important formulas**

- **Crude death rate** = deaths in a year ÷ mid-year population × 1000
- **Infant mortality rate (IMR)** = deaths of infants below 1 year in a year ÷ live births in that year × 1000. IMR is the best single indicator of the health and living standard of a community.
- **Maternal mortality ratio (MMR)** = maternal deaths ÷ live births × 1,00,000
- **Life expectancy at birth** = average number of years a newborn is expected to live if current death rates continue.

**PQLI** combines three things: IMR, life expectancy at age one, and literacy (scale 0–100).
**HDI** (UNDP) combines: a long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (schooling) and a decent standard of living (income per head).

## 1.4 National Health Policy — Indian perspective

A health policy is the government's written plan of goals and the way to reach them.

| Policy | Main ideas |
| --- | --- |
| **NHP 1983** | "Health for All by 2000 AD"; expand primary health care through PHCs and sub-centres; community participation; health volunteers |
| **NHP 2002** | Increase public spending on health; strengthen primary care; decentralise; more role for the private sector; control of TB, malaria, HIV, blindness |
| **NHP 2017** | Highest possible level of health and well-being for all at all ages through preventive and promotive care; universal access to good-quality health care without financial hardship |

**Key features of NHP 2017 (learn these for 5 marks)**

1. Move from "sick care" to **wellness** — preventive and promotive health.
2. **Comprehensive primary care** through Health and Wellness Centres (now Ayushman Arogya Mandirs).
3. **Free essential medicines and diagnostics** in public hospitals.
4. Raise public health spending as a share of GDP in a time-bound way.
5. **Strategic purchasing** of services from the private sector where needed.
6. Focus on non-communicable diseases, mental health, and reducing out-of-pocket spending.
7. Mainstreaming of **AYUSH**; use of digital health (health records).
8. Encourage generic medicines and a strong drug regulatory system.

## 1.5 Public and private health system in India

Health is a **state subject** in the Constitution, but the centre funds national programmes and sets policy.

**Levels of administration:** Union Ministry of Health & Family Welfare (MoHFW) and Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) → State health departments → District (Chief Medical Officer) → Block → Village.

**Three-tier public health-care delivery**

```text
TERTIARY   Medical colleges, AIIMS, super-speciality hospitals
              ^
SECONDARY  District hospital, sub-district hospital, Community Health Centre (CHC)
              ^
PRIMARY    Primary Health Centre (PHC)
              ^
           Sub-Centre / Health and Wellness Centre  <-- ASHA, Anganwadi worker in the village
```

| Unit | Population covered (plains) | Population covered (hilly/tribal) | Staff and work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sub-centre | 5,000 | 3,000 | ANM and male health worker; immunisation, ANC, family planning |
| PHC | 30,000 | 20,000 | Medical officer, pharmacist, nurses; curative + preventive care, 4–6 beds |
| CHC | 1,20,000 | 80,000 | Specialists (surgeon, physician, gynaecologist, paediatrician); 30 beds; first referral unit |

These norms matter in Himachal Pradesh, where many areas are hilly, so the lower figures apply.

**Other public systems:** ESI (Employees' State Insurance) hospitals for workers, CGHS for central government employees, railway and defence hospitals, AYUSH hospitals.

**Private sector:** clinics, nursing homes, corporate hospitals, chemists, private labs. It provides a large share of out-patient care in India, but costs are higher and quality varies. NGOs, charitable trusts and voluntary organisations form a third sector.

| Public sector | Private sector |
| --- | --- |
| Free or low cost | Fee for service, often costly |
| Strong in rural preventive care and programmes | Strong in urban curative and specialist care |
| Crowding, staff shortages | Variable regulation and quality |

### National Health Mission (NHM)

- **NRHM** started in 2005 for rural areas; **NUHM** in 2013 for urban poor; both are now sub-missions of **NHM**.
- **Aim:** universal access to equitable, affordable and quality health care that is accountable and responsive to people's needs.

**Main components**

1. Health systems strengthening — infrastructure, staff, free drugs and diagnostics, ambulances (108/102).
2. **RMNCH+A** — reproductive, maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health.
3. Communicable disease control programmes (TB, malaria, leprosy, etc.).
4. Non-communicable disease control.

**Key features:** ASHA (Accredited Social Health Activist) in every village; Janani Suraksha Yojana (cash help for institutional delivery); Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (free delivery and care of sick newborns); Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (child screening); Rashtriya Kishor Swasthya Karyakram (adolescents); Rogi Kalyan Samitis (hospital committees); Village Health Sanitation and Nutrition Committees; flexible funds to states.

## 1.6 Development goals

### Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), 2000–2015

Agreed by UN member countries in 2000. Eight goals:

1. Eradicate extreme poverty and hunger
2. Achieve universal primary education
3. Promote gender equality and empower women
4. Reduce child mortality
5. Improve maternal health
6. Combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases
7. Ensure environmental sustainability
8. Develop a global partnership for development

Goals 4, 5 and 6 are directly health goals.

### Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), 2015–2030

They replaced the MDGs. There are **17 goals** covering poverty, hunger, health, education, gender, water, energy, work, industry, inequality, cities, consumption, climate, life below water, life on land, peace and partnerships.

**SDG 3 — Good health and well-being:** ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages. Its targets include reducing maternal and newborn deaths, ending epidemics of AIDS, TB, malaria and neglected tropical diseases, reducing deaths from non-communicable diseases, preventing substance abuse, reducing road-traffic deaths, universal access to reproductive health care, **universal health coverage** and access to safe, affordable essential medicines and vaccines.

Other health-linked SDGs: SDG 2 (zero hunger), SDG 6 (clean water and sanitation), SDG 5 (gender equality).

| MDGs | SDGs |
| --- | --- |
| 8 goals | 17 goals |
| 2000–2015 | 2015–2030 |
| Mainly for developing countries | Universal — all countries |
| Health in 3 separate goals | One broad health goal (SDG 3) plus links in others |

### FIP Development Goals

FIP (International Pharmaceutical Federation) launched its **Development Goals** in 2020 to transform pharmacy worldwide. They are **21 goals** grouped into three areas:

- **Academy (education):** academic capacity, early-career training, quality assurance of education.
- **Practice (professional services):** people-centred care, antimicrobial stewardship, communicable and non-communicable disease prevention, medicine safety, equity and access to medicines, digital health, sustainability.
- **Workforce:** leadership, competency development, workforce planning, gender and diversity balance.

They link directly to the SDGs and universal health coverage: the pharmacy workforce is seen as a key tool to reach SDG 3.

## Remember

- WHO health definition (1948): complete **physical, mental and social** well-being, not merely absence of disease.
- Six main dimensions: physical, mental, social, spiritual, emotional, vocational.
- IMR = infant deaths < 1 year per 1000 live births; MMR per 1,00,000 live births.
- Sub-centre 5,000 / PHC 30,000 / CHC 1,20,000 (hilly: 3,000 / 20,000 / 80,000).
- NHM = NRHM (2005) + NUHM (2013); ASHA is the village link worker.
- NHP 2017 = wellness, Health and Wellness Centres, free drugs and diagnostics.
- MDGs 8 (2000–2015); SDGs 17 (2015–2030), SDG 3 = health; FIP DGs 21 (2020).
