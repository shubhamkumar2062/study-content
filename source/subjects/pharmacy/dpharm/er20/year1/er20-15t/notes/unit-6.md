# Unit 6: Pharmacoeconomics

Health resources (money, staff, medicines, beds) are always limited. **Pharmacoeconomics** helps decide which medicine or service gives the **best value** — the most health for the money spent. It is a short chapter (about 2 hours) but gives easy marks for definitions and the four types of analysis.

## 6.1 Introduction and definition

**Pharmacoeconomics** is the branch of health economics that identifies, measures and compares the **costs** and the **consequences (outcomes)** of drug therapy and pharmaceutical services, to help choose the most efficient option.

In simple words: *"Is drug A worth its price compared with drug B?"*

```text
        COSTS (inputs)                 CONSEQUENCES (outputs)
  drug price, doctor visits,   --->    cures, lives saved, fewer
  tests, hospital stay, lost           complications, better quality
  work days                            of life
              \                         /
               ---- compare the two ----
                 (cost per unit of outcome)
```

**Why it is needed:**

- Health spending keeps rising (new costly drugs, ageing population, chronic diseases).
- In India much health spending is **out of pocket**, so medicine prices push families into poverty.
- Governments, hospitals and insurers must choose what to buy and what to cover.

## 6.2 Basic terminologies

### Costs

| Type of cost | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direct medical costs** | Money spent on medical goods and services | Medicines, doctor fees, tests, hospital stay, nursing, surgery |
| **Direct non-medical costs** | Money spent because of illness but not on medical care | Travel to hospital, food and stay for attendants, special diet, child care |
| **Indirect costs** | Loss of productivity because of illness or death | Lost wages, days off work or school, early death |
| **Intangible costs** | Non-money costs of pain and suffering | Pain, anxiety, grief, stigma; hard to measure |

Other cost terms:

- **Fixed cost:** does not change with the number of patients (building rent, equipment).
- **Variable cost:** changes with the number of patients (medicines, syringes).
- **Opportunity cost:** the value of the best alternative given up when resources are used for one purpose. Money spent on one costly drug cannot be used for vaccines.
- **Incremental cost:** extra cost of one option over another.
- **Average cost:** total cost ÷ number of units (patients).

### Outcomes (consequences) — the ECHO model

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **E**conomic | Outcomes counted in money | Costs saved, hospital days avoided |
| **C**linical | Medical events | Blood pressure controlled, infections cured, deaths avoided |
| **H**umanistic | The patient's view of health | Quality of life, patient satisfaction, functional status |

### Other key terms

- **Perspective:** whose costs are counted — the **patient**, the **provider** (hospital), the **payer** (government, insurer) or **society** (all costs; the broadest).
- **Utility:** a value for a health state from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health), showing how people prefer that state.
- **QALY (quality-adjusted life year):** years of life × quality (utility).
  *Example:* 4 years lived at a utility of 0.5 = 2 QALYs.
- **DALY (disability-adjusted life year):** years of healthy life **lost** because of early death or disability (used to measure disease burden).
- **Efficacy vs effectiveness:** efficacy = how well a drug works in ideal trial conditions; effectiveness = how well it works in everyday practice.
- **Efficiency:** getting the most outcome for the resources used.
- **Discounting:** future costs and benefits are given a lower present value.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** repeating the calculation with different values (e.g. a higher drug price) to see whether the conclusion still holds.
- **Decision analysis (decision tree):** a diagram that maps choices, their chances and results, to compare options.

## 6.3 Types of pharmacoeconomic analysis

| Method | Outcome measured as | When used | Result expressed as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cost-minimisation analysis (CMA)** | Outcomes proven **equal** | Two drugs equally effective (e.g. brand vs generic) | Cheaper option is chosen |
| **Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)** | Natural clinical units (mmHg lowered, cases cured, life-years gained) | Same kind of outcome, different effectiveness | Cost per unit of outcome; ICER |
| **Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)** | Money (outcomes converted into rupees) | Comparing programmes with different outcomes | Benefit-to-cost ratio; net benefit |
| **Cost-utility analysis (CUA)** | QALYs | When quality of life matters (chronic disease, cancer) | Cost per QALY gained |

### 1. Cost-minimisation analysis (CMA)

Used only when the outcomes of the options are shown to be the **same**. Then simply choose the lower cost.

*Example:* a branded and a generic amlodipine 5 mg tablet are bioequivalent. Brand costs ₹60 per strip and generic ₹15 — the generic is chosen.

### 2. Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA)

Compares costs with outcomes in **clinical units**.

*Example:*

| Antihypertensive | Cost per month (₹) | Patients reaching target BP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug A | 200 | 60 out of 100 |
| Drug B | 500 | 75 out of 100 |

- Cost per patient controlled: A = 200 × 100 ÷ 60 = about ₹333; B = 500 × 100 ÷ 75 = about ₹667.
- **Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER)** = (cost B − cost A) ÷ (effect B − effect A)
  = (₹50,000 − ₹20,000) ÷ (75 − 60) = ₹30,000 ÷ 15 = **₹2,000 per extra patient controlled** (for 100 patients a month).

Whether B is "worth it" depends on how much the payer is willing to pay for each extra controlled patient.

### 3. Cost-benefit analysis (CBA)

Both costs and outcomes are valued in **money**.

- **Benefit-to-cost ratio** = total benefits ÷ total costs (> 1 means worthwhile).
- **Net benefit** = benefits − costs (> 0 means worthwhile).

*Example:* a vaccination drive costs ₹10 lakh and prevents hospital and work-loss costs worth ₹40 lakh. Ratio = 4 : 1; net benefit = ₹30 lakh → worthwhile.

Drawback: putting a money value on life and health is difficult and controversial.

### 4. Cost-utility analysis (CUA)

A special kind of CEA where outcome is in **QALYs**.

*Example:* treatment X costs ₹1,00,000 and gives 2 extra QALYs → ₹50,000 per QALY. Treatment Y costs ₹60,000 and gives 1 QALY → ₹60,000 per QALY. X gives better value per QALY.

**Cost-of-illness study:** measures the total cost a disease puts on society (direct + indirect) — useful to show the burden of diseases such as diabetes or TB, though it does not compare treatments.

### Steps in a pharmacoeconomic evaluation

1. Define the problem and objective.
2. Choose the perspective.
3. Identify the options (alternatives) to compare.
4. Choose the type of analysis (CMA, CEA, CBA, CUA).
5. Identify and measure costs and outcomes.
6. Adjust for time (discounting) where needed.
7. Do the analysis (ratios, ICER, decision tree).
8. Do sensitivity analysis.
9. Present the results and make the decision.

## 6.4 Importance of pharmacoeconomics

1. **Formulary decisions:** hospitals choose which drugs to stock (drugs and therapeutics committees).
2. **Essential medicines lists:** the National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM) favours cost-effective drugs.
3. **Drug pricing:** price control of essential medicines by the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA) under the Drugs (Prices Control) Order.
4. **Insurance and government schemes:** deciding which treatments PM-JAY and state schemes should cover; **Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn)** studies cost-effectiveness for the government.
5. **Rational prescribing:** guiding doctors to equally effective, cheaper drugs; generic prescribing.
6. **Clinical guidelines:** standard treatment guidelines that consider cost.
7. **Drug development:** companies show value to get their drug used.
8. **Patients:** lower out-of-pocket spending; better adherence when medicines are affordable.

### Role of the pharmacist

- Suggest **generic substitutes** and Jan Aushadhi medicines where allowed.
- Avoid unnecessary and irrational drugs (e.g. antibiotics for colds, irrational fixed-dose combinations).
- Improve adherence — a medicine that is bought but not taken wastes money.
- Manage inventory to reduce expiry and wastage.
- Collect data on costs and outcomes for hospital studies.
- Counsel patients on the cheapest effective option.

### Limitations of pharmacoeconomic studies

- Costs and prices differ between countries, states and hospitals, so a foreign study may not apply in India.
- Intangible costs and quality of life are hard to measure; utilities depend on whose opinion is taken.
- Putting a money value on life (CBA) raises ethical objections.
- Trials measure efficacy in ideal patients; real-world effectiveness may be lower.
- Studies sponsored by manufacturers may be biased; results must be read critically.

### Quick worked QALY and DALY example

A patient with heart failure is expected to live 6 more years at a utility of 0.5 without a new drug (6 × 0.5 = 3 QALYs). With the drug he lives 8 years at 0.6 (8 × 0.6 = 4.8 QALYs). **QALYs gained = 1.8.** If the extra cost of the drug over those years is ₹90,000, the cost per QALY gained = 90,000 ÷ 1.8 = **₹50,000 per QALY**.

**DALY** = years of life lost (YLL) due to early death + years lived with disability (YLD). For example, if a disease causes 10 years of early death and 5 years of life with a disability weighted at 0.4, DALYs = 10 + (5 × 0.4) = 12. One DALY = one lost year of healthy life.

## Remember

- Pharmacoeconomics = costs vs consequences of drug therapy.
- Costs: direct medical, direct non-medical, indirect (lost productivity), intangible (pain).
- ECHO = Economic, Clinical, Humanistic outcomes.
- CMA → equal outcomes; CEA → clinical units, ICER; CBA → money, benefit-cost ratio; CUA → QALY.
- QALY = years × quality (0 to 1); DALY = healthy years lost.
- Uses: formulary, NLEM, NPPA pricing, insurance, HTAIn, generics.
