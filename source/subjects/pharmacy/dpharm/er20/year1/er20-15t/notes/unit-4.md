# Unit 4: Introduction to Microbiology, Epidemiology and Communicable Diseases

This is the heaviest unit of the syllabus (about 28 of the 75 hours). Learn it disease by disease using one fixed format: **agent → spread → incubation → clinical features → prevention → pharmacist's role.**

## 4.1 Introduction to microbiology and common micro-organisms

**Microbiology** is the study of living things too small to be seen without a microscope (micro-organisms or microbes). Most microbes are harmless or useful (curd, fermentation, gut flora, antibiotics production). A few cause disease; these are **pathogens**.

| Group | Size and structure | Examples of diseases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bacteria | Single-celled, no nucleus (prokaryotes), 0.5–5 µm, cell wall | TB, typhoid, cholera, tetanus, diphtheria |
| Viruses | Smallest (20–300 nm); DNA or RNA in a protein coat; grow only inside living cells | Measles, polio, influenza, HIV, dengue, COVID-19 |
| Fungi | Yeasts (single cells) and moulds (threads called hyphae) | Ringworm, oral thrush (Candida) |
| Protozoa | Single-celled animals with a nucleus | Malaria, amoebiasis, giardiasis, kala-azar |
| Helminths | Many-celled worms | Roundworm, hookworm, tapeworm, filaria |
| Rickettsia, Chlamydia | Very small bacteria that live inside cells | Typhus, trachoma |

**Shapes of bacteria**

```text
Cocci (round)      o  oo  oooo (chains: Streptococcus)   cluster (Staphylococcus)
Bacilli (rods)     ===  ===  (E. coli, Salmonella, Mycobacterium)
Vibrio (comma)     (  (  (  (Vibrio cholerae)
Spirochaetes       ~~~~~  (Treponema of syphilis)
```

**Gram stain** divides bacteria into **Gram-positive** (thick cell wall, stain purple/violet — Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Clostridium, Corynebacterium) and **Gram-negative** (thin wall with outer membrane, stain pink/red — E. coli, Salmonella, Vibrio, Neisseria). Mycobacteria are **acid-fast** (Ziehl–Neelsen stain shows red rods).

## 4.2 Epidemiology

**Epidemiology** is the study of the **distribution** (who, where, when) and **determinants** (why) of health-related states in populations, and the use of this study to **control** health problems.

**Applications of epidemiology**

1. Finding the cause and risk factors of a disease (e.g. smoking and lung cancer).
2. Measuring the burden of disease in a community (incidence, prevalence).
3. Studying the natural history of disease.
4. Planning and evaluating health services and national programmes.
5. Investigating outbreaks and controlling them.
6. Testing new vaccines and drugs (clinical trials).
7. Identifying high-risk groups for screening.

**Epidemiological triad**

```text
             AGENT (microbe, toxin)
              /            \
             /   DISEASE    \
            /                \
        HOST ---------------- ENVIRONMENT
 (age, immunity,          (water, climate,
  nutrition, habits)       vectors, crowding)
```

**Chain of infection:** reservoir/source → portal of exit → mode of transmission → portal of entry → susceptible host. Breaking any link stops spread.

### Important terms

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Endemic | Constant usual presence of a disease in an area | Malaria in some districts |
| Epidemic | Cases clearly more than expected in an area and time | Cholera after floods |
| Pandemic | Epidemic spread over several countries or continents | COVID-19, H1N1 in 2009 |
| Outbreak | A small, localised epidemic | Food poisoning after a wedding meal |
| Sporadic | Scattered cases with no link | Tetanus cases |
| Incubation period | Time from entry of the agent to the first symptoms | Cholera hours to 5 days |
| Isolation | Separating **sick (infected)** people so they do not spread disease | TB ward, COVID isolation |
| Quarantine | Restricting movement of **healthy contacts** for the longest incubation period | Contacts of Ebola |
| Contact tracing | Finding, testing and watching people who met a case | COVID-19, TB |
| Morbidity | Illness in a population (incidence, prevalence) | Cases of dengue per 1000 |
| Mortality | Death in a population | Deaths due to TB per 1,00,000 |
| Carrier | Infected person without symptoms who spreads the agent | Typhoid carrier |

**Incidence** = new cases in a period ÷ population at risk. **Prevalence** = all existing cases at a time ÷ total population.

### Modes of transmission

```text
DIRECT                                INDIRECT
- Direct contact (touch, sex)         - Vehicle-borne (water, food, blood)
- Droplet (cough, sneeze < 1-2 m)     - Vector-borne (mosquito, fly, flea)
- Contact with soil (tetanus)         - Airborne (droplet nuclei, dust)
- Inoculation into skin (bites)       - Fomites (towels, utensils, toys)
- Transplacental (mother to foetus)   - Unclean hands and fingers
```

## 4.3 Respiratory infections

These spread mainly by **droplets** and close contact. Common prevention: vaccination, cough and sneeze etiquette, masks, hand hygiene, ventilation, isolation of cases.

### Chickenpox (varicella)

- **Agent:** varicella-zoster virus (VZV).
- **Spread:** droplets and contact with fluid from blisters.
- **Incubation:** about 14–16 days (range 10–21).
- **Features:** mild fever, then itchy rash that appears in crops — macule → papule → vesicle ("dew drop") → crust. Rash is mostly on the trunk (centripetal). Different stages are seen together. Infectious from 1–2 days before rash until all lesions crust.
- **Complications:** secondary skin infection, pneumonia, encephalitis; later reactivation as **herpes zoster (shingles)**.
- **Prevention:** varicella vaccine (not in UIP); isolate till crusting; avoid aspirin in children (Reye's syndrome). Acyclovir for high-risk cases.

### Measles (rubeola)

- **Agent:** measles virus (paramyxovirus family). Highly infectious.
- **Spread:** droplets and airborne.
- **Incubation:** about 10 days to fever, about 14 days to rash.
- **Features:** fever with the "3 Cs" — cough, coryza, conjunctivitis; **Koplik's spots** (white spots inside cheeks); then red maculopapular rash starting behind ears and face, spreading down.
- **Complications:** pneumonia, diarrhoea, malnutrition, ear infection, encephalitis; rare late SSPE.
- **Prevention:** MR vaccine at 9–12 and 16–24 months; vitamin A for cases; isolation.

### Rubella (German measles)

- **Agent:** rubella virus.
- **Incubation:** 2–3 weeks.
- **Features:** mild fever, pink rash, swollen glands behind ears and at the back of the neck.
- **Danger:** infection in early pregnancy causes **congenital rubella syndrome** — cataract, deafness, heart defects, mental retardation.
- **Prevention:** MR vaccine; girls should be immune before pregnancy.

### Mumps

- **Agent:** mumps virus (paramyxovirus).
- **Incubation:** about 2–3 weeks (usually around 18 days).
- **Features:** fever, painful swelling of the **parotid glands** (one or both sides).
- **Complications:** orchitis (in adult males), meningitis, pancreatitis, deafness.
- **Prevention:** MMR vaccine (not in UIP, which uses MR).

### Influenza (including avian flu and H1N1)

- **Agent:** influenza virus types A, B, C (orthomyxovirus). Type A changes often: small changes (**antigenic drift**) cause yearly epidemics; big changes (**antigenic shift**) cause pandemics.
- **Incubation:** 1–3 days.
- **Features:** sudden fever, chills, headache, body ache, sore throat, cough, tiredness.
- **Avian flu (H5N1):** bird flu passing from infected poultry to humans; severe pneumonia; human-to-human spread is limited.
- **H1N1 ("swine flu"):** caused the 2009 pandemic; now a seasonal strain.
- **Treatment and prevention:** rest, fluids, paracetamol; **oseltamivir** for high-risk or severe cases; yearly influenza vaccine for elderly, pregnant women, health workers and chronic patients; cough etiquette.

### Coronavirus infections: SARS, MERS and COVID-19

| Disease | Virus | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SARS | SARS-CoV | Outbreak in 2002–03, starting in China; severe pneumonia |
| MERS | MERS-CoV | First seen in 2012 in the Middle East; camels are a source |
| COVID-19 | SARS-CoV-2 | Pandemic declared by WHO in March 2020 |

**COVID-19:** spreads by droplets, aerosols and contaminated surfaces; incubation 2–14 days (often about 5). Features — fever, dry cough, sore throat, tiredness, loss of smell and taste, breathlessness; severe pneumonia in elderly and people with diabetes, heart disease or poor immunity. Diagnosis: RT-PCR, rapid antigen test. Prevention: vaccination, masks, distancing, hand hygiene, ventilation, isolation, quarantine of contacts, contact tracing.

### Diphtheria

- **Agent:** *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* (Gram-positive rod); its **exotoxin** causes most damage.
- **Incubation:** 2–6 days.
- **Features:** sore throat, fever, a **greyish-white membrane** on tonsils and throat that bleeds when removed; swollen neck ("bull neck"); breathing obstruction.
- **Complications:** myocarditis, nerve paralysis.
- **Treatment:** diphtheria **antitoxin** plus penicillin or erythromycin; contacts get antibiotics.
- **Prevention:** pentavalent vaccine and DPT boosters, Td.

### Whooping cough (pertussis)

- **Agent:** *Bordetella pertussis* (Gram-negative).
- **Incubation:** 7–14 days.
- **Stages:** catarrhal (cold, most infectious) → paroxysmal (bouts of coughing ending in a "whoop", vomiting) → convalescent. Lasts weeks.
- **Complications:** pneumonia, convulsions, hernia, malnutrition; dangerous in infants.
- **Treatment:** azithromycin or erythromycin. **Prevention:** pentavalent/DPT vaccine.

### Meningococcal meningitis

- **Agent:** *Neisseria meningitidis* (Gram-negative diplococcus).
- **Spread:** droplets from carriers; crowding (hostels, camps, pilgrimages).
- **Incubation:** usually 3–4 days.
- **Features:** sudden fever, severe headache, vomiting, **neck stiffness**, light sensitivity, petechial rash, confusion; can kill within hours.
- **Treatment:** emergency hospital care with antibiotics (ceftriaxone, penicillin). **Close contacts** get chemoprophylaxis (rifampicin or ciprofloxacin). Meningococcal vaccines for travellers and outbreaks.

### Acute respiratory infections (ARI)

Infections of the nose, throat, ears, airways and lungs; **pneumonia** is a leading cause of death in children under 5.

- **Agents:** viruses (most colds); bacteria — *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b.
- **Danger sign — fast breathing (child at rest):** 60 or more per minute below 2 months; 50 or more at 2–12 months; 40 or more at 1–5 years. Chest in-drawing, inability to drink, convulsions, lethargy → refer urgently.
- **Management:** colds — home care, fluids, no antibiotics; pneumonia — amoxicillin as per guidelines; severe — hospital.
- **Prevention:** breastfeeding, good nutrition, Hib and PCV vaccines, less indoor smoke, no smoking near children.

### Tuberculosis (TB)

- **Agent:** *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (acid-fast bacillus).
- **Spread:** droplet nuclei when a patient with lung TB coughs; infection is common, disease develops in a smaller number, more in people with HIV, diabetes, malnutrition, smoking.
- **Features of pulmonary TB:** **cough for 2 weeks or more**, fever (evening rise), night sweats, loss of weight and appetite, blood in sputum, chest pain. Extra-pulmonary TB affects lymph nodes, spine, brain, abdomen.
- **Diagnosis:** sputum microscopy, rapid molecular tests (CBNAAT/Truenat), chest X-ray.
- **Treatment (drug-sensitive TB, NTEP):** 2 months of isoniazid (H), rifampicin (R), pyrazinamide (Z), ethambutol (E), then 4 months of HRE, as daily fixed-dose combinations. Drug-resistant TB needs longer special regimens.
- **Prevention:** BCG at birth (protects against severe childhood TB), early diagnosis and full treatment, cough etiquette, ventilation, preventive therapy for contacts, nutrition support.
- **Pharmacist's role:** refer anyone with cough over 2 weeks; ensure the full course; counsel on red-orange urine (rifampicin), hepatitis signs, vision problems (ethambutol), pyridoxine with isoniazid; notify TB patients to the programme.

### Ebola virus disease

- **Agent:** Ebola virus (filovirus). Reservoir thought to be fruit bats.
- **Spread:** direct contact with blood and body fluids of patients or dead bodies; health workers at high risk.
- **Incubation:** 2–21 days.
- **Features:** sudden fever, weakness, muscle pain, then vomiting, diarrhoea, rash and bleeding; high death rate.
- **Prevention:** strict infection control with PPE, isolation, safe burial, contact tracing; a vaccine exists for outbreak use. Monitoring of travellers from affected countries.

## 4.4 Intestinal infections

Mostly spread by the **faeco-oral route** (5 Fs: faeces, fingers, flies, fluids, food). Common prevention: safe water, sanitary toilets, hand-washing with soap, food hygiene, fly control, vaccination where available.

### Poliomyelitis

- **Agent:** poliovirus types 1, 2, 3 (enterovirus).
- **Incubation:** usually 7–14 days.
- **Features:** most infections are silent; a few cause **acute flaccid paralysis** (sudden floppy weakness, usually of legs, asymmetric, no sensory loss).
- **Prevention:** OPV and IPV in UIP; Pulse Polio campaigns; surveillance of every case of acute flaccid paralysis in children under 15. India was certified **polio-free in 2014**; vaccination continues because the virus still exists elsewhere.

### Viral hepatitis

| Type | Spread | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Faeco-oral | Children; usually mild; vaccine available |
| E | Faeco-oral, water-borne epidemics | Very dangerous in pregnancy |
| B | Blood, sexual, mother to baby, needles | Carrier state, chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis, liver cancer; vaccine in UIP (birth dose) |
| C | Mainly blood (transfusion, needles) | Often chronic; curable with oral antivirals |
| D | Blood; only with B | Worsens hepatitis B |

**Features:** fever, nausea, loss of appetite, pain in right upper abdomen, dark urine, **jaundice**.
**Prevention:** safe water and food (A, E); vaccination, screened blood, sterile needles, safe sex (B, C, D). The National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme provides free testing and treatment.

### Cholera

- **Agent:** *Vibrio cholerae* (O1, including the El Tor biotype, and O139).
- **Incubation:** a few hours to 5 days (usually 1–2 days).
- **Features:** sudden profuse painless watery diarrhoea (**"rice-water stools"**), vomiting, rapid severe dehydration, muscle cramps; can kill within hours.
- **Treatment:** **rehydration** is life-saving — ORS; IV Ringer lactate in severe cases; antibiotics (doxycycline) shorten illness.
- **Prevention:** safe chlorinated water, sanitation, food hygiene, oral cholera vaccine in outbreaks.

### Acute diarrhoeal diseases

Three or more loose stools in 24 hours. Causes: rotavirus (commonest in infants), *E. coli*, *Shigella* (dysentery with blood), *Campylobacter*, protozoa.

**Assessing dehydration:** restless/irritable, sunken eyes, drinks eagerly, skin pinch goes back slowly → some dehydration; lethargic, unable to drink, skin pinch very slow → severe dehydration.

**Management:**

1. **Low-osmolarity ORS** (osmolarity 245 mOsm/L) — continue during diarrhoea.
2. **Zinc** for 14 days (10 mg/day under 6 months, 20 mg/day above 6 months).
3. Continue feeding and breastfeeding.
4. Antibiotics only for bloody diarrhoea (dysentery) or cholera; avoid anti-motility drugs in children.
5. Refer if danger signs.

**Prevention:** exclusive breastfeeding, rotavirus vaccine, safe water, hand-washing, sanitation, measles vaccine, vitamin A.

### Typhoid (enteric fever)

- **Agent:** *Salmonella* Typhi (paratyphoid: *S.* Paratyphi A, B).
- **Spread:** food and water contaminated by patients or **carriers** (bacteria live in the gall bladder).
- **Incubation:** usually 10–14 days.
- **Features:** fever rising step by step ("step-ladder"), headache, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhoea, coated tongue, relatively slow pulse, rose spots, enlarged spleen.
- **Complications:** intestinal bleeding and **perforation** (third week).
- **Diagnosis:** blood culture (first week), Widal test (later, less reliable).
- **Treatment:** antibiotics as per sensitivity (e.g. azithromycin, ceftriaxone); full course.
- **Prevention:** safe water and food, sanitation, treat carriers, typhoid vaccines (Vi polysaccharide, typhoid conjugate vaccine, oral Ty21a).

### Amoebiasis

- **Agent:** *Entamoeba histolytica* (protozoan); spread by **cysts** in contaminated food and water.
- **Features:** amoebic dysentery — stools with blood and mucus, abdominal cramps; may cause **amoebic liver abscess** (fever, painful enlarged liver).
- **Treatment:** metronidazole or tinidazole, followed by a luminal agent (e.g. diloxanide furoate) to clear cysts.
- **Prevention:** safe water (boiling kills cysts; normal chlorine doses may not), food hygiene, hand-washing.

### Worm infestations

| Worm | How it enters | Main effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roundworm (*Ascaris*) | Swallowing eggs from soil-contaminated food | Malnutrition, intestinal blockage |
| Hookworm (*Ancylostoma*, *Necator*) | Larvae penetrate skin of bare feet | Blood loss → iron-deficiency anaemia |
| Pinworm/threadworm (*Enterobius*) | Swallowing eggs; self-reinfection | Itching around anus at night |
| Whipworm (*Trichuris*) | Swallowing eggs | Diarrhoea, rectal prolapse |
| Pork tapeworm (*Taenia solium*) | Undercooked pork; eggs | Cysticercosis of brain → fits |

**Treatment:** albendazole 400 mg single dose (200 mg for children 1–2 years). **National Deworming Day** gives albendazole to children and adolescents twice a year.
**Prevention:** toilets, wearing footwear, hand-washing, clean nails, washing vegetables, cooking meat well.

### Food poisoning

Illness caused by eating food contaminated with microbes or their toxins; typically many people who ate the same meal fall ill.

| Organism | Typical food | Onset after eating | Features |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* (toxin) | Milk products, sweets handled by infected hands | 1–6 hours | Vomiting, cramps |
| *Bacillus cereus* | Reheated fried rice | 1–6 hours (vomiting type) | Vomiting |
| *Salmonella* | Eggs, poultry, meat | 12–24 hours or more | Fever, diarrhoea |
| *Clostridium perfringens* | Meat dishes kept warm | 8–24 hours | Diarrhoea, cramps |
| *Clostridium botulinum* | Badly canned or preserved foods | 12–36 hours | Descending paralysis, double vision; antitoxin needed |

**Prevention:** cook well, eat hot or refrigerate quickly, avoid keeping cooked food at room temperature, hand hygiene of food handlers, discard swollen cans. **Treatment:** ORS and supportive care; botulism is an emergency.

## 4.5 Arthropod-borne infections

Spread by **vectors** (mostly mosquitoes). Common prevention: remove breeding sites, insecticide-treated nets, repellents, full-sleeve clothes, window screens, larvicides and larvivorous fish, indoor residual spraying.

| Mosquito | Habits | Diseases |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Aedes aegypti* | Day biter; breeds in clean water in coolers, tyres, pots | Dengue, chikungunya, Zika, yellow fever |
| Female *Anopheles* | Night biter; clean water | Malaria |
| *Culex* | Night biter; dirty water, drains | Filariasis (W. bancrofti), Japanese encephalitis |

### Dengue

- **Agent:** dengue virus, 4 serotypes (DENV 1–4).
- **Incubation:** usually 4–7 days (range 3–10).
- **Features:** sudden high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, severe muscle and joint pains ("break-bone fever"), rash, low platelet count.
- **Warning signs:** abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding gums, restlessness, falling platelets → **dengue haemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome**; a second infection with another serotype raises the risk.
- **Treatment:** fluids, rest, **paracetamol only**; **avoid aspirin, ibuprofen and other NSAIDs** (bleeding risk).
- **Diagnosis:** NS1 antigen (early), IgM antibody.

### Malaria

- **Agent:** *Plasmodium vivax* (commonest in India), *P. falciparum* (most dangerous), *P. malariae*, *P. ovale*.
- **Vector:** female *Anopheles* mosquito.
- **Incubation:** about 12 days for falciparum and about 14 days for vivax (varies).
- **Features:** fever with **chills and rigors**, then sweating; headache, vomiting; anaemia, enlarged spleen. Vivax can relapse from dormant liver stages (hypnozoites).
- **Complications (falciparum):** cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, kidney failure, death.
- **Diagnosis:** rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or blood smear.
- **Treatment (national guidelines):** vivax — chloroquine for 3 days plus **primaquine for 14 days** (radical cure, check for G6PD deficiency where possible; not in pregnancy or infants); falciparum — **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)** plus a single dose of primaquine; severe malaria — injectable artesunate in hospital.
- **Prevention:** long-lasting insecticidal nets, indoor spraying, larvivorous fish (*Gambusia*, guppy), repellents, chemoprophylaxis for travellers.

### Filariasis (lymphatic filariasis)

- **Agent:** *Wuchereria bancrofti* (most cases, spread by *Culex*) and *Brugia malayi* (spread by *Mansonia*).
- **Features:** fever, swollen lymph nodes; years later **lymphoedema** of legs (elephantiasis), hydrocele.
- **Diagnosis:** night blood smear (microfilariae appear in blood at night), antigen tests.
- **Control:** **mass drug administration** in endemic areas — DEC + albendazole, or triple drug IDA (ivermectin + DEC + albendazole); care of swollen limbs (washing, skin care); hydrocele surgery; mosquito control.

### Chikungunya

- **Agent:** chikungunya virus (alphavirus); spread by *Aedes*.
- **Incubation:** usually 3–7 days.
- **Features:** sudden fever and **severe joint pain** that may last weeks or months (the name means "that which bends up"); rash.
- **Treatment:** rest, fluids, paracetamol; avoid NSAIDs until dengue is ruled out. **Prevention:** as for dengue.

## 4.6 Surface infections

### Trachoma

- **Agent:** *Chlamydia trachomatis*.
- **Spread:** eye discharge via fingers, towels, flies; poor water and hygiene.
- **Features:** chronic conjunctivitis with follicles → scarring of the eyelid → in-turned lashes (trichiasis) → corneal damage → **blindness**.
- **Control — SAFE strategy:** **S**urgery for trichiasis, **A**ntibiotics (azithromycin, tetracycline eye ointment), **F**acial cleanliness, **E**nvironmental improvement (water, sanitation). WHO validated India as having eliminated trachoma as a public health problem in 2024; surveillance continues.

### Tetanus

- **Agent:** *Clostridium tetani* — spores live in soil, dust and animal dung; enter through wounds (including the umbilical cord of newborns).
- **Incubation:** usually 6–10 days (range 3–21).
- **Features:** toxin causes painful muscle spasms — **lockjaw (trismus)**, fixed grin (risus sardonicus), arched back (opisthotonus), spasms triggered by noise or touch.
- **Neonatal tetanus:** from unclean cord cutting; prevented by Td for mothers and clean delivery. India was validated as having eliminated maternal and neonatal tetanus in 2015.
- **Treatment:** hospital care, human tetanus immunoglobulin, wound cleaning, metronidazole, muscle relaxants.
- **Prevention:** vaccination (pentavalent, DPT, Td); clean wounds; Td booster after dirty wounds.

### Leprosy (Hansen's disease)

- **Agent:** *Mycobacterium leprae*.
- **Spread:** prolonged close contact; droplets from the nose of untreated patients. Most people are naturally resistant.
- **Incubation:** long — on average several years (commonly 3–5).
- **Features:** pale or reddish skin patches with **loss of sensation**; thickened, tender nerves; weakness of hands and feet; deformities from repeated painless injury.
- **Types:** paucibacillary (PB, up to 5 patches) and multibacillary (MB, more than 5 patches or nerve involvement or positive smear).
- **Treatment — multidrug therapy (MDT):** rifampicin, dapsone and clofazimine; WHO recommends the three drugs for both types, for 6 months (PB) and 12 months (MB). Free under the National Leprosy Eradication Programme.
- **Prevention:** early detection and MDT, examining contacts, single-dose rifampicin for contacts, fighting stigma, care of hands, feet and eyes.

## 4.7 Sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) and HIV/AIDS

### Common STDs

| Disease | Agent | Main features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Syphilis | *Treponema pallidum* | Painless genital ulcer (chancre) → rash → late heart and brain damage; congenital syphilis in babies |
| Gonorrhoea | *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* | Pus discharge from urethra, painful urination; infertility |
| Chlamydia | *Chlamydia trachomatis* | Often silent; pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility |
| Chancroid | *Haemophilus ducreyi* | Painful genital ulcer, tender lymph nodes |
| Genital herpes | Herpes simplex virus (mainly HSV-2) | Painful recurrent blisters |
| Trichomoniasis | *Trichomonas vaginalis* | Frothy vaginal discharge, itching |
| Genital warts | Human papilloma virus (HPV) | Warts; some HPV types cause cervical cancer |

**Management:** **syndromic management** — treating the syndrome (urethral discharge, genital ulcer, vaginal discharge) at the first visit with standard drug kits; treat partners; counsel on condoms; test for HIV and syphilis.

### HIV/AIDS

- **Agent:** human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1, HIV-2), a **retrovirus** that destroys **CD4 T-lymphocytes**.
- **Spread:** unprotected sex (main route), infected blood and needles (injecting drug users), mother to child (pregnancy, delivery, breastfeeding). **Not** spread by touching, hugging, sharing food, mosquitoes.
- **Window period:** time after infection when antibody tests may still be negative.
- **Course:** acute flu-like illness → long symptom-free period (years) → falling CD4 count → **AIDS** with opportunistic infections (TB is the commonest in India, oral thrush, pneumocystis pneumonia, cryptococcal meningitis), weight loss, chronic diarrhoea.
- **Diagnosis:** HIV antibody tests at an Integrated Counselling and Testing Centre (ICTC), confirmed by approved testing strategy.
- **Treatment:** lifelong **antiretroviral therapy (ART)** started soon after diagnosis ("test and treat"); first-line in India is a fixed-dose combination of tenofovir + lamivudine + dolutegravir. ART cannot cure but keeps the virus suppressed so the person lives a near-normal life and does not transmit it sexually.
- **Prevention:** condoms, safe blood, sterile needles, prevention of parent-to-child transmission (ART for pregnant women), post-exposure prophylaxis after needle-stick injury, awareness. National AIDS Control Programme (NACO).

## 4.8 Role of pharmacists in educating the public

1. **Explain how each disease spreads** and how to block it — posters, leaflets, talks in local language.
2. **Promote vaccination** and remind about due doses.
3. **Teach hygiene:** hand-washing, safe water, food safety, toilet use, cough etiquette, masks.
4. **Vector control advice:** emptying coolers weekly, nets, repellents.
5. **Early referral:** cough over 2 weeks, fever with chills, jaundice, bloody stools, danger signs in children, genital ulcers.
6. **Rational drug use:** no antibiotics for colds; paracetamol, not NSAIDs, in dengue; complete TB, leprosy and malaria courses; ORS and zinc for diarrhoea.
7. **Confidential, non-judgemental counselling** for STDs and HIV; supply condoms.
8. **Support national programmes** and report cases and adverse reactions.

## Remember

- Isolation = sick people; quarantine = healthy contacts.
- Epidemiological triad: agent, host, environment.
- Measles: Koplik's spots, 3 Cs; rubella in pregnancy → congenital rubella syndrome.
- Diphtheria: grey membrane, bull neck, antitoxin; pertussis: whoop, azithromycin.
- TB: cough ≥ 2 weeks; 2HRZE + 4HRE; BCG at birth.
- Cholera = rice-water stools → ORS; diarrhoea = low-osmolarity ORS + zinc 14 days.
- Dengue: Aedes day-biter, paracetamol only, no NSAIDs; malaria: Anopheles, vivax = chloroquine + primaquine 14 days.
- Trachoma SAFE; tetanus lockjaw; leprosy = anaesthetic patch + MDT.
- HIV: retrovirus, CD4 cells; TLD-based lifelong ART; not spread by casual contact.
