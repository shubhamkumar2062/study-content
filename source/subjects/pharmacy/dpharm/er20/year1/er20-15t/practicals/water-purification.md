# Water purification: water testing kit and bleaching powder dose for wells and tanks

**Aim:** (a) To learn household water purification methods and use a water-testing kit; (b) to determine the available chlorine in bleaching powder; (c) to calculate the amount of bleaching powder needed to disinfect a well or tank.

## Principle

Chlorine kills most bacteria and viruses in water. **Bleaching powder** (chlorinated lime) contains about **33% available chlorine** when fresh, but loses strength on storage, so its strength should be checked. After chlorination, a **free residual chlorine of about 0.5 mg/L after 1 hour** shows that the dose was enough.

**Available chlorine (iodometry):** bleaching powder releases chlorine in acid; chlorine liberates iodine from potassium iodide; the iodine is titrated with sodium thiosulphate using starch.

Cl2 + 2KI → 2KCl + I2  I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6

**Horrock's apparatus** finds the dose of bleaching powder for a well directly: a series of cups of well water receives increasing amounts of a bleaching powder solution; the first cup that turns blue with starch-iodide shows the number of level spoonfuls (2 g each) needed per 455 litres (100 gallons).

## Requirements

Bleaching powder, potassium iodide, glacial acetic acid, 0.1 N sodium thiosulphate, starch solution, conical flask, burette, mortar and pestle, volumetric flask; field water-testing kit (residual chlorine/orthotoluidine or DPD comparator, H2S bacterial strip test, pH strips); measuring tape for well dimensions; Horrock's apparatus if available.

## Procedure

**A. Available chlorine in bleaching powder**

1. Weigh about 2.5 g of bleaching powder (W), triturate with water and make up to 250 ml in a volumetric flask.
2. Pipette 25 ml into a conical flask, add about 1 g potassium iodide and 5 ml acetic acid (liberates iodine — brown colour).
3. Titrate with 0.1 N sodium thiosulphate till pale yellow; add starch (blue) and continue till colourless. Note volume V ml.

**B. Testing water with a kit**

1. Residual chlorine: add the reagent to the water sample and compare the colour with the standard (target about 0.5 mg/L).
2. Bacterial (H2S strip) test: add sample to the bottle, keep at room temperature 24–48 hours; black colour suggests faecal contamination.
3. pH: dip strip and compare.

**C. Dose for a well**

1. Measure the diameter (d) of the well and the depth of water (h) in metres.
2. Calculate the volume and the bleaching powder needed.

## Observations and calculations

**A. Available chlorine**

1 ml of 0.1 N sodium thiosulphate ≡ 0.003545 g of chlorine.

% available chlorine = (V × N × 35.45 × 250 × 100) ÷ (W × 1000 × 25)

*Worked example:* W = 2.5 g, V = 23.5 ml, N = 0.1 → (23.5 × 0.1 × 35.45 × 10 × 100) ÷ (2.5 × 1000) = **about 33.3%**.

**C. Well disinfection**

Volume of water (litres) = 3.14 × (d/2)² × h × 1000

*Example:* d = 2 m, h = 4 m → 3.14 × 1 × 4 × 1000 = **12,560 litres**.

A commonly used guide is about **2.5 g of good bleaching powder per 1000 litres** of water (for routine well disinfection). Dose = 12.56 × 2.5 ≈ **31 g**. Adjust if the powder is weaker (multiply by 33 ÷ actual % available chlorine) or use Horrock's test.

Method: make a thin paste of the powder in a bucket, add water, let lime settle, pour the clear supernatant into the well, mix by lowering and raising the bucket, wait at least 1 hour before use and check residual chlorine.

**Potassium permanganate** was once used for wells but is a weak disinfectant and not recommended for this purpose; chlorination is the method of choice. A 0.1% (1 in 1000) KMnO4 solution = 1 g in 1 litre, sometimes used for washing.

## Precautions

- Store bleaching powder in airtight, dark, cool containers; it loses chlorine in air and light.
- Titrate promptly after adding acid; add starch only near the end point.
- Chlorinate wells preferably at night so the water stands for the contact period.

## Viva questions

1. **What is available chlorine in fresh bleaching powder?** About 33%.
2. **What residual chlorine should remain after 1 hour?** About 0.5 mg/L.
3. **What is Horrock's apparatus used for?** To find the bleaching powder dose for a well.
4. **Why is starch added near the end point?** Starch–iodine gives a sharp blue colour; added early it binds iodine firmly and blurs the end point.
5. **What does a positive H2S strip test indicate?** Probable faecal contamination of water.
