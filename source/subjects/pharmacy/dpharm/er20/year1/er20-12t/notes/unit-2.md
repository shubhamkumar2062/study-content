# Unit 2: Volumetric and gravimetric analysis

Most assays in the Indian Pharmacopoeia are titrations. This unit explains the basic ideas of volumetric analysis, the five types of titration in the syllabus, and the principle of gravimetric analysis.

## 2.1 Fundamentals of volumetric analysis

**Volumetric (titrimetric) analysis** finds the amount of a substance by measuring the **volume** of a solution of known strength (the **titrant**) that reacts completely with it.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Titrant | Solution of known strength, taken in the burette |
| Titrand (analyte) | Solution being analysed, taken in the conical flask |
| Titration | Gradual addition of titrant until the reaction is complete |
| Equivalence point | Point where exactly equivalent amounts have reacted (theoretical) |
| End point | Point where the indicator changes colour (practical); should be very close to the equivalence point |
| Indicator | Substance that shows the end point by a colour change |
| Standard solution | Solution whose exact concentration is known |
| Standardisation | Finding the exact strength of a solution by titrating it against a primary standard |

**Requirements of a reaction for titration**

1. It must be fast.
2. It must go to completion (stoichiometric, no side reactions).
3. There must be a way to see the end point.
4. It must be described by a balanced equation.

### 2.1.1 Ways of expressing concentration

| Unit | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Molarity (M) | Moles of solute in 1 litre of solution |
| Normality (N) | Gram-equivalents of solute in 1 litre of solution |
| Molality (m) | Moles of solute in 1 kg of solvent |
| % w/v | Grams in 100 ml of solution |
| ppm | Parts per million (mg per kg or µg per ml in dilute solutions) |

**Equivalent weight** = molecular weight ÷ n, where n is the number of H⁺/OH⁻ exchanged (acids/bases) or electrons gained/lost (redox).

Examples: HCl 36.46 (n = 1); H2SO4 98.08 ÷ 2 = 49.04; oxalic acid dihydrate 126.07 ÷ 2 = 63.03; KMnO4 in acid 158.03 ÷ 5 = 31.61.

**Normality equation:** N1V1 = N2V2 (equal equivalents react).

### 2.1.2 Primary and secondary standards

A **primary standard** is a substance pure enough that a solution of exact strength can be made by direct weighing.

Qualities of a primary standard:

1. Very high purity (99.9%) and easy to purify.
2. Stable in air: not hygroscopic, does not absorb CO2, not oxidised.
3. Easy to dry and weigh.
4. High equivalent weight (reduces weighing error).
5. Reacts stoichiometrically and quickly.
6. Easily soluble.

| Titration type | Primary standards |
| --- | --- |
| Acid–base | Potassium hydrogen phthalate (for bases), anhydrous sodium carbonate, borax (for acids) |
| Redox | Oxalic acid / sodium oxalate, potassium dichromate, arsenic trioxide |
| Precipitation | Sodium chloride |
| Complexometric | Zinc metal / zinc sulphate, calcium carbonate |

A **secondary standard** (e.g. NaOH, HCl, KMnO4 solutions) cannot be weighed exactly or is not stable, so its strength is fixed by **standardisation** against a primary standard.

By technique: **direct** titration, **back (residual)** titration (excess reagent added and the excess titrated back), **replacement** titration, and **blank** titration.

## 2.2 Acid–base (neutralisation) titrations

**Principle:** acid + base → salt + water (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H2O).

- **Acidimetry** – estimating a base with a standard acid.
- **Alkalimetry** – estimating an acid with a standard base.

### 2.2.1 Acid–base indicators

Indicators are weak organic acids or bases whose two forms (ionised and un-ionised) have different colours.

| Indicator | pH range | Acid colour | Base colour |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Methyl orange | 3.1–4.4 | Red | Yellow |
| Methyl red | 4.2–6.3 | Red | Yellow |
| Bromothymol blue | 6.0–7.6 | Yellow | Blue |
| Phenol red | 6.8–8.4 | Yellow | Red |
| Phenolphthalein | 8.3–10.0 | Colourless | Pink |
| Thymol blue (second change) | 8.0–9.6 | Yellow | Blue |

**Theories of indicators**

1. **Ostwald's theory:** the indicator is a weak acid HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻. HIn and In⁻ have different colours. Acid pushes the equilibrium left (acid colour); base pulls it right (base colour).
2. **Quinonoid theory:** the indicator exists in two tautomeric forms, a benzenoid form and a quinonoid form, of different colour. Change of pH changes which form dominates (e.g. phenolphthalein becomes pink quinonoid in alkali).

### 2.2.2 Neutralisation curves and choice of indicator

A **neutralisation curve** plots pH against volume of titrant. Near the equivalence point the pH changes sharply. The indicator must change colour within this steep part.

| Titration | pH at equivalence | Steep region | Suitable indicator |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Strong acid–strong base (HCl–NaOH) | 7 | About 3–10 | Any: methyl orange, methyl red, phenolphthalein |
| Weak acid–strong base (acetic acid–NaOH) | Above 7 | About 7–10 | Phenolphthalein |
| Strong acid–weak base (HCl–NH3) | Below 7 | About 3–7 | Methyl orange, methyl red |
| Weak acid–weak base | No sharp change | — | Not titrated; no good indicator |

```text
 pH

  6 |                        |      equivalence point
  4 |                        |
  2 |__________________ _____|
    +-----------------------------------> ml NaOH
        Strong acid vs strong base
```

**Pharmaceutical examples:** assay of ibuprofen and aspirin (alkalimetry with NaOH), assay of sodium bicarbonate (acidimetry with HCl, methyl orange), boric acid assayed with NaOH after adding glycerin (glycerin forms a stronger complex acid).

## 2.3 Non-aqueous titrations

Many drugs are very weak acids or bases, or are insoluble in water. In water their end points are not sharp because water competes as a weak acid and weak base. Using a **non-aqueous solvent** solves this.

**Principle (Brønsted–Lowry):** an acid is a proton donor, a base a proton acceptor. A suitable solvent **enhances** the acidic or basic strength of the weak substance (levelling effect), giving a sharp end point.

### 2.3.1 Types of solvents

| Type | Behaviour | Examples | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aprotic (inert) | Neither give nor take protons | Benzene, chloroform, carbon tetrachloride | Diluents |
| Protophilic (basic) | Accept protons; enhance acidity of weak acids | Pyridine, dimethylformamide, ethylenediamine, liquid ammonia | Titrating weak acids |
| Protogenic (acidic) | Donate protons; enhance basicity of weak bases | Glacial acetic acid, formic acid | Titrating weak bases |
| Amphiprotic | Both donate and accept | Water, alcohols, acetic acid (weakly) | General |

### 2.3.2 Titration of weak bases (acidimetry in non-aqueous medium)

- **Titrant:** 0.1 M **perchloric acid in glacial acetic acid** (the strongest acid in this medium; acetic acid converts it to CH3COOH2⁺, the onium ion, which protonates the base).
- **Indicator:** **crystal violet** (violet → blue-green → green / yellowish-green), also oracet blue B, quinaldine red.
- **Standardised against:** potassium hydrogen phthalate.
- **Halide salts of bases** (e.g. ephedrine hydrochloride): halide is too weak a base to be titrated, so **mercuric acetate** is added; it converts the halide into undissociated mercuric halide and liberates acetate, which is then titrated.
- **Examples:** ephedrine, chlorpromazine hydrochloride, diazepam, amines, salts of organic acids.

Water must be kept out: acetic anhydride is added during preparation of the titrant to react with water present in the perchloric acid.

### 2.3.3 Titration of weak acids (alkalimetry in non-aqueous medium)

- **Titrant:** 0.1 M **sodium methoxide** (or lithium/potassium methoxide, or tetrabutylammonium hydroxide) in toluene–methanol.
- **Solvent:** dimethylformamide or pyridine.
- **Indicator:** thymol blue, azo violet.
- **Standardised against:** benzoic acid.
- **Examples:** barbiturates, phenytoin, sulphonamides, ethosuximide.
- Protect from atmospheric CO2 and moisture.

## 2.4 Precipitation titrations (argentometry)

**Principle:** the analyte forms a sparingly soluble precipitate with the titrant. Most use **silver nitrate** to estimate halides (chloride, bromide, iodide) and thiocyanate.

Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓

| Method | Medium | Indicator | End point | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Mohr's method** | Neutral or faintly alkaline (pH 6.5–9) | Potassium chromate | First permanent reddish-brown silver chromate | Direct titration of chloride with AgNO3; fails in acid (chromate turns to dichromate) |
| **Volhard's method** | Strongly acidic (HNO3) | Ferric ammonium sulphate (ferric alum) | Red colour of ferric thiocyanate | Back titration: excess AgNO3 added, excess titrated with ammonium/potassium thiocyanate |
| **Modified Volhard's method** | Acidic | Ferric alum | Red colour | Nitrobenzene (or dibutyl phthalate) added to coat AgCl and stop it reacting with thiocyanate |
| **Fajans' method** | Depends on indicator | Adsorption indicators: fluorescein, dichlorofluorescein, eosin | Colour change on the precipitate surface | Indicator anion adsorbs on the positively charged precipitate after the equivalence point |

**Why nitrobenzene in modified Volhard?** Silver chloride is more soluble than silver thiocyanate, so near the end point AgCl would react with SCN⁻ and give a fading end point and low results. Nitrobenzene coats the AgCl particles and prevents this.

**Pharmaceutical example:** assay of sodium chloride and potassium chloride.

## 2.5 Complexometric titrations

**Principle:** a metal ion reacts with a **chelating agent** to form a stable, water-soluble complex. The most used agent is **EDTA (disodium edetate)**.

**EDTA** (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) has six donor atoms (two N and four O). It reacts with metal ions in a **1 : 1 ratio**, whatever the charge of the metal, forming a cage-like complex (chelate).

M²⁺ + H2Y²⁻ → MY²⁻ + 2H⁺

The reaction releases H⁺, so a **buffer** is needed (pH 10 ammonia–ammonium chloride buffer for Ca and Mg).

### 2.5.1 Metal ion (metallochromic) indicators

These are dyes that form a coloured complex with the metal, weaker than the metal–EDTA complex. At the end point EDTA takes the metal away and the free indicator colour appears.

| Indicator | Metals | Colour change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mordant black II (Eriochrome black T) | Mg, Zn, Ca (with Mg) at pH 10 | Wine red → blue |
| Murexide (ammonium purpurate) | Ca, Ni, Cu | Pink/red → purple/violet |
| Calcon / hydroxynaphthol blue | Ca at pH 12–13 | Red → blue |
| Xylenol orange | Bi, Pb, Zn in acid | Red → yellow |
| Catechol violet | Bi, others | Blue → yellow |

### 2.5.2 Types of complexometric titrations

1. **Direct titration** – metal titrated directly with EDTA (e.g. zinc, magnesium salts).
2. **Back titration** – excess EDTA added, excess titrated with standard Zn or Mg solution; used for metals that react slowly or precipitate at the working pH (e.g. aluminium, lead).
3. **Replacement titration** – a Mg–EDTA complex is added; the analyte metal (forming a stronger complex) displaces Mg, which is titrated. Used for calcium with Eriochrome black T, which gives a poor end point with calcium alone.
4. **Indirect titration** – anions (sulphate, phosphate) precipitated with excess metal, the excess metal then titrated.

**Masking:** adding an agent (e.g. cyanide, triethanolamine, fluoride) to stop an interfering metal from reacting with EDTA. **Demasking** releases it again.

**Pharmaceutical examples:** calcium gluconate, calcium lactate, magnesium sulphate, zinc oxide, aluminium hydroxide gel (back titration).

## 2.6 Redox titrations

**Principle:** a transfer of electrons between oxidising and reducing agents. **Oxidation** = loss of electrons; **reduction** = gain of electrons.

### 2.6.1 Main types

| Type | Titrant | Indicator | Example assay |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Permanganometry** | KMnO4 in H2SO4 | Self (KMnO4 itself, colourless → pink) | Ferrous sulphate, hydrogen peroxide, oxalic acid |
| **Dichrometry** | K2Cr2O7 | Diphenylamine / barium diphenylamine sulphonate | Ferrous salts |
| **Cerimetry** | Ceric ammonium sulphate | Ferroin (o-phenanthroline ferrous complex) | Ferrous sulphate, paracetamol (after hydrolysis) |
| **Iodimetry** (direct) | Standard iodine solution | Starch (blue at end) | Ascorbic acid, sodium thiosulphate |
| **Iodometry** (indirect) | Iodine liberated by the analyte from KI, titrated with sodium thiosulphate | Starch (blue disappears) | Copper sulphate, chlorine in bleaching powder, potassium dichromate |
| **Bromometry / bromatometry** | KBrO3 + KBr (gives Br2) | Methyl orange / iodometric back titration | Phenol |

**Permanganate in acid:** MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O. KMnO4 acts as its own indicator (purple → colourless until the first excess drop gives faint pink). Sulphuric acid is used, not HCl (permanganate oxidises chloride) and not nitric acid (itself an oxidant).

**Iodimetry vs iodometry**

| Iodimetry | Iodometry |
| --- | --- |
| Direct titration with iodine | Iodine produced in the reaction is titrated with thiosulphate |
| Analyte is a reducing agent | Analyte is an oxidising agent |
| End point: blue colour appears | End point: blue colour disappears |
| Example: ascorbic acid | Example: copper sulphate, bleaching powder |

Starch is added **near the end point** in iodometry, because a large amount of iodine forms a starch–iodine complex that is slow to release.

**Redox indicators** change colour at a particular electrode potential (diphenylamine, ferroin, N-phenylanthranilic acid).

## 2.7 Gravimetric analysis

**Gravimetric analysis** determines the amount of a constituent by **weighing** it, or a compound of known composition derived from it, after separating it from the sample.

**Principle:** the constituent is converted into a pure, stable, insoluble substance of known formula, which is collected, dried or ignited and weighed. Its amount is calculated using the **gravimetric factor**.

**Gravimetric factor** = (formula weight of substance sought) ÷ (formula weight of substance weighed), adjusted for the number of atoms.

Example: sulphate weighed as BaSO4. Weight of SO4 = weight of BaSO4 × (96.06 ÷ 233.39).

### 2.7.1 Steps of a gravimetric method

```text
Weigh sample → dissolve → precipitate (add reagent slowly, hot, dilute, with stirring)
      → digest (keep warm with mother liquor; small crystals dissolve, large grow)
      → filter (ashless filter paper or sintered/Gooch crucible)
      → wash (remove adsorbed impurities)
      → dry or ignite to constant weight → cool in desiccator → weigh
      → calculate using gravimetric factor
```

**Requirements of a good precipitate:** very low solubility, easy to filter (large particles), pure, and of known constant composition after drying or ignition.

### 2.7.2 Problems

- **Co-precipitation** – soluble impurities carried down with the precipitate (by adsorption or occlusion). Reduced by digestion, washing, reprecipitation.
- **Post-precipitation** – an impurity precipitates on the surface after the main precipitate has formed (e.g. magnesium oxalate on calcium oxalate on standing). Reduced by filtering soon.
- **Peptisation** – a coagulated precipitate turns colloidal again during washing with pure water; prevented by washing with an electrolyte solution.

### 2.7.3 Pharmaceutical examples

- Sulphate as barium sulphate.
- Chloride as silver chloride.
- Calcium as calcium oxalate, ignited to calcium oxide.
- Loss on drying and residue on ignition (sulphated ash) tests also use weighing.

| | Volumetric | Gravimetric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measures | Volume of titrant | Mass of product |
| Speed | Fast | Slow |
| Accuracy | Good | Very high |
| Use | Most IP assays | Reference methods, ash tests |

## Remember

- N1V1 = N2V2; equivalent weight = MW ÷ n.
- Primary standards: KHP, Na2CO3, oxalic acid, K2Cr2O7, NaCl.
- Weak acid–strong base → phenolphthalein; strong acid–weak base → methyl orange.
- Non-aqueous: perchloric acid in glacial acetic acid + crystal violet for weak bases; sodium methoxide + thymol blue for weak acids; mercuric acetate for halide salts.
- Mohr = chromate, neutral; Volhard = ferric alum, acid, back titration; modified Volhard adds nitrobenzene; Fajans = adsorption indicators.
- EDTA binds metal 1 : 1; buffer pH 10; Eriochrome black T wine red → blue.
- KMnO4 is self-indicating in H2SO4; iodimetry direct, iodometry indirect.
- Gravimetry: precipitate, digest, filter, wash, ignite, weigh; gravimetric factor.
