# Unit 1: Introduction, errors and impurities

This unit has three parts: what pharmaceutical chemistry is, how errors creep into an analysis, and how we check that a medicine is pure enough (impurities and limit tests). Limit tests are one of the most repeated long questions in this paper.

## 1.1 Pharmaceutical chemistry: scope and objectives

**Pharmaceutical chemistry** is the branch of pharmacy that deals with the chemical nature of drugs: where they come from, how they are made, how their structure decides their action, and how their quality is tested.

**Scope (what it covers)**

| Area | What a pharmacist learns |
| --- | --- |
| Inorganic pharmaceutical chemistry | Salts and minerals used as drugs: iron salts, antacids, antiseptics, dental agents, medicinal gases |
| Organic / medicinal chemistry | Classification, structure, chemical name and uses of synthetic drugs (CNS, CVS, anti-infectives, anticancer …) |
| Pharmaceutical analysis | Identification, purity tests (limit tests) and assays as given in the Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP) |
| Stability and storage | Why a drug decomposes (oxidation, hydrolysis, light) and how to store it |
| Formulations and brands | Dosage forms in which a chemical reaches the patient |

**Objectives of the course**

1. Know the chemical class, chemical name and structure of commonly used drugs.
2. Know their uses, doses, stability problems and storage conditions.
3. Know the dosage forms and well-known brand names.
4. Carry out impurity tests and basic quality-control tests as per the pharmacopoeia.

*Why it matters to a diploma pharmacist:* when you dispense, store or counsel, you are using chemistry. Storing adrenaline away from light, or telling a patient not to take ciprofloxacin with an antacid, are chemistry decisions.

## 1.2 Errors in analysis

Every measurement differs a little from the true value. The difference is called **error**.

**Absolute error** = observed value − true value
**Relative error** = absolute error ÷ true value (often × 100 to give % error)

### 1.2.1 Types of errors

```text
                         ERRORS
          ┌────────────────┼──────────────────┐
   Determinate          Indeterminate          Gross
   (systematic)          (random)            (blunders)
   • can be traced       • cannot be traced  • spilling, wrong
   • same direction      • scatter both ways   reading, wrong
   • can be corrected    • reduced by          calculation
                           repeating
```

**A. Determinate (systematic) errors** have a definite cause, act in one direction and can be found and corrected.

| Type | Cause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instrumental | Faulty or uncalibrated apparatus | Burette marked wrongly, balance out of calibration |
| Method (methodic) | Fault in the chemistry of the method | Indicator changing colour before the true end point; slight solubility of a precipitate |
| Personal (operative) | Habit or limitation of the analyst | Colour blindness, always reading the burette from above (parallax) |
| Reagent | Impure chemicals or water | Distilled water containing chloride in a chloride limit test |

**B. Indeterminate (random) errors** come from small, uncontrollable changes (temperature, vibration, judging the last drop). They scatter results on both sides of the mean. They cannot be removed but are reduced by repeating the experiment and taking the mean.

**C. Gross errors** are mistakes (wrong sample, spillage, wrong arithmetic). The result is rejected and the experiment repeated.

### 1.2.2 Ways to reduce errors

1. **Calibration** of burettes, pipettes, flasks and balances.
2. **Blank determination** – run the test without the sample; subtract the blank reading. Corrects reagent errors.
3. **Control determination** – analyse a standard substance of known purity under the same conditions.
4. **Independent method** – check the result by a different method.
5. **Parallel (replicate) determinations** – do two or three titrations and take concordant readings.
6. **Standard addition** – add a known amount of analyte and see whether it is fully recovered.
7. **Internal standard** – used in instrumental methods to correct for variation.

## 1.3 Accuracy, precision and significant figures

**Accuracy** is how close a result is to the true value. It is expressed as error.

**Precision** is how close repeated results are to each other. It is expressed as deviation (or standard deviation).

```text
   Accurate and precise     Precise, not accurate    Neither
        . . .                      ...                .     .
        (bull's eye)           (tight group,            .
                                off target)          .      .
```

| | Accuracy | Precision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Nearness to true value | Nearness of results to each other |
| Expressed as | Error | Deviation, standard deviation |
| Affected by | Systematic errors | Random errors |
| Can exist alone? | Accuracy needs precision | Results can be precise but inaccurate |

**Significant figures** are all certain digits of a measurement plus the first uncertain digit.

Rules:

1. All non-zero digits are significant: 25.6 has 3.
2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant: 2.05 has 3.
3. Leading zeros are not significant: 0.0052 has 2.
4. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant: 25.00 ml has 4.
5. In addition/subtraction, keep the same number of decimal places as the least precise value: 10.2 + 3.456 = 13.7.
6. In multiplication/division, keep the same number of significant figures as the value with the fewest: 2.5 × 3.14 = 7.9.
7. Rounding: if the dropped digit is above 5, round up; below 5, leave; exactly 5, round to the even digit (common convention).

A burette reading such as 20.35 ml has 4 significant figures; the last digit (5) is the estimated one.

## 1.4 Impurities in pharmaceuticals

An **impurity** is any foreign substance present in a drug that is not the drug itself or an intended excipient. A completely pure chemical is impossible, so the pharmacopoeia fixes how much of each impurity is allowed.

### 1.4.1 Sources of impurities

| Source | How it enters | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raw materials | Impure starting chemicals | Rock salt used for NaCl brings calcium and magnesium salts |
| Method of manufacture | Reagents, catalysts, solvents, side products | Arsenic from sulphuric acid; lead from equipment; unreacted intermediates |
| Reaction vessels and apparatus | Metal vessels, glass | Iron, copper, lead dissolving from vessels; alkali from soft glass |
| Atmosphere / environment | Dust, gases, moisture | Moisture making salts lump; CO2 converting NaOH to Na2CO3 |
| Defective manufacturing / storage | Poor control of temperature, light, containers | Ferrous sulphate oxidising to ferric salt; aspirin hydrolysing to salicylic acid |
| Decomposition (instability) | Chemical breakdown with time | Adrenaline turning pink-brown on oxidation |
| Packaging and closures | Leaching from plastic, rubber, metal | Plasticisers from PVC |
| Physical changes | Crystal form or particle size changes | Change in polymorph affecting dissolution |
| Accidental contamination / adulteration | Mix-ups, poor hygiene, deliberate dilution | Microbial contamination |

### 1.4.2 Effects of impurities

1. **Toxicity** – lead, arsenic and other heavy metals are poisonous even in traces (cumulative).
2. **Lower activity** – the dose contains less active drug.
3. **Instability** – traces of metal ions catalyse oxidation; moisture speeds hydrolysis.
4. **Changes in physical properties** – colour, odour, taste, solubility.
5. **Incompatibility** – impurities can react with other ingredients.
6. **Wrong analysis** – they can interfere with assays.

### 1.4.3 Importance of limit tests

A **limit test** is a quantitative or semi-quantitative test that checks that a small, allowed amount of a known impurity is **not exceeded**. It does not measure the exact amount.

Principle of all limit tests: the impurity is made to give a measurable effect (turbidity, opalescence, colour or stain). This effect is compared with that produced by a **standard** containing the permitted amount, under identical conditions. If the sample effect is **less** than the standard, the sample **passes**.

Why limit tests are important:

- They keep toxic impurities (lead, arsenic) below safe levels.
- They are simple, quick and need little apparatus.
- They control the quality of raw materials and finished products.
- They are an official (pharmacopoeial) requirement.

**Nessler cylinders** are used for comparison: flat-bottomed glass cylinders of the same size and glass, marked at 50 ml, viewed against a black or white background.

## 1.5 Limit test for chlorides

**Principle:** Chloride ions react with silver nitrate in the presence of dilute nitric acid to give silver chloride. In small amounts AgCl is not a precipitate but a faint **opalescence** (turbidity). This is compared with the opalescence from a known quantity of chloride.

Cl− + AgNO3 → AgCl (white opalescence) + NO3−

**Role of dilute nitric acid:** makes the solution acidic so that other ions (carbonate, phosphate, hydroxide) do not precipitate with silver; gives a uniform, fine opalescence.

**Procedure (outline)**

| Test (sample) cylinder | Standard cylinder |
| --- | --- |
| Specified quantity of substance dissolved in water | Specified volume of standard chloride solution (dilute NaCl, e.g. 0.05845% w/v) |
| 10 ml dilute nitric acid | 10 ml dilute nitric acid |
| Dilute to 50 ml with water | Dilute to 50 ml with water |
| 1 ml of 0.1 M silver nitrate, stir | 1 ml of 0.1 M silver nitrate, stir |
| Stand 5 minutes away from direct light | Same |

**Observation:** compare the opalescence by viewing down the cylinders against a black background.
**Result:** if the sample opalescence is not more than the standard, the sample passes.

Light is avoided because silver chloride darkens in light.

## 1.6 Limit test for sulphates

**Principle:** Sulphate ions react with barium chloride in the presence of dilute hydrochloric acid to give **barium sulphate turbidity**, compared with a standard.

SO4²⁻ + BaCl2 → BaSO4 (white turbidity) + 2Cl−

**Barium sulphate reagent** contains barium chloride, sulphate-free alcohol and a little potassium sulphate. The **alcohol** prevents supersaturation and gives a uniform turbidity; the **potassium sulphate** gives tiny seed crystals so turbidity forms quickly and reproducibly.

**Role of dilute HCl:** prevents precipitation of other barium salts such as carbonate and phosphate.

**Procedure (outline)**

| Test | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Substance in water in a Nessler cylinder | Standard potassium sulphate solution (e.g. 0.1089% w/v), specified volume |
| 2 ml dilute HCl | 2 ml dilute HCl |
| Dilute to 45 ml | Dilute to 45 ml |
| 5 ml barium sulphate reagent, stir | 5 ml barium sulphate reagent, stir |
| Stand 5 minutes | Stand 5 minutes |

The sample passes if its turbidity is not greater than the standard.

## 1.7 Limit test for iron

**Principle:** Iron (in any oxidation state) is reduced to the ferrous state by **thioglycollic acid**. In a solution made alkaline with ammonia (and containing citric acid), ferrous iron forms **ferrous thioglycollate**, which is **pale pink to deep reddish-purple**. The colour is compared with a standard made from a known amount of iron.

Fe³⁺ → (thioglycollic acid) → Fe²⁺ → ferrous thioglycollate (purple)

**Role of reagents**

| Reagent | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Citric acid (20% w/v, iron-free) | Forms a soluble complex with iron and prevents iron precipitating as hydroxide when ammonia is added |
| Thioglycollic acid | Reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ and forms the coloured complex |
| Iron-free ammonia | Makes the solution alkaline; the colour appears only in alkaline medium |

**Procedure (outline)**

| Test | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Substance dissolved in 40 ml water | 2 ml standard iron solution (from ferric ammonium sulphate, 20 µg Fe per ml) diluted with 40 ml water |
| 2 ml of 20% w/v iron-free citric acid | Same |
| 0.1 ml thioglycollic acid | Same |
| Make alkaline with iron-free ammonia, dilute to 50 ml | Same |
| Stand 5 minutes | Same |

The sample passes if the purple colour is not deeper than the standard.

## 1.8 Limit test for heavy metals

**Heavy metals** are metals such as lead, copper, mercury, bismuth, antimony, tin, cadmium and silver that give a coloured sulphide with sulphide ions. The limit is usually expressed as **parts per million (ppm) of lead**.

**Principle:** In a weakly acidic medium (pH about 3–4, set with acetate buffer or dilute acetic acid), heavy metals react with **hydrogen sulphide** (or sodium sulphide / thioacetamide reagent) to form a **brown to black colloidal sulphide**. The colour is compared with that from a standard lead solution.

Pb²⁺ + H2S → PbS (brown–black) + 2H⁺

**Why pH 3–4?** At this pH the metal sulphides form a uniform colour, while iron and other metals that would interfere do not precipitate as sulphide.

**Procedure (outline, IP method A for substances that give a clear colourless solution)**

| Test | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Solution of sample in water in a Nessler cylinder | Specified volume of standard lead solution (from lead nitrate) |
| Adjust to pH 3–4 with dilute acetic acid or ammonia; add acetate buffer | Same |
| Dilute to 35 ml | Same |
| Add 10 ml freshly prepared hydrogen sulphide solution (or the sulphide reagent in newer editions) | Same |
| Dilute to 50 ml, stand 5 minutes, view down against white background | Same |

The IP gives other methods (B, C) for substances that are coloured, organic or insoluble; these first destroy the organic matter by ignition with sulphuric acid.

## 1.9 Limit test for arsenic (Gutzeit test)

Arsenic is highly toxic and cumulative, so its limit is very low (a few ppm).

**Principle:** Arsenic in the sample is converted to **arsine gas (AsH3)** by **nascent hydrogen** produced from **zinc and hydrochloric acid**. Arsine passes up the tube and reacts with **mercuric chloride paper** to give a **yellow to brown stain**. The stain is compared with a **standard stain** produced from a known quantity of arsenic.

```text
Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + 2[H]          (nascent hydrogen)
As(V)  —(KI, SnCl2)→  As(III)      (reduction)
H3AsO3 + 6[H] → AsH3↑ + 3H2O       (arsine)
AsH3 + HgCl2 paper → yellow/brown stain (mixed arsenic–mercury compounds)
```

**Role of reagents**

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Arsenic-free zinc + HCl | Produce nascent hydrogen |
| Potassium iodide | Reduces pentavalent arsenic to trivalent |
| Stannous chloride (acid) | Reduces As(V) to As(III), keeps reduction going, helps even evolution of gas |
| Lead acetate cotton wool | Traps hydrogen sulphide (from sulphide impurities in zinc), which would also stain the paper |
| Mercuric chloride paper | Detects arsine as a coloured stain |

**Apparatus:** a wide-mouthed bottle (about 120 ml) fitted with a rubber bung carrying a glass tube. The lower part of the tube holds lead acetate cotton; the top has a flat end where mercuric chloride paper is clamped between two rubber bungs.

```text
        ┌──┐   <- mercuric chloride paper clamped here
        │  │
        │░░│   <- glass tube with lead acetate cotton
        │  │
     ┌──┴──┴──┐
     │  bung  │
   ┌─┴────────┴─┐
   │  sample +  │  <- bottle: sample, KI, SnCl2, Zn, HCl
   │  Zn + HCl  │
   └────────────┘
```

**Procedure (outline):** place test solution (with KI, SnCl2 and HCl) in the bottle, add arsenic-free zinc, quickly fit the tube, keep at about 40 °C for 40 minutes. Do the standard in the same way with a known volume of dilute standard arsenic solution. Compare the stains: the sample passes if its stain is not deeper than the standard.

**Precautions:** use arsenic-free reagents; keep temperature steady (gas evolution must be even); paper must be protected from light and moisture; do test and standard at the same time.

## 1.10 Summary table of limit tests

| Impurity | Reagent | Medium | Effect compared | Standard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chloride | AgNO3 | Dilute HNO3 | Opalescence (AgCl) | Sodium chloride |
| Sulphate | BaCl2 (barium sulphate reagent) | Dilute HCl | Turbidity (BaSO4) | Potassium sulphate |
| Iron | Thioglycollic acid + citric acid | Ammoniacal | Purple colour | Ferric ammonium sulphate |
| Heavy metals | H2S / sulphide reagent | Acetate buffer pH 3–4 | Brown colour | Lead nitrate |
| Arsenic | Zn + HCl (nascent H) | Acid | Yellow-brown stain on HgCl2 paper | Arsenic trioxide solution |

## Remember

- Systematic errors are traceable and one-directional; random errors scatter and are reduced by replicates.
- Accuracy = closeness to truth; precision = closeness of repeats.
- Limit test = compare with a standard; sample passes if its effect is **less** than the standard.
- Chloride: AgNO3 + HNO3 → opalescence. Sulphate: BaCl2 + HCl → turbidity (alcohol and K2SO4 in reagent).
- Iron: thioglycollic acid reduces Fe³⁺, citric acid stops precipitation, purple colour in ammonia.
- Heavy metals: sulphide at pH 3–4, brown colour, expressed as ppm lead.
- Arsenic: Gutzeit; Zn + HCl → arsine → stain on mercuric chloride paper; lead acetate cotton traps H2S.
