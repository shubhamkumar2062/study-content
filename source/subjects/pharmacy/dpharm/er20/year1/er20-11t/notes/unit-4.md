# Unit 4: Tablets and capsules

This unit covers the first part of syllabus chapter 5: tablets (coated and uncoated, modified-release tablets), and hard and soft gelatin capsules. Manufacturing steps, defects and quality tests are included because exam papers ask about them often.

## Part A: Tablets

## 4.1 Definition, advantages and disadvantages

**Tablets** are solid unit dosage forms containing one or more drugs, with or without excipients, made by **compressing** (or moulding) a powder or granules into a definite shape (usually round, flat or biconvex).

**Advantages**

1. Accurate, uniform dose in each unit.
2. Most stable dosage form (dry); long shelf life.
3. Easy to carry, count, pack and take; cheapest to make in bulk.
4. Taste and smell can be masked by coating.
5. Release can be controlled (enteric, sustained release).
6. Can be printed or embossed for identification.

**Disadvantages**

1. Difficult to swallow for children, elderly and unconscious patients.
2. Some drugs do not compress well (amorphous, low density).
3. Bitter, irritant or moisture-sensitive drugs need coating or special care.
4. Slower onset than liquids (tablet must disintegrate and dissolve first).
5. Bioavailability can be poor if the drug dissolves badly.

## 4.2 Types of tablets

### By route and purpose

| Type | Key feature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compressed (plain, uncoated) | Simple compression | Paracetamol tablets |
| Multiple compressed – layered | Two or three layers, compressed one over another; separates incompatible drugs | Bilayer tablets |
| Multiple compressed – compression coated (tablet in tablet) | Core tablet surrounded by an outer compressed coat | Incompatible drugs |
| Sugar-coated | Thick sugar coat; masks taste and smell | Vitamin B-complex |
| Film-coated | Thin polymer film | Most modern tablets |
| Enteric-coated | Coat resists stomach acid, dissolves in the intestine | Aspirin, diclofenac, pantoprazole |
| Chewable | Chewed before swallowing; mannitol base | Antacids, albendazole for children |
| Effervescent | Dissolved in water before use; release CO2 | Effervescent vitamin C, soluble aspirin |
| Dispersible | Disperse in water before taking | Dispersible amoxicillin |
| Soluble | Dissolve completely in water | Soluble aspirin |
| Buccal | Placed between gum and cheek | Some nitrate tablets |
| Sublingual | Placed under the tongue; rapid absorption, avoids first-pass metabolism | Glyceryl trinitrate, isosorbide dinitrate |
| Lozenges / troches | Dissolve slowly in the mouth; local action | Throat lozenges |
| Vaginal tablets | Inserted into the vagina | Clotrimazole vaginal tablets |
| Implants | Placed under the skin for slow release | Hormone implants |

### Modified-release tablets (named in the syllabus)

**Modified release** means the time course or place of release is changed compared with a plain tablet.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Delayed release** | Drug is released not immediately but later, e.g. in the intestine | Enteric-coated tablets |
| **Sustained release (SR)** | Releases drug slowly over a long time, giving a prolonged effect | Diclofenac SR |
| **Extended release (ER/XR)** | Release extended so dosing can be less frequent (once or twice daily) | Metformin ER |
| **Controlled release (CR)** | Releases drug at a planned, steady rate (often near-constant) | Osmotic tablets |
| **Fast dissolving / orally disintegrating (ODT)** | Disintegrate on the tongue within seconds, without water | Ondansetron ODT |
| **Multi-layered tablets** | Two or three layers, e.g. one immediate-release and one sustained-release layer, or two incompatible drugs | Bilayer metformin combinations |

**Advantages of SR/ER tablets:** fewer doses per day, better patient compliance, steady blood levels with fewer peaks and troughs, fewer side effects. **Disadvantages:** costly; cannot be crushed or chewed (dose dumping); dose cannot be adjusted easily; if toxicity occurs, the drug cannot be removed quickly.

## 4.3 Tablet excipients

| Excipient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diluent (filler) | Gives bulk when dose is small | Lactose, microcrystalline cellulose, starch, dicalcium phosphate, mannitol |
| Binder | Holds particles together in granules | Starch paste, gelatin, acacia, PVP (povidone), HPMC, sucrose |
| Disintegrant | Helps tablet break up in fluid | Starch, microcrystalline cellulose; superdisintegrants: croscarmellose sodium, crospovidone, sodium starch glycolate |
| Lubricant | Reduces friction between tablet and die wall | Magnesium stearate, stearic acid, talc |
| Glidant | Improves flow of granules into the die | Colloidal silicon dioxide, talc |
| Antiadherent | Stops sticking to punch faces | Talc, magnesium stearate, corn starch |
| Colours, flavours, sweeteners | Appearance, taste | Iron oxides, lakes; mint; saccharin |
| Wetting agent / adsorbent | Wetting of poorly soluble drug; absorbing oily ingredients | Sodium lauryl sulphate; magnesium oxide, colloidal silica |

## 4.4 Granulation and tablet manufacturing

**Granulation** is the process of making small particles stick together to form larger, free-flowing aggregates called granules.

**Why granulate:** better flow into the die (uniform weight), better compressibility, prevents segregation of ingredients (uniform content), reduces dust.

### Methods

```text
WET GRANULATION
 Weigh → mill and sieve drug + excipients → dry mix
 → add binder solution (wet massing) → screen wet mass (sieve 8–12)
 → dry granules (tray dryer / FBD) → dry screening (sieve 16–20)
 → add lubricant, glidant, (extra-granular disintegrant) → blend → COMPRESS

DRY GRANULATION (slugging / roller compaction)
 Weigh → mix drug + excipients (+ part lubricant)
 → compress into large slugs (or sheets with a roller compactor)
 → break slugs and screen → add rest of lubricant → COMPRESS
 (For drugs sensitive to moisture or heat, e.g. aspirin)

DIRECT COMPRESSION
 Weigh → sieve → blend drug with directly compressible excipients
 (MCC, spray-dried lactose, dicalcium phosphate) + lubricant → COMPRESS
 (Fewest steps; needs good-flowing, compressible materials)
```

| Method | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wet granulation | Good flow and compression for most materials; uniform distribution of low-dose drugs | Many steps, time and space; heat and moisture can harm the drug |
| Dry granulation | No heat or water; suits moisture-sensitive drugs | Dust, slugs may give hard tablets; less uniform |
| Direct compression | Fastest, cheapest, no heat or water | Only for suitable materials; costly special excipients; segregation risk |

## 4.5 General tableting equipment

### Single punch (eccentric) press

One die and one pair of punches. The lower punch is fixed during compression and the upper punch moves down to compress. Output is low (laboratory and small batches).

**Main parts:** hopper (holds granules), feed shoe (fills the die), die (cavity that gives the size and shape), upper and lower punches, cams and adjusting screws (control fill weight and ejection, and pressure).

**Compression cycle**

```text
 1. FILL        feed shoe over die; lower punch down → granules fill die
 2. SCRAPE      feed shoe moves away, levelling excess granules (weight set)
 3. COMPRESS    upper punch comes down and presses granules into a tablet
 4. EJECT       upper punch rises; lower punch rises and pushes tablet out
 5. PUSH OFF    feed shoe sweeps tablet away and refills die
```

### Rotary (multi-station) press

A rotating **die table (turret)** carries many dies and pairs of punches (for example 16, 27 or more stations). Granules flow from the hopper into a feed frame; punches move on fixed **cam tracks**; compression happens between two **pressure rollers** that press upper and lower punches together. Output is thousands of tablets per minute in large machines. Both punches move during compression, giving more even pressure.

### Other equipment

| Step | Equipment |
| --- | --- |
| Milling | Hammer mill, multi-mill, oscillating granulator |
| Dry mixing / blending | Double cone blender, V-blender, octagonal blender |
| Wet granulation | Planetary mixer, sigma blade mixer, rapid mixer granulator |
| Drying | Tray dryer, fluidized bed dryer |
| Coating | Conventional coating pan, perforated (side-vented) pan, fluidized bed coater |
| Packing | Strip and blister packing machines |

## 4.6 Tablet coating

**Reasons for coating:** mask taste and smell; protect from light, moisture and air; protect drug from stomach acid or protect stomach from drug (enteric); control release; improve appearance and identification; make swallowing easier.

### Sugar coating – steps

1. **Sealing (waterproofing):** thin coat of shellac or cellulose acetate phthalate to stop water reaching the core.
2. **Subcoating:** sugar syrup with gelatin/acacia and a powder (talc, calcium carbonate) to round the edges and build bulk.
3. **Smoothing (grossing/syruping):** several coats of plain syrup to make a smooth surface.
4. **Colouring:** syrup coats containing colour.
5. **Polishing:** with waxes (beeswax, carnauba wax) in a canvas-lined pan to give gloss.
6. **Printing:** product name or code with edible ink.

Sugar coating is slow, needs skill and increases tablet weight greatly.

### Film coating

A thin (about 20–100 µm) polymer layer is sprayed on, usually in a perforated pan.

| Component | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Film former | Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), methylcellulose, ethylcellulose, povidone |
| Plasticiser | Polyethylene glycol, propylene glycol, glycerin, castor oil |
| Colourant / opacifier | Iron oxides, lakes, titanium dioxide |
| Solvent | Water (aqueous coating) or alcohol/organic solvents |

**Enteric coating polymers:** cellulose acetate phthalate (CAP), hydroxypropyl methylcellulose phthalate (HPMCP), polyvinyl acetate phthalate, methacrylic acid copolymers (e.g. Eudragit L and S), shellac. These are insoluble at the acid pH of the stomach and dissolve at the higher pH of the intestine.

**Film vs sugar coating:** film coating is faster, adds little weight (2–3%), keeps the embossed logo visible and is easy to automate; sugar coating looks elegant but takes days and adds much weight.

## 4.7 Tablet defects (processing problems)

| Defect | What it looks like | Causes | Remedies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Capping** | Top (or bottom) of the tablet separates as a cap | Air trapped in granules; too many fines; too dry granules; worn or wrongly set punches; too fast | Use more binder; correct moisture; remove fines; reduce speed; precompression; check tooling |
| **Lamination** | Tablet splits into two or more layers | Same as capping; too much lubricant; rapid release of pressure | Reduce lubricant; slower speed; precompression |
| **Picking** | Small amount of material is pulled off the tablet surface by the punch face, especially at embossed letters | Too much moisture; low-melting ingredients; poor lettering design | Dry granules properly; add antiadherent (talc, colloidal silica); polish or chrome-plate punches; larger letters |
| **Sticking** | Granules stick to the die wall or punch face | Excess moisture; too little lubricant; rough punch faces | Dry granules; increase lubricant; polish punches |
| **Chipping** | Edges break off | Too dry or hard granules; damaged punches | Correct moisture; repair tooling |
| **Cracking** | Small cracks on top centre | Rapid expansion; deep concave punches | Use flatter punches; adjust granulation |
| **Mottling** | Uneven colour on the surface | Colour migrates during drying; drug of different colour from excipients | Use lakes or insoluble colours; change solvent; reduce particle size |
| **Double impression** | Two images of the embossing | Lower punch moves during ejection on machines without control | Use anti-turning device on punches |
| **Weight variation** | Tablets differ in weight | Poor flow, uneven granule size, feeder problems | Add glidant; uniform granules; maintain machine |

## 4.8 Evaluation of tablets

### Official (pharmacopoeial) tests

**1. Uniformity of weight (weight variation).** Weigh 20 tablets individually and find the average. Not more than 2 tablets may deviate from the average by more than the limit, and none by more than double the limit.

| Average weight (IP) | Limit |
| --- | --- |
| 80 mg or less | ±10% |
| More than 80 mg and less than 250 mg | ±7.5% |
| 250 mg or more | ±5% |

**2. Content uniformity.** For low-dose tablets (generally 10 mg or less, or drug forming a small part of the tablet), the drug content of individual tablets is measured (usually 10 tablets) to make sure each has the right amount.

**3. Disintegration test.** Apparatus: a basket-rack with **6 glass tubes** with a 10-mesh screen at the bottom, moved up and down 28–32 times per minute in a liquid kept at **37 ± 2 °C**. One tablet per tube (discs may be added).

| Tablet type | Time limit (usual) |
| --- | --- |
| Uncoated | 15 minutes |
| Film-coated | 30 minutes |
| Sugar-coated | 60 minutes |
| Dispersible / soluble | 3 minutes |
| Enteric-coated | No disintegration in 0.1 M HCl for 2 hours; then disintegrate within 60 minutes in phosphate buffer pH 6.8 |

**4. Dissolution test.** Measures how much drug dissolves in a set time. Medium usually 900 ml at **37 ± 0.5 °C**. Apparatus 1 = **basket** (often 100 rpm); Apparatus 2 = **paddle** (often 50 rpm). Samples are withdrawn at set times and analysed. The usual limit is that not less than a set percentage (often 70–80%, as per monograph) dissolves in a stated time.

**5. Assay** – amount of drug in a powdered sample of 20 tablets is within the monograph limits.

### Non-official tests

**Hardness (crushing strength):** force needed to break a tablet across its diameter; measured with Monsanto, Pfizer or Strong-Cobb testers. Usual range about **4–6 kg** for ordinary tablets (chewable and lozenges are harder or softer as needed).

**Friability:** measures resistance to chipping and abrasion. Weigh tablets (a sample of about 6.5 g for tablets of unit weight up to 650 mg; 10 tablets if heavier), place in a **Roche friabilator** rotated at **25 rpm for 4 minutes (100 revolutions)**, dedust and weigh again.

```text
 % friability = (initial weight − final weight) ÷ initial weight × 100
 Limit: weight loss not more than 1%
```

**Thickness and diameter:** measured with a vernier caliper or micrometer; should be uniform for packing.

## Part B: Capsules

## 4.9 Definition, advantages and disadvantages

**Capsules** are solid dosage forms in which the drug (with or without excipients) is enclosed in a hard or soft soluble shell, usually made of **gelatin**.

**Advantages:** tasteless and odourless shell masks taste; easy to swallow; release drug quickly (shell dissolves fast); fewer excipients than tablets; liquids and oils can be given in soft capsules; attractive and easy to identify by colour.

**Disadvantages:** not suitable for very soluble salts (irritate stomach) or efflorescent and deliquescent drugs (they soften or harden the shell); shells are affected by humidity; gelatin is of animal origin (not acceptable to some patients); costlier than tablets in some cases.

### Gelatin

Gelatin is obtained by partial hydrolysis of collagen from animal skin and bones.

| Type | Made by | Isoelectric point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type A | Acid treatment (mainly pork skin) | About pH 7–9 |
| Type B | Alkaline (lime) treatment (bones and hides) | About pH 4.7–5 |

Vegetarian shells are made of HPMC (hypromellose).

## 4.10 Hard gelatin capsules

### Shell composition and manufacture

Shell = gelatin + water + colourant + opacifier (titanium dioxide) + sometimes preservative. Hard shells contain about **13–16% moisture**.

**Manufacture of empty shells (dipping method):**

```text
 1. DIPPING      Rows of stainless-steel pins (moulds) dipped in warm gelatin solution
 2. SPINNING     Pins rotated so the gelatin film spreads evenly
 3. DRYING       Film dried with controlled cool air
 4. STRIPPING    Dried caps and bodies stripped off the pins
 5. TRIMMING     Cut to exact length
 6. JOINING      Cap and body fitted together, sorted, printed, packed
```

Modern shells have a **locking (snap-fit) ring** so the cap and body do not separate.

### Capsule sizes

| Size | Approximate volume (ml) |
| --- | --- |
| 000 | 1.37 |
| 00 | 0.95 |
| 0 | 0.68 |
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.37 |
| 3 | 0.30 |
| 4 | 0.21 |
| 5 | 0.13 |

Size 000 is the largest and size 5 the smallest. For humans, sizes 0 to 4 are the most common.

### Formulation of the fill

Drug + diluent (lactose, starch, microcrystalline cellulose) + glidant (colloidal silica, talc) + lubricant (magnesium stearate) + sometimes disintegrant or wetting agent. Fill can be powder, granules, pellets, tablets, or semisolids.

**Calcium-containing diluents** (such as dicalcium phosphate) must not be used with tetracycline because calcium binds the drug and reduces its absorption.

### Filling of hard capsules

1. **Hand filling** – small scale; the powder is pressed into the bodies on a clean surface (punch method).
2. **Capsule filling board** (manual/semi-automatic) – a board with holes (for example 100 or 300) that holds the bodies; powder is spread over and levelled into them, then caps are replaced.
3. **Automatic machines** – separate caps and bodies, fill by dosator or tamping (dosing disc) systems, rejoin and eject.

After filling, capsules are cleaned (dedusted) and polished.

## 4.11 Soft gelatin capsules

**Soft gelatin capsules (softgels)** are one-piece, hermetically sealed, soft and elastic shells, usually containing liquids or suspensions. Shapes: round, oval, oblong, tube.

**Shell composition:** gelatin + **plasticiser** (glycerin, sorbitol or propylene glycol, which makes the shell elastic) + water + colour, opacifier, preservative.

**Fill materials:** oils and oily solutions (vitamins A, D, E; cod liver oil), suspensions in oil, PEG-based solutions. Water and water-miscible solvents in large amounts, low-pH and high-pH liquids are avoided because they attack the shell.

**Manufacturing methods**

| Method | Short description |
| --- | --- |
| Plate process | Gelatin sheet placed over a mould plate with pockets, filled, second sheet laid on top and pressed to cut and seal |
| **Rotary die process** (most common) | Two gelatin ribbons pass between two rotating dies; fill is injected between them through a wedge; the dies seal and cut the capsules at the same time |
| Reciprocating die process | Similar to rotary but with vertical dies |
| Accogel process | Rotary process that can fill dry powders into soft capsules |

After forming, softgels are washed and dried to the right hardness.

**Advantages:** liquids given as solid dosage; accurate dose of liquid; better bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs in oily solution; protect oxidation-prone drugs; hermetically sealed.

## 4.12 Evaluation of capsules

| Test | Main points |
| --- | --- |
| **Uniformity of weight** | 20 capsules weighed one by one (whole, then empty shell for hard capsules). IP limits: less than 300 mg ±10%; 300 mg or more ±7.5% |
| **Disintegration** | Same apparatus as tablets; hard and soft capsules usually within **30 minutes** |
| **Dissolution** | As per monograph; sinkers may be used so capsules do not float |
| **Content uniformity** | For low-dose capsules |
| **Assay** | Drug content within limits |
| **Moisture content / permeation** | Shells should not become brittle or sticky |
| **Leak test (soft capsules)** | Capsules should not leak their contents |
| Visual inspection | No defects, uniform colour, closed properly |

## Remember

- Wet granulation steps: weigh → mix → wet mass → wet screen → dry → dry screen → lubricate → compress.
- Dry granulation (slugging) is for moisture- or heat-sensitive drugs; direct compression has the fewest steps.
- Lubricant = magnesium stearate; glidant = colloidal silica; superdisintegrant = croscarmellose, crospovidone, sodium starch glycolate.
- Capping and lamination = air entrapment, fines, dry granules; picking and sticking = moisture and poor lubrication.
- Weight variation (IP): ≤80 mg ±10%, 80–250 mg ±7.5%, ≥250 mg ±5%.
- Disintegration: uncoated 15 min, film 30 min, sugar 60 min, dispersible 3 min; 37 ± 2 °C.
- Friability: 100 revolutions at 25 rpm, loss not more than 1%.
- Capsule sizes 000 (largest) to 5; Type A gelatin = acid, Type B = alkali.
- Softgels need a plasticiser (glycerin, sorbitol); made mainly by the rotary die process.
