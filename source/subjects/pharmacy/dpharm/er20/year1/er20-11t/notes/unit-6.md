# Unit 6: Powders, granules, sterile formulations and immunological products

This unit covers the last part of syllabus chapter 5: powders and granules (insufflations, dusting powders, effervescent powders and granules), sterile formulations (injectables, eye drops and eye ointments), and immunological products (sera, vaccines, toxoids) with their manufacture.

## Part A: Powders and granules

## 6.1 Powders as a dosage form

**Powders** are mixtures of finely divided dry drugs and excipients, for internal or external use.

**Advantages:** stable (dry), flexible dosing, fast dissolution and absorption (large surface), large doses that are hard to compress can be given, easy to swallow when dispersed in water.

**Disadvantages:** unpleasant taste is hard to mask; not suitable for hygroscopic, deliquescent or volatile drugs unless specially packed; inaccurate if the patient measures doses from bulk; time-consuming to divide.

### Classification

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Bulk powders for internal use | Antacid powders, ORS (oral rehydration salts) sachets |
| Divided powders (single doses in paper or sachets) | Powder papers dispensed for children |
| Dusting powders (external) | Zinc, starch and talc dusting powder |
| Insufflations | Powders blown into body cavities |
| Effervescent powders and granules | Effervescent granules of sodium citrate |
| Dentifrices / tooth powders | Tooth powder |

**Mixing of powders (small scale):** trituration in a mortar, spatulation on a slab, sifting through a sieve, tumbling in a closed container. For a small amount of potent drug, use **geometric dilution**: mix the drug with an equal amount of diluent, then add diluent equal to the mixture each time.

**Problems:** eutectic mixtures (some powders such as camphor and menthol liquefy when mixed; mix with an inert absorbent such as light magnesium oxide or kaolin), hygroscopic and deliquescent powders (pack in airtight containers, double wrap), explosive mixtures (oxidising and reducing agents must be powdered separately and mixed lightly).

## 6.2 Insufflations

**Insufflations** are finely divided powders meant to be **blown into body cavities** such as the nose, throat, ear or tooth socket, using an **insufflator** (a powder blower).

**Features:** very fine powder for even spreading; local action (antiseptic, decongestant) or systemic action (some drugs by the nasal or pulmonary route). Disadvantage: dose is not accurate with a simple insufflator; the patient can inhale the powder and cough.

Modern **dry powder inhalers (DPI)** are a controlled form of insufflation for the lungs.

## 6.3 Dusting powders

**Dusting powders** are fine powders (free from grittiness) applied to the skin for local action – lubricant, protective, absorbent, antiseptic or antifungal.

**Ingredients:** talc, starch, zinc oxide, kaolin, zinc stearate, boric acid (limited use), plus drug (clotrimazole, neomycin, etc.).

**Requirements:**

- Very fine (should pass a fine sieve) so they are smooth and do not irritate.
- Free-flowing and spread easily; adhere to the skin.
- **Dusting powders meant for wounds or broken skin must be sterile**, because talc and starch can carry spores of microbes such as tetanus bacilli.
- Packed in sifter-top containers.

**Example:** Zinc, Starch and Talc Dusting Powder.

**Label:** "For external use only"; "Not to be applied to open wounds" (unless sterile).

## 6.4 Effervescent powders and granules

**Effervescent preparations** contain an acid and a carbonate/bicarbonate. When added to water, they react to release **carbon dioxide**, giving a pleasant fizzy drink that masks bitter or salty taste and may help absorption.

```text
Citric acid (monohydrate) + 3 NaHCO3 → sodium citrate + 3 CO2 + 4 H2O
Tartaric acid + 2 NaHCO3           → sodium tartrate + 2 CO2 + 2 H2O
```

**Why use both citric and tartaric acid:** citric acid alone gives a sticky mass that is hard to granulate; tartaric acid alone gives friable, crumbly granules. A mixture (usually citric : tartaric = 1 : 2) gives good granules.

**Effervescent granules – preparation**

1. **Fusion method (heat):** the powders (citric acid, tartaric acid, sodium bicarbonate, drug, sugar) are mixed and heated on a hot plate or in an oven at about 93–104 °C. The **water of crystallisation of citric acid** is released and acts as the binder, making a damp mass. The mass is passed through a sieve to make granules and dried at not more than 50 °C.
2. **Wet method:** alcohol (or a little water with alcohol) is used as the granulating liquid (it moistens without starting the reaction strongly); then sieved and dried.

**Advantages of granules over powders:** less hygroscopic, dissolve more slowly so the reaction is controlled (less foaming), better flow, look attractive.

**Packing and storage:** airtight containers, kept dry (moisture starts the reaction early). Label: "Dissolve in water before taking", "Keep the container tightly closed".

## Part B: Sterile formulations

## 6.5 Parenteral preparations (injectables)

**Definition:** parenteral preparations are **sterile** preparations intended for administration by **injection, infusion or implantation** into the body, bypassing the alimentary canal ("para" = beside, "enteron" = intestine).

### Routes

| Route | Site | Usual volume | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intravenous (IV) | Vein | Small to very large (infusions) | Immediate action; solutions only (fat emulsions are the special case) |
| Intramuscular (IM) | Muscle (deltoid, gluteal, thigh) | Up to a few ml | Solutions, suspensions, oily depots |
| Subcutaneous (SC) | Under the skin | Small (about 1–2 ml) | Insulin, heparin, vaccines |
| Intradermal (ID) | Within the skin | About 0.1 ml | BCG vaccine, skin tests |
| Intrathecal | Spinal fluid | Small | Must be free of preservatives |
| Intra-articular, intracardiac, others | Joint, heart | Small | Special uses |

### Types of parenteral products

1. Solutions (aqueous or oily) – e.g. Sodium Chloride Injection.
2. Suspensions – e.g. depot hormone injections (not for IV).
3. Emulsions – e.g. IV fat emulsions.
4. Dry powders to be dissolved or suspended before use – e.g. antibiotic powders for injection.
5. Large-volume parenterals (infusions, usually 100 ml or more) and small-volume parenterals (ampoules, vials).

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages:** quick action (emergencies); useful for unconscious or vomiting patients; drugs destroyed in the gut or poorly absorbed orally (insulin, heparin) can be given; local action (dental anaesthesia); sustained depot effect; nutrition and fluid replacement.

**Disadvantages:** pain and fear; needs trained person; once given cannot be removed; risk of infection, thrombosis and pyrogenic reactions; costly to make (sterile areas).

### Formulation of injections

| Component | Examples / purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Vehicle – aqueous | **Water for Injections** (WFI): pyrogen-free, sterile when packed; made by distillation (or equivalent process) |
| Vehicle – non-aqueous | Fixed oils (sesame, arachis, ethyl oleate) for oily injections; ethanol, propylene glycol, PEG as cosolvents |
| Tonicity adjusters | Sodium chloride, dextrose (to make isotonic) |
| Buffers | Phosphate, citrate, acetate (keep pH stable, near 7.4 where possible) |
| Antimicrobial preservatives (multi-dose) | Benzyl alcohol, chlorocresol, phenol, parabens |
| Antioxidants | Sodium metabisulphite, ascorbic acid; nitrogen in the head space |
| Chelating agents | Disodium edetate |
| Suspending, wetting, emulsifying agents | For suspensions and emulsions |

**Isotonicity:** solutions with the same osmotic pressure as blood/tears are isotonic. **0.9% w/v sodium chloride** and **5% w/v dextrose** are isotonic with blood. Blood and tears freeze at about **−0.52 °C**, so an isotonic solution also freezes at −0.52 °C. Hypotonic solutions make red cells swell and burst (haemolysis); hypertonic solutions make them shrink (crenation).

### Requirements (special properties) of parenteral products

1. **Sterile** (free from living microorganisms).
2. **Pyrogen-free** (free from fever-producing substances, mainly bacterial endotoxins).
3. **Free from visible particles** (clear solutions).
4. **Isotonic** (as far as possible) and suitable pH.
5. Stable, non-toxic, compatible with the container.
6. Accurate dose; correct fill volume (a slight excess is filled so the labelled volume can be withdrawn).

### Production of injections (steps)

```text
 Clean room (Grade A/B for aseptic work, Grade C/D for less critical steps)
 1. Cleaning and sterilisation of containers and closures
    (glass: wash, rinse with WFI, dry-heat sterilise / depyrogenate)
 2. Preparation of solution (weighing, dissolving in WFI, pH adjustment)
 3. Filtration (clarifying filter, then 0.22 µm membrane for aseptic process)
 4. Filling into ampoules / vials (under laminar air flow)
 5. Sealing (ampoules fused; vials stoppered and crimped with aluminium seals)
 6. Sterilisation – terminal (autoclave) if the drug can stand heat
 7. Quality control tests → labelling → packing
```

### Methods of sterilisation (summary)

| Method | Conditions | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moist heat (autoclave) | 121 °C, 15 minutes (at about 15 psi) | Aqueous injections, rubber closures, dressings |
| Dry heat (hot air oven) | 160 °C for 2 hours (or 170 °C 1 hour, 180 °C 30 minutes) | Glassware, oils, powders; higher temperatures (about 250 °C) also destroy pyrogens |
| Filtration | 0.22 µm membrane filter | Heat-sensitive solutions (followed by aseptic filling) |
| Gaseous | Ethylene oxide | Plastics, heat-sensitive devices |
| Radiation | Gamma rays (cobalt-60), electron beam | Disposable syringes, sutures, some drugs |

### Evaluation (quality control) of injections

| Test | Principle in short |
| --- | --- |
| **Sterility test** | Sample is incubated in culture media (fluid thioglycollate medium for bacteria, soybean–casein digest medium for fungi and aerobes) by direct inoculation or membrane filtration; there must be no growth |
| **Pyrogen test (rabbit test)** | Injected into rabbits; body temperature rise is measured and must stay within limits |
| **Bacterial endotoxin test (LAL test)** | Limulus amoebocyte lysate (from horseshoe crab blood) forms a gel with endotoxin; faster, more sensitive and widely used |
| **Clarity / particulate matter** | Visual inspection against black and white backgrounds; particle counters |
| **Leakage test (ampoules)** | Ampoules dipped in coloured dye (e.g. methylene blue) under vacuum; leaking ampoules take up dye |
| Extractable volume, pH, assay | Within limits |

## 6.6 Eye drops

**Eye drops** are **sterile** aqueous or oily solutions or suspensions meant for instillation into the eye (conjunctival sac).

**Requirements / formulation points**

1. **Sterile** – the eye is easily infected (especially by *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*); multi-dose bottles must contain a preservative.
2. **Isotonic** with tears (0.9% NaCl equivalent); the eye tolerates a small range around this.
3. **pH** close to tears (about 7.4); buffered where possible (comfort vs stability of drug).
4. **Clear** (free from particles) for solutions; suspensions must have very fine particles.
5. **Viscosity** agents (methylcellulose, HPMC, PVA) increase contact time.
6. **Preservatives:** benzalkonium chloride (about 0.01%), chlorhexidine, phenylmercuric nitrate/acetate, chlorobutanol, thiomersal.
7. **Containers:** sterile, with dropper; plastic squeeze bottles or glass with a dropper.

**Sterilisation:** autoclaving in the final container (if the drug is heat stable) or filtration through 0.22 µm and aseptic filling.

**Label:** "Sterile until opened"; "Discard 4 weeks after opening" (multi-dose, home use); "For external use only"; "Do not touch the dropper tip".

**Examples:** chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin, timolol, pilocarpine eye drops.

## 6.7 Eye ointments

**Eye ointments** are **sterile** semisolid preparations for application to the eye (along the lower eyelid margin).

**Base:** commonly the eye ointment basis of **yellow soft paraffin, liquid paraffin and wool fat** (wool fat helps absorb tears and spread). It must be smooth and non-irritant.

**Requirements:** sterile; drug particles very fine (no grittiness, to avoid injury); free from foreign particles; packed in small sterile collapsible tubes with a fine nozzle.

**Preparation:** base melted, filtered and sterilised by dry heat; drug (sterile, micronised) incorporated aseptically; filled into sterile tubes.

**Advantages over drops:** longer contact time; less drainage through the tear duct; useful at night. **Disadvantages:** blurred vision; greasy.

**Examples:** chloramphenicol, acyclovir eye ointments.

## Part C: Immunological products

## 6.8 Immunity – basic terms

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antigen | Substance that makes the body produce antibodies | Bacteria, viruses, toxins |
| Antibody | Protein (immunoglobulin) made against an antigen | IgG |
| **Active immunity** | Body makes its own antibodies (after infection or vaccination); slow to develop, long-lasting | Vaccines, toxoids |
| **Passive immunity** | Ready-made antibodies given; immediate, short-lived | Sera, antitoxins, immunoglobulins |

**Immunological products** are biological preparations used for prevention (prophylaxis), treatment or diagnosis of disease through the immune system.

## 6.9 Vaccines

**Vaccines** are preparations of antigens (killed or live-weakened organisms, their parts or products) that produce **active immunity** when given.

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live attenuated | Living organisms weakened so they cannot cause disease | BCG, oral polio (OPV), measles, MMR, rotavirus |
| Killed (inactivated) | Organisms killed by heat or chemicals (formaldehyde, phenol) | Injectable polio (IPV), rabies vaccine, whole-cell pertussis, typhoid |
| Toxoids | Inactivated toxins | Tetanus toxoid, diphtheria toxoid |
| Subunit / recombinant | Purified parts of the organism, made by genetic engineering | Hepatitis B vaccine, HPV vaccine |
| Conjugate | Polysaccharide linked to a protein carrier | Hib vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate |
| Mixed (combined) | Several antigens together | DPT, pentavalent vaccine |

### Manufacture of bacterial vaccines (general steps)

```text
 1. Seed lot: selected, well-characterised strain kept as stock
 2. Culture: grown in suitable liquid medium in large fermenters
 3. Harvest: bacteria separated (centrifugation) and suspended in saline
 4. Killing (for killed vaccines): by heat or formaldehyde / phenol
 5. Standardisation: count of bacteria per ml / antigen content
 6. Blending: diluted, preservative (e.g. phenol, thiomersal) and adjuvant added
 7. Filling into sterile containers (aseptic), sealing
 8. Quality control: sterility, safety (abnormal toxicity), potency, identity
```

### Manufacture of viral vaccines (general steps)

Viruses grow only in living cells, so they are grown in **embryonated hen's eggs** or **cell cultures** (for example chick embryo cells, monkey kidney cells, human diploid cells, Vero cells).

1. Seed virus is inoculated into eggs or cell cultures and incubated.
2. Virus is harvested (fluid collected), clarified and purified.
3. For inactivated vaccines the virus is killed with formaldehyde or β-propiolactone; for live vaccines it is used as an attenuated strain.
4. Stabilisers added; often freeze-dried; filled aseptically.
5. Tested for sterility, safety, potency, and absence of unwanted agents.

## 6.10 Toxoids

**Toxoids** are bacterial **exotoxins** that have been treated (usually with **formaldehyde** and warmth) so that they lose their toxicity but keep their ability to cause antibody formation (antigenicity).

**Manufacture (e.g. tetanus or diphtheria toxoid):**

1. The toxin-producing bacteria (*Clostridium tetani* or *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*) are grown in a liquid medium.
2. The toxin is released into the medium; the bacteria are removed by filtration.
3. Formaldehyde is added and the toxin is incubated until it becomes a non-toxic toxoid ("toxoiding").
4. The toxoid is purified and concentrated; strength is expressed in **Lf (limit of flocculation) units**.
5. **Adsorbed toxoids:** the toxoid is adsorbed on **aluminium hydroxide or aluminium phosphate** (adjuvant), which gives a stronger and longer-lasting response.
6. Preservative added; filled aseptically; tested for sterility, absence of toxicity (no reversion) and potency.

## 6.11 Sera (antisera) and antitoxins

**Sera (antisera)** are preparations containing **antibodies** obtained from the blood of immunised animals (usually **horses**) or humans. They give **passive immunity** at once, used for treatment or for immediate protection.

| Product | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Anti-tetanus serum (ATS) / tetanus antitoxin | Prevention or treatment of tetanus |
| Anti-diphtheria serum | Diphtheria |
| Anti-snake venom (ASV, polyvalent) | Snake bite (common Indian snakes) |
| Anti-rabies serum | Post-exposure, with vaccine |
| Human immunoglobulins (e.g. tetanus immunoglobulin, rabies immunoglobulin) | Fewer allergic reactions than horse sera |

**Manufacture of antisera (e.g. antitoxin from horses):**

1. Healthy horses are injected with increasing doses of toxoid, then toxin (or venom), over weeks, to raise high antibody levels.
2. The horse is bled under aseptic conditions; blood is allowed to clot, or plasma is separated.
3. Antibodies are purified: proteins precipitated with ammonium sulphate and **digested with pepsin** to remove the part of the antibody that causes most reactions (refined, concentrated antitoxin).
4. Standardised in units, preservative added, sterile-filtered and filled aseptically; may be freeze-dried.
5. Tested for sterility, safety, potency and pyrogens.

**Caution:** horse sera can cause allergic reactions (anaphylaxis, serum sickness); a sensitivity (test) dose is given first as per instructions.

## 6.12 Storage of immunological products – cold chain

- Most vaccines and sera are stored at **2–8 °C** (refrigerator); some live vaccines (e.g. OPV) are stored frozen at central stores.
- **Do not freeze** adsorbed vaccines and toxoids (DPT, TT, hepatitis B): freezing destroys them.
- **Cold chain** = system of keeping vaccines at the right temperature from manufacture to the patient (walk-in coolers, ice-lined refrigerators, deep freezers, cold boxes, vaccine carriers with ice packs).
- **Vaccine vial monitor (VVM):** a heat-sensitive label that changes colour if the vial has been exposed to too much heat.
- Protect from light (e.g. BCG, measles); use reconstituted vaccines within the stated time.

## Remember

- Insufflation = powder **blown** into body cavities with an insufflator.
- Dusting powders for wounds must be **sterile** (talc can carry tetanus spores).
- Effervescent granules: citric : tartaric = 1 : 2; fusion method uses the water of crystallisation of citric acid as binder.
- Injections must be **sterile, pyrogen-free, particle-free, isotonic**.
- 0.9% NaCl and 5% dextrose are isotonic; freezing point of blood and tears −0.52 °C.
- Autoclave 121 °C/15 min; hot air oven 160 °C/2 h; membrane 0.22 µm.
- Sterility test media: fluid thioglycollate and soybean–casein digest; LAL test detects endotoxin.
- Eye drops: sterile, isotonic, buffered, preserved (benzalkonium); discard 4 weeks after opening.
- Vaccines (active immunity) vs sera (passive immunity); toxoid = toxin + formaldehyde, adsorbed on alum.
- Store vaccines at 2–8 °C; never freeze adsorbed vaccines; VVM shows heat exposure.
