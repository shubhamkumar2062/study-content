# Unit 2: Packaging materials and pharmaceutical aids

This unit covers syllabus chapters 2 and 3: the materials used to pack medicines (glass, plastic, metal, rubber), and the additives that make a medicine look, taste and keep well (colours, flavours, sweeteners and preservatives).

## Part A: Packaging materials

## 2.1 Packaging: meaning and functions

**Packaging** is the science and art of enclosing a product in a container so that it reaches the patient safe, stable, identifiable and easy to use, at an acceptable cost.

**Functions of a pharmaceutical package**

1. **Containment** – holds the product without leaking.
2. **Protection** – against light, moisture, air (oxygen), microbes, dust and breakage.
3. **Presentation and identification** – carries the label, batch number, expiry date and warnings.
4. **Convenience** – easy to open, measure and use (dropper, spray, unit dose).
5. **Tamper evidence and child resistance** where needed.

### Levels of packaging

| Level | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | In direct contact with the product | Bottle, ampoule, vial, blister, strip, tube |
| Secondary | Holds one or more primary packs | Carton, box with leaflet |
| Tertiary | For bulk transport | Corrugated shipper, pallet |

**Container** = the part that holds the product. **Closure** = the part that seals it (cap, stopper, crown).

### Types of containers (pharmacopoeial terms)

| Type | Protects against | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Well-closed | Solids and dust getting in; loss of contents | Paper or cardboard packs for some solids |
| Tightly closed | Air, moisture, solids, liquids, evaporation | Screw-cap bottle |
| Hermetically sealed | Air and all gases; impervious | Ampoule |
| Light-resistant | Light (amber or opaque) | Amber bottle for light-sensitive drugs |
| Single-dose | One dose only | Ampoule, single-dose vial |
| Multi-dose | Several doses; contents can be withdrawn repeatedly | Multi-dose vial with rubber closure |
| Aerosol / pressurised | Holds product under pressure | Metered-dose inhaler |

## 2.2 Selection criteria for a packaging material

The material must:

1. Be **compatible** with the product: no reaction, no leaching of substances into the product, no sorption (taking up) of drug or preservative.
2. **Protect** the product against light, moisture, oxygen and microbes as needed.
3. Be **strong** enough to survive filling, transport and use.
4. Be **non-toxic** and odourless, and not change the taste of the product.
5. Withstand **sterilisation** if the product is sterile.
6. Suit the **dosage form** and **route** (dropper for eye drops, collapsible tube for ointments).
7. Be **economical**, easily available and acceptable for printing and labelling.
8. Meet **legal and pharmacopoeial standards**; be disposable or recyclable where possible.

## 2.3 Glass

Glass is made mainly of **silica (SiO2)** with sodium oxide, calcium oxide, and other oxides. Boric oxide makes **borosilicate** glass.

**Advantages**

- Transparent: contents can be inspected.
- Chemically inert (good grades), impermeable to gases and moisture.
- Rigid, can be sterilised by heat, easy to clean and reuse.
- Can be coloured (amber) to protect from light.

**Disadvantages**

- Brittle; breaks easily, glass pieces are dangerous.
- Heavy, so transport costs more.
- Soda-lime glass can release **alkali** into solutions, raising the pH; can shed tiny flakes.

### Types of glass (USP/IP classification)

| Type | Composition | Resistance | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Type I | Borosilicate | Highest; very low alkali release | Parenterals, all injections, blood, laboratory glassware |
| Type II | Soda-lime glass, surface treated (de-alkalised, e.g. with sulphur dioxide) | High surface resistance | Buffered aqueous injections with pH below 7 |
| Type III | Ordinary soda-lime glass | Moderate | Dry powders for injection, non-aqueous injections |
| Type NP | General-purpose soda-lime | Low | Non-parenteral products: tablets, oral liquids, external preparations |

**Coloured glass:** amber glass (iron oxide with manganese or with sulphur and carbon) cuts off ultraviolet and blue light and protects light-sensitive drugs.

**Tests for glass containers:** powdered glass test (Types I and III), water attack test (Type II, surface treated), test for light transmission (coloured glass), and arsenic test for injection containers.

## 2.4 Plastics

Plastics are synthetic polymers mixed with additives (plasticisers, stabilisers, fillers, colours, antioxidants).

| Polymer | Main features | Pharmaceutical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polyethylene (LDPE, HDPE) | Good moisture barrier, poor gas barrier; HDPE more rigid | Bottles for tablets, dropper bottles, squeeze bottles |
| Polypropylene (PP) | Can be autoclaved, good chemical resistance | Closures, bottles, syringes |
| Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) | Clear, rigid, easily formed | Blister packs, IV fluid bags |
| Polystyrene | Clear, rigid, brittle | Jars for ointments and creams, tablet tubes |
| Polyethylene terephthalate (PET) | Clear, strong, good gas barrier | Bottles for oral liquids |
| Polycarbonate | Very strong, clear | Reusable containers, devices |

**Advantages:** light, unbreakable, flexible, cheap, easily moulded into any shape, suitable for squeeze bottles and unit-dose packs.

**Disadvantages (drug–plastic interactions):**

| Problem | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permeation | Gas, moisture or volatile ingredients pass through the wall | Loss of volatile oils; moisture enters |
| Leaching | Additives move from the plastic into the product | Plasticiser from PVC into IV fluid |
| Sorption | Drug or preservative is taken up by the plastic | Loss of preservatives from eye drops |
| Chemical reaction | Ingredient reacts with the polymer | Discolouration |
| Modification | Product changes the plastic | Oils soften polyethylene; container deforms |

Plastics also cannot all withstand heat sterilisation, and many are not transparent.

## 2.5 Metals

Metals used: **tin**, **aluminium**, **lead** (now rarely used; toxic) and **tin-plated steel**.

**Uses:** collapsible tubes for ointments, creams and pastes; aerosol cans; aluminium foil for strip and blister packs; screw caps; seals on vials.

**Advantages**

- Impermeable to light, moisture and gases.
- Strong, unbreakable, light (aluminium).
- Collapsible tubes do not let air back in, so the remaining product stays protected.

**Disadvantages**

- Costly (tin especially).
- May react with the product (corrosion); so the inside is coated with lacquer or wax.
- Opaque: contents cannot be seen.
- Dents easily; lead is toxic.

## 2.6 Rubber

Rubber is used for **closures**: vial stoppers, plugs, dropper bulbs, plungers of syringes, and gaskets.

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Natural rubber | Elastic, reseals well after needle puncture, but leaches more and ages |
| Butyl rubber | Low permeability to gases and moisture; widely used for vials |
| Neoprene | Resistant to oils |
| Nitrile rubber | Resistant to oils and solvents |
| Silicone rubber | Heat resistant, inert, but permeable to gases and costly |

**Advantages:** elastic, makes a tight seal, re-seals after a needle is withdrawn (important for multi-dose vials).

**Disadvantages:**

- **Sorption** of preservatives (for example chlorocresol, phenylmercuric nitrate) from the solution, so the product loses protection.
- **Leaching** of rubber additives (accelerators, fillers, zinc) into the product.
- Permeable to gases and water vapour; ages and hardens.
- Can shed particles (coring) when a needle pierces it.

**Treatment of rubber closures:** washed with detergent, boiled, and then soaked in a solution of the preservative to be used in the product, so that the closure is already "saturated" and will not remove preservative from the product later. Coated closures are also used.

## Part B: Pharmaceutical aids

**Pharmaceutical aids (excipients)** are substances other than the active drug added to a formulation to help manufacture, improve stability, appearance or taste, or help patient acceptance. They have no intended therapeutic action.

## 2.7 Organoleptic agents

Organoleptic agents act on the senses (sight, taste and smell). They improve the look and taste of a medicine, which matters especially for children.

### Colouring agents

**Uses:** make the product attractive, identify different strengths or products, mask an unpleasant appearance, match the flavour (red for cherry, yellow for lemon).

| Class | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Natural (plant) | Chlorophyll (green), carotenoids (yellow–orange), saffron, turmeric |
| Natural (animal) | Cochineal / carmine (red, from an insect) |
| Mineral (inorganic) | Red, yellow and black iron oxides; titanium dioxide (white, opaque) |
| Synthetic (coal-tar) dyes | Tartrazine (yellow), sunset yellow, amaranth (red), erythrosine (red), brilliant blue, indigo carmine |
| Lakes | Dyes adsorbed on aluminium hydroxide; insoluble; used in tablet coatings |
| Others | Caramel (brown, burnt sugar) |

**Ideal colour:** non-toxic, stable to light, heat and pH, compatible with other ingredients, permitted by law, effective in small amount. Only permitted colours may be used; tartrazine can cause allergy in some people.

### Flavouring agents

Flavour is a mix of taste and smell. There are four basic tastes: **sweet, salty, sour and bitter**. A good flavour matches and masks the natural taste of the drug.

| Taste to mask | Suitable flavours |
| --- | --- |
| Salty | Butterscotch, apricot, peach, raspberry, liquorice |
| Bitter | Chocolate, anise, mint, wild cherry, liquorice, coffee |
| Sour (acidic) | Citrus fruits (lemon, orange, lime), raspberry, cherry |
| Sweet | Vanilla, fruit and berry flavours |

**Other taste-masking methods:** adding a sweetener, chilling the drug, using an insoluble form of the drug (salt or ester), coating tablets, effervescence, and mild anaesthesia of taste buds with menthol or peppermint.

**Types of flavours:** natural (fruit juices, volatile oils such as peppermint, lemon and orange oil, vanilla), synthetic (methyl salicylate, vanillin, ethyl acetate for fruity notes) and artificial blends.

### Sweetening agents

| Sweetener | Approximate sweetness vs sucrose | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sucrose | 1 | Also gives body (viscosity); not for diabetics; can cause dental caries |
| Glucose (liquid glucose) | Less than sucrose | Used in syrups and lozenges |
| Sorbitol, mannitol | About half | Sugar alcohols; mannitol in chewable tablets (cooling taste); may cause loose stools in large amounts |
| Glycerin | About half | Also solvent and humectant |
| Saccharin (sodium) | About 300–500 times | Bitter after-taste at high levels; no calories |
| Aspartame | About 180–200 times | Not for patients with phenylketonuria; breaks down on heating |
| Sucralose | About 600 times | Heat stable |
| Acesulfame potassium | About 200 times | Heat stable |
| Stevia (steviol glycosides) | About 200–300 times | From the stevia plant |

## 2.8 Preservatives

**Definition:** a preservative is a substance added to a product to **prevent or slow the growth of microorganisms** (bacteria, fungi) during storage and use. In a wider sense, antioxidants that prevent chemical spoilage are also called preservatives.

**Why needed:** liquids containing water and sugar support microbial growth; multi-dose containers are opened many times; contamination can cause infection and spoil the product.

**Ideal preservative**

1. Effective against a wide range of microbes at low concentration.
2. Non-toxic, non-irritant and non-sensitising.
3. Stable to heat and over the shelf life; effective over the pH of the product.
4. Compatible with other ingredients and the container (not lost to rubber or plastic).
5. Free from colour, odour and taste.

### Types of antimicrobial preservatives with examples

| Class | Examples (usual concentration) | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acids and their salts | Benzoic acid and sodium benzoate (about 0.1–0.2%); sorbic acid, potassium sorbate | Oral liquids; work best at acidic pH (below about 4.5) |
| Esters of p-hydroxybenzoic acid (parabens) | Methylparaben and propylparaben, often together (for injections, methyl 0.18% + propyl 0.02%) | Oral liquids, creams, injections |
| Alcohols | Ethanol (about 15–20% preserves a liquid), benzyl alcohol, chlorobutanol (0.5%) | Elixirs, injections, eye drops |
| Phenols | Phenol (0.5%), cresol (0.3%), chlorocresol (0.1%) | Multi-dose injections, creams |
| Quaternary ammonium compounds | Benzalkonium chloride (about 0.01%), cetrimide | Eye drops, nasal drops |
| Organic mercury compounds | Phenylmercuric nitrate (0.002%), thiomersal | Eye drops, vaccines |

**Using two preservatives** (for example methyl- and propylparaben) widens the range of microbes covered.

### Antioxidants (chemical preservatives)

Antioxidants prevent oxidation (rancidity of oils, discolouration of solutions).

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Water-soluble | Ascorbic acid, sodium metabisulphite, sodium bisulphite, sodium sulphite |
| Oil-soluble | Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA), butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT), tocopherols (vitamin E), propyl gallate |
| Synergists / chelating agents | Disodium edetate (EDTA), citric acid (bind metal ions that speed oxidation) |

Other ways to reduce oxidation: replace air with nitrogen, use amber containers, keep cool.

## Remember

- Type I = borosilicate (all injections); Type II = treated soda-lime; Type III = soda-lime; NP = non-parenteral.
- Amber glass protects light-sensitive drugs.
- Plastic problems: **permeation, leaching, sorption, chemical reaction, modification**.
- Rubber closures take up preservatives, so they are pre-soaked in the preservative solution.
- Collapsible metal tubes are lined with lacquer; they do not suck air back.
- Flavours: citrus for sour, chocolate/mint for bitter, butterscotch for salty.
- Saccharin about 300–500× and aspartame about 200× sweeter than sucrose; aspartame is unsafe in phenylketonuria.
- Parabens (methyl + propyl), benzoates (acid pH), benzalkonium (eye drops), chlorocresol (injections).
- Antioxidants: BHA, BHT, tocopherol (oils); ascorbic acid, metabisulphite (water); EDTA as synergist.
