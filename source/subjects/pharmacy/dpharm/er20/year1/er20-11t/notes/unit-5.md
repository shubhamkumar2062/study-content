# Unit 5: Liquid oral, topical, nasal and ear preparations

This unit covers the second part of syllabus chapter 5: liquid oral preparations (solution, syrup, elixir, emulsion, suspension, dry powder for reconstitution), topical preparations (ointments, creams, pastes, gels, liniments, lotions, suppositories and pessaries), and nasal and ear preparations.

## Part A: Liquid oral preparations

## 5.1 General advantages and disadvantages of oral liquids

**Advantages:** easy to swallow (children, elderly); faster absorption than solids (drug already dissolved or finely divided); dose can be adjusted easily; irritant drugs are diluted; taste can be improved with flavour and sweetener.

**Disadvantages:** bulky to carry; less stable (hydrolysis, oxidation, microbial growth); need a measuring device so dosing is less accurate; unpleasant taste is harder to hide; glass bottles can break.

## 5.2 Solutions

**Definition:** a solution is a clear, homogeneous liquid preparation containing one or more substances dissolved in a suitable solvent (usually water).

**Types:** oral solutions, mouthwashes and gargles, topical solutions, solutions for injection (sterile). Examples: Aqueous Iodine Solution (Lugol's solution), Paediatric Paracetamol Oral Solution.

**Methods of preparation:** simple solution (dissolve and make up to volume), solution with chemical reaction, and extraction.

**Factors that increase solubility / rate of solution:** heat (for most solids), particle size reduction, stirring, cosolvents (alcohol, glycerin, propylene glycol), pH adjustment, complexation (for example potassium iodide dissolves iodine by forming soluble KI3).

## 5.3 Syrups

**Definition:** syrups are concentrated, viscous, sweet aqueous solutions of sucrose or other sugars, with or without flavours and medicinal substances.

- **Simple Syrup** contains about **66.7% w/w sucrose** (IP/BP). The USP simple syrup is 85% w/v.
- A concentrated syrup is **self-preserving**: so little free water is present that microbes cannot grow (high osmotic pressure).
- **Medicated syrups** contain a drug (e.g. chlorpheniramine syrup); **flavoured syrups** are vehicles (e.g. orange syrup).

**Methods of preparation:** solution with heat (quick, but heat can cause inversion of sucrose into glucose and fructose, which may darken – caramelise), agitation without heat, percolation, and adding sucrose to a medicated liquid.

**Advantages:** pleasant taste, masks bitterness, viscosity soothes the throat (cough syrups), self-preserving when concentrated. **Disadvantages:** not for diabetics (sucrose), can cause dental caries, may crystallise on the cap ("cap locking"), and dilute syrups support mould growth.

**Problems:** inversion of sucrose (with heat and acid), crystallisation, fermentation in dilute syrups. Sorbitol or glycerin may be added to stop crystallisation.

## 5.4 Elixirs

**Definition:** elixirs are clear, sweetened, **hydroalcoholic** (water + alcohol) solutions for oral use, usually flavoured.

- Alcohol content varies (low-alcohol to high-alcohol elixirs); alcohol helps dissolve drugs that are poorly soluble in water and acts as a preservative.
- Sweetened with sucrose, syrup, sorbitol, glycerin or saccharin.
- Examples: Piperazine Citrate Elixir, Paediatric Paracetamol Elixir.

**Advantages:** clear, stable, easy to prepare, alcohol helps solubility and preservation. **Disadvantages:** alcohol is unsuitable for children and some adults; volatile flavours can be lost; less sweet and less able to mask taste than syrups.

## 5.5 Emulsions

**Definition:** an emulsion is a thermodynamically unstable system of two **immiscible liquids** (usually oil and water), in which one liquid is dispersed as small globules (**dispersed phase**) in the other (**continuous phase**), stabilised by an **emulsifying agent**.

### Types and tests to identify them

| Type | Dispersed / continuous | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oil-in-water (o/w) | Oil in water | Oral emulsions, IV fat emulsions, vanishing creams |
| Water-in-oil (w/o) | Water in oil | Cold creams, many ointment-like creams |
| Multiple (w/o/w, o/w/o) | Drops inside drops | Controlled release |

| Test | o/w result | w/o result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dilution test (add water) | Mixes easily (dilutes) | Does not mix |
| Dye test (water-soluble dye, e.g. amaranth) | Continuous phase coloured | Only droplets coloured |
| Conductivity | Conducts electricity | Does not conduct |
| Fluorescence (under UV) | Spotty fluorescence | Whole field fluoresces (oils fluoresce) |

### Emulsifying agents

| Class | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Natural (carbohydrate) | Acacia, tragacanth, agar, pectin |
| Natural (protein, sterol) | Gelatin, egg yolk, wool fat (wool alcohols) |
| Finely divided solids | Bentonite, magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide |
| Synthetic surfactants – anionic | Sodium lauryl sulphate, soaps (sodium/potassium oleate for o/w; calcium oleate for w/o) |
| Synthetic – cationic | Cetrimide, benzalkonium chloride |
| Synthetic – non-ionic | Spans (sorbitan esters, w/o) and Tweens (polysorbates, o/w) |

**HLB (hydrophile–lipophile balance)** scale: low HLB (about 3–6) emulsifiers give w/o emulsions; higher HLB (about 8–18) give o/w emulsions.

### Methods of preparation (small scale)

| Method | Steps | Ratio of oil : water : gum (primary emulsion) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dry gum (continental) method** | Triturate gum with oil in a dry mortar, add all the water for the primary emulsion at once, triturate rapidly until a clicking sound and thick white emulsion forms, then dilute | Fixed oil 4 : 2 : 1; volatile oil 2 : 2 : 1; mineral oil 3 : 2 : 1 |
| **Wet gum (English) method** | Triturate gum with water to a mucilage, add oil in small portions with trituration, then dilute | Same ratios |
| **Bottle method** | Shake gum and volatile oil in a bottle, add water and shake | For volatile oils of low viscosity |

### Stability problems (instability)

| Problem | Meaning | Reversible? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Creaming** | Globules rise (or sink) and collect as a concentrated layer | Yes, by shaking |
| **Cracking (breaking)** | Globules join (coalesce) and oil separates completely | No |
| **Phase inversion** | o/w changes to w/o or the reverse | Partly |
| Microbial growth | Mould, change of smell | No |

**Stokes' law** explains creaming and sedimentation:

```text
        2 r² (ρ1 − ρ2) g
 v  =  ──────────────────
              9 η

 v = rate of creaming or settling    r = radius of globule/particle
 ρ1 − ρ2 = density difference        η = viscosity of continuous phase
```

So creaming is reduced by **smaller globules** (homogenisation), **higher viscosity** (thickeners) and **smaller density difference**.

**Evaluation of emulsions:** type test, globule size (microscopy), viscosity, phase separation on storage and centrifugation, pH, microbial limits, assay.

## 5.6 Suspensions

**Definition:** a suspension is a coarse dispersion of **insoluble, finely divided solid particles** in a liquid vehicle (usually aqueous).

**Reasons:** drug is insoluble; to mask bitter taste (insoluble form); better stability than solution for some drugs; prolonged action (depot injections); external use (calamine lotion).

**Properties of a good suspension:** particles settle slowly; sediment is **easily redispersed** on gentle shaking; does not form a hard cake; pours easily; uniform dose after shaking.

### Flocculated vs deflocculated

| Feature | Flocculated | Deflocculated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Particles | Form loose clusters (flocs) | Remain separate |
| Settling | Fast | Slow |
| Sediment | Loose, high volume, easily redispersed | Compact, may form a hard **cake** |
| Supernatant | Clear quickly | Stays cloudy |

Controlled flocculation (using electrolytes, surfactants or polymers) is often used so the sediment never cakes.

**Formulation ingredients:** wetting agents (glycerin, polysorbates; help disperse hydrophobic powders), suspending/thickening agents (tragacanth, acacia, sodium CMC, methylcellulose, bentonite, xanthan gum, sodium alginate), flocculating agents, buffers, preservatives, colours, flavours.

**Evaluation of suspensions:** sedimentation volume (F = final height of sediment ÷ original height; nearer to 1 is better), ease of redispersion, particle size, viscosity, pH, assay, zeta potential.

**Label:** "Shake well before use."

## 5.7 Dry powder for reconstitution

Some drugs are unstable in water (for example many antibiotics such as amoxicillin, ampicillin, cefalexin). They are supplied as a **dry powder or granules** in a bottle; the pharmacist or patient adds a measured amount of water (up to the mark) to make a suspension or solution just before use.

**Advantages:** long shelf life of the dry product; reconstituted product is used within a short period (usually 7–14 days, often refrigerated). **Disadvantages:** must add the correct volume of water; must be stored properly after reconstitution and discarded after the stated time.

**Label points:** "Reconstitute with ___ ml of water", "Shake well before use", "Store in a refrigerator after reconstitution", "Discard ___ days after reconstitution".

## Part B: Topical (semisolid) preparations

## 5.9 Ointments

**Definition:** ointments are soft, greasy or non-greasy semisolid preparations meant for external application to the skin or mucous membranes.

**Uses:** emollient, protective, as a vehicle for drugs (antiseptic, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, keratolytic).

### Ointment bases

| Base | Examples | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oleaginous (hydrocarbon)** | Soft paraffin (white or yellow), hard paraffin, liquid paraffin, plant oils | Very occlusive, emollient, greasy, not washable, cheap |
| **Absorption bases** | Wool fat (lanolin), wool alcohols, hydrophilic petrolatum, beeswax | Can absorb water to form w/o emulsions; greasy |
| **Emulsion bases – w/o** | Cold cream, oily cream | Less greasy than oleaginous |
| **Emulsion bases – o/w (water-removable)** | Emulsifying ointment, aqueous cream, vanishing cream | Washable, non-greasy, cosmetically acceptable |
| **Water-soluble bases** | Polyethylene glycol (macrogol) bases | Non-greasy, washable, non-occlusive |

**Ideal ointment base:** inert, non-irritant, non-sensitising, stable, compatible with drug, easily spread and removed, releases drug well.

### Methods of preparation

1. **Trituration (incorporation):** fine insoluble powder is levigated with a little base or liquid paraffin on an ointment slab with a spatula, then mixed with the rest of the base (e.g. sulphur ointment).
2. **Fusion:** ingredients are melted together, starting with the highest-melting one, then stirred until cool (e.g. simple ointment).
3. **Chemical reaction / emulsification:** as in creams.

## 5.10 Creams

**Definition:** creams are viscous semisolid **emulsions** (o/w or w/o) for external use. They are softer and less greasy than ointments.

- **o/w creams (vanishing creams):** non-greasy, water-washable, "vanish" on rubbing; e.g. aqueous cream, cetrimide cream.
- **w/o creams:** greasy, cooling, emollient; e.g. cold cream, oily cream.

**Preparation:** oil phase ingredients melted together at about 70–75 °C; aqueous phase heated to the same temperature; one added to the other with stirring; stirred until cool; volatile ingredients and perfumes added near the end.

## 5.11 Pastes

**Definition:** pastes are stiff semisolid preparations containing a **high proportion of finely powdered solids** (often 50% or more) in a fatty or aqueous base.

**Features:** stiff and less greasy than ointments; stay where applied; absorb secretions (porous); protective. Example: Zinc oxide paste, Compound Zinc Paste.

## 5.12 Gels (jellies)

**Definition:** gels are semisolid systems in which a liquid is held in a three-dimensional network of a gelling agent; usually transparent.

**Gelling agents:** sodium alginate, tragacanth, carbomers (Carbopol), methylcellulose, sodium CMC, HPMC, gelatin, pectin, bentonite.

**Uses:** lubricant jellies (catheters), antiseptic and anti-inflammatory gels, dental gels, ultrasound gel. They are non-greasy, cooling, easily washed off.

## 5.13 Liniments and lotions

| Feature | **Liniments** | **Lotions** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Liquid or semi-liquid preparations for application to the skin **with friction (rubbing)** | Liquid preparations for application to the skin **without friction** (dabbed or spread) |
| Vehicle | Oily, alcoholic or soapy (often emulsions) | Mostly aqueous (solutions, suspensions, emulsions) |
| Use | Counter-irritant, rubefacient, analgesic for muscle and joint pain | Cooling, soothing, protective, antiseptic |
| Caution | Not on broken skin | Shake suspension lotions well |
| Examples | Turpentine Liniment, White Liniment, Methyl Salicylate Liniment | Calamine Lotion |

**Label:** "For external use only"; liniments: "Not to be applied to broken skin".

## 5.14 Suppositories and pessaries

**Suppositories** are solid dosage forms of various shapes and weights meant for insertion into the **rectum** (also vagina or urethra), where they melt, soften or dissolve and release the drug for local or systemic action. A rectal suppository for adults weighs about **2 g** (about 1 g for children); shape is usually torpedo or bullet-shaped.

**Pessaries** (vaginal suppositories) are for insertion into the **vagina**; usually oval or cone-shaped and heavier (often about 3–8 g). Vaginal tablets and capsules are also called pessaries.

**Uses:** local action (laxative glycerin suppositories, haemorrhoids, vaginal antifungal and antiseptic); systemic action when oral route is not possible (vomiting, unconscious patient, children) – e.g. paracetamol, diclofenac, antiemetic suppositories.

### Suppository bases

| Type | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fatty (oleaginous) | **Theobroma oil (cocoa butter)**, hard fat (synthetic glycerides such as Witepsol) | Melt at body temperature. Theobroma oil melts at about 30–35 °C, but shows **polymorphism**: if overheated it sets as unstable forms with a lower melting point, so melt it gently (not above about 36 °C) |
| Water-soluble / miscible | **Glycerinated gelatin** (glycerin + gelatin + water), **polyethylene glycols (PEG)** | Dissolve or soften in body fluids; glycerin–gelatin used for pessaries; PEG does not need refrigeration |
| Emulsifying | Bases with surfactants (polysorbate-containing) | Help release and spread |

**Displacement value:** the number of parts by weight of drug that displaces one part by weight of the base. It is used to calculate how much base is needed when a mould is filled with a medicated mass (moulds are calibrated with the base alone).

**Methods of preparation:** hand rolling (old), **fusion (pour moulding)** – most common: melt base, mix drug, pour into lubricated metal moulds, cool, trim and remove – and compression moulding.

**Evaluation:** weight uniformity, melting range / softening time, disintegration, hardness (breaking test), drug content, visual (no cracks, fat bloom).

## Part C: Nasal and ear preparations

## 5.15 Nasal preparations

**Nasal preparations** are liquid, semisolid or solid preparations for administration into the nasal cavities for local or systemic effect.

**Types:** nasal drops, nasal sprays (pump sprays), nasal powders (insufflations), nasal ointments/gels, nasal washes.

**Formulation points:**

- Usually aqueous; **isotonic** and pH near that of nasal secretions (about 5.5–6.5) to avoid irritation and damage to cilia.
- Preservative (benzalkonium chloride) in multi-dose containers; buffer; viscosity agent to increase contact time.
- Oily nasal drops are avoided (oil can reach the lungs and cause lipid pneumonia).

**Examples:** saline nasal drops, xylometazoline and oxymetazoline (decongestants), corticosteroid sprays (fluticasone); systemic: desmopressin and some vaccines.

## 5.16 Ear (otic) preparations

**Ear preparations** are liquid or semisolid preparations instilled or applied into the external ear canal.

**Types:** ear drops (solutions, suspensions), ear washes, ear ointments, ear powders.

**Vehicles:** water, glycerin, propylene glycol, alcohol, oils; glycerin and propylene glycol are viscous and keep the drug in contact longer, and they are hygroscopic.

**Uses:** to soften and remove ear wax (wax softeners like sodium bicarbonate ear drops, olive oil), antibacterial and antifungal drops, anti-inflammatory and analgesic drops.

**Points:** ear drops should be warmed to body temperature before use (cold drops can cause dizziness); if the eardrum is perforated, only sterile preparations prescribed for that use should be put in.

## Remember

- Simple syrup = **66.7% w/w** sucrose (IP); USP 85% w/v; concentrated syrup is self-preserving.
- Elixir = clear, sweet, **hydroalcoholic**.
- Primary emulsion ratio: fixed oil **4:2:1**, volatile oil 2:2:1, mineral oil 3:2:1 (oil : water : gum).
- Creaming is reversible; cracking is not. Stokes' law: smaller particles, higher viscosity → slower settling.
- Flocculated suspension: fast settling, loose sediment, easy to redisperse, no caking.
- Dry powders for reconstitution: for drugs unstable in water (antibiotics).
- Liniments **with** friction, lotions **without** friction.
- Theobroma oil melts at about 30–35 °C; overheating causes unstable polymorphs; displacement value adjusts the base.
- Nasal preparations: isotonic, pH about 5.5–6.5, no oily drops; ear drops warmed to body temperature.
