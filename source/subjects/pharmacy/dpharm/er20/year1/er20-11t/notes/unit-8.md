# Unit 8: Novel drug delivery systems

This unit covers syllabus chapter 7: introduction to novel drug delivery systems (NDDS), their classification with examples, advantages and challenges.

## 8.1 Introduction

**Drug delivery system (DDS):** the method, formulation or device by which a drug is brought to its site of action in the body.

**Conventional dosage forms** (plain tablets, capsules, syrups, injections) release the whole dose quickly. The blood level rises to a peak and then falls, so several doses a day are needed. Levels may go above the safe (toxic) level after a dose and below the effective level before the next.

**Novel drug delivery systems (NDDS)** are newer approaches that **control the rate of release, the duration of action, or the site of delivery** of a drug, so that it works better and more safely.

```text
 Blood level
   │        toxic level ─────────────────────────────────────
   │   ▲         ▲         ▲      conventional: peaks and troughs
   │  ╱ ╲       ╱ ╲       ╱ ╲
   │ ╱   ╲_____╱   ╲_____╱   ╲
   │ ────────────────────────────  controlled release: steady level
   │        effective level ─────────────────────────────────
   └────────────────────────────────────────────── Time
       dose 1    dose 2    dose 3
```

### Aims of an NDDS

1. Keep the drug level within the **therapeutic range** for a long time (sustained or controlled release).
2. Deliver the drug to the **target site** (organ, tissue or cell) and reduce exposure elsewhere (targeted delivery).
3. Reduce the number of doses and improve patient compliance.
4. Protect unstable drugs (peptides, proteins, genes) until they reach the site.

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Sustained release | Releases drug slowly over a long period; level may still decline gradually |
| Controlled release | Releases drug at a predetermined, often constant (zero-order) rate |
| Targeted delivery | Drug carried preferentially to a particular site |
| Zero-order release | Same amount of drug released per unit time, regardless of how much is left |
| First-order release | Amount released per unit time is proportional to the amount remaining |

## 8.2 Classification of NDDS with examples

### A. By route / site of administration

| Class | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oral controlled release systems** | Matrix tablets, reservoir (membrane-coated) systems, **osmotic pumps** (e.g. OROS – a semipermeable coat with a laser-drilled hole; water enters and pushes drug out at a constant rate), ion-exchange resins | Nifedipine osmotic tablets, metformin ER, diclofenac SR |
| **Gastroretentive systems** | Stay in the stomach for a long time: floating (low density) systems, swelling and mucoadhesive systems | Floating tablets of drugs absorbed in the upper gut |
| **Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDS / patches)** | Patch on the skin releases drug through the skin into the blood at a controlled rate | Nitroglycerin (angina), nicotine (smoking cessation), fentanyl (pain), scopolamine (motion sickness), estradiol (hormone therapy), rivastigmine |
| **Ocular delivery systems** | Inserts placed in the eye release drug slowly | Ocusert (pilocarpine insert, about one week); in-situ gels |
| **Intrauterine devices (IUD) with drug** | Release hormone in the uterus | Levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system; progesterone-releasing IUD |
| **Implants** | Placed under the skin; release drug for months or years | Levonorgestrel and etonogestrel contraceptive implants; goserelin implant |
| **Mucoadhesive (buccal, nasal) systems** | Stick to mucosa for a long time | Buccal tablets and films |
| **Pulmonary systems** | Drug to the lungs | Metered-dose inhalers, dry powder inhalers, nebulisers |
| **Parenteral depot systems** | Long-acting injections | Oily depot injections, microsphere depot injections (e.g. leuprolide) |

### B. By mechanism of control

| Mechanism | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diffusion-controlled – **reservoir** | Drug core surrounded by a rate-controlling membrane | Membrane-coated pellets, Ocusert, some patches |
| Diffusion-controlled – **matrix** | Drug spread through a polymer; diffuses out | HPMC matrix tablets |
| Dissolution-controlled | Drug coated or embedded in slowly dissolving polymer | Coated beads in capsules |
| **Osmotically controlled** | Osmotic pressure pushes drug out at a constant rate | OROS tablets |
| Ion-exchange | Drug bound to resin, released by exchange with body ions | Resinate suspensions |
| Chemically controlled / biodegradable | Polymer breaks down and releases drug | PLGA microspheres and implants |
| Stimulus-responsive | Release triggered by pH, temperature, enzymes | pH-sensitive colon delivery |

### C. Targeted (site-specific) drug delivery carriers

| Carrier | Description | Examples / uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Liposomes** | Tiny vesicles made of phospholipid bilayers (with cholesterol); can carry water-soluble drugs inside and lipid-soluble drugs in the bilayer | Liposomal amphotericin B (fungal infections, kala-azar), liposomal doxorubicin (cancer) |
| **Niosomes** | Vesicles made of non-ionic surfactants instead of phospholipids; cheaper, more stable | Cosmetic and topical delivery |
| **Nanoparticles** | Solid particles about 1–1000 nm (polymeric, lipid, metal) | Albumin-bound paclitaxel; cancer targeting |
| **Microspheres / microcapsules** | Small spheres (about 1–1000 µm) of polymer containing drug | Depot injections |
| **Monoclonal antibody conjugates** | Drug attached to an antibody that recognises target cells | Antibody–drug conjugates in cancer |
| **Resealed erythrocytes** | Red blood cells loaded with drug | Experimental targeting to liver and spleen |
| Prodrugs | Inactive form converted to the active drug at the site | Levodopa (to dopamine in the brain) |

Targeting can be **passive** (carriers collect naturally in some tissues, such as tumours with leaky blood vessels or the liver and spleen) or **active** (carrier carries a ligand or antibody that binds receptors on target cells).

## 8.3 Advantages of NDDS

1. **Less frequent dosing** (once daily, weekly, or monthly) – better patient compliance.
2. **Steady blood levels** – avoids peaks (side effects, toxicity) and troughs (loss of effect).
3. **Reduced side effects** and lower total dose because the drug goes where it is needed.
4. **Improved bioavailability** for some drugs; avoidance of first-pass metabolism (transdermal, buccal, implants).
5. **Protection** of sensitive drugs (proteins, peptides) from degradation.
6. **Better treatment of chronic diseases** (hypertension, diabetes, angina, pain) and night-time control of symptoms.
7. **Targeting** of potent drugs (anticancer) to diseased cells, sparing normal cells.
8. Can be removed in some cases (patch, implant) if side effects occur.
9. Commercial: extends product life and adds value.

## 8.4 Challenges (disadvantages and limitations)

1. **High cost** of development and manufacture; costly for patients.
2. **Dose dumping:** if the system fails (crushing, chewing, alcohol with some products), the whole dose can be released at once, causing toxicity.
3. **Less flexibility** in adjusting dose; fixed strengths.
4. Drug stays in the body longer, so **toxic effects cannot be stopped quickly** (except removable systems).
5. Not suitable for all drugs: drugs with very short or very long half-life, very large doses, poor absorption, or a narrow absorption window are difficult.
6. **Variable absorption** due to gut transit time, food and disease.
7. Skin irritation or allergy with patches; local reactions with implants and devices.
8. Complex manufacturing, scale-up and quality control; stability problems (liposomes leak, nanoparticles aggregate).
9. Need for **patient education** (how to apply patches, not to crush tablets) and sometimes for a trained professional (implants, IUDs).
10. Regulatory requirements are demanding (special in-vitro and in-vivo studies).

## 8.5 Brief notes on important systems

### Transdermal patch – structure

```text
   ┌─────────────────────────────┐  backing layer (impermeable, protects)
   │  drug reservoir / matrix    │  holds the drug
   ├─────────────────────────────┤  rate-controlling membrane (in reservoir type)
   │  adhesive layer             │  sticks patch to skin
   └─────────────────────────────┘  release liner (peeled off before use)
                skin
```

Points: suitable drugs are potent (small daily dose), of low molecular weight, and with suitable oil–water solubility. Apply to clean, dry, hairless skin; rotate sites.

### Osmotic pump (OROS) – working

A tablet core with drug and an osmotic agent is coated with a **semipermeable membrane** having a tiny **laser-drilled orifice**. Water enters through the membrane, dissolves the core and builds osmotic pressure, which pushes the drug solution out of the hole at a nearly **constant (zero-order) rate**, almost independent of gut pH and food. The empty shell may appear in stools.

```text
        inner phospholipid layer
```

## Remember

- NDDS control **rate**, **duration** or **site** of drug delivery.
- Conventional = peaks and troughs; controlled release = steady level in the therapeutic window.
- Examples to quote: **OROS** (osmotic), **transdermal patches** (nitroglycerin, nicotine, fentanyl, scopolamine), **Ocusert** (pilocarpine), **levonorgestrel implant / IUD**, **liposomal amphotericin B / doxorubicin**.
- Targeted carriers: liposomes, niosomes, nanoparticles, microspheres, antibody conjugates.
- Advantages: fewer doses, steady levels, fewer side effects, targeting, protection, avoids first-pass.
- Challenges: cost, **dose dumping**, inflexible dosing, cannot stop toxicity fast, not for all drugs, complex manufacture.
