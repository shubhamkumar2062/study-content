# Unit 1: History of pharmacy in India and pharmacopoeias

This unit covers syllabus chapter 1: how the pharmacy profession grew in India (education, industry, practice and professional bodies), pharmacy as a career, and the official books of standards called pharmacopoeias.

## 1.1 What pharmacy is

**Pharmacy** is the art and science of preparing, compounding, preserving, testing and dispensing medicines, and of advising patients on their safe use. The word comes from the Greek *pharmakon* (a drug or remedy).

A **pharmacist** is a person registered under the Pharmacy Act, 1948 with a State Pharmacy Council. Only a registered pharmacist may compound or dispense medicines on a prescription.

## 1.2 History of pharmacy in India

### Ancient and medieval period

- Indian systems of medicine (Ayurveda, Siddha and later Unani) had their own medicine makers. *Charaka Samhita* and *Sushruta Samhita* describe the collection, processing and preparation of herbal, mineral and animal drugs.
- In these systems the physician (vaidya or hakim) usually prepared and gave the medicine himself. Pharmacy was not a separate profession.

### British period

- Modern (allopathic) medicine came with the British. Medicines were imported, and the first chemist shops were set up in the port cities. Bathgate and Co. opened in Calcutta in 1811 and is often quoted as one of the earliest such shops.
- Hospital "compounders" were trained on the job. They had no formal education and no legal status.
- **Acharya P. C. Ray** founded **Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works** in Calcutta (registered as a company in 1901). It is regarded as the beginning of the Indian pharmaceutical industry.
- **Drugs Enquiry Committee (Chopra Committee), 1930–31**, chaired by Col. R. N. Chopra, studied the poor quality of drugs sold in India. It recommended a law to control drug quality, a national pharmacopoeia and proper training and registration of pharmacists.

### Laws that shaped the profession

| Year | Law or event | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1940 | Drugs Act (now Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940) | Controls import, manufacture, sale and distribution of drugs |
| 1946 | Indian Pharmacopoeial List | First Indian list of drug standards, before the first IP |
| 1948 | Pharmacy Act | Regulates the profession and pharmacy education |
| 1949 | Pharmacy Council of India (PCI) set up | Sets minimum standards of education; approves institutions |
| 1955 | First Indian Pharmacopoeia | Official Indian standards for drugs |
| 1985 | Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act | Control of habit-forming drugs |

### Pharmacy education

- **Prof. M. L. Schroff** started a pharmacy course at **Banaras Hindu University in 1932**. He is called the *father of pharmacy education in India*.
- After the Pharmacy Act, the PCI framed **Education Regulations** for the Diploma in Pharmacy. The diploma was revised several times; the current one is **Education Regulations 2020 (ER-2020)**, a two-year course followed by a practical training period.
- Today the main courses are D.Pharm (diploma), B.Pharm (degree), M.Pharm, Pharm.D and Ph.D.

| Course | Duration | What it allows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D.Pharm | 2 years + practical training | Registration as a pharmacist |
| B.Pharm | 4 years | Registration; industry, hospital, community work |
| Pharm.D | 6 years (including internship) | Clinical pharmacy practice |
| M.Pharm | 2 years after B.Pharm | Specialisation, teaching, research |

### Pharmaceutical industry

- Before independence most drugs were imported. After independence public sector units such as **Hindustan Antibiotics Ltd (Pimpri)** and **Indian Drugs and Pharmaceuticals Ltd (IDPL)** were set up to make antibiotics and bulk drugs.
- The **Indian Patents Act, 1970** recognised only process patents for medicines. Indian firms could make cheaper versions of new drugs by different processes, and the industry grew fast.
- India is now a major producer of generic medicines and vaccines and is often called the *pharmacy of the world*.

### Pharmacy practice

Pharmacy practice in India covers:

- **Community (retail) pharmacy:** dispensing on prescription, counselling, health awareness.
- **Hospital pharmacy:** purchase, storage and supply of medicines inside a hospital, drug information, formulary work.
- **Clinical pharmacy:** working with doctors on the ward to make therapy safe and effective.

The PCI's **Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015** describe the duties and standards for registered pharmacists.

## 1.3 Professional associations

Professional associations bring pharmacists together, run conferences and journals, and speak for the profession.

| Association | Main role |
| --- | --- |
| Indian Pharmaceutical Association (IPA) | Oldest national body of pharmacists; publishes the *Indian Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences* |
| Indian Pharmaceutical Congress Association (IPCA) | Holds the yearly Indian Pharmaceutical Congress with member bodies |
| Association of Pharmaceutical Teachers of India (APTI) | Body of pharmacy teachers; works on education quality |
| Indian Hospital Pharmacists' Association (IHPA) | Hospital pharmacists |
| Indian Pharmacy Graduates' Association (IPGA) | Pharmacy graduates |
| All India Organisation of Chemists and Druggists (AIOCD) | Trade body of chemists and druggists (retail and wholesale) |
| State Pharmacy Councils | Register pharmacists in each state (statutory bodies, not voluntary associations) |

Internationally, the **International Pharmaceutical Federation (FIP)** links national pharmacy bodies.

## 1.4 Pharmacy as a career

A diploma holder can register as a pharmacist and work in many fields.

| Field | Typical jobs |
| --- | --- |
| Community pharmacy | Retail pharmacist, own medical store (with a drug licence) |
| Hospital | Hospital pharmacist, store in-charge |
| Government | Pharmacist in health centres, dispensaries, armed forces, railways |
| Industry | Production, packaging, quality control, stores |
| Marketing | Medical representative, sales |
| Wholesale and distribution | Wholesale drug business |
| Further study | B.Pharm (lateral entry to second year), then higher studies |
| Regulatory | Drug inspector (needs a degree as per rules) |

**Qualities of a good pharmacist:** honesty, accuracy, good communication with patients, up-to-date knowledge, and respect for the code of ethics.

## 1.5 Pharmacopoeia

### Definition

A **pharmacopoeia** is a legally recognised book of standards for drugs, published under the authority of a government. It gives the standards of identity, purity and strength of drugs and their formulations, and the methods to test them.

The word comes from Greek *pharmakon* (drug) and *poiein* (to make).

**Formulary vs pharmacopoeia:** a pharmacopoeia mainly gives *standards and tests*; a formulary mainly gives *formulas* of preparations and information for prescribing.

### Contents of a typical monograph

```text
Monograph (one drug or one preparation)
 ├── Name, chemical structure, molecular formula and weight
 ├── Definition and content limits (e.g. not less than 98.0% and not more than 101.0%)
 ├── Category (e.g. analgesic) and dose (in older editions)
 ├── Description and solubility
 ├── Identification tests
 ├── Tests for purity (limits of impurities, loss on drying, sulphated ash, heavy metals)
 ├── Assay (how much drug is present)
 └── Storage and labelling
```

### Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)

- Published by the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad**, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.
- It is the official book of standards under the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**. Drugs sold in India must meet IP standards.

| Edition | Year |

**Salient features of the Indian Pharmacopoeia (recent editions):**

1. Published in more than one volume: general notices and general chapters (appendices) first, then monographs of drug substances, dosage forms, and special groups.
2. **General notices** explain how to read the book: terms like "about", "constant weight", storage words (cool place, room temperature), and solubility terms.
3. **General chapters (appendices)** give official test methods: disintegration, dissolution, uniformity of weight, sterility, pyrogen and bacterial endotoxin tests, limit tests, sieves and powder grades.
4. Monographs on **drug substances**, **dosage forms** (tablets, capsules, injections, etc.), **vaccines and immunological products**, **blood products**, **herbal products**, **radiopharmaceuticals** and **veterinary products**.
5. Uses **SI units** and modern instrumental methods (HPLC, UV, IR).
6. Doses and categories are usually not part of the monograph in recent editions; the IP is a book of standards, not a prescribing guide.
7. Updated by addenda between editions.

### Other important pharmacopoeias and references

| Book | Country / publisher | Key facts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| British Pharmacopoeia (BP) | UK; British Pharmacopoeia Commission | First edition 1864; now revised every year; includes European Pharmacopoeia monographs |
| United States Pharmacopeia (USP) | USA; United States Pharmacopeial Convention | First edition 1820; standards of drug substances and products |
| National Formulary (NF) | USA | First published in 1888 by the American Pharmaceutical Association; mainly excipients; combined with USP as **USP–NF** |
| Extra Pharmacopoeia (Martindale) | UK; Royal Pharmaceutical Society | First compiled by William Martindale in 1883; now *Martindale: The Complete Drug Reference*; gives information on almost all drugs used anywhere |
| International Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Int.) | World Health Organization | Standards for use mainly in developing countries |
| National Formulary of India (NFI) | India | Prescribing guide for doctors and pharmacists |

**Extra Pharmacopoeia is not an official book of standards.** It is a reference book: drug monographs with uses, doses, adverse effects, interactions and proprietary names from many countries.

## Remember

- Pharmacy Act **1948**; PCI constituted **1949**; Drugs and Cosmetics Act **1940**.
- Father of pharmacy education in India: **Prof. M. L. Schroff** (BHU, 1932).
- Chopra Committee (1930–31) asked for drug laws, a national pharmacopoeia and trained pharmacists.
- First IP **1955**; current edition **IP 2022**, published by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad.
- A pharmacopoeia gives *standards*; a formulary gives *formulas*; Martindale gives *information*.
- BP 1864, USP 1820, NF 1888; USP and NF are now published together.
