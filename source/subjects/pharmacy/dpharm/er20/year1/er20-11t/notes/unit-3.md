# Unit 3: Unit operations

This unit covers syllabus chapter 4. For each operation you need: **definition, objectives/applications, principle, construction and working** of the named equipment.

A **unit operation** is a basic physical step that is used again and again in making many different products, for example size reduction, mixing or drying. A whole manufacturing process is a chain of unit operations.

```text
Typical solid dosage line:
 Raw drug → SIZE REDUCTION → SIZE SEPARATION → MIXING → (granulation)
          → DRYING → compression / filling → packing
 Liquid line: dissolving → MIXING → FILTRATION → filling
 Herbal drug: size reduction → EXTRACTION → filtration → concentration / drying
```

## 3.1 Size reduction

**Definition:** size reduction (comminution, milling) is the process of breaking larger particles of a solid into smaller ones by mechanical forces.

**Objectives / applications**

1. Increases surface area, so **dissolution and absorption** are faster (better bioavailability).
2. Helps **uniform mixing** of powders and uniform dose in tablets and capsules.
3. Makes **extraction** of crude drugs faster (solvent penetrates small pieces easily).
4. Helps **drying** (more surface for evaporation).
5. Improves stability of **suspensions and emulsions** (small particles settle slowly).
6. Needed for uniform **flow** and filling.

**Mechanisms (forces):** cutting, compression, impact, attrition (rubbing) and combinations.

### Hammer mill

**Principle:** **impact.** Rapidly rotating hammers strike the material and break it; particles also hit the casing.

**Construction**

- A strong metal casing (chamber) with a central horizontal shaft.
- Hammers (swinging or fixed) attached to the shaft.
- A feed hopper at the top.
- A **screen** (sieve) at the bottom that decides the final particle size.

```text
          Feed hopper
              │
      ┌───────▼────────┐
      │   ╲  │  ╱      │   casing
      │ ── ( ● ) ──    │   hammers on rotating shaft
      │   ╱  │  ╲      │
      │▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒│   screen (sieve)
      └───────┬────────┘
              ▼
        Milled powder
```

**Working:** material is fed into the hopper. The shaft rotates at high speed; hammers hit the particles and throw them against the casing. Particles keep breaking until they are small enough to pass through the screen and are collected below.

**Uses:** milling dry granules, crystalline and fibrous materials, wet masses (with knife-edge hammers), crude drugs.

**Advantages:** fast, compact, easy to clean, particle size controlled by changing screen and speed, continuous operation.

**Disadvantages:** heat is produced (not good for heat-sensitive or sticky materials), screen can clog, not suitable for very hard abrasive materials (wear), noisy.

### Ball mill

**Principle:** **impact and attrition.** Balls inside a rotating cylinder fall on the material (impact) and rub against it (attrition).

**Construction**

- A hollow cylinder (steel, porcelain or rubber-lined) mounted horizontally on a shaft.
- Filled about 30–50% with balls of steel, porcelain or flint, of different sizes.
- Material is added to fill the gaps between balls.

**Working and the importance of speed**

| Speed | What happens | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too low | Balls roll over each other (cascading) | Little size reduction, mostly attrition |
| Optimum (about one-half to two-thirds of the critical speed) | Balls are lifted and fall from the top on the material (cataracting) | Best size reduction by impact |
| Critical speed and above | Balls stick to the wall by centrifugal force (centrifuging) | No size reduction |

**Critical speed** is the speed at which the balls just start to stay against the wall throughout the rotation.

**Uses:** fine grinding of dyes, pigments and hard or abrasive drugs; can be used dry or wet; can be closed (for toxic or sterile materials).

**Advantages:** very fine powder; wet or dry grinding; closed system protects workers and product; works for toxic materials and for sterile milling.

**Disadvantages:** slow, noisy, contamination from wear of balls, long cleaning time.

## 3.2 Size separation

**Definition:** size separation (sieving, sifting, classification) is the process of separating a mixture of particles into fractions of different sizes.

**Applications:** to get powders of a definite size for uniform dosing; to remove coarse or fine particles from granules before compression; quality control of powders; to separate the product from oversize material after milling.

### Sieves and standards of sieves

A sieve is a woven wire mesh in a frame. The **sieve number** is the number of meshes (openings) in one inch (25.4 mm) length. A higher sieve number means smaller openings.

Standard sieves should have uniform square openings, wires of known diameter, be made of non-reactive material (stainless steel, brass, bronze) and be strong.

**IP sieve numbers and opening sizes (examples)**

| Sieve number | Nominal opening |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | 1700 µm |
| 22 | 710 µm |
| 44 | 355 µm |
| 60 | 250 µm |
| 85 | 180 µm |
| 120 | 125 µm |

### Classification of powders according to IP

| Grade of powder | All particles pass through sieve | Not more than 40% pass through sieve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coarse powder | 10 | 44 |
| Moderately coarse powder | 22 | 60 |
| Moderately fine powder | 44 | 85 |
| Fine powder | 85 | — |
| Very fine powder | 120 | — |

So, for coarse powder, "10/44" means all pass sieve 10 and not more than 40% pass sieve 44.

**Mechanical sieve shaker:** sieves stacked with the coarsest at the top and the finest at the bottom, shaken for a set time; the weight on each sieve gives the size distribution.

### Cyclone separator

**Principle:** **centrifugal force.** A stream of air carrying particles is made to spin; the heavier (larger) particles are thrown outward to the wall and fall down, while light (fine) particles leave with the air.

**Construction**

- A short vertical cylinder joined to a cone at the bottom.
- A tangential **inlet** near the top for the air–solid mixture.
- A central **outlet pipe** at the top for air (and fines).
- An outlet at the bottom of the cone for collected solids.

```text
          air + fines out
               ▲
        ┌──────┴──────┐
 in ═══►│   (spiral)  │  ← tangential inlet
        │             │
         ╲    vortex ╱
          ╲         ╱      cone
           ╲       ╱
            ╲     ╱
             └─┬─┘
               ▼
        coarse solids collected
```

**Working:** the air–solid mixture enters tangentially and swirls down in a spiral (vortex). Larger particles move to the wall, lose speed and slide down to the bottom outlet. The air turns upward at the cone and leaves through the central pipe with very fine particles.

**Uses:** separating solids from air in spray dryers and fluid bed dryers; collecting dust; separating coarse and fine fractions.

**Advantages:** no moving parts, cheap, continuous, works at high temperature. **Disadvantages:** poor for very fine particles; wall wears with abrasive powders.

## 3.3 Mixing

**Definition:** mixing is a process in which two or more components (solids, liquids or both) are distributed so that each part of the mixture has the same composition as every other part.

**Applications:** blending drug with excipients for tablets and capsules (dose uniformity), preparation of solutions, emulsions, suspensions, ointments and creams, and granulation.

**Mechanisms:** convective mixing (bulk movement), shear mixing (layers slip over each other), diffusive mixing (random movement of single particles).

### Double cone blender (powders)

**Principle:** **tumbling (diffusive mixing).** Powder falls over and over as the container rotates.

**Construction:** two cones joined at their bases by a short cylinder, mounted on a horizontal shaft through the middle. A charging/discharging port with a lid at one end. Usually stainless steel. Filled to about 50–60% of its volume, leaving free space for the powder to move.

**Working:** the blender rotates slowly (about 30–100 rpm). The powder slides and falls from one cone into the other, splitting and mixing again and again. After the set time it is stopped and discharged through the valve.

**Uses:** gentle blending of free-flowing powders and granules, adding lubricant before compression.

**Advantages:** gentle (no breaking of granules), easy to load, unload and clean, uniform mix. **Disadvantages:** not good for powders of very different sizes or densities (they can separate), needs head room, no intense shear for cohesive powders.

### Turbine mixer (liquids)

**Principle:** **high-speed rotation of a turbine impeller** causes strong radial and tangential flow and shear in the liquid.

**Construction:** a vessel with a central shaft carrying a turbine (flat or curved blades set on a disc), driven by a motor. Baffles may be fitted to the vessel wall to stop swirling and vortex formation.

**Working:** the turbine rotates at high speed and throws the liquid outward to the wall. The liquid then moves up and down and returns to the centre, so the whole vessel is circulated. Shear near the blades breaks liquid drops.

**Uses:** mixing liquids of low to moderate viscosity, preparing solutions and emulsions, dissolving solids.

**Advantages:** efficient, works for a wide viscosity range, gives good shear. **Disadvantages:** may cause foaming and air entrapment; vortex without baffles.

### Triple roller mill (semisolids)

**Principle:** **shear.** Three rollers rotate at different speeds; the material passing through the narrow gaps between them is sheared and dispersed.

**Construction:** three horizontal rollers (stainless steel or porcelain) close to each other. The gaps between rollers can be adjusted. Each roller turns faster than the one before it. A scraper blade at the last roller removes the product.

```text
 feed → [ R1 slow ] ⇄ gap ⇄ [ R2 medium ] ⇄ gap ⇄ [ R3 fast ] → scraper → product
```

**Working:** the ointment or paste is placed between the first and second rollers. Because the rollers turn at different speeds, the material is sheared, carried to the next gap and sheared again, and then scraped off the third roller.

**Uses:** smooth ointments and pastes; breaking aggregates of solids in ointment bases; creams and printing inks.

**Advantages:** very fine, smooth, uniform product. **Disadvantages:** heat produced; volatile ingredients can evaporate because the product is spread thin and exposed.

### Silverson mixer homogenizer

**Principle:** **high shear in a rotor–stator head.** A rotor turning at very high speed close to a fixed perforated stator (screen) sucks material in and forces it out through the holes with intense shear.

**Construction:** a mixing head on a shaft with a rotor (blades) inside a stator (cylinder with holes or slots), driven by a high-speed motor; the head dips into the vessel.

**Working:**

1. The rotor's high speed creates suction that draws liquid and solids into the head.
2. Material is thrown to the edge of the rotor and milled in the narrow gap between rotor and stator.
3. It is forced out through the stator holes at high speed and mixed with the rest of the batch.
4. The cycle repeats, so all the batch passes through the head many times.

**Uses:** emulsions, creams, lotions, fine suspensions, dissolving gums.

**Advantages:** very fine droplets, fast, versatile. **Disadvantages:** heat and air may be built in; not for very viscous materials.

## 3.4 Filtration

**Definition:** filtration is the process of separating solids from a liquid (or gas) by passing it through a porous medium that holds back the solids. The liquid that passes is the **filtrate**; the solids left behind are the **cake** or residue.

**Applications:** clarifying solutions, syrups and elixirs; sterilisation of heat-sensitive solutions (membrane filters); removing particles from injections and eye drops; collecting crystals; air filtration in clean rooms (HEPA).

### Theory of filtration

**Mechanisms by which solids are held:** straining (particles larger than pores), impingement (particles hit and stick to the medium), entanglement (in fibrous media), and attraction (electrostatic forces).

**Rate of filtration** is described by **Darcy's law** (based on Poiseuille's equation for flow through capillaries):

```text
          dV       K × A × ΔP
 Rate  =  ──  =  ─────────────
          dt         η × L

 V  = volume of filtrate        A  = filter area
 ΔP = pressure difference       η  = viscosity of the liquid
 L  = thickness of medium + cake  K = permeability coefficient
```

**Factors affecting the rate**

| Factor | Effect on rate |
| --- | --- |
| Larger filter area | Faster |
| Higher pressure difference (pressure or vacuum) | Faster (unless the cake compresses) |
| Higher viscosity of liquid | Slower (warming the liquid reduces viscosity) |
| Thicker cake or medium | Slower |
| Larger pore size | Faster but less clarity |
| Filter aids (kieselguhr, talc) | Keep the cake porous; faster |

### Membrane filter

**Principle:** **sieving (screening)** by a thin film with millions of uniform tiny pores; particles and microbes larger than the pore size are retained on the surface.

**Construction:** a thin (about 0.1–0.15 mm) porous sheet of cellulose esters (cellulose acetate, cellulose nitrate), nylon, PVDF or PTFE, held on a support in a holder (disc holder or cartridge). Pore sizes range from about 0.1 to 10 µm; **0.22 µm** (often called 0.2 µm) filters are used for sterilisation.

**Working:** the solution is forced through the membrane by pressure or vacuum. Particles and bacteria larger than the pores are held on the surface. A prefilter is often used before it to stop clogging.

**Uses:** sterilisation of heat-sensitive injections and eye drops, clarification, sterility testing, particle testing.

**Advantages:** absolute and uniform pore size, no shedding of fibres, little adsorption of drug, fast flow. **Disadvantages:** clogs easily (needs prefilter), some solvents dissolve certain membranes, fragile.

### Sintered glass filter

**Principle:** filtration through a **porous plate made by fusing (sintering) small glass particles** together.

**Construction:** powdered borosilicate glass of a selected particle size is heated until the particles just fuse at their contact points, forming a porous disc. The disc is sealed into a glass funnel or crucible. Filters are graded by pore size, from **grade 1 (coarsest)** to **grade 5 (finest)**; grade 5 is fine enough to hold back bacteria.

**Working:** the liquid is poured on the disc and passes through, usually helped by vacuum; solids remain on the disc.

**Uses:** filtering corrosive and strong chemical liquids, sterilising (grade 5), collecting precipitates in analysis.

**Advantages:** resistant to most chemicals (except hydrofluoric acid and hot strong alkali), no fibres shed, no adsorption of drug, reusable, can be sterilised. **Disadvantages:** brittle, expensive, difficult to clean if clogged.

## 3.5 Drying

**Definition:** drying is the removal of a liquid (usually water) from a material by applying heat, to give a dry solid. It removes small amounts of water (unlike evaporation, which concentrates a solution).

**Applications:** drying of wet granules before compression, preparing dry extracts, preserving heat-sensitive products (freeze drying), reducing bulk and weight, improving stability (water causes hydrolysis and microbial growth).

### Fluidized bed dryer (FBD)

**Principle:** hot air is blown up through a bed of wet granules at a speed high enough to **lift and suspend the particles** (fluidize them). Each particle is surrounded by hot air, so heat and moisture transfer are very fast.

**Construction**

- A **product container (bowl)** with a perforated bottom (wire mesh) to hold the wet material.
- Below it, an **air inlet** with a fan, air filter and heater.
- Above it, a **drying chamber** and **bag filters** (finger bags) to stop fine particles escaping.
- An exhaust outlet with a fan at the top.

```text
            exhaust fan → out
           ┌─────────────┐
           │ bag filters │
           ├─────────────┤
           │ ∘ ∘  ∘ ∘ ∘  │  fluidized granules
           │∘  ∘ ∘  ∘ ∘ ∘│
           ├▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒┤  perforated base of bowl
           └──────▲──────┘
          hot filtered air (heater + fan)
```

**Working:**

1. Wet granules are loaded into the bowl and the bowl is pushed into place.
2. Fan and heater are switched on; heated filtered air passes up through the mesh.
3. As air velocity rises, the bed lifts and the granules move freely like a boiling liquid.
4. Water evaporates quickly and leaves with the exhaust air; fines are caught by the bag filters.
5. Drying is usually complete in about 20–40 minutes. The bowl is removed and granules unloaded.

**Advantages:** fast (much quicker than tray dryers), uniform drying with no hot spots, less handling, can also be used for granulation and coating, suitable for heat-sensitive materials (short exposure).

**Disadvantages:** fine powders can be lost or stick to bags; static charge can build up (risk with organic solvents unless earthed); granules may break by attrition; not good for sticky materials.

### Freeze drying (lyophilization)

**Principle:** **sublimation.** Water is frozen, and then the ice is turned directly into vapour without melting, under very low pressure. This is possible below the **triple point of water** (about 0.01 °C and 4.58 mm Hg), where solid, liquid and vapour exist together.

```text
 Pressure
   │   SOLID │ LIQUID
   │  (ice)  │ (water)
   │         ●──────────   ● triple point (0.01 °C, 4.58 mm Hg)
   │ sublimation ↘  VAPOUR
   └────────────────────── Temperature
 Freeze drying works in the low-pressure, low-temperature region: ice → vapour directly.
```

**Process / steps:**

1. **Preparation and filling:** solution is sterile-filtered and filled into vials (stoppers partly inserted).
2. **Freezing:** vials are cooled on shelves to well below the freezing point (often around −40 °C or lower) until completely solid.
3. **Primary drying (sublimation):** the chamber pressure is reduced by a vacuum pump; gentle heat is given to the shelves. Ice sublimes, and the vapour is collected as ice on a very cold **condenser**. Most of the water is removed here.
4. **Secondary drying (desorption):** the temperature is raised (still under vacuum) to remove water bound to the solid, giving very low final moisture.
5. **Stoppering and sealing:** vials are closed inside the chamber (often under nitrogen), then removed and capped.

**Main parts of a freeze dryer:** drying chamber with temperature-controlled shelves, refrigeration system, condenser, vacuum pump.

**Advantages:** suitable for heat-sensitive drugs (antibiotics, vaccines, proteins, enzymes, blood plasma); product is a porous cake that dissolves quickly; long shelf life; low moisture; sterile handling possible.

**Disadvantages:** very costly equipment and running cost; long cycle (often a day or more); needs skilled operation; volatile components are lost.

## 3.6 Extraction

**Definition:** extraction is the process of separating the active (soluble) constituents of plant or animal tissues from the inactive (insoluble) material by using a suitable solvent, called the **menstruum**. The insoluble residue left behind is called the **marc**.

**Classification of extraction methods**

| Method | How it is done | Example use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Infusion** | Drug is soaked in cold or boiling water for a short time, then strained | Fresh infusions of soft drugs (flowers, leaves) |
| **Decoction** | Drug is boiled in water for a fixed time (about 15 minutes), cooled and strained | Hard, woody drugs with heat-stable, water-soluble constituents |
| **Digestion** | Maceration with gentle heat | When moderate heat helps extraction |
| **Maceration** | Drug is kept in a closed vessel with the whole menstruum for about 7 days with frequent shaking; liquid strained, marc pressed | Tinctures of soft or unorganised drugs (gums, resins) |
| **Percolation** | Moistened drug packed in a **percolator**; menstruum flows slowly down through it and is collected as percolate | Tinctures and liquid extracts of potent drugs |
| **Continuous hot extraction (Soxhlet)** | Fresh hot solvent repeatedly passes over the drug and siphons back, recycling a small volume | Fixed oils, alkaloids; when drug is not heat-sensitive |
| Modern methods | Supercritical fluid (CO2), microwave- and ultrasound-assisted extraction | Volatile and heat-sensitive constituents |

**Factors affecting extraction:** particle size of drug, nature of solvent, temperature, time, agitation, and pH.

**Applications:** preparing tinctures, extracts (liquid, soft, dry), herbal formulations, isolation of active constituents (alkaloids, glycosides, oils), and enzymes.

## Remember

- Hammer mill = **impact**; ball mill = **impact + attrition**; ball mill works best at about 1/2–2/3 of critical speed.
- IP powder grades: coarse 10/44, moderately coarse 22/60, moderately fine 44/85, fine 85, very fine 120.
- Cyclone separator = centrifugal force; no moving parts.
- Double cone blender = tumbling (powders); turbine = liquids; triple roller mill = ointments; Silverson = rotor–stator high shear.
- Darcy's law: rate ∝ area × pressure ÷ (viscosity × thickness).
- Membrane filter 0.22 µm sterilises; sintered glass grade 5 holds bacteria.
- FBD: hot air fluidizes granules; fast and uniform drying.
- Freeze drying = freezing → primary drying (sublimation) → secondary drying (desorption).
- Extraction: infusion, decoction, digestion, maceration, percolation, Soxhlet; menstruum and marc.
