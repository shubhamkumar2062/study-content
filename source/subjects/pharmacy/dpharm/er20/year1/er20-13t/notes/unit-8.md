# Chapter 8: Role of medicinal and aromatic plants in national economy and their export potential

India is rich in plant wealth and traditional knowledge. Medicinal and aromatic plants (often shortened to **MAPs**) give income to farmers and forest dwellers, raw material to industry, and foreign exchange through exports. This is a short chapter (2 hours) and is usually asked as a short note.

## 8.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medicinal plants | Plants used to prevent or treat disease, or as a source of drugs | Senna, isabgol, ashwagandha, rauwolfia |
| Aromatic plants | Plants that yield volatile (essential) oils used in perfumes, flavours and medicine | Mint, lemongrass, palmarosa, vetiver, sandalwood |
| Spices | Aromatic plant parts used to flavour food, many also medicinal | Black pepper, cardamom, ginger, turmeric, clove |

## 8.2 Why India is important

- India is one of the world's **mega-biodiversity countries**, with several biodiversity hotspots (Himalaya, Western Ghats, North-East, Andaman and Nicobar Islands).
- It has a wide range of climates — tropical, temperate, alpine, desert and coastal — so very different plants grow here.
- It has an ancient, documented system of plant medicine (Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani, Sowa-Rigpa) and a large tribal knowledge base.
- Thousands of plant species are used in Indian traditional medicine; a smaller number are traded in large volume.

## 8.3 Role of medicinal and aromatic plants in the national economy

### 8.3.1 Income and employment

1. **Farmers:** cultivation of crops such as isabgol, senna, mint, ashwagandha, aloe, safed musli, stevia and lemongrass gives better returns than some food crops on dry or poor land.
2. **Forest dwellers and tribal people:** collection of wild drugs (harad, bahera, amla, gums, resins, honey) is a major source of cash income.
3. **Industry:** employment in processing, extraction, distillation, packaging, formulation and marketing.
4. **Women's self-help groups:** drying, grading and making simple products such as herbal powders and oils.

### 8.3.2 Supply to industries

| Industry | Plant material used |
| --- | --- |
| AYUSH (Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha, Homoeopathy) medicines | Crude herbs, extracts |
| Modern pharmaceuticals | Plant-derived actives and starting materials (sennosides, reserpine, codeine from licit opium, diosgenin, quinine, vinca alkaloids) |
| Nutraceuticals and health foods | Ashwagandha, turmeric (curcumin), green tea, spirulina, isabgol |
| Cosmetics and toiletries | Aloe gel, sandalwood oil, neem, turmeric, almond oil |
| Food and flavour | Spices, essential oils, oleoresins, natural colours |
| Perfumery and aromatherapy | Mint oils, lemongrass, palmarosa, vetiver, rose, jasmine |
| Pharmaceutical aids | Guar gum, gum karaya, agar, starches |

### 8.3.3 Health care

- Low-cost primary health care through traditional systems, especially in villages.
- Home remedies (tulsi, ginger, turmeric, ajwain) reduce health spending.

### 8.3.4 Foreign exchange

Exports of herbs, extracts, essential oils, spices and gums bring foreign exchange and support the balance of trade.

### 8.3.5 Environment and land use

- Many medicinal crops grow on wasteland or dry land (isabgol, senna, guar, aloe in Gujarat and Rajasthan).
- Agroforestry with medicinal trees (neem, arjuna, amla) improves soil and adds income.

## 8.4 Export potential

India is a major exporter of plant-based products. Important export items:

| Product | Note |
| --- | --- |
| **Isabgol (psyllium) seed and husk** | India is the world's leading producer and exporter |
| **Senna leaves and pods** | Tinnevelly senna exported for laxative manufacture |
| **Guar gum** | India is the largest producer; used in food, pharma and oil drilling |
| **Mint oils and menthol** | India (mainly Uttar Pradesh) is a leading producer of mint oil |
| **Spices** | Black pepper, cardamom, ginger, turmeric, chillies, cumin, coriander, fennel |
| **Spice oils and oleoresins** | Pepper, ginger, turmeric, paprika oleoresins |
| **Herbal extracts** | Ashwagandha, curcumin, boswellia, gymnema, bacopa |
| **Essential oils** | Lemongrass, palmarosa, vetiver, sandalwood, eucalyptus |
| **Gums and resins** | Karaya, guar, guggul |
| **AYUSH formulations** | Exported to many countries |
| **Natural colours** | Turmeric, annatto, marigold (lutein) |

### 8.4.1 Agencies that support the sector

| Agency | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB)**, Ministry of AYUSH (set up in 2000) | Promotes cultivation, conservation, quality planting material and marketing of medicinal plants |
| **Spices Board** | Promotes production, quality and export of spices |
| **Pharmexcil** | Export promotion council for pharmaceuticals including herbal products |
| **CSIR institutes** (e.g. CSIR–CIMAP, Lucknow) | Research on medicinal and aromatic crops, improved varieties, distillation technology |
| **ICAR** institutes | Agricultural research on medicinal and aromatic crops |
| **State Medicinal Plant Boards** | State-level schemes |

### 8.4.2 Protecting traditional knowledge

- **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)** records Indian traditional knowledge so that others cannot wrongly patent it.
- India successfully challenged a US patent on the wound-healing use of **turmeric** and a European patent on a **neem**-based fungicide, because the uses were already known in India.

## 8.5 Problems (constraints)

1. **Over-collection from the wild** — many species have become rare (e.g. *Podophyllum hexandrum*, *Rauwolfia serpentina*, *Saussurea costus*, *Nardostachys*).
2. **Poor quality** — adulteration, mixing of species, poor drying, pesticide and heavy-metal residues; this leads to rejection of consignments.
3. **Lack of standardised cultivation practice** and of quality planting material.
4. **Small, scattered growers** with weak bargaining power; middlemen take most profit.
5. **Limited processing** — India often exports raw herbs and imports value-added extracts.
6. **Strict regulations abroad** — need for certification, documentation and safety data.
7. **Price fluctuation** of herbal raw materials.

## 8.6 Measures to improve the sector

- Cultivation instead of wild collection, under **Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP)**.
- Conservation of rare plants in their natural habitat (in situ) and in herbal gardens and gene banks (ex situ).
- Quality control: pharmacopoeial standards (Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India), GMP (Schedule T for ASU drugs), certification and testing laboratories.
- Value addition: extraction, standardised extracts, finished products instead of raw herbs.
- Contract farming and buy-back arrangements to assure growers of a market.
- Research on improved varieties, tissue culture and post-harvest technology.
- Better market information and organised trade.

## Remember

- India is a mega-biodiversity country with an ancient tradition of plant medicine.
- MAPs give **income and employment**, **raw material for industry**, **low-cost health care** and **foreign exchange**.
- Leading exports: **isabgol**, **senna**, **guar gum**, **mint oil/menthol**, spices, oleoresins, herbal extracts and essential oils.
- **NMPB** (2000, under AYUSH) promotes medicinal plants; **Spices Board** promotes spices; **CSIR–CIMAP** does research.
- Problems: over-harvesting, adulteration, poor quality, raw export without value addition.
- Solutions: cultivation, GACP, conservation, GMP, value addition, TKDL protection.
