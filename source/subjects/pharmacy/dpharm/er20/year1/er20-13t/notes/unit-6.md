# Chapter 6: Plant fibres used as surgical dressings; sutures

The syllabus covers **cotton, silk, wool and regenerated fibres** as surgical dressings, and **sutures — surgical catgut and ligatures**. Silk and wool are animal fibres, but they are studied here because they are used in the same way.

## 6.1 Surgical dressings — basic terms

A **surgical dressing** is any material applied to a wound or injured part to protect it, absorb discharge, stop bleeding, hold medicaments or support the part.

| Type | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary (wound-contact) dressing | Lies directly on the wound | Paraffin gauze, absorbent gauze |
| Absorbents | Soak up blood and pus | Absorbent cotton, gauze, cellulose wadding |
| Bandages | Hold dressings in place, give support or pressure | Cotton crepe, roller bandage, elastic bandage |
| Adhesive dressings | Stick to skin | Adhesive plaster |

**Fibres** are long, thin, flexible structures. They are grouped by origin:

```text
                       Fibres
                         |
     -------------------------------------------
     |                   |                     |
 Vegetable           Animal              Man-made
 (cellulose)         (protein)           |
 Cotton, jute,       Silk, wool          ----------------------
 flax, hemp                              |                    |
                                  Regenerated            Synthetic
                                  (from natural          (fully made)
                                  cellulose/protein)     Nylon, polyester,
                                  Viscose rayon,         polypropylene
                                  cellulose acetate,
                                  oxidised cellulose
```

## 6.2 Cotton (absorbent cotton)

- **Synonyms:** Absorbent cotton wool, Surgical cotton.
- **Biological source:** Epidermal **trichomes (hairs) of the seeds** of cultivated species of *Gossypium herbaceum* Linn., *G. hirsutum*, *G. barbadense* and others, freed from fat (dewaxed), bleached and sterilised.
- **Family:** Malvaceae.
- **Geographical source:** India (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana, Punjab), USA, China, Egypt.

**Preparation of absorbent cotton**

1. Raw cotton is separated from seeds (ginning). Long hairs are lint; short hairs are linters.
2. It is boiled with dilute alkali (NaOH) under pressure to remove the natural wax and fat — this makes it **absorbent**.
3. Bleached with sodium hypochlorite, washed with dilute acid and water.
4. Dried, carded (fibres combed parallel) and packed; sterilised for surgical use.

**Chemical nature:** Almost pure **cellulose** (about 90%), with small amounts of water, wax, fat and protein in raw cotton.

**Microscopy:** Each fibre is a single long cell, a **flattened, twisted tube** with a narrow lumen and a rounded tip.

```text
  Cotton fibre under the microscope

  ~~~~~~~~\/~~~~~~~~\/~~~~~~~~\/~~~~~~~~   ← flat ribbon with twists
          (twist)       (twist)
```

**Identification tests**

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Iodine + 80% sulphuric acid | Blue colour (cellulose) |
| Ammoniacal copper oxide (cuprammonium) | Fibre swells and dissolves |
| Conc. sulphuric acid | Dissolves |
| Phloroglucinol + HCl | No pink (no lignin) |
| Burning | Burns quickly with smell of burning paper, little ash |
| Sinks in water within seconds | Shows good absorbency |

**Uses:** Absorbent dressing, swabs, pads; filtering medium; packing of tablets; making gauze and bandages.

## 6.3 Silk

- **Biological source:** Fibre obtained from the **cocoons** of the silkworm *Bombyx mori* Linn., the larva of the silk moth.
- **Family:** Bombycidae.
- **Geographical source:** China, India (Karnataka, West Bengal, Assam), Japan.

**Preparation:** The silkworm spins a cocoon of a continuous filament. Cocoons are heated to kill the pupa, softened in hot water, and the filament is unwound (reeled). Boiling with soap water removes the gum (degumming).

**Chemical nature:** Protein — **fibroin** (inner fibre) coated with **sericin** (silk gum). Fibroin is rich in glycine, alanine, serine and tyrosine. Silk contains no sulphur.

**Microscopy:** Smooth, glassy, solid, structureless threads without a lumen; raw silk shows two fibroin filaments joined by sericin.

**Identification tests**

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Burning | Burns slowly with smell of burnt hair/feathers, leaves a black bead |
| Millon's reagent | Red colour (protein with tyrosine) |
| Conc. HCl | Dissolves |
| Cuprammonium | Dissolves |
| Lead acetate + NaOH | No black colour (no sulphur) — distinguishes from wool |

**Uses:** **Non-absorbable sutures and ligatures**; silk fabrics for dressings and wound covers; sieves (bolting cloth).

## 6.4 Wool

- **Biological source:** Hairs from the fleece of the sheep *Ovis aries* Linn.
- **Family:** Bovidae.
- **Geographical source:** Australia, New Zealand, Argentina; India (Rajasthan, Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh).

**Preparation:** Fleece is sheared, sorted, washed with soap and alkali (scouring) to remove grease (the grease is recovered as wool fat, i.e. lanolin), dried and carded.

**Chemical nature:** Protein — **keratin**, rich in the sulphur-containing amino acid **cystine**.

**Microscopy:** Round fibre with overlapping **scales** on the surface (like roof tiles) and a central medulla in coarse fibres.

```text
  Wool fibre

  |\/\/\/\/\/\/\/|   ← overlapping cuticle scales
  |  medulla     |
  |/\/\/\/\/\/\/\|
```

**Identification tests**

| Test | Result |
| --- | --- |
| Burning | Burns slowly with smell of burnt hair |
| 5% KOH (boil) | Dissolves |
| Lead acetate + NaOH, warm | Black colour (lead sulphide — sulphur present) |
| Millon's reagent | Red colour |
| Picric acid | Stains yellow |

**Uses:** Crepe bandages and elastic bandages (with cotton); warm protective coverings; source of lanolin.

## 6.5 Regenerated fibres

Regenerated fibres are made by dissolving a natural polymer (usually cellulose from wood pulp or cotton linters) and forcing the solution through fine holes (spinnerets) into a setting bath, so a new continuous fibre forms.

### 6.5.1 Viscose rayon

- **Source:** Wood pulp or cotton linters (cellulose).
- **Preparation:** Cellulose → treated with NaOH (alkali cellulose) → treated with carbon disulphide (cellulose xanthate, "viscose" solution) → forced through spinnerets into dilute sulphuric acid → regenerated cellulose filaments.
- **Microscopy:** Smooth, uniform rods with fine longitudinal striations; no twists.
- **Tests:** Dissolves in 80% H2SO4; iodine + H2SO4 gives blue (cellulose); loses strength when wet.
- **Uses:** Absorbent rayon (alone or mixed with cotton) in gauze, swabs and sanitary pads; non-woven dressings.

### 6.5.2 Other regenerated and modified fibres

| Fibre | Note |
| --- | --- |
| Cellulose acetate | Made by acetylating cellulose; dissolves in acetone |
| Oxidised cellulose | Cotton or gauze oxidised to contain carboxyl groups; **absorbable haemostatic** dressing |
| Calcium alginate fibre | From seaweed alginate; haemostatic, forms gel with wound fluid |

## 6.6 Sutures and ligatures

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Suture** | A strand used to stitch (sew) together the edges of a wound or surgical cut |
| **Ligature** | A strand used to tie (close) a blood vessel or duct to stop bleeding |

The same materials are used for both.

**Ideal suture:** strong, sterile, easy to tie, knot holds, causes little tissue reaction, absorbed after healing (for absorbable types), does not carry infection.

### 6.6.1 Classification of sutures

| Absorbable (broken down in the body) | Non-absorbable (remain or are removed) |
| --- | --- |
| Surgical catgut (plain and chromic) | Silk |
| Reconstituted collagen | Linen, cotton thread |
| Polyglycolic acid (synthetic) | Nylon (polyamide) |
| Polyglactin 910 (synthetic) | Polyester, polypropylene |
| Polydioxanone (synthetic) | Stainless steel wire |

They can also be **monofilament** (single strand, e.g. nylon) or **multifilament** (braided or twisted, e.g. silk).

### 6.6.2 Surgical catgut

- **Definition:** A sterile, absorbable strand prepared from the **collagen** of the **submucosa** (connective tissue layer) of the small intestine of healthy sheep (*Ovis aries*), or of the serosa of cattle intestine. The name has nothing to do with cats.
- **Chemical nature:** Collagen (protein), absorbed in the body by enzymatic digestion.

**Preparation**

1. Intestines of freshly slaughtered healthy sheep are cleaned.
2. Mucous, muscular and serous layers are scraped off, leaving the tough submucosa.
3. The ribbons are treated with dilute alkali, split, and twisted into strands of the required thickness.
4. Strands are dried under tension, polished to uniform diameter and graded by size.
5. Sterilised (by gamma irradiation in modern practice) and sealed in sterile packs with a preserving fluid.

**Types**

| Type | Treatment | Absorption |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain catgut | Untreated | Faster (about one to two weeks for strength loss) |
| Chromic catgut | Treated with chromium salts (tanned) | Slower; less tissue reaction |

**Tests (as per pharmacopoeia):** sterility, tensile strength (minimum breaking load for the size), diameter, length, and limit of soluble chromium for chromic catgut.

**Uses:** Stitching internal tissues that heal quickly (mucosa, subcutaneous tissue), tying small vessels, where suture removal is not possible.

### 6.6.3 Ligatures and non-absorbable sutures

- **Silk** — braided, easy to handle and tie; used for skin and tying vessels; removed after healing.
- **Linen** (flax fibres) and **cotton thread**.
- **Nylon and polypropylene** — monofilament, low tissue reaction, used for skin and blood vessels.
- **Stainless steel wire** — for bone and abdominal wall closure.

### 6.6.4 Sterilisation and storage of sutures

- Supplied in individually sealed sterile packs; once opened, unused material should not be resterilised.
- Store away from heat and moisture; catgut is kept in a preserving fluid so it stays flexible.

## 6.7 Distinguishing the fibres — summary table

| Feature | Cotton | Silk | Wool | Viscose rayon |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Origin | Seed hair | Silkworm cocoon | Sheep hair | Regenerated cellulose |
| Chemistry | Cellulose | Fibroin protein | Keratin protein | Cellulose |
| Microscopy | Flat twisted ribbon | Smooth glassy thread | Scaly surface | Smooth striated rod |
| Burning smell | Burning paper | Burnt hair | Burnt hair | Burning paper |
| Iodine + H2SO4 | Blue | Yellow | Yellow | Blue |
| Sulphur test | Negative | Negative | Positive (black) | Negative |
| Dissolves in 5% KOH (boiling) | No | Yes | Yes | No |

## Remember

- Absorbent cotton = seed hairs of *Gossypium* (Malvaceae), **dewaxed** with alkali, bleached; almost pure cellulose; twisted ribbons under microscope.
- Silk = *Bombyx mori* cocoon; **fibroin + sericin**; no sulphur.
- Wool = sheep hair; **keratin** with sulphur (black with lead acetate + NaOH); scaly surface.
- Viscose rayon = regenerated cellulose made via cellulose xanthate.
- Suture stitches a wound; ligature ties a vessel.
- Catgut = collagen from **sheep intestine submucosa**; absorbable; plain or **chromic** (slower absorption).
- Non-absorbable: silk, linen, nylon, polyester, steel wire.
