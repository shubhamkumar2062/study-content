# Chapter 1: Definition, history, present status and scope of Pharmacognosy

Pharmacognosy is the oldest branch of pharmacy. Long before chemists could make a single tablet, healers used leaves, roots, barks, resins and animal products as medicine. This chapter explains what the subject means, how it grew, where it stands today and why a pharmacist still needs it.

## 1.1 Meaning of the word

The word comes from two Greek words:

| Greek word | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *pharmakon* | drug |
| *gnosis* | knowledge |

So **pharmacognosy = knowledge of drugs**, but in practice the subject is limited to drugs of **natural origin**.

**Who named it?**

- **J. A. Schmidt** used the term in his book on materia medica in **1811**.
- **C. A. Seydler**, a medical student, used it as the title of his work *Analectica Pharmacognostica* in **1815**.

## 1.2 Definition

**Pharmacognosy** is the study of crude drugs obtained from plants, animals and minerals, covering their identification, cultivation, collection, preparation, chemical constituents, evaluation, preservation and uses.

A shorter modern definition: *the study of the physical, chemical, biochemical and biological properties of drugs and potential drugs of natural origin, and the search for new drugs from natural sources.*

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Crude drug | A natural substance that has only been collected and dried, not processed further | Senna leaves, cinchona bark, acacia gum |
| Organised drug | Crude drug with a cellular structure (a plant or animal part) | Leaves, barks, roots, seeds |
| Unorganised drug | Crude drug without cells; a product secreted or extracted from a plant | Gums, resins, latex, oils, extracts |
| Active constituent | The chemical responsible for the drug's action | Quinine in cinchona, morphine in opium |
| Phytochemical | Any chemical made by a plant | Alkaloids, glycosides, tannins |

## 1.3 History of pharmacognosy

The history is best learnt in the order of civilisations.

### 1.3.1 Ancient India

- The **Rigveda** and **Atharvaveda** (earliest Indian texts) mention many medicinal plants. The Atharvaveda is the root of Ayurveda.
- **Charaka Samhita** (by Charaka) deals mainly with internal medicine and describes several hundred plant drugs.
- **Sushruta Samhita** (by Sushruta) deals mainly with surgery and also lists many drugs. Sushruta is called the father of Indian surgery.
- Plants such as turmeric, neem, tulsi, ginger, black pepper, rauwolfia (sarpagandha) and ashwagandha have been used in India for thousands of years.

### 1.3.2 China

- The legendary emperor **Shen Nung** is said to have tasted hundreds of herbs and recorded them in the *Pen Tsao* (herbal). *Ma Huang* (Ephedra) was one of the drugs described.

### 1.3.3 Egypt and Mesopotamia

- The **Ebers papyrus** (about 1550 BC) is an Egyptian medical scroll listing hundreds of formulas using drugs like aloe, castor oil, opium, myrrh and garlic.
- Clay tablets from Mesopotamia also list plant remedies.

### 1.3.4 Greece and Rome

| Person | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| **Hippocrates** (about 460–370 BC) | Father of medicine; described disease as a natural process and used many herbs |
| **Theophrastus** | Father of botany; described and classified plants |
| **Dioscorides** (1st century AD) | Wrote *De Materia Medica*, describing about 600 plants; used for more than 1,500 years |
| **Pliny the Elder** | Wrote *Historia Naturalis*, which included medicinal plants |
| **Galen** (2nd century AD) | Prepared medicines by mixing and extracting plant drugs; such preparations are still called **galenicals** (tinctures, extracts) |

### 1.3.5 The Arab period

- Arab physicians preserved Greek knowledge and added drugs such as senna, camphor and nutmeg.
- **Avicenna (Ibn Sina)** wrote the *Canon of Medicine*, a basis of the Unani system.

### 1.3.6 The age of isolation (19th century)

This is when pharmacognosy turned scientific. Chemists began isolating the pure active compounds from crude drugs:

| Compound | Source | Approximate time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Morphine | Opium | Early 1800s (Sertürner) |
| Quinine | Cinchona bark | 1820 (Pelletier and Caventou) |
| Atropine | Belladonna | 1830s |
| Cocaine | Coca leaves | 1860 |

### 1.3.7 The 20th century

- **Penicillin** was discovered by Alexander Fleming in **1928** from the mould *Penicillium notatum*, opening the era of antibiotics from microbes.
- **Reserpine** from rauwolfia (1950s) became an early antihypertensive.
- **Vincristine and vinblastine** from *Catharanthus roseus* became anticancer drugs.
- **Artemisinin** from *Artemisia annua* was found by Chinese scientists (Tu Youyou, Nobel Prize 2015) and is now central to malaria treatment.

```text
Timeline (learn the order, not exact years)

Vedas, Charaka, Sushruta  →  Ebers papyrus  →  Hippocrates, Dioscorides, Galen
        →  Avicenna (Arab)  →  Isolation of morphine, quinine (1800s)
        →  Penicillin (1928)  →  Reserpine, vinca alkaloids  →  Artemisinin, taxol
```

## 1.4 Present status of pharmacognosy

1. **Worldwide use of herbal medicine.** The World Health Organization estimates that a large majority of people in developing countries depend on traditional medicine for primary health care.
2. **Plant-derived drugs in modern medicine.** A large share of modern medicines are natural products, are made from them, or copy their structures.

| Drug | Natural source | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digoxin | *Digitalis lanata* | Heart failure |
| Morphine, codeine | Opium poppy | Pain, cough |
| Atropine | Belladonna | Antispasmodic, eye dilation |
| Quinine | Cinchona | Malaria |
| Reserpine | Rauwolfia | Hypertension |
| Vincristine | Catharanthus | Leukaemia |
| Paclitaxel (taxol) | Pacific yew (*Taxus brevifolia*) | Breast and ovarian cancer |
| Artemisinin | *Artemisia annua* | Malaria |
| Ephedrine | Ephedra | Asthma, nasal congestion |

3. **Herbal industry in India.** Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani and Homoeopathy are recognised by the Government of India through the **Ministry of AYUSH**. Many companies make herbal tablets, syrups, oils and cosmetics.
4. **Quality concerns.** Adulteration, pesticide residues, heavy metals and wrong identity of plants are real problems, so standardisation and quality control are major parts of present-day pharmacognosy.
5. **New tools.** Pharmacognosy now uses chromatography (TLC, HPTLC, HPLC), spectroscopy, DNA barcoding and tissue culture.
6. **Conservation.** Over-collection of wild plants (for example, *Podophyllum* and *Rauwolfia*) has made some species rare, so cultivation and conservation are encouraged.

## 1.5 Scope of pharmacognosy

The scope means the areas where knowledge of pharmacognosy is used.

1. **Identification of crude drugs** — by their shape, size, colour, odour, taste and microscopic features. A pharmacist in a hospital or drug store must recognise genuine drugs.
2. **Detection of adulteration** — finding out whether a drug is mixed with a cheaper or false material.
3. **Standardisation and quality control** — fixing limits for moisture, ash, extractive values and active constituents so that every batch gives the same effect.
4. **Source of new drugs** — screening plants, microbes and marine organisms for new medicines.
5. **Source of starting material** — some drugs are made by modifying a natural compound (semi-synthesis), e.g. diosgenin → steroid hormones, podophyllotoxin → etoposide.
6. **Pharmaceutical aids** — gums, waxes, oils and starches used as excipients (acacia, tragacanth, beeswax, lanolin).
7. **Herbal formulations, nutraceuticals and cosmetics** — growing industries that need trained people.
8. **Cultivation and plant biotechnology** — improving yield of active compounds by good agricultural practice, tissue culture and genetic methods.
9. **Traditional medicine** — scientific study of Ayurvedic, Unani and Siddha drugs.
10. **Export and economy** — India exports isabgol, senna, spices and essential oils.

## 1.6 Branches related to pharmacognosy

| Branch | What it studies |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmaceutical botany | Morphology and anatomy of medicinal plants |
| Phytochemistry | Chemistry of plant constituents |
| Ethnobotany | How tribal and local people use plants |
| Ethnopharmacology | Scientific study of traditional remedies |
| Plant taxonomy | Naming and classifying plants |
| Plant tissue culture | Growing plant cells and tissues in the laboratory |
| Marine pharmacognosy | Drugs from sea organisms |
| Zoopharmacognosy | How animals use plants to treat themselves |

## 1.7 Why a D.Pharm student should study it

- To dispense and sell herbal products safely.
- To advise patients on common home remedies and herbal supplements.
- To recognise adulterated or substandard crude drugs.
- To work in herbal, Ayurvedic, nutraceutical and cosmetic industries.

## Remember

- Pharmacognosy = *pharmakon* (drug) + *gnosis* (knowledge); term used by **Schmidt (1811)** and **Seydler (1815)**.
- Crude drugs are **organised** (cellular: leaves, barks, roots) or **unorganised** (non-cellular: gums, resins, oils, latex).
- Ebers papyrus (Egypt), Charaka and Sushruta Samhita (India), Dioscorides' *De Materia Medica* (Greece) are key historical records.
- Galen gave his name to **galenicals**; Hippocrates is the father of medicine; Theophrastus the father of botany.
- 19th century = isolation of pure compounds (morphine, quinine).
- Modern plant-derived drugs: digoxin, morphine, quinine, reserpine, vincristine, taxol, artemisinin.
- Scope: identification, adulteration, standardisation, new drugs, excipients, herbal products, conservation and export.
