# Chapter 10: Introduction to herbal formulations

A crude herb is rarely given as it is. It is turned into a **formulation** — a dosage form with a known amount of herb or extract, made in a way that is safe, stable and easy to take. This chapter explains the basic ideas.

## 10.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Herb / herbal material** | Whole plant or plant parts (leaves, roots, seeds…), fresh or dried, and products such as gums, oils and resins |
| **Herbal drug** | Plant material used for a medicinal purpose |
| **Herbal preparation** | Product obtained by processing herbal material: powders, extracts, tinctures, expressed juices, oils |
| **Herbal formulation (herbal medicine / herbal product)** | Finished, labelled dosage form containing one or more herbal preparations as active ingredients, with or without excipients |
| **Polyherbal formulation** | A formulation containing two or more herbs (common in Ayurveda, e.g. Triphala) |
| **Phytopharmaceutical** | A purified and standardised fraction of a plant extract with defined active markers, studied like a modern drug (a category under the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules in India) |

According to WHO, finished herbal products may also contain excipients; products to which chemically defined synthetic actives are added are **not** considered herbal.

## 10.2 Why formulate herbs?

1. Correct and uniform **dose** of the active principle.
2. **Stability** — protection from moisture, light, microbes and oxidation.
3. **Convenience and acceptability** — masking bitter or bad taste and odour.
4. Better **absorption** (bioavailability).
5. Easy packaging, transport and storage.
6. Commercial production with **quality control**.

## 10.3 Types of herbal formulations

### 10.3.1 Traditional (classical) dosage forms

Prepared as per classical texts (AFI, Unani and Siddha formularies):

| Form | Nature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Churna | Powder | Triphala churna |
| Gutika / Vati | Pill or tablet | Chandraprabha vati |
| Kwatha | Decoction | Dashmool kwatha |
| Swarasa | Fresh expressed juice | Tulsi swarasa |
| Asava / Arishta | Self-fermented liquid | Dashmularishta |
| Avaleha / Lehya | Semi-solid jam | Chyawanprash |
| Taila / Ghrita | Medicated oil / ghee | Mahanarayan taila, Brahmi ghrita |
| Bhasma | Calcined mineral | Shankha bhasma |

### 10.3.2 Modern (conventional) dosage forms

| Group | Dosage forms | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | Tablets, capsules, granules, powders, lozenges | Ashwagandha capsules, senna tablets, isabgol granules |
| Liquid | Syrups, tinctures, fluid extracts, elixirs, drops, mouthwashes | Vasaka syrup, tincture of ginger, compound benzoin tincture |
| Semi-solid | Ointments, creams, gels, pastes | Neem cream, aloe gel, turmeric cream |
| Others | Inhalations, suppositories, oils | Eucalyptus inhalation |

### 10.3.3 Novel herbal drug delivery systems

Many plant actives (curcumin, flavonoids, silymarin) are poorly absorbed. New carriers are used to improve absorption and stability:

| System | Idea |
| --- | --- |
| **Phytosomes** | Herbal extract bound to phospholipid (phosphatidylcholine) to cross membranes better |
| Liposomes | Tiny lipid vesicles that carry the active |
| Nanoparticles, nanoemulsions | Very small particles to increase absorption |
| Microspheres | Small spheres for controlled release |
| Transdermal patches | Drug through skin |

## 10.4 Extracts used in herbal formulations

### 10.4.1 Methods of extraction

| Method | Brief procedure | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Infusion** | Soak the drug in hot or cold water for a short time, strain | Soft drugs with water-soluble actives (leaves, flowers) |
| **Decoction** | Boil the drug in water, strain | Hard, woody drugs that are heat-stable (roots, barks) |
| **Maceration** | Soak the drug in solvent in a closed vessel for days with occasional shaking, strain and press | Tinctures, unorganised drugs |
| **Percolation** | Solvent passed slowly through a column of packed drug in a percolator | Complete extraction for fluid extracts |
| **Digestion** | Maceration with gentle heat | When moderate heat helps |
| **Continuous hot extraction (Soxhlet)** | Same solvent is boiled, condensed and passed through the drug repeatedly | Laboratory extraction, fats and oils |
| Modern methods | Ultrasound-assisted, microwave-assisted, supercritical CO2 extraction | Faster, cleaner extraction |

### 10.4.2 Types of extracts

| Extract | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Tincture** | Alcoholic or hydro-alcoholic liquid; usually 1 part drug in 5 or 10 parts liquid |
| **Fluid extract** | Liquid where 1 ml corresponds to 1 g of drug |
| **Soft extract** | Semi-solid, sticky |
| **Dry extract** | Solvent removed completely; powder |
| **Standardised extract** | Adjusted to contain a fixed amount of a marker or active (e.g. senna extract with a stated sennoside content) |

## 10.5 Steps in developing a herbal formulation

```text
1. Selection of herb (traditional use, literature, scientific evidence)
            ↓
2. Collection and authentication of raw material (botanical identity, voucher specimen)
            ↓
3. Quality testing of raw material (foreign matter, ash, extractive, moisture, markers)
            ↓
4. Processing: drying, size reduction, extraction, concentration
            ↓
5. Standardisation of extract (marker compound by TLC / HPTLC / HPLC)
            ↓
6. Formulation with suitable excipients (tablet, capsule, syrup, cream…)
            ↓
7. Quality control of finished product
            ↓
8. Stability studies (shelf life)
            ↓
9. Safety and efficacy evaluation (toxicity, pharmacological and clinical studies)
            ↓
10. Packaging, labelling and regulatory approval
```

## 10.6 Quality control of herbal formulations

| Parameter | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Identity (organoleptic, microscopy, TLC fingerprint) | Confirms the right herb is present |
| Physicochemical tests (pH, viscosity, ash, extractive values, moisture, disintegration) | Product consistency |
| Assay of marker compounds | Strength (potency) |
| **Heavy metals** (lead, mercury, arsenic, cadmium) | Safety |
| **Pesticide residues** | Safety |
| **Microbial load** (total count, absence of pathogens), **aflatoxins** | Safety |
| Stability testing | Shelf life |

**Challenges in standardising herbal products**

1. Herbs contain many compounds; the active one is often unknown.
2. Constituents vary with climate, soil, season, harvesting time, drying and storage.
3. Adulteration and substitution of raw material.
4. Polyherbal products are hard to analyse.
5. Lack of reference standards for many markers.

## 10.7 Regulatory aspects in India

- **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940** and Rules, 1945 cover Ayurvedic, Siddha, Unani and Homoeopathic drugs (Chapter IV-A of the Act for ASU drugs).
- **Schedule T** — Good Manufacturing Practices for Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani medicines.
- **Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India** and the formularies (AFI, NFUM) give standards.
- Herbal food supplements and nutraceuticals come under **FSSAI**.
- The **Ministry of AYUSH** oversees traditional medicine; licences are issued by state licensing authorities.
- WHO guidelines on quality, safety and good manufacturing of herbal medicines are followed internationally.

## 10.8 Advantages and limitations of herbal formulations

| Advantages | Limitations |
| --- | --- |
| Long history of traditional use | Variable composition between batches |
| Often well tolerated | Slow onset for some products |
| Useful in chronic conditions and as supportive care | Limited clinical evidence for many products |
| Widely available and accepted | Risk of adulteration and contamination |
| Many act on several targets (polyherbal) | **Herb–drug interactions** |

**Examples of herb–drug interactions (good to know)**

- Garlic, ginkgo and ginger may increase bleeding with warfarin or aspirin.
- St John's wort reduces blood levels of many drugs by inducing liver enzymes.
- Isabgol taken with other medicines may reduce their absorption — keep a time gap.

## 10.9 Role of the pharmacist

- Dispense only licensed products from reliable makers.
- Check labels: name of herb, part used, quantity per dose, batch, expiry, licence number.
- Ask patients about herbal products they take along with prescribed medicines.
- Explain correct dose, storage and possible side effects.

## Remember

- Herbal formulation = finished dosage form containing herbal preparations as active ingredients.
- Forms: **traditional** (churna, vati, asava, lehya, taila, bhasma), **modern** (tablets, capsules, syrups, creams), **novel** (phytosomes, liposomes, nanoparticles).
- Extraction: infusion, decoction, maceration, percolation, digestion, Soxhlet; modern — ultrasound, microwave, supercritical CO2.
- Tincture 1 : 5 or 1 : 10; fluid extract 1 ml = 1 g drug.
- Steps: authentication → standardisation → formulation → QC → stability → safety → labelling.
- QC must check identity, markers, heavy metals, pesticides, microbes and aflatoxins.
- Law: Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940; **Schedule T** = GMP for ASU drugs; supplements under FSSAI.
