# Transverse section of barks: Cinnamon and Cinchona

**Aim:** To prepare and study the transverse sections of cinnamon bark and cinchona bark and to compare their diagnostic characters.

> Follow your lab manual for the staining schedule. Handle razor blades with care.

## Principle

Barks consist of tissues outside the cambium: cork, cortex, pericycle and secondary phloem with medullary rays. The type and arrangement of **stone cells, fibres, crystals, oil cells and starch** differ between barks. Phloroglucinol + HCl stains lignified stone cells and fibres pink-red; iodine stains starch blue.

## Requirements

Pieces of cinnamon and cinchona bark soaked in water (or boiled briefly), potato block, razor blade, chloral hydrate, phloroglucinol (1% alcoholic), conc. HCl, iodine solution, dilute glycerine, slides, cover slips, microscope.

## Procedure

1. Soften the bark pieces by soaking or boiling.
2. Cut thin transverse sections across the bark (at right angles to its length).
3. Mount one section in iodine to see starch.
4. Clear another section in chloral hydrate, stain with phloroglucinol + conc. HCl and mount in dilute glycerine.
5. Observe under low and high power; draw labelled diagrams.

## Observations

| Tissue (outside → inside) | Cinnamon bark | Cinchona bark |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cork | Absent (outer bark is scraped off in preparation) | Present: several layers of thin-walled, reddish-brown cork cells |
| Cortex | Mostly removed | Parenchyma with starch, **idioblasts filled with microcrystals (sandy crystals)** of calcium oxalate; some secretion cells |
| Pericycle | A nearly **continuous ring of stone cells (sclereids)** with groups of **pericyclic fibres** — very characteristic | No continuous ring of stone cells |
| Secondary phloem | Sieve tissue, phloem parenchyma, **isolated lignified phloem fibres**, many **oil cells** and **mucilage cells**, starch grains | Many **large, isolated, spindle-shaped phloem fibres** with very thick, striated, lignified walls and funnel-shaped pits; phloem parenchyma with starch and microcrystals |
| Medullary rays | 1–2 cells wide, cells contain small **acicular crystals** of calcium oxalate | 1–3 cells wide, widening outwards |
| Stone cells | Present (pericycle ring) | Absent or very few |
| With phloroglucinol + HCl | Stone-cell ring and fibres pink | Phloem fibres pink |

## Result

The section with a continuous pericyclic ring of stone cells, oil and mucilage cells and acicular crystals in narrow rays is **cinnamon bark**. The section with cork, idioblasts of microcrystals, large isolated thick-walled phloem fibres and no stone cells is **cinchona bark**.

## Precautions

1. Cut sections exactly across the bark.
2. Sections should be thin; thick ones hide the stone cells.
3. Use iodine on a separate, uncleared section for starch.
4. Wipe off excess acid after staining.

## Viva questions

1. **What is the most diagnostic feature of cinnamon bark?** A continuous ring of stone cells in the pericycle.
2. **Why is cork absent in cinnamon of commerce?** The outer bark is scraped off before drying.
3. **What crystals are present in cinnamon bark?** Small acicular (needle) crystals of calcium oxalate in the medullary rays.
4. **Describe the phloem fibres of cinchona.** Large, spindle-shaped, isolated, very thick-walled and lignified, with striations and funnel-shaped pits.
5. **What are idioblasts?** Single cells different from their neighbours, here filled with microcrystals of calcium oxalate.
6. **How do you tell cassia bark from cinnamon?** Cassia is thicker, has cork, a broken (not continuous) stone-cell layer and more starch.
7. **Name the antimalarial alkaloid of cinchona.** Quinine.
8. **What is the use of phloroglucinol in this experiment?** It stains lignified stone cells and fibres pink-red.
