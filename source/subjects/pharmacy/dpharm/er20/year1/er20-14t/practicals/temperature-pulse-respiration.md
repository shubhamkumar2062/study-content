# Recording of body temperature, pulse rate and respiratory rate

**Aim:** To record body temperature with mercury, digital and infrared thermometers at different sites, the pulse/heart rate at different sites before and after exertion, and the respiratory rate of a human subject.

## Principle

Temperature, pulse, respiration and BP are the **vital signs**. Body temperature is kept near 37 °C by the hypothalamus; it varies slightly with the site of measurement. The **pulse** is the rhythmic expansion of an artery with each heartbeat, so the pulse rate normally equals the heart rate. Respiratory rate is the number of breaths per minute.

## Requirements

Clinical mercury thermometer (handle carefully – mercury is toxic; many labs now use digital ones), digital thermometer, infrared (forehead/ear) thermometer, spirit swabs, watch with a seconds hand, stethoscope.

## Procedure A: Body temperature

1. **Mercury (oral):** clean the bulb with spirit, shake the mercury below 35 °C, place under the tongue with lips closed for 2–3 minutes, read at eye level.
2. **Axillary:** dry the armpit, keep the bulb in the axilla with the arm pressed to the side for about 5 minutes (mercury) or until the digital beep.
3. **Digital:** switch on, place orally or in the axilla, read after the beep.
4. **Infrared:** aim at the forehead (or in the ear canal for a tympanic model) as per instructions; read instantly.
5. The subject should not eat, drink hot/cold liquids or smoke for 15 minutes before an oral reading.

## Procedure B: Pulse rate

1. Seat the subject relaxed. Place the tips of index, middle and ring fingers on the **radial artery** at the wrist (thumb side).
2. Count for a full minute; note rate, rhythm (regular/irregular) and volume.
3. Repeat at other sites: carotid (neck – press one side only, gently), brachial (elbow), femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, dorsalis pedis.
4. Compare with the **apex beat** (heart rate) counted with a stethoscope at the 5th left intercostal space.
5. Ask the subject to exercise (spot jogging 2 minutes); count the pulse immediately and every minute until it returns to resting.

## Procedure C: Respiratory rate

1. Without telling the subject (breathing changes when noticed), watch or feel the rise and fall of the chest/abdomen while appearing to count the pulse.
2. Count for one full minute; note rhythm and depth.

## Normal values (adult, approx.)

- Oral temperature about 37 °C (98.6 °F), range about 36.5–37.5 °C; axillary reading is usually about 0.5 °C lower and rectal about 0.5 °C higher than oral. Fever = above about 37.5–38 °C.
- Pulse 60–100/min (average 72); above 100 = tachycardia, below 60 = bradycardia (normal in trained athletes).
- Respiratory rate 12–20/min.
- After exercise, pulse and respiration rise and return to resting levels within a few minutes in a fit person.

## Precautions

1. Clean thermometers between users; handle mercury thermometers carefully (spill = toxic).
2. Do not use the thumb to feel the pulse (it has its own pulse).
3. Do not press both carotids together.
4. Count respiration without the subject noticing.

## Viva questions

1. **Which part of the brain controls body temperature?** Hypothalamus.
2. **Why is the thumb not used to feel the pulse?** Its own pulse can be confused with the subject's.
3. **What is pulse deficit?** Difference between heart rate (apex) and pulse rate, as in atrial fibrillation.
4. **Why does the pulse rate rise after exercise?** Sympathetic activity and adrenaline raise the heart rate to supply more O2 to muscles.
5. **Normal respiratory rate in adults?** 12–20 breaths per minute.
