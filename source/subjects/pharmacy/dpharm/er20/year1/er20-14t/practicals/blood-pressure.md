# Recording of blood pressure in different postures, arms and after exertion

**Aim:** To record the arterial blood pressure of a human subject by the auscultatory method in different postures, in both arms, and before and after exercise, and to interpret the results.

## Principle

A cuff on the arm is inflated to stop flow in the brachial artery and then slowly deflated. When cuff pressure falls just below systolic pressure, blood spurts through the narrowed artery and makes tapping sounds (**Korotkoff sounds**) heard by a stethoscope.

- **Systolic pressure** = pressure at the first clear tapping sound (phase 1).
- **Diastolic pressure** = pressure when the sounds disappear (phase 5).

## Requirements

Sphygmomanometer (mercury, aneroid or digital) with a cuff of correct size, stethoscope, couch, stopwatch.

## Procedure

1. Let the subject rest for 5 minutes. Seat them with the arm supported at heart level, palm up.
2. Wrap the deflated cuff snugly round the upper arm, its lower edge 2–3 cm above the elbow crease.
3. **Palpatory method first:** feel the radial pulse, inflate until it disappears, note this value (approximate systolic), deflate.
4. **Auscultatory method:** place the stethoscope over the brachial artery at the elbow. Inflate to about 30 mmHg above the palpatory value.
5. Deflate slowly (2–3 mmHg per second). Note the first tapping sound (systolic) and the point where sounds disappear (diastolic).
6. Deflate fully; wait 1–2 minutes before repeating.
7. Repeat in: (a) **lying** (supine), (b) **sitting**, (c) **standing** (record immediately and after 2 minutes); (d) the **other arm**; (e) **immediately after exercise** (e.g. 2 minutes of spot jogging or stair climbing) and again after 5 minutes rest.

**Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic; mean arterial pressure ≈ diastolic + ⅓ pulse pressure.**
Example: 120/80 mmHg → pulse pressure 40, MAP ≈ 93 mmHg.

## Interpretation

- Normal adult: about 120/80 mmHg; persistent readings of 140/90 mmHg or more suggest hypertension.
- **Standing:** blood pools in the legs; baroreceptor reflex keeps BP nearly steady (diastolic may rise slightly). A fall of 20 mmHg systolic or more on standing = **orthostatic (postural) hypotension**.
- **Two arms:** a small difference (usually under about 10 mmHg) is normal.
- **After exercise:** systolic rises (higher cardiac output) while diastolic changes little or falls (vasodilation in muscles); values return to normal within a few minutes of rest.

## Precautions

1. Correct cuff size; cuff at heart level.
2. Do not deflate too fast; avoid prolonged inflation (pain, error).
3. Subject should be relaxed, not talking, no smoking or caffeine just before.

## Viva questions

1. **What are Korotkoff sounds?** Sounds from turbulent flow in a partly compressed artery, heard during deflation of the cuff.
2. **Why is the palpatory method done first?** To avoid missing an auscultatory gap and to know how high to inflate.
3. **What is pulse pressure?** Systolic minus diastolic pressure, normally about 40 mmHg.
4. **Why does systolic BP rise after exercise?** Heart rate and stroke volume increase.
5. **What is postural hypotension?** A fall in BP on standing, causing dizziness.
