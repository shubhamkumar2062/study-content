# Unit 3: Coarse dispersions (suspensions and emulsions)

Coarse dispersions have particles larger than about 0.5 µm. The two main pharmaceutical types are **suspensions** (solid in liquid) and **emulsions** (liquid in liquid). Both are thermodynamically unstable: given time, suspended particles settle and cake, and emulsion droplets join and separate. The formulator's job is to slow these changes enough for the product to stay uniform and acceptable for its whole shelf life.

## 3.1 Suspensions: definition and desirable properties

A **pharmaceutical suspension** is a coarse dispersion of insoluble solid particles (usually 0.5 to about 10 µm, though some are larger) in a liquid, usually water.

**Why suspensions are made**

- The drug is insoluble or too unstable in solution (suspensions of insoluble esters, antacids).
- To mask a bitter taste (the drug is not dissolved, so it tastes less).
- For depot or prolonged action (intramuscular suspensions).
- For local action (calamine lotion, antacid suspensions coating the stomach).
- For children and the elderly who cannot swallow solids.

**A good suspension should**

1. Settle slowly and **redisperse easily** on gentle shaking; it must **never cake**.
2. Pour readily from the bottle and give a uniform dose.
3. Have particles that stay the same size on storage (no crystal growth).
4. Be pleasant to look at, smell and taste, and be free from microbial growth.
5. For topical use, spread easily and not be sticky; for injection, pass easily through the needle.

## 3.2 Interfacial properties of suspended particles

Reducing a solid to fine particles creates a large new solid–liquid interface. The increase in surface free energy is:

ΔG = γSL × ΔA

where γSL = interfacial tension between solid and liquid and ΔA = increase in surface area. Because systems tend toward lower free energy, fine particles tend to **aggregate** to reduce their total surface. ΔG can be reduced by lowering γSL with a **wetting agent** (surfactant), which also helps the liquid displace air from the particle surface.

### Wetting

Hydrophobic powders (sulfur, charcoal, magnesium stearate, many drugs) float on water, trapping air, and form clumps. The **contact angle** tells how well a liquid wets a solid: 0° = complete wetting, above 90° = poor wetting.

**Wetting agents:** surfactants with HLB about 7–9 (polysorbates, sodium lauryl sulfate in small amounts), hydrophilic colloids such as acacia, bentonite and tragacanth that coat the particles, and solvents such as glycerin, alcohol and propylene glycol, which are worked into the powder before water is added.

### Surface charge and zeta potential

Suspended particles carry a charge (from ionisation of surface groups or adsorption of ions). The surrounding double layer gives a **zeta potential**. Particles with a high zeta potential repel each other strongly; with a low zeta potential they come together.

### Potential energy of approach (DLVO picture)

```text
 Potential   ^
 energy      |        ___  repulsion barrier (from the double layer)
 between two |      /    \
 particles   |     /      \
             |    /        \__ ______  secondary minimum (weak,
             |---/------------\-/------  reversible attraction ->
             |  /              V         loose FLOCCULATION)
             | /   distance between particles  ->
             |/
             V  primary minimum (deep -> close, irreversible contact -> CAKING)
```

If particles are pushed over the barrier into the **primary minimum**, they bind closely and irreversibly: this is **caking**. Particles that associate loosely in the **secondary minimum**, at a distance, form **flocs** that are easily broken by shaking: this is **controlled flocculation**.

## 3.3 Flocculated and deflocculated suspensions

| Property | Deflocculated | Flocculated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Particles | Exist as separate entities | Form loose aggregates (flocs) |
| Rate of settling | Slow, as each particle settles separately and is small | Fast, as flocs are large |
| Sediment | Formed slowly; closely packed; **caking** (hard sediment) | Formed rapidly; loose, high volume; **no caking** |
| Redispersion | Difficult; the cake may not redisperse | Easy on gentle shaking |
| Supernatant | Stays cloudy for a long time | Clears quickly; clear boundary seen |
| Appearance | Elegant, uniform for a long time | Less elegant (clear liquid above sediment) |
| Zeta potential | High | Low (but controlled) |

Neither extreme is ideal. The usual aim is **controlled flocculation**: flocs that settle into a loose, large-volume sediment that does not cake, with a vehicle viscous enough to slow the settling.

## 3.4 Settling in suspensions

### Stokes's law

The velocity of sedimentation of a spherical particle in a dilute suspension is:

v = d²(ρs − ρ0)g / 18η

where d = particle diameter, ρs = density of particle, ρ0 = density of medium, g = acceleration due to gravity (981 cm/s²), η = viscosity of medium (poise).

**Worked example.** A particle of diameter 10 µm (10⁻³ cm) and density 1.3 g/cm³ settles in water (ρ0 = 1.0 g/cm³, η = 0.01 poise):
v = (10⁻³)² × (1.3 − 1.0) × 981 / (18 × 0.01) = (10⁻⁶ × 0.3 × 981)/0.18 = **1.64 × 10⁻³ cm/s**, about 5.9 cm per hour.

**Ways to slow settling (from Stokes's law)**

| Factor in the equation | Practical step |
| --- | --- |
| Diameter d (squared) | Reduce particle size by milling; halving d cuts v to one quarter |
| Density difference (ρs − ρ0) | Raise vehicle density with sorbitol, sucrose, glycerin |
| Viscosity η | Add suspending agents (methylcellulose, tragacanth, xanthan, bentonite) |

**Limitations of Stokes's law:** it assumes spheres of uniform size settling freely in a dilute suspension (below about 2% solids) without colliding, and no Brownian motion. In concentrated suspensions particles interfere (**hindered settling**), and the Higuchi modification v′ = vεⁿ is used, where ε is the initial porosity and n is a constant. Very small particles (below about 2–5 µm) show Brownian motion that opposes settling.

### Sedimentation parameters

**Sedimentation volume (F):** the ratio of the final (equilibrium) volume of the sediment, Vu, to the original volume of the suspension, V0:

F = Vu / V0 (or Hu/H0 when heights are measured in a uniform cylinder)

F usually lies between 0 and 1. F = 1 means the sediment fills the whole volume (no clear liquid above): the ideal, called **flocculation equilibrium**. F can exceed 1 when the loose floc network occupies more volume than the original suspension. A larger F means a better suspension.

**Degree of flocculation (β):** the ratio of the sedimentation volume of the flocculated suspension (F) to that of the same suspension when completely deflocculated (F∞):

β = F / F∞ = Vu / V∞

β measures how much the sediment volume is increased by flocculation. **Worked example:** 50 ml of a flocculated suspension settles to 30 ml of sediment (F = 0.60); the deflocculated suspension settles to 5 ml (F∞ = 0.10). β = 0.60/0.10 = **6**.

## 3.5 Formulation of suspensions

### Approaches

```text
 Wetted, dispersed particles
        |
   +----+-------------------------+-------------------------------+
   |                              |                               |
 Structured vehicle          Controlled flocculation        Flocculation in a
 (deflocculated particles    (electrolyte, surfactant       structured vehicle
  held in a viscous           or polymer -> loose flocs)    (best of both)
  pseudoplastic/plastic       -> no caking, but settles     -> flocs held up by
  vehicle)                    fast                          the vehicle
 -> may still cake slowly
```

1. **Structured vehicle with deflocculated particles:** the particles are dispersed in a viscous vehicle (usually pseudoplastic or plastic and preferably thixotropic) that slows settling. Risk: deflocculated particles still settle eventually and form a cake.
2. **Controlled flocculation:** flocculating agents make loose flocs that give a non-caking sediment.
3. **Flocculation in a structured vehicle:** controlled flocs are suspended in a viscous vehicle, so they settle slowly and still redisperse easily. This is usually the best approach.

### Flocculating agents

- **Electrolytes:** reduce the zeta potential and bridge particles. Trivalent ions are strongest (Schulze–Hardy rule). Example: negatively charged sulfamerazine particles flocculated by aluminium chloride; positively charged bismuth subnitrate flocculated by monobasic potassium phosphate.
- **Surfactants:** ionic surfactants neutralise particle charge; nonionic surfactants adsorb and form bridges.
- **Polymers:** long chains adsorb onto several particles at once and bridge them (xanthan gum, starch, alginates, cellulose derivatives).

**Effect of adding electrolyte (sulfamerazine–AlCl3 type plot)**

```text
 zeta potential ^                          Caking
 (mV)        +  |                         /   (zeta again high, positive)
                |                   ____/
              0 |-------------[ no caking zone: flocculated ]----------
                |       ___/
             -  |  ____/   caking (zeta high, negative; deflocculated)
                +-------------------------------------------> concentration
                                                              of AlCl3
```

At low concentration the particles stay negative and deflocculated (they cake). As Al³⁺ is added, the zeta potential falls toward zero and the particles flocculate: sediment volume is high and there is no caking. With excess Al³⁺ the charge reverses, the particles deflocculate again and caking returns. The formulator chooses the concentration in the middle zone.

### Typical formulation components

| Component | Examples | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wetting agents | Polysorbate 80, glycerin, alcohol | Displace air, wet hydrophobic powders |
| Suspending agents | Acacia, tragacanth, methylcellulose, sodium CMC, xanthan gum, bentonite, Veegum (magnesium aluminium silicate), carbomers | Raise viscosity, give a structured vehicle |
| Flocculating agents | Electrolytes, surfactants, polymers | Controlled flocculation |
| Buffers | Phosphate, citrate | pH for stability |
| Preservatives | Parabens, sodium benzoate | Prevent microbial growth |
| Others | Sweeteners, flavours, colours, antioxidants | Acceptability and stability |

**Physical stability problems:** caking; crystal growth by **Ostwald ripening** (small particles dissolve and redeposit on larger ones, worse with temperature cycling); polymorphic change to a less soluble form. **Evaluation:** sedimentation volume, degree of flocculation, ease of redispersion (number of inversions), particle size on storage, zeta potential and rheology.

## 3.6 Emulsions

An **emulsion** is a thermodynamically unstable system of at least two immiscible liquids, one dispersed as globules (the **dispersed or internal phase**) in the other (the **continuous or external phase**), stabilised by an **emulsifying agent**. Globule size is usually 0.1 to 10 µm or more.

### Types

| Type | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oil-in-water (o/w) | Oil droplets in water | Oral emulsions (liquid paraffin), intravenous fat emulsions, vanishing creams, most lotions |
| Water-in-oil (w/o) | Water droplets in oil | Cold creams, many ointments and barrier creams |
| Multiple (w/o/w, o/w/o) | Droplets that themselves contain smaller droplets | Prolonged release, taste masking, vaccine adjuvants |
| Microemulsion | Transparent, thermodynamically stable | Oral cyclosporin systems, some topical products |

### Tests to identify the type

| Test | o/w result | w/o result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dilution | Mixes with water | Mixes with oil |
| Dye (water-soluble, e.g. amaranth) | Continuous phase coloured | Only droplets coloured |
| Dye (oil-soluble, e.g. Sudan III) | Only droplets coloured | Continuous phase coloured |
| Electrical conductivity | Conducts (bulb glows) | Does not conduct |
| Cobalt chloride filter paper | Paper turns pink | No change |
| Fluorescence (oils fluoresce in UV) | Spotty fluorescence | Whole field fluoresces |

### Emulsifying agents

1. **Synthetic surfactants:** anionic (sodium lauryl sulfate, soaps), cationic (cetrimide; also antiseptic), nonionic (sorbitan esters [Spans], polysorbates [Tweens], polyoxyethylene ethers). Nonionics are the most widely used because they are compatible and less toxic.
2. **Natural (hydrophilic colloids):** acacia, tragacanth, gelatin, lecithin, wool fat, cholesterol, alginates.
3. **Finely divided solids:** bentonite, Veegum, aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, colloidal silica.
4. **Auxiliary emulsifiers:** cetyl alcohol, stearyl alcohol, glyceryl monostearate, which add consistency and stabilise the film.

## 3.7 Theories of emulsification

### Interfacial tension theory

Emulsifying agents lower the interfacial tension between oil and water, so less work (W = γΔA) is needed to break the oil into droplets, and the tendency of droplets to coalesce falls. Lowering tension alone, however, does not explain why emulsions stay stable for long periods.

### Oriented wedge theory

Emulsifying agents arrange themselves at the interface according to their solubility, with the bulkier part in the phase that suits it, like a wedge. Sodium soaps (one hydrocarbon chain, large hydrated head) form o/w emulsions; calcium and magnesium soaps (two chains, bulky non-polar part) form w/o emulsions. This theory is now regarded mainly as a historical explanation, but it agrees with **Bancroft's rule**: the phase in which the emulsifier is more soluble becomes the **continuous phase**.

### Monomolecular adsorption theory

Surfactants adsorb at the oil–water interface as a **monomolecular film**. The film lowers interfacial tension and forms a coherent, flexible barrier around each droplet. Ionic surfactants also give the droplets a charge, so they repel each other. A **combination** of a hydrophilic and a lipophilic surfactant (for example, polysorbate 80 with sorbitan oleate, or sodium cetyl sulfate with cholesterol) packs more tightly at the interface and forms a **stronger complex film** than either agent alone.

### Multimolecular adsorption theory

**Hydrophilic colloids** (acacia, gelatin) form a strong, elastic **multimolecular film** around the droplets. They lower interfacial tension only slightly; their protection comes from the mechanical strength of the film and from raising the viscosity of the continuous phase. Some are charged (acacia negative), which adds repulsion. They nearly always give o/w emulsions.

### Solid particle adsorption theory

Finely divided solids collect at the interface and form a dense layer of particles around the droplets that prevents them from touching. The particles must be wetted by both phases. If they are wetted **more by water** (bentonite, aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide) they form o/w emulsions; if wetted **more by oil** (carbon black, talc) they form w/o emulsions.

```text
 Monomolecular film      Multimolecular film       Solid particle film
   ~~o~~o~~o~~             //////////////             o o o o o o
  o  oil drop  o          //  oil drop  //           o  oil drop  o
   ~~o~~o~~o~~             //////////////             o o o o o o
 (surfactant layer)      (thick acacia layer)       (bentonite particles)
```

## 3.8 Microemulsions and multiple emulsions

### Microemulsions

Microemulsions are **clear, isotropic and thermodynamically stable** dispersions of oil and water stabilised by a surfactant, usually with a **cosurfactant** (a short- or medium-chain alcohol such as butanol, or a glycol ether). Droplets are very small, roughly 10–100 nm, so they do not scatter much light and look transparent. They form **spontaneously** (with little stirring) because the interfacial tension becomes extremely low.

| Property | Macroemulsion | Microemulsion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Milky, opaque | Clear or slightly bluish |
| Droplet size | About 0.1–10 µm or more | About 10–100 nm |
| Stability | Thermodynamically unstable; separates in time | Thermodynamically stable |
| Formation | Needs energy (stirring, homogenisation) | Forms spontaneously |
| Surfactant needed | Relatively small amount | Large amount, plus cosurfactant |

Types: o/w, w/o and **bicontinuous** (interlinked channels of oil and water). Uses: improve solubility and oral absorption of poorly soluble drugs (lipid-based systems), topical and transdermal delivery, parenteral delivery. Self-emulsifying systems (oil + surfactant mixtures that form fine emulsions in the gut) work on related principles.

### Multiple emulsions

In a **w/o/w** emulsion, water droplets are enclosed in oil globules, which are themselves dispersed in water. An **o/w/o** emulsion is the reverse. They are usually made in two steps: first a primary w/o emulsion with a lipophilic (low HLB) emulsifier, then this is dispersed in water containing a hydrophilic (high HLB) emulsifier. Uses: prolonged release (a drug in the inner phase must cross the oil layer), taste masking, protection of sensitive drugs, vaccine adjuvants and drug overdose treatment research. Their main problem is instability (the inner droplets leak or join the outer phase).

## 3.9 Stability of emulsions

```text
   Emulsion  --creaming-->  cream layer at top (o/w) or bottom
      |                    (reversible: shake to redisperse)
      |--flocculation--> clumps of droplets that still keep their film
      |                    (reversible, but leads to faster creaming)
      |--coalescence---> droplets merge, film destroyed
      |                    --> BREAKING / cracking: oil separates (irreversible)
      |--phase inversion--> o/w becomes w/o or the reverse
```

| Instability | What happens | Reversible? | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Creaming (upward) / sedimentation (downward) | Droplets concentrate in one layer due to density difference | Yes, by shaking | Smaller droplets (homogenise), raise viscosity of continuous phase, reduce density difference (Stokes's law) |
| Flocculation | Droplets clump loosely | Yes | Adequate charge and emulsifier film |
| Coalescence and breaking (cracking) | Film ruptures; droplets fuse; phases separate | **No** | Strong interfacial film; correct HLB; avoid incompatible additives, freezing, heat |
| Phase inversion | Type changes, o/w ↔ w/o | Depends | Keep phase volume ratio sensible; avoid ions that change the emulsifier (calcium with sodium soaps); avoid temperature near the phase inversion temperature of nonionics |
| Ostwald ripening | Small droplets dissolve into large ones | No | Use oil of very low water solubility |

**Phase volume:** for uniform rigid spheres the theoretical maximum packing is about 74% of the volume, so an emulsion with more than about 74% internal phase tends to invert or crack (real emulsions vary because droplets are not uniform). Causes of breaking include addition of an opposite-type emulsifier, electrolytes, alcohol (dehydrates hydrocolloids), temperature extremes, freezing and microbial growth.

## 3.10 Preservation of emulsions

Emulsions contain water, carbohydrates, proteins and natural gums, so they easily support microbial growth and oxidation.

- **Preservatives** must be in the **aqueous phase**, where microbes grow. Problems: preservatives partition into the oil phase (lowering their aqueous concentration); some are bound by nonionic surfactants inside micelles (parabens with polysorbates), so a higher total amount is needed. Common preservatives: methyl and propyl parabens (often together), benzoic acid and sodium benzoate, sorbic acid, chlorocresol, benzalkonium chloride (external use), phenoxyethanol.
- **Antioxidants** protect unsaturated oils and drugs from rancidity: oil-soluble (butylated hydroxyanisole [BHA], butylated hydroxytoluene [BHT], propyl gallate, tocopherols) and water-soluble (ascorbic acid, sodium metabisulfite), often with chelating agents (disodium edetate, citric acid).
- **Packing and storage:** well-closed containers, protection from light and heat.

## 3.11 Rheological properties of emulsions

- **Dilute emulsions** (low phase volume) are usually Newtonian.
- **Concentrated emulsions** are pseudoplastic or plastic, and creams may be thixotropic.

**Factors affecting emulsion viscosity**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Phase volume ratio | Viscosity rises sharply as the internal phase increases; near close packing it rises steeply |
| Viscosity of continuous phase | The main determinant; thickening the external phase thickens the emulsion |
| Droplet size and distribution | Smaller, more uniform droplets give higher viscosity |
| Emulsifier type and concentration | More emulsifier and auxiliary emulsifiers (cetyl alcohol) raise consistency |
| Flocculation | Flocculated droplets give higher viscosity and plastic flow |

Rheology matters for pourability of oral emulsions, spreading of creams, and slowing creaming.

## 3.12 Emulsion formulation by the HLB method

**HLB (hydrophile–lipophile balance)** is a number that expresses the balance between the water-loving and oil-loving parts of a surfactant. Griffin's scale runs roughly from 1 to 20: low HLB = lipophilic, high HLB = hydrophilic.

| HLB range | Use |
| --- | --- |
| 1–3 | Antifoaming agents |
| 3–6 | w/o emulsifying agents |
| 7–9 | Wetting and spreading agents |
| 8–18 | o/w emulsifying agents |
| 13–16 | Detergents |
| 15–18 | Solubilising agents |

For polyhydric alcohol fatty acid esters, HLB = 20(1 − S/A), where S = saponification number of the ester and A = acid number of the fatty acid. For polyoxyethylene derivatives where only the ethylene oxide part is hydrophilic, HLB = E/5, where E = weight percentage of oxyethylene.

### Required HLB

Each oil needs an emulsifier (or blend) of a particular HLB, called the **required HLB (RHLB)**, to form a stable emulsion of a given type (o/w or w/o). The RHLB of a mixture of oils is the weighted average:

RHLB(mix) = Σ (fraction of each oil × its RHLB)

**Worked example 1.** An oil phase has 30 g liquid paraffin (RHLB 12 for o/w) and 10 g cetyl alcohol (RHLB 15). RHLB = (30 × 12 + 10 × 15)/40 = 510/40 = **12.75**.

### Blending emulsifiers

A blend of a hydrophilic and a lipophilic surfactant usually gives a stronger film than either alone. The HLB of the blend is:

HLB(blend) = (fraction of A × HLB of A) + (fraction of B × HLB of B)

**Worked example 2.** A blend of polysorbate 80 (HLB 15.0) and sorbitan monooleate (HLB 4.3) is needed with HLB 12. Let x = fraction of polysorbate 80:
15.0x + 4.3(1 − x) = 12 → 10.7x = 7.7 → x = 0.72.
So use **72% polysorbate 80 and 28% sorbitan monooleate**. For 5 g of emulsifier: 3.6 g polysorbate 80 and 1.4 g sorbitan monooleate.

**Steps of the HLB method**

1. Find the RHLB of the oil phase (from tables, or the weighted average for mixtures).
2. Choose a pair of emulsifiers of the same chemical type (for example a Span and a Tween).
3. Calculate the blend proportions to match the RHLB.
4. In practice, prepare a series of emulsions with blends around the calculated HLB and select the most stable (least creaming, smallest droplets).

The HLB system ignores concentration of emulsifier, temperature, phase volume and the chemical match between oil and emulsifier, so the calculated value is a starting point to be confirmed experimentally.

## Remember

- Suspensions: ΔG = γSL ΔA; wetting agents lower γSL; zeta potential controls flocculation.
- Flocculated: fast settling, loose high-volume sediment, no caking; deflocculated: slow settling but cakes. Aim: controlled flocculation in a structured vehicle.
- Stokes: v = d²(ρs − ρ0)g/18η; reduce d, density difference, raise η.
- F = Vu/V0 (ideal 1); β = F/F∞.
- Emulsion type tests: dilution, dye, conductivity, CoCl2 paper, fluorescence.
- Theories: interfacial tension, oriented wedge (Bancroft's rule), monomolecular (surfactants), multimolecular (hydrocolloids), solid particle.
- Microemulsions: clear, 10–100 nm, thermodynamically stable, need cosurfactant. Multiple emulsions: w/o/w, two-step preparation.
- Instability: creaming (reversible), flocculation, coalescence/cracking (irreversible), phase inversion, Ostwald ripening.
- Preservative must work in the aqueous phase; watch partition into oil and binding by nonionic surfactants.
- HLB: w/o 3–6, o/w 8–18; HLB(blend) = Σ fraction × HLB.
