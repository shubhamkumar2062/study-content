# Unit 1: Colloidal dispersions

A **dispersed system** has one substance (the **dispersed phase**) spread as particles through another (the **dispersion medium** or continuous phase). Blood, milk, gelatin jelly, colloidal sulfur, plasma expanders, liposomes and most creams are dispersed systems. How large the particles are decides how the system looks, whether it settles, whether it passes through filters and membranes, and how stable it is. This unit covers the middle size range, the **colloids**.

## 1.1 Classification of dispersed systems by particle size

| Class | Particle size (approx.) | Main characteristics | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Molecular dispersion (true solution) | Less than 1 nm | Invisible even in an electron microscope; pass through filter paper and semipermeable membranes; diffuse fast | Oxygen molecules, glucose, ordinary ions |
| Colloidal dispersion | 1 nm to 0.5 µm | Not seen in an ordinary microscope but seen in an electron microscope; pass through filter paper but **not** through semipermeable membranes; diffuse very slowly | Colloidal silver sols, natural and synthetic polymers, cheese, gelatin |
| Coarse dispersion | Greater than 0.5 µm | Seen in an ordinary microscope; do not pass through ordinary filter paper or membranes; do not diffuse; settle under gravity | Suspensions, emulsions, grains of sand, red blood cells |

Some textbooks give the colloidal range as 1–500 nm or 1–1000 nm. The idea is the same: colloidal particles are too small to settle quickly or to be seen in a light microscope, but much larger than ordinary molecules.

**Why colloids behave differently:** a small particle size gives an enormous **specific surface** (surface per gram). Surface effects (adsorption, charge, solvation) therefore control colloid behaviour.

## 1.2 Dispersed systems by state of the phases

| Dispersed phase | Dispersion medium | Name | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Liquid | Gas | Liquid aerosol (fog) | Fog, mist, nasal sprays, inhaler sprays |
| Solid | Gas | Solid aerosol (smoke) | Smoke, dust, powder inhalers |
| Gas | Liquid | Foam | Shaving foam, foam on detergent water |
| Liquid | Liquid | Emulsion | Milk, mineral oil emulsion |
| Solid | Liquid | Sol / suspension | Colloidal gold, magnesia magma, gelatin solution |
| Gas | Solid | Solid foam | Pumice, foam rubber |
| Liquid | Solid | Solid emulsion (gel) | Cheese, butter, jellies |
| Solid | Solid | Solid suspension | Coloured glass, alloys |

A gas cannot be dispersed in another gas as a colloid, because gases mix to a true solution.

## 1.3 Size and shape of colloidal particles

Colloidal particles are rarely uniform. Most sols are **polydisperse** (a range of sizes); a few, such as some viruses and proteins, are **monodisperse** (one size).

```text
   sphere        ellipsoid (rod)      flattened disc      random coil
     ( )          (=========)            ______              ~ ~ ~
                                        (______)            ~ ~ ~ ~
   albumin,      tobacco mosaic         some clays        linear polymers
   many sols     virus, fibrous          (bentonite)       in solution
                 proteins
```

Shape matters because it changes:

- **Viscosity:** long rods and coils raise viscosity much more than spheres of the same volume.
- **Sedimentation and diffusion:** non-spherical particles meet more resistance and move more slowly.
- **Optical behaviour:** rods and discs scatter light differently from spheres.
- **Pharmaceutical action:** for example, the size and shape of a carrier (liposome, nanoparticle) decide how it is taken up by the body.

The **specific surface** rises sharply as size falls. A 1 cm cube has a surface of 6 cm²; divided into cubes of 10 nm (10⁻⁶ cm) edge, the same material has a surface of 6 × 10⁶ cm² (600 m²). This is why colloidal adsorbents such as charcoal and colloidal silica are so effective.

## 1.4 Classification of colloids (by attraction for the medium)

### Lyophilic colloids (solvent-loving)

The dispersed particles have strong affinity for the medium and become **solvated** (hydrated when the medium is water). They form colloidal dispersions **spontaneously** when mixed with the medium. Examples in water: gelatin, acacia, tragacanth, albumin, starch, methylcellulose, sodium carboxymethylcellulose, povidone. Examples in non-aqueous media: rubber and polystyrene in benzene.

### Lyophobic colloids (solvent-hating)

The particles have little attraction for the medium. They do not disperse on their own; special methods are needed to make them. They are mostly inorganic substances in water: gold, silver, sulfur, arsenic sulfide, silver iodide.

**Preparation of lyophobic colloids**

| Approach | Method | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dispersion (breaking down large particles) | Colloid mill | Many pharmaceutical sols |
| | Ultrasonic waves | Breaking of coarse particles |
| | Electric arc (Bredig's arc) | Gold, silver, platinum sols |
| | Peptization (see 1.9) | Ferric hydroxide with ferric chloride |
| Condensation (building up from molecules) | Change of solvent | Sulfur in alcohol poured into water |
| | Chemical reaction: oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, double decomposition | Ferric hydroxide by hydrolysis of ferric chloride in boiling water; arsenic sulfide from arsenious oxide and hydrogen sulfide |

**Purification** removes excess electrolytes that would destabilise the sol: **dialysis** (through a semipermeable membrane), **electrodialysis** (dialysis speeded by an electric field) and **ultrafiltration** (filtration under pressure through very fine membranes).

### Association colloids (amphiphilic colloids)

Surfactant molecules (amphiphiles) exist singly at low concentration. Above the **critical micelle concentration (CMC)** they aggregate into **micelles** of about 50 or more molecules, which are of colloidal size. In water the hydrocarbon tails point inward and the polar heads face the water; in oils the arrangement is reversed.

```text
    Micelle in water                 Reversed micelle in oil
        o   o   o                        ----   ----
     o  \   |   /  o                    /    \ /    \
   o -- tails inside -- o             heads (o) inside, tails
     o  /   |   \  o                    out in the oil
        o   o   o
   o = polar head facing water
```

Micelles **solubilise** poorly soluble drugs (vitamin A and steroid preparations, iodine in povidone-iodine solutions) and are used in mouthwashes, injections and eye drops.

## 1.5 Comparison of the three classes

| Property | Lyophilic | Lyophobic | Association |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nature of particles | Large organic molecules (polymers, proteins) | Inorganic particles, insoluble materials | Micelles of small surfactant molecules |
| Formation | Spontaneous | Needs special methods | Spontaneous above the CMC |
| Solvation | Highly solvated | Little or none | Polar heads hydrated (in water) |
| Viscosity | Raised markedly | Close to that of the medium | Rises as micelles grow at high concentration |
| Visibility of particles | Hard to see, as their refractive index is close to the medium | Seen in the ultramicroscope as bright points | Hard to see |
| Stability toward electrolytes | Stable to small amounts; need large amounts to "salt out" | Very sensitive; small amounts coagulate them | CMC is lowered by electrolytes (ionic surfactants) |
| Reversibility | Reversible (dried material redisperses) | Irreversible (once coagulated, will not redisperse simply) | Reversible |
| Main stabilising factor | Solvation sheath (and charge) | Electric charge (double layer) | Hydration and charge of heads |

## 1.6 Optical properties

### Faraday–Tyndall effect

When a strong beam of light passes through a colloid at right angles to the line of sight, the path appears as a bright cone (the **Tyndall cone**), because the particles scatter light. A true solution shows almost no such cone. Sunlight beams in a dusty room show the same effect.

### Ultramicroscope

An ordinary microscope cannot resolve particles below about 0.2 µm. In the **ultramicroscope (dark-field microscope)**, light is sent across the sample from the side against a dark background. Each colloidal particle scatters light and shows as a bright point. The particles are not seen in shape or true size, but they can be **counted** and their **Brownian motion** followed.

### Electron microscope

Uses a beam of electrons instead of light, so it can resolve particles down to about 1 nm. It shows the **size, shape and structure** of colloidal particles directly (for example, the rod shape of tobacco mosaic virus, the bilayers of liposomes).

### Light scattering

The intensity of scattered light depends on the size, shape and molecular weight of the particles. **Turbidity (τ)** is the fractional decrease in intensity of a beam passing through 1 cm of the dispersion. For dilute polymer solutions:

Hc/τ = 1/M + 2Bc

where c = concentration (g/cm³), M = molecular weight, B = an interaction constant, and H is an optical constant for the system. Plotting Hc/τ against c gives a straight line; the **intercept is 1/M**. Light scattering is used to find the molecular weight of polymers and proteins and to study micelle size.

## 1.7 Kinetic properties

Kinetic properties are those related to the **motion of particles** in the medium.

### Brownian motion

Under the ultramicroscope, colloidal particles move in a rapid, random zig-zag path. The motion is caused by uneven bombardment of each particle by the molecules of the medium. It becomes less vigorous as particles get larger, and it is hardly seen above about 5 µm. Brownian motion helps keep colloidal particles from settling.

### Diffusion

Particles move spontaneously from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration. Fick's first law: the amount dq diffusing in time dt across area S is

dq/dt = −DS (dc/dx)

where D = diffusion coefficient and dc/dx = concentration gradient. For spherical particles the **Stokes–Einstein equation** gives

D = kT / (6πηr) = RT / (6πηrN)

where k = Boltzmann constant, T = absolute temperature, η = viscosity of the medium, r = particle radius, N = Avogadro's number. Larger particles and more viscous media give slower diffusion. From D, the radius and (for spheres) the molecular weight can be found.

### Osmotic pressure

Colloidal solutions show small osmotic pressures because the number of particles per gram is small. The **van't Hoff equation** applies to dilute solutions:

π = cRT/M, or π/c = RT/M

For real polymer solutions, π/c changes with concentration, so π/c is plotted against c and extrapolated to c = 0:

π/c = RT(1/M + Bc)

The intercept gives the **number-average molecular weight** of the polymer (used for dextran, povidone and proteins).

### Sedimentation

Under gravity, a spherical particle settles at a velocity given by **Stokes's law**:

v = 2r²(ρ − ρ0)g / 9η

where r = radius, ρ = density of particle, ρ0 = density of medium, g = acceleration due to gravity, η = viscosity of medium. Colloidal particles settle so slowly under gravity that Brownian motion cancels it. The **ultracentrifuge**, which can produce forces many thousand times gravity, is used to make them settle. The sedimentation rate gives the **sedimentation coefficient** and, with diffusion data, the molecular weight of proteins and polymers.

### Viscosity

The **Einstein equation** describes dilute dispersions of rigid spheres:

η = η0(1 + 2.5φ)

where η0 = viscosity of the medium and φ = volume fraction of particles. Linear polymers raise viscosity far more than this. The **intrinsic viscosity [η]** (the limit of ηsp/c as c → 0) is related to molecular weight by the **Mark–Houwink equation** [η] = KMᵃ. Viscosity also tells us about the shape and solvation of particles.

## 1.8 Electrical properties

### How particles get a charge

1. **Ionisation** of surface groups (for example, -COOH and -NH2 of proteins; charge depends on pH, and is zero at the **isoelectric point**).
2. **Preferential adsorption of ions** from the medium. For example, silver iodide adsorbs I⁻ ions when KI is in excess (negative sol) and Ag⁺ ions when AgNO3 is in excess (positive sol).
3. **Frictional charging** in non-aqueous media.

### Electrical double layer

```text
 particle | fixed (Stern) layer | diffuse layer          | bulk
 surface  |  tightly bound      |  counter-ions thin out | electrically
   + + +  |  - - -              |  -   +   -   -   +     | neutral
   + + +  |  - - -              |   -    +   -          |
          |                     |                       |
 Nernst potential (E): surface ---> bulk
 Zeta potential (ζ): plane of shear ---> bulk
          ^ shear plane (boundary of the layer that moves with the particle)
```

- The **Nernst (electrothermodynamic) potential** is the difference in potential between the actual surface and the bulk of the solution.
- The **zeta (electrokinetic) potential** is the difference in potential between the **plane of shear** (the outer limit of the liquid layer that moves with the particle) and the bulk.
- Zeta potential, not Nernst potential, controls stability. A high zeta potential (roughly above 30 mV, positive or negative) gives strong repulsion and a stable sol; a low zeta potential allows particles to approach and aggregate.

### Electrokinetic phenomena

| Phenomenon | What moves | What is applied / measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electrophoresis | Particles move toward the oppositely charged electrode | Apply a field; measure particle velocity |
| Electro-osmosis | Liquid moves through a fixed porous plug or capillary | Apply a field; measure liquid flow (opposite of electrophoresis) |
| Sedimentation potential (Dorn effect) | Particles settle through the liquid | A potential develops (reverse of electrophoresis) |
| Streaming potential | Liquid is forced through a bed of particles | A potential develops (reverse of electro-osmosis) |

**Zeta potential from electrophoresis.** The particle velocity per unit field (electrophoretic mobility, v/E) is measured in a microelectrophoresis cell. The Helmholtz–Smoluchowski relation is ζ = ηv/(εE) in SI units, where η = viscosity and ε = permittivity of the medium. For water at 20 °C this works out to ζ (volts) ≈ 141 × v/E, with v/E in cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. **Worked example:** mobility 2 × 10⁻⁴ cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ → ζ ≈ 141 × 2 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.028 V = 28 mV.

### Donnan membrane equilibrium

When a charged colloid (for example, sodium salt of a protein, Na⁺R⁻) is separated from a simple electrolyte (NaCl) by a membrane that lets small ions pass but not R⁻, the small ions distribute **unequally** at equilibrium. The side with the colloid holds more Na⁺ and less Cl⁻. The Donnan equation:

[Cl⁻]o = [Cl⁻]i √(1 + [R⁻]i/[Cl⁻]i)

(o = outside, i = inside, the colloid side). It explains unequal distribution of ions across cell membranes and the absorption of drugs bound to charged polymers, and it must be allowed for in osmotic pressure measurements of proteins.

## 1.9 Stability: effect of electrolytes, coacervation, peptization and protective action

### Effect of electrolytes on lyophobic sols

Lyophobic sols are stabilised by their charge. Adding an electrolyte supplies ions of opposite charge (**counter-ions**) that compress the double layer and lower the zeta potential. Below a critical value, particles aggregate: **coagulation**.

**Schulze–Hardy rule:** the coagulating power of an electrolyte rises steeply with the **valency of the counter-ion**. For a negative sol (As2S3), Al³⁺ coagulates far more effectively than Ba²⁺, which is far more effective than Na⁺. For a positive sol (Fe(OH)3), PO4³⁻ is more effective than SO4²⁻, which is more effective than Cl⁻. The ratio is roughly with the sixth power of valency (about 1 : 64 : 729 for mono-, di- and trivalent ions).

Mixing a positive and a negative sol in suitable amounts also causes **mutual precipitation**.

### Effect of electrolytes on lyophilic sols

Lyophilic sols are stabilised mainly by **solvation**. Small amounts of electrolyte do not coagulate them. Large amounts **salt out** the colloid by competing for water (desolvation). Ions are ranked by salting-out power in the **Hofmeister (lyotropic) series**; for anions: citrate > tartrate > sulfate > acetate > chloride > nitrate > bromide > iodide. Dehydrating liquids such as alcohol and acetone also precipitate lyophilic colloids (alcohol precipitates gelatin and acacia). Removing both charge and solvation (adding alcohol and an electrolyte) precipitates them most completely.

### Coacervation

**Coacervation** is the separation of a lyophilic colloid dispersion into **two liquid phases**: a colloid-rich layer (the **coacervate**) and a colloid-poor layer. It is not a solid precipitate.

- **Simple coacervation:** caused by a dehydrating agent such as alcohol, sodium sulfate or a change in temperature (gelatin with sodium sulfate or alcohol).
- **Complex coacervation:** caused by mixing two oppositely charged hydrophilic colloids. Gelatin is positively charged at a pH below its isoelectric point, while acacia is negatively charged. When mixed at such a pH, they neutralise each other's charges and separate as a coacervate.

**Uses:** microencapsulation of drugs (oils, vitamins, aspirin), where the coacervate deposits as a wall around dispersed drug droplets or particles and is then hardened, for example with formaldehyde or by cooling.

### Peptization

**Peptization** is the conversion of a freshly formed precipitate into a colloidal sol by adding a small amount of an electrolyte (the **peptizing agent**). The precipitate particles adsorb one ion of the electrolyte, gain a charge and repel each other back into a sol. Examples: freshly precipitated ferric hydroxide with a small amount of ferric chloride (adsorbs Fe³⁺); silver chloride with a little silver nitrate. Peptization is the reverse of coagulation; washing a precipitate with water can also peptize it by removing coagulating ions.

### Protective action

Adding a lyophilic colloid to a lyophobic sol makes it much **less sensitive to electrolytes**. The lyophilic colloid is adsorbed on the lyophobic particles and forms a solvated layer around them. Such colloids are called **protective colloids**. Example: gelatin in colloidal silver preparations, which prevents coagulation.

The **gold number** (Zsigmondy) measures protective power: it is the minimum weight in milligrams of a protective colloid that prevents the colour change of 10 ml of a standard red gold sol to violet (a sign of coagulation) when 1 ml of a 10% sodium chloride solution is added. **The smaller the gold number, the greater the protective power.**

| Protective colloid | Approximate gold number |
| --- | --- |
| Gelatin | 0.005–0.01 |
| Acacia | 0.15–0.25 |
| Starch | about 25 |

**Sensitization:** a very small amount of a lyophilic colloid can sometimes make a lyophobic sol *more* sensitive to electrolytes, because it bridges particles together instead of covering them.

## 1.10 Pharmaceutical applications of colloids

| Application | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Therapy | Colloidal silver (antiseptic), colloidal sulfur, colloidal gold (diagnostics) |
| Plasma substitutes | Dextran, gelatin, hydroxyethyl starch solutions given to restore blood volume |
| Suspending and thickening | Acacia, tragacanth, bentonite, carbomers, cellulose derivatives |
| Solubilisation | Micelles of polysorbates to dissolve vitamins and steroids |
| Drug delivery | Liposomes, nanoparticles, microspheres made by coacervation |

| Absorption | Colloidal size increases surface area and speeds dissolution |

## Remember

- Colloids: 1 nm to about 0.5 µm; pass filter paper but not semipermeable membranes.
- Lyophilic = solvated, spontaneous, reversible, stable to small electrolyte amounts; lyophobic = charge-stabilised, sensitive to electrolytes, irreversible; association = micelles above the CMC.
- Optical: Tyndall effect, ultramicroscope (counts particles), electron microscope (shape), light scattering (Hc/τ = 1/M + 2Bc).
- Kinetic: Brownian motion, diffusion (D = kT/6πηr), osmotic pressure (π/c vs c intercept = RT/M), sedimentation (Stokes), viscosity (η = η0(1 + 2.5φ)).
- Zeta potential (at the shear plane) controls stability; measured by electrophoresis.
- Schulze–Hardy: higher counter-ion valency coagulates more strongly.
- Coacervation = two liquid phases (gelatin + acacia, microencapsulation); peptization = precipitate back to sol; protective colloids measured by gold number (smaller = better).
