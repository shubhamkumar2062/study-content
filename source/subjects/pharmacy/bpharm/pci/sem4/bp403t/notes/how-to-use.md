# How to use this guide

Physical Pharmaceutics II links physical chemistry to real dosage forms: colloids, flow behaviour, suspensions and emulsions, powders, and drug stability. About half of the scoring answers need a diagram (rheogram, double layer, hysteresis loop, sedimentation plot, Arrhenius plot) and many need a short calculation. Study one unit a day: read the notes, redo every worked example with a calculator without looking, draw each diagram once from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

The notes follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Unit 2 also contains "Deformation of solids" (stress, strain, elastic modulus, Heckel equation), which the syllabus places under rheology.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays: attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers: attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High:** asked in most years; prepare the full answer with diagrams, equations and a worked numerical.
- **Medium:** asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low:** rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Topics that keep coming back |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Colloids | Lyophilic vs lyophobic vs association colloids, optical and kinetic properties, electrical double layer and zeta potential, protective colloids and gold number, coacervation |
| 2 Rheology | Newton's law, types of non-Newtonian flow with rheograms, thixotropy and its uses, viscometers (Ostwald, falling sphere, cup-and-bob, cone-and-plate), Heckel equation |
| 3 Coarse dispersions | Flocculated vs deflocculated suspensions, Stokes's law, sedimentation volume and degree of flocculation, theories of emulsification, emulsion stability, HLB calculations |
| 4 Micromeritics | Methods of particle size analysis (microscopy, sieving, sedimentation), mean diameters, surface area methods, densities and porosity, flow properties |
| 5 Drug stability | Order of reaction with derivations, half-life and shelf life, determination of order, Arrhenius equation and accelerated stability testing, hydrolysis and oxidation prevention |

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start every answer with a one-line definition and the key equation; then explain each symbol with its unit.
2. For derivations (first order, second order, Stokes, Arrhenius), write the differential form, integrate, give the linear plot and the half-life. Examiners mark each step.
3. Numericals: write the formula, substitute with units, and box the answer with its unit (cP, day⁻¹, M⁻¹ min⁻¹, cm²/g, %). Method marks are given even if the arithmetic slips.
4. Draw the matching graph every time: rheograms of the four flow types, hysteresis loop, double layer with zeta potential, zeta potential vs electrolyte concentration (caking diagram), log-normal distribution, log k vs pH, log k vs 1/T.
5. Comparison tables score well: lyophilic vs lyophobic, flocculated vs deflocculated, macro- vs microemulsion, elastic vs plastic deformation, specific vs general catalysis.
6. End essays with 3–4 pharmaceutical applications (suspension design, thixotropic depot injections, micronisation for absorption, shelf-life prediction).
7. Practise these numerical types until they are automatic: Ostwald viscosity, Stokes settling rate, sedimentation volume and β, HLB of a blend and required HLB of an oil mix, mean diameters from a frequency table, specific surface, porosity and Carr's index, angle of repose, k, t½ and t90 for each order, and Arrhenius extrapolation.
8. Learn the standard values: t90 = 0.105/k, t½ = 0.693/k, water ≈ 1 cP at 20 °C, closest and open packing porosity 26% and 48%, w/o HLB 3–6 and o/w HLB 8–18, accelerated condition 40 °C/75% RH.
