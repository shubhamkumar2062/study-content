# Particle size and size distribution by microscopy

**Aim:** To determine the particle size distribution and mean particle diameters of a given powder by the optical microscopic method.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ.

## Principle

Particles spread on a slide are measured against an **eyepiece micrometer**, a glass disc in the eyepiece engraved with an arbitrary scale. Because the value of one eyepiece division depends on the objective in use, the eyepiece scale is first **calibrated** with a **stage micrometer**, a slide engraved with a scale of known size (commonly 1 mm divided into 100 divisions, so 1 stage division = 0.01 mm = 10 µm).

Value of one eyepiece division (ED) = (number of stage divisions × 10 µm) / (number of eyepiece divisions that coincide with them)

Each particle is measured along a fixed direction (Feret's or Martin's diameter), and the number of particles in each size class is counted. This gives a **number distribution** of projected diameters, from which mean diameters are calculated:

dln = Σnd/Σn; dsn = √(Σnd²/Σn); dvn = ∛(Σnd³/Σn); dvs = Σnd³/Σnd²

## Requirements

Compound microscope, eyepiece micrometer, stage micrometer, glass slides and cover slips, sample powder (for example calamine, zinc oxide, starch or sulfur), a liquid in which it is insoluble (for example liquid paraffin or glycerin), dropper, tally counter.

## Procedure

**A. Calibration**

1. Place the eyepiece micrometer in the eyepiece and the stage micrometer on the stage.
2. Focus with the objective to be used (for example 45×).
3. Rotate the eyepiece so the two scales are parallel; make the zero lines coincide.
4. Find a point farther along where a line of the eyepiece scale exactly coincides with a line of the stage scale. Note both readings.
5. Calculate the value of one eyepiece division. Repeat at two or three places and take the mean.

**B. Measurement**

6. Disperse a small amount of powder in a drop of liquid paraffin on a slide; cover with a cover slip, avoiding air bubbles and aggregates.
7. Remove the stage micrometer and focus on the particles.
8. Measure each particle along the eyepiece scale in a fixed direction (do not rotate the slide), and record the size in eyepiece divisions.
9. Move the slide systematically across different fields so no particle is counted twice. Count at least 300 particles (500 is better).
10. Group the readings into size classes, convert to µm, and calculate the mean diameters. Plot a frequency histogram and a cumulative frequency curve.

## Precautions

1. Calibrate with the same objective used for measurement; recalibrate if the objective is changed.
2. The dispersion must be dilute so particles do not overlap or aggregate.
3. Measure all particles in a fixed direction; do not select particles.
4. Count enough particles (300–500 or more) from many fields.
5. The liquid must not dissolve or swell the powder.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is calibration needed?** The value of one eyepiece division changes with the objective magnification.
2. **What is the size range of optical microscopy?** About 0.2–100 µm.
3. **What type of distribution does microscopy give?** A number distribution of projected diameters.
4. **Define Feret's diameter.** Distance between two parallel tangents on opposite sides of the particle image, drawn in a fixed direction.
5. **Define Martin's diameter.** Length of the line in a fixed direction that divides the particle image into two equal areas.
6. **Why count at least 300 particles?** To get a statistically reliable distribution.
7. **Limitations of the method?** Slow and tiring; measures only two dimensions; operator bias; a small sample may not represent the batch.
