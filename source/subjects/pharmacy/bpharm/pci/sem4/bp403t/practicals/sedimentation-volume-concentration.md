# Sedimentation volume with different concentrations of one suspending agent

**Aim:** To study the effect of the concentration of a single suspending agent (sodium carboxymethylcellulose) on the sedimentation volume of a suspension and to select a suitable concentration.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. The readings below are example values to show the calculation; your values will depend on the materials used.

## Principle

As the concentration of a suspending agent rises, the viscosity of the vehicle rises. By Stokes's law (v = d²(ρs − ρ0)g/18η), particles settle more slowly. A polymer at adequate concentration also gives a structured (pseudoplastic, often thixotropic) vehicle that holds the particles in a loose network. The sediment therefore occupies a larger volume and the **sedimentation volume F = Hu/H0** rises toward 1.

Above a certain concentration, the gain in F becomes small while the suspension becomes too thick to pour or to redisperse easily. The best concentration balances a high F with good pourability.

## Requirements

Insoluble powder (for example calcium carbonate, 5% w/v), sodium carboxymethylcellulose (sodium CMC), distilled water, mortar and pestle, five 50 ml stoppered measuring cylinders, balance, clock.

## Procedure

1. Prepare 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1.0% w/v dispersions of sodium CMC in water: sprinkle the polymer slowly on stirred water and allow it to hydrate fully (or use a stock solution and dilute).
2. For each concentration, triturate 2.5 g of the powder with a little of the polymer solution to a smooth paste; transfer to a 50 ml cylinder and make up to 50 ml with the same solution.
3. Prepare a control with water only.
4. Invert each cylinder the same number of times and leave undisturbed. Record H0 = 50 ml.
5. Read the sediment volume Hu at 15 and 30 minutes, 1, 2 and 24 hours, then daily up to 7 days.
6. Calculate F at each time and plot F against time for each concentration, and the final F against concentration.
7. Note pourability (pours freely, pours slowly, does not pour) and the number of inversions needed to redisperse.

## Precautions

1. Hydrate the polymer completely; lumps (fish-eyes) give an uneven vehicle.
2. Use identical cylinders, volumes and shaking for all samples.
3. Keep the cylinders undisturbed at constant temperature.
4. Read heights at eye level, at the sediment boundary.

## Viva questions

1. **Why does F increase with polymer concentration?** Higher viscosity slows settling, and the polymer forms a structure that holds particles loosely.
2. **Why not use a very high concentration?** The suspension becomes too viscous to pour, measure and redisperse.
3. **Which law relates settling rate to viscosity?** Stokes's law, v = d²(ρs − ρ0)g/18η.
4. **What type of flow does a sodium CMC solution show?** Pseudoplastic (shear thinning).
5. **Name two other synthetic or semisynthetic suspending agents.** Methylcellulose, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (also carbomers and xanthan gum).
6. **What does F = 0.16 for the control show?** The particles settled into a compact sediment that is likely to cake.
