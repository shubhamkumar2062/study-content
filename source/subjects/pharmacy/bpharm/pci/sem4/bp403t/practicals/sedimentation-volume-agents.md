# Sedimentation volume with different suspending agents

**Aim:** To prepare suspensions of a given insoluble powder with different suspending agents and to compare them by their sedimentation volume.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. The readings below are example values to show the calculation; your values will depend on the materials used.

## Principle

The **sedimentation volume (F)** is the ratio of the ultimate (equilibrium) volume of the sediment, Vu, to the original volume of the suspension, V0. In a uniform measuring cylinder, heights can be used instead of volumes:

**F = Vu / V0 = Hu / H0**

F lies between 0 and 1 (it can exceed 1 for a very loose floc network). The closer F is to 1, the larger and looser the sediment and the better the suspension, because a voluminous sediment does not cake and redisperses easily. F = 1 means no clear liquid separates (flocculation equilibrium).

Suspending agents (natural gums, cellulose derivatives, clays) increase the viscosity of the vehicle (slowing settling by Stokes's law, v = d²(ρs − ρ0)g/18η) and may also adsorb on particles and promote a loose structure, both of which raise F.

## Requirements

Insoluble powder (for example calcium carbonate or zinc oxide, 5% w/v), suspending agents (for example acacia 2%, tragacanth 1%, sodium carboxymethylcellulose 1%, bentonite 2% w/v), distilled water, mortar and pestle, five 50 ml stoppered measuring cylinders, balance, stopwatch or clock, beakers.

## Procedure

1. For each suspension, weigh 2.5 g of the powder (5% w/v in 50 ml).
2. Prepare the suspending agent: triturate the gum or cellulose with a little water (or wet tragacanth and acacia first with a little alcohol or glycerin to avoid lumps); disperse bentonite by sprinkling it on water and allowing it to hydrate.
3. Triturate the powder with the vehicle to a smooth paste, then add more vehicle, transfer to a 50 ml measuring cylinder and make up to 50 ml with water.
4. Prepare a control suspension without any suspending agent.
5. Shake every cylinder the same number of times (for example 10 inversions) and stand them on a level bench, undisturbed. Record the initial height H0 (50 ml).
6. Record the height (volume) of the sediment Hu at intervals: 5, 10, 15, 30 and 60 minutes, then at 1, 2, 3 and 7 days.
7. Calculate F = Hu/H0 at each time. Plot F against time for all suspensions on one graph.
8. At the end, note the number of inversions needed to redisperse each sediment completely.

## Precautions

1. Use cylinders of the same size and shape, and the same total volume for all suspensions.
2. Shake all cylinders the same way and keep them undisturbed on a level, vibration-free bench.
3. Read the sediment boundary at eye level.
4. Disperse gums without lumps; incompletely hydrated gums give wrong results.
5. Keep the temperature constant, as viscosity changes with temperature.

## Viva questions

1. **Define sedimentation volume.** F = Vu/V0, the ratio of the ultimate sediment volume to the original suspension volume.
2. **What is the ideal value of F?** 1 (no clear supernatant separates).
3. **Can F exceed 1?** Yes, when the loose floc network occupies more volume than the original suspension.
4. **How do suspending agents act?** They raise vehicle viscosity (slowing settling) and may form a structured, often thixotropic vehicle.
5. **What is degree of flocculation?** β = F/F∞, F of the flocculated suspension over F of the fully deflocculated one.
6. **Why is caking undesirable?** A caked sediment does not redisperse, so the dose is not uniform.
7. **Give two clay suspending agents.** Bentonite and magnesium aluminium silicate (Veegum).
