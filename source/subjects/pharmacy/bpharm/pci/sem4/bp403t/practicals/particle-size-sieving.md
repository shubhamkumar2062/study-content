# Particle size and size distribution by sieving

**Aim:** To determine the particle size distribution and the average particle size of a given powder or granules by sieve analysis.

> Quantities and sieve sizes are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ.

## Principle

A weighed sample is shaken through a nest of standard sieves stacked with the largest aperture at the top and the smallest at the bottom, above a receiving pan. Each particle passes down until it meets a sieve whose aperture is too small. The weight retained on each sieve is the weight of particles whose size lies between that sieve's aperture and the aperture of the sieve above. Sieving gives a **weight distribution** and is suitable for particles larger than about 40–50 µm.

The average particle size (weight-based) is calculated as:

**Average size = Σ (% weight retained × mean aperture of the fraction) / 100**

where the mean aperture of a fraction is the average of the aperture it passed and the aperture on which it was retained.

## Requirements

Powder or granules (for example lactose granules, sand, or a granulated blend), set of standard sieves (for example 1000, 710, 500, 355, 250, 180 and 125 µm) with lid and receiving pan, mechanical sieve shaker, balance, brush, butter paper.

## Procedure

1. Clean and dry all sieves; weigh each empty sieve and the pan if weighing by difference.
2. Arrange the sieves in order of decreasing aperture from top to bottom, with the pan at the bottom.
3. Weigh accurately about 100 g of the sample and place it on the top sieve; close with the lid.
4. Shake on the mechanical shaker for a fixed time (for example 10–20 minutes) at a fixed amplitude.
5. Remove each sieve and carefully transfer the material retained to butter paper with a brush; weigh it (or weigh the sieve with its contents and subtract the empty weight).
6. Check that the total of all fractions is close to the weight taken (loss should be small, about 1–2%).
7. Calculate % retained, cumulative % retained, cumulative % undersize and the average size.
8. Plot (a) a frequency histogram of % retained against mean size and (b) cumulative % undersize against aperture size; read the median (50% size) from the cumulative curve.

## Precautions

1. Sieves must be clean, dry and undamaged (no torn or stretched mesh).
2. Do not overload the top sieve; too much powder blinds the apertures.
3. Keep shaking time and amplitude constant between runs.
4. Brush retained material out gently so as not to force particles through the mesh.
5. Avoid sieving very fine or hygroscopic powders that clump; dry the sample if needed.

## Viva questions

1. **What type of distribution does sieving give?** A weight distribution.
2. **What is the lower limit of sieving?** About 40–50 µm; finer powders clump and block the mesh.
3. **What is a sieve number?** A pharmacopoeial designation of a standard sieve, related to the nominal size of its aperture.
4. **Why is the shaking time fixed?** Longer shaking passes more elongated particles and breaks fragile ones (attrition), changing the result.
5. **How is the median read?** From the cumulative % undersize curve at 50%.
6. **Name two disadvantages of sieving.** Unsuitable for fine and cohesive powders; elongated particles may pass end-on; attrition during shaking.
7. **Why should the weight lost be small?** Large loss means particles were lost or stuck in the mesh, so the distribution is not reliable.
