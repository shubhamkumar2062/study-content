# Viscosity of a semisolid by Brookfield viscometer

**Aim:** To determine the viscosity of a given semisolid (cream, gel or ointment) at different speeds using a Brookfield viscometer and to identify its type of flow.

> Quantities, spindles and speeds are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual, the instrument's manual and your teacher where they differ. The factors below are example values to show the calculation; always use the factor chart supplied with your instrument.

## Principle

The Brookfield viscometer is a **rotational (multipoint)** viscometer. A spindle immersed in the sample is rotated by a motor through a calibrated spring. The viscous drag of the sample on the spindle twists the spring; the twist (torque) is shown on a dial as a reading from 0 to 100 (or as % torque on a digital model).

**Viscosity (cP) = dial reading × factor**

The factor depends on the spindle and the speed; for a given spindle it is inversely proportional to speed (doubling the speed halves the factor). Digital models display viscosity in cP directly.

Because the speed (and so the rate of shear) can be changed, the instrument can show non-Newtonian behaviour:

- Viscosity constant at all speeds → Newtonian.
- Viscosity falls as speed rises → pseudoplastic or plastic (shear thinning).
- Viscosity rises as speed rises → dilatant.
- Readings on the way down lower than on the way up → **thixotropy** (hysteresis loop when dial reading is plotted against speed).

For stiff semisolids that a cylindrical spindle would only carve a channel through, a **T-bar spindle on a helipath stand** moves slowly downward so it always meets fresh material.

## Requirements

Brookfield viscometer (LV or RV model) with spindle set, helipath stand and T-bar spindles (for stiff samples), sample (for example a cream, a carbomer gel or hydrophilic ointment), 250 ml beaker of standard size, thermometer, water bath (25 °C), spatula.

## Procedure

1. Level the instrument using its spirit level and zero it as per the manual (switch on and allow to stabilise).
2. Fill the beaker with the sample without trapping air, and bring it to 25 °C.
3. Choose a spindle and speed such that the reading falls roughly between 10% and 100% of the scale.
4. Attach the spindle, lower it into the sample up to the immersion groove, avoiding air bubbles under the spindle.
5. Start at the lowest speed (for example 2.5 rpm). Allow the reading to stabilise (for example 30–60 seconds or a set number of revolutions) and note the dial reading.
6. Increase the speed stepwise (5, 10, 20 rpm), recording the reading at each (up-curve).
7. Then decrease the speed stepwise back to 2.5 rpm, recording readings again (down-curve).
8. Calculate viscosity at each speed. Plot (a) viscosity against rpm and (b) dial reading (proportional to shear stress) against rpm for up and down runs.

## Observations and calculations

Sample: cream; spindle: example spindle; temperature 25 °C. Example factors for this spindle: 400 at 2.5 rpm, 200 at 5 rpm, 100 at 10 rpm, 50 at 20 rpm.

| Up | 2.5 | 60.0 | 400 | 24,000 |
| Up | 5 | 70.0 | 200 | 14,000 |

| Down | 2.5 | 47.5 | 400 | 19,000 |

**Worked calculation.** At 5 rpm on the up-curve: viscosity = 70.0 × 200 = **14,000 cP** (14 Pa s).

**Interpretation.** Viscosity falls from 24,000 to 4,900 cP as speed rises: the cream is **shear thinning** (pseudoplastic or plastic). On the way down, readings at each speed are lower than on the way up (for example 11,500 vs 14,000 cP at 5 rpm), so the down-curve lies below the up-curve: the cream is **thixotropic**.

## Precautions

1. Level the instrument and keep the sample at constant temperature.
2. Immerse the spindle exactly to the groove; avoid air bubbles under it.
3. Use readings between about 10% and 100% of the scale; change spindle or speed if outside this range.
4. Allow each reading to stabilise for the same time.
5. Clean and dry the spindle immediately after use; handle spindles carefully to avoid bending.

## Viva questions

1. **What type of viscometer is the Brookfield?** A rotational, multipoint viscometer.
2. **How is viscosity calculated from a dial reading?** Viscosity (cP) = dial reading × factor for that spindle and speed.
3. **What does a falling viscosity with rising speed show?** Shear-thinning (pseudoplastic or plastic) flow.
4. **How is thixotropy detected?** Down-curve readings are lower than up-curve readings, giving a hysteresis loop.
5. **Why is a helipath stand used?** So a T-bar spindle cuts fresh material in stiff samples instead of rotating in a channel.
6. **Why not use an Ostwald viscometer for creams?** It works at a single shear rate and cannot handle non-Newtonian or very thick materials.
7. **Why is temperature control important?** Viscosity falls as temperature rises, so readings must be at a fixed temperature.
