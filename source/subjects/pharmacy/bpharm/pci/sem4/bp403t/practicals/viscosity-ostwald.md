# Viscosity of a liquid by Ostwald's viscometer

**Aim:** To determine the viscosity of a given liquid (for example a syrup or a glycerin solution) relative to water using Ostwald's viscometer.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ.

## Principle

In a capillary viscometer, a fixed volume of liquid flows through a narrow capillary under its own weight. The flow obeys **Poiseuille's law**:

η = πr⁴tΔP / (8lV)

For the same viscometer, r, l and V are fixed, and the pressure head ΔP is proportional to the density of the liquid. So for a test liquid (1) and a reference liquid of known viscosity (2), usually water:

**η1 = η2 × (ρ1 t1) / (ρ2 t2)**

where t = flow time between the two marks and ρ = density. The method measures at a single rate of shear, so it is suitable only for **Newtonian** liquids.

## Requirements

Ostwald viscometer, thermostatic water bath (25 ± 0.1 °C), stopwatch, specific gravity bottle, pipette, rubber suction bulb, distilled water, test liquid (for example simple syrup diluted, or 30% glycerin), chromic acid or detergent and acetone for cleaning.

## Procedure

1. Clean the viscometer with detergent or chromic acid, rinse with water and then acetone, and dry.
2. Clamp the viscometer vertically in the water bath at 25 °C.
3. Pipette a fixed volume (for example 10 ml) of distilled water into the wider limb.
4. Allow about 10 minutes for temperature equilibrium.
5. Suck the water up the capillary limb above the upper mark A using a bulb.
6. Release and start the stopwatch when the meniscus passes mark A; stop it when it passes the lower mark B. Record t2.
7. Repeat to get three concordant readings (within about 0.2 s).
8. Empty and dry the viscometer (or rinse with the test liquid), add the **same volume** of the test liquid and repeat steps 4–7 to get t1.
9. Determine the densities of water and the test liquid at 25 °C with a specific gravity bottle: ρ = (weight of bottle + liquid − weight of empty bottle)/volume of bottle.
10. Calculate the viscosity of the test liquid.

## Observations and calculations

Temperature: 25 °C; viscosity of water at 25 °C = 0.890 cP; density of water = 0.997 g/ml.

| Liquid | Flow time trial 1 (s) | Trial 2 (s) | Trial 3 (s) | Mean t (s) | Density (g/ml) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Water | 100.2 | 99.8 | 100.0 | 100.0 | 0.997 |
| Test liquid | 150.1 | 149.8 | 150.1 | 150.0 | 1.10 |

**Worked calculation.**
η1 = 0.890 × (1.10 × 150.0)/(0.997 × 100.0) = 0.890 × 165.0/99.7 = 0.890 × 1.655 = **1.47 cP**

Relative viscosity = 1.47/0.890 = 1.65; kinematic viscosity = 1.47/1.10 = 1.34 cS.

## Precautions

1. Use the same volume of liquid for both water and the test liquid.
2. Keep the viscometer vertical and at constant temperature; viscosity changes markedly with temperature.
3. The viscometer must be completely clean; dust or grease changes the flow time.
4. Avoid air bubbles in the capillary.
5. Start and stop the watch exactly as the meniscus passes the marks, viewing at eye level.

## Viva questions

1. **State Poiseuille's law.** η = πr⁴tΔP/(8lV); flow of liquid through a capillary.
2. **Why is the same volume used for both liquids?** The pressure head and the volume flowing depend on the volume charged; equal volumes make the comparison valid.
3. **Why is the Ostwald viscometer not used for non-Newtonian liquids?** It works at a single shear rate, while their viscosity depends on shear rate.
4. **What does a capillary viscometer actually measure?** Kinematic viscosity (η/ρ), since flow is driven by the liquid's own weight.
5. **Unit of viscosity?** Poise (dyne s cm⁻²); 1 cP = 1 mPa s.
6. **Effect of temperature on liquid viscosity?** It decreases as temperature rises (η = A e^(Ev/RT)).
7. **Name an improved capillary viscometer.** The Ubbelohde (suspended-level) viscometer, whose result does not depend on the volume charged.
