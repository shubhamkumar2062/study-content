# Unit 5: CNS drugs II — general anaesthetics, analgesics and anti-inflammatory agents

This unit covers drugs that remove the sensation of pain: general anaesthetics (which remove consciousness as well), narcotic analgesics acting at opioid receptors, their antagonists, and the non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) that block prostaglandin synthesis. Starred syntheses: **halothane, methohexital sodium, ketamine, fentanyl, methadone, mefenamic acid and ibuprofen**.

## 5.1 General anaesthetics

A general anaesthetic produces loss of consciousness, loss of pain sensation, loss of reflexes and muscle relaxation, all reversible.

### Inhalation anaesthetics

These are small, volatile, highly lipid-soluble molecules. They are thought to act on **GABA-A and glycine channels** (which they open) and on **NMDA and nicotinic channels** (which they block), rather than at a classical receptor. The old **Meyer–Overton rule** states that potency runs parallel to solubility in olive oil, which still describes the general trend.

Two numbers describe them:

- **MAC (minimum alveolar concentration)**: the concentration that stops movement in half of the patients on skin incision. **Low MAC = high potency.**
- **Blood : gas partition coefficient**: a **low** value means little drug dissolves in blood, so the alveolar pressure rises quickly and **induction and recovery are fast** (desflurane, sevoflurane, nitrous oxide).

| Drug | Chemical nature | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Halothane*** | Halogenated alkane (CF3–CHBrCl) | Potent, pleasant smelling, non-irritant and non-flammable; sensitises the heart to catecholamines, relaxes the uterus, and in rare cases causes hepatitis. Stored in amber bottles with thymol as stabiliser because light causes decomposition |
| Methoxyflurane | Halogenated ether | Very potent, slow, now avoided because fluoride release harms the kidney |
| Enflurane | Halogenated ether | Can cause seizure-like EEG activity |
| Isoflurane | Isomer of enflurane | Widely used, stable, little metabolism |
| Sevoflurane | Fluorinated ether | Non-irritant, fast and smooth: the usual choice for inhalation induction in children |
| Desflurane | Fluorinated ether | Fastest recovery, but pungent and needs a heated vaporiser |

Replacing hydrogen by **fluorine** reduces flammability and metabolism; **bromine and chlorine** raise potency but also toxicity and the risk of arrhythmia. Ethers are generally safer for the heart than alkanes.

### Synthesis of halothane

1. **Trichloroethylene** is treated with **hydrogen fluoride** in the presence of an antimony halide catalyst. Two chlorines are replaced by fluorine and HF adds across the double bond, giving **1-chloro-2,2,2-trifluoroethane (CF3–CH2Cl)**.
2. This is **brominated with bromine** at high temperature (or under ultraviolet light). A hydrogen on the CH2 group is replaced by bromine.
3. The product is **2-bromo-2-chloro-1,1,1-trifluoroethane (halothane)**, purified by fractional distillation.

### Intravenous anaesthetics

**Ultra-short-acting barbiturates**: **methohexital sodium***, thiamylal sodium and thiopental sodium. Injected into a vein, they reach the brain in one circulation time because of very high lipid solubility, and the patient wakes in minutes as the drug **redistributes** into muscle and fat (not because it is metabolised). Methohexital is the methylated oxybarbiturate and is about twice as potent as thiopental, with faster recovery but more hiccup and muscle movement. The sodium salts give strongly alkaline solutions, so extravasation and intra-arterial injection are dangerous.

### Synthesis of methohexital sodium

1. **Diethyl malonate** is alkylated twice with sodium ethoxide: first with **allyl bromide**, then with the halide carrying the **1-methyl-2-pentynyl** group, giving diethyl allyl(1-methyl-2-pentynyl)malonate.
2. The substituted malonate is condensed with **N-methylurea** in the presence of **sodium ethoxide**; the ring closes with loss of ethanol, giving methohexital (an N-methyl barbiturate with a 2-oxo group).
3. Treatment with the calculated amount of **sodium hydroxide** (or sodium methoxide) gives the water-soluble **sodium salt**, supplied as a sterile powder.

### Dissociative anaesthetics

**Ketamine hydrochloride*** is an arylcycloalkylamine related to phencyclidine. It **blocks the NMDA receptor channel**, producing dissociative anaesthesia: the patient appears awake but is unresponsive, with analgesia, amnesia and preserved airway reflexes. Blood pressure and heart rate rise (useful in shock and in trauma), but emergence may bring vivid dreams or hallucinations, reduced by a benzodiazepine. The (S) enantiomer is more potent and causes fewer emergence reactions.

### Synthesis of ketamine

1. **2-Chlorobenzonitrile** is treated with **cyclopentylmagnesium bromide**; hydrolysis of the ketimine gives **(2-chlorophenyl) cyclopentyl ketone**.
2. The ketone is **brominated** on the ring carbon next to the carbonyl, giving the 1-bromocyclopentyl ketone.
3. Reaction with **methylamine** gives an intermediate hydroxyimine.
4. Heating this intermediate in a high-boiling solvent such as decalin causes a **ring expansion** (α-hydroxyimine rearrangement): the five-membered ring becomes a six-membered cyclohexanone and the nitrogen moves to the ring carbon, giving **ketamine**, isolated as the hydrochloride.

## 5.2 Narcotic (opioid) analgesics

Opioids act at **µ (mu), κ (kappa) and δ (delta)** receptors, all G-protein coupled and inhibitory: they open potassium channels, close calcium channels and reduce transmitter release. µ agonism gives analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, constipation, miosis and dependence.

### SAR of morphine and its analogues

Morphine is a pentacyclic molecule; the pharmacophore is often drawn as a "T":

```text
   aromatic ring (with phenolic 3-OH)
        │
   quaternary carbon C13
        │
   two-carbon bridge
        │
   basic nitrogen (N-methyl), protonated at body pH
```

1. The **phenolic 3-OH** is needed for high potency. Methylation of it (codeine) reduces potency about ten-fold; codeine is a prodrug, O-demethylated by CYP2D6 to morphine.
2. **Acetylation of both the 3- and 6-OH** (heroin) raises lipid solubility and speeds entry into the brain, increasing abuse potential.
3. The **6-OH** is less important; removing it or oxidising it to a ketone with saturation of the 7,8 double bond (hydromorphone, oxycodone) increases potency.
4. The **tertiary basic nitrogen** is essential. **N-methyl** gives an agonist; **N-allyl, N-cyclopropylmethyl or N-cyclobutylmethyl** converts the molecule into an antagonist or a mixed agonist–antagonist (nalorphine, naloxone, pentazocine).
5. The **ether bridge** and ring E can be removed step by step: morphinans (levorphanol), benzomorphans (pentazocine), 4-phenylpiperidines (pethidine) and finally the open-chain methadone all keep activity, showing that the minimum requirement is a basic nitrogen, a quaternary carbon and an aromatic ring held in the right geometry.
6. Only the **(−)-levo** isomer is active; the dextro isomers (dextromethorphan) keep only antitussive activity.

**Drugs**: morphine sulphate (severe pain, myocardial infarction), codeine (cough, mild pain), meperidine (pethidine) hydrochloride, anileridine hydrochloride, diphenoxylate hydrochloride and loperamide hydrochloride (antidiarrhoeals; loperamide does not enter the brain), **fentanyl citrate*** (about 80–100 times as potent as morphine, used in anaesthesia and as a transdermal patch), **methadone hydrochloride*** (long acting, oral, used in maintenance treatment of opioid dependence), propoxyphene hydrochloride, pentazocine (mixed agonist–antagonist), levorphanol tartrate.

### Narcotic antagonists

**Nalorphine hydrochloride** (N-allyl morphine, a partial agonist), **levallorphan tartrate** and **naloxone hydrochloride** (a pure antagonist, the drug used for opioid overdose; given intravenously it reverses respiratory depression within minutes, but its short action means repeated doses are needed). Naltrexone is the longer-acting oral relative.

### Synthesis of fentanyl

1. **N-Phenethyl-4-piperidone** is condensed with **aniline** to give the imine (Schiff base).
2. The imine is **reduced with sodium borohydride** to give **4-anilino-1-phenethylpiperidine**.
3. The secondary aromatic amine is **acylated with propionic anhydride** (or propionyl chloride) to give the propionanilide, **fentanyl**, isolated as the citrate.

### Synthesis of methadone

1. **Diphenylacetonitrile** is treated with **sodamide** and then with **1-dimethylamino-2-chloropropane**. Alkylation gives **4-dimethylamino-2,2-diphenylvaleronitrile**.
2. This nitrile is treated with **ethylmagnesium bromide** (Grignard reagent); the reagent adds to the nitrile carbon to give a magnesium ketimine salt.
3. **Hydrolysis with dilute acid** converts the ketimine to the ketone, giving **methadone**, isolated as the hydrochloride. The molecule has no ring other than the two phenyls, yet it folds into a shape resembling morphine's pharmacophore.

## 5.3 Anti-inflammatory agents (NSAIDs)

NSAIDs inhibit **cyclo-oxygenase (COX)**, the enzyme that converts arachidonic acid to prostaglandins and thromboxane. Reduced prostaglandin E2 means less pain, less fever and less inflammation; reduced gastric prostaglandin means ulceration, and reduced renal prostaglandin means salt and water retention. **COX-1** is constitutive (stomach, platelets, kidney), **COX-2** is induced at sites of inflammation.

Aspirin is unique: it **acetylates a serine residue** in the COX channel, so its effect is irreversible and lasts the lifetime of the platelet (7–10 days) — the reason a low dose prevents thrombosis.

| Chemical class | Drugs | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salicylates | Sodium salicylate, aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) | Analgesic, antipyretic, antiplatelet; Reye's syndrome in children with viral fever |
| Fenamates (N-arylanthranilic acids) | **Mefenamic acid***, meclofenamate | Dysmenorrhoea and mild pain; diarrhoea is common |
| Aryl and heteroaryl acetic acids | Indomethacin, sulindac (a sulphoxide prodrug), tolmetin, zomepirac, diclofenac, ketorolac | Potent; indomethacin also closes the patent ductus arteriosus; ketorolac is used for short-term severe pain |
| Arylpropionic acids ("profens") | **Ibuprofen***, naproxen, ketoprofen | Best gastric tolerance of the older NSAIDs; the (S) enantiomer is active |
| Oxicams (enolic acids) | Piroxicam, tenoxicam | Long half-life, once-daily dosing |
| Aniline derivatives | Phenacetin (withdrawn, nephrotoxic), acetaminophen (paracetamol) | Analgesic and antipyretic but almost no anti-inflammatory action; overdose causes liver necrosis treated with N-acetylcysteine |
| Pyrazolones | Antipyrine, phenylbutazone | Largely withdrawn because of agranulocytosis |

**General SAR of acidic NSAIDs**: an **acidic group** (carboxylic acid or an enol) separated by **one carbon or none** from a flat aromatic ring, with a second lipophilic ring or halogen placed out of the plane of the first. The acid binds the arginine residue at the mouth of the COX channel; the lipophilic part fills the channel.

### Synthesis of mefenamic acid

1. **2-Chlorobenzoic acid** and **2,3-dimethylaniline** are heated together in a solvent such as dimethylformamide or amyl alcohol.
2. **Copper or copper(II) acetate** is used as catalyst with **potassium carbonate** as base (Ullmann condensation). The amine nitrogen displaces the chlorine on the ring.
3. Acidification of the reaction mixture gives **N-(2,3-dimethylphenyl)anthranilic acid**, which is mefenamic acid; it is recrystallised from toluene or alcohol.

### Synthesis of ibuprofen

1. **Isobutylbenzene** is acetylated with **acetic anhydride** and **aluminium chloride** (Friedel–Crafts acylation) to give **4-isobutylacetophenone**.
2. The ketone is condensed with **ethyl chloroacetate** and **sodium ethoxide** (Darzens reaction) to give the **glycidic ester** (an epoxy ester).
3. **Hydrolysis and decarboxylation** of the glycidic ester gives the aldehyde, **2-(4-isobutylphenyl)propanal**.
4. The aldehyde is converted to the acid: it is treated with **hydroxylamine** to give the oxime, which is dehydrated to the **nitrile** and then **hydrolysed** with acid to **ibuprofen**. (Direct oxidation of the aldehyde also gives the acid.)

A shorter modern route uses hydrogenation of the same ketone to the alcohol followed by carbonylation with carbon monoxide and a palladium catalyst, producing far less waste.

## Remember

1. Low MAC means a potent anaesthetic; low blood : gas partition coefficient means a fast one.
2. Halothane is CF3–CHBrCl: non-flammable, stored with thymol in amber bottles, sensitises the heart to catecholamines.
3. Thiopental and methohexital wake the patient by **redistribution**, not metabolism.
4. Ketamine blocks the NMDA channel; it raises blood pressure and causes emergence reactions.
5. Morphine SAR: free phenolic 3-OH for potency, basic nitrogen essential, N-methyl = agonist and N-allyl/N-cyclopropylmethyl = antagonist, only the levo isomer works.
6. Aspirin acetylates COX irreversibly; paracetamol has almost no anti-inflammatory action; the active profen is the (S) enantiomer.
7. Syntheses to practise: halothane (HF then Br2), methohexital (malonate + N-methylurea), ketamine (Grignard, brominate, methylamine, ring expansion), fentanyl (piperidone + aniline, reduce, acylate), methadone (nitrile alkylation then Grignard), mefenamic acid (Ullmann), ibuprofen (Friedel–Crafts, Darzens, then to the acid).
