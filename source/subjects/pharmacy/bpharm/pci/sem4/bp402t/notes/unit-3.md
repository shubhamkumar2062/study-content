# Unit 3: Cholinergic drugs

Acetylcholine is the transmitter at all autonomic ganglia, at parasympathetic nerve endings, at the neuromuscular junction and at many brain synapses. This unit covers how it is made and destroyed, its two receptor families, drugs that copy it (parasympathomimetics), drugs that protect it (cholinesterase inhibitors), the antidote pralidoxime, and drugs that block its muscarinic receptors. Starred syntheses: **carbachol, neostigmine, ipratropium bromide, dicyclomine and procyclidine**.

## 3.1 Biosynthesis and catabolism of acetylcholine

```text
Choline (from diet and from hydrolysis of ACh, taken up by a
sodium-dependent carrier — the rate-limiting step; blocked by hemicholinium)
      +
Acetyl coenzyme A (from pyruvate in the mitochondria)
      │  choline acetyltransferase (ChAT), in the cytoplasm
Acetylcholine
      │  stored in vesicles by a vesicular transporter (blocked by vesamicol)
      │  released by exocytosis when Ca²⁺ enters (blocked by botulinum toxin)
Acetylcholine in the synapse
      │  acetylcholinesterase (AChE) — hydrolysis within milliseconds
Choline + Acetic acid   (about half the choline is taken back up)
```

**Acetylcholinesterase (true cholinesterase)** sits in the synapse and acts almost only on acetylcholine. **Butyrylcholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase)** is in plasma and liver and hydrolyses succinylcholine, procaine and many esters.

The enzyme has two parts:

- an **anionic site** (a glutamate residue) that holds the quaternary nitrogen by ionic and van der Waals forces, and
- an **esteratic site** (a serine hydroxyl helped by histidine) that attacks the carbonyl carbon.

Hydrolysis goes in two steps: choline leaves, the enzyme stays acetylated for a moment, then water frees the acetyl group and the enzyme is ready again. This mechanism explains every anticholinesterase drug: carbamates leave a **carbamoylated** enzyme that takes minutes to hours to recover, and organophosphates leave a **phosphorylated** enzyme that practically never recovers.

## 3.2 Cholinergic receptors and their distribution

| Receptor | Type | Where | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M1 | G protein (Gq) | Brain, gastric parietal cells, ganglia | Memory, acid secretion |
| M2 | G protein (Gi) | Heart | Slower rate, slower conduction |
| M3 | G protein (Gq) | Smooth muscle, glands, eye | Bronchoconstriction, gut motility, secretions, miosis, accommodation, detrusor contraction |
| M4, M5 | G protein | Brain | Central effects |
| Nm (muscle) | Ion channel | Neuromuscular junction | Skeletal muscle contraction |
| Nn (neuronal) | Ion channel | Autonomic ganglia, adrenal medulla, CNS | Ganglionic transmission, catecholamine release |

Muscarinic receptors are blocked by atropine; nicotinic receptors by tubocurarine (muscle) and hexamethonium (ganglia).

## 3.3 SAR of parasympathomimetic agents

Acetylcholine is CH3–CO–O–CH2–CH2–N⁺(CH3)3.

1. **Quaternary nitrogen** gives the strongest binding at the anionic site; the three methyl groups are the best size. Replacing one methyl with ethyl lowers activity sharply, and larger groups turn the molecule into an antagonist. A tertiary amine (pilocarpine) still works and crosses membranes better.
2. **Ethylene bridge**: exactly two carbons between the nitrogen and the ester oxygen are needed — the "five-atom rule" (N and the four atoms up to the carbonyl carbon).
3. **Ester group**: an acetate gives maximum potency; longer acyl chains lose activity. Replacing the acetyl with a **carbamate** (carbachol, bethanechol) makes the molecule resistant to cholinesterase, giving a long action.
4. **Beta-methyl group** (methacholine, bethanechol) makes the molecule resistant to hydrolysis and gives **muscarinic selectivity** (little nicotinic action).
5. **Alpha-methyl group** gives nicotinic selectivity instead.
6. The active conformation keeps nitrogen and the ester oxygen *gauche*; the (S) enantiomer of methacholine is the more active.

| Drug | Ester | Resistant to AChE? | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acetylcholine | Acetate | No | Only in eye surgery (miosis) |
| Methacholine | Acetate + β-methyl | Partly | Bronchial challenge test |
| **Carbachol*** | Carbamate | Yes | Glaucoma, bladder atony (also nicotinic) |
| Bethanechol | Carbamate + β-methyl | Yes | Urinary retention, post-operative gut atony |
| Pilocarpine | Natural tertiary amine (imidazole lactone) | — | Glaucoma, dry mouth after radiotherapy |

### Synthesis of carbachol

1. **2-Chloroethanol** is treated with **phosgene** to give **2-chloroethyl chloroformate**.
2. The chloroformate is reacted with **ammonia**, which replaces the remaining chlorine of the carbonyl group and gives **2-chloroethyl carbamate**.
3. The carbamate is heated with **trimethylamine** in a sealed vessel. The nitrogen displaces the chloride on the ethyl chain and becomes quaternary, giving **carbamoylcholine chloride (carbachol)**.

## 3.4 Cholinesterase inhibitors

**Reversible, simple competitive**: edrophonium chloride (short, used in the test for myasthenia gravis), tacrine (an old drug for Alzheimer's disease).

**Carbamates (slowly reversible)**: physostigmine (tertiary amine from the Calabar bean, enters the eye and the brain), **neostigmine*** (quaternary, used in myasthenia gravis and to reverse non-depolarising muscle relaxants), pyridostigmine (longer acting), ambenonium chloride, rivastigmine (central, for dementia).

**Organophosphates (irreversible)**: isoflurophate and echothiophate iodide (used in the eye), parathion and malathion (insecticides). Parathion and malathion are **prodrugs**: desulphuration in the liver gives the active oxon. Malathion is safe for mammals because our plasma carboxylesterases destroy it quickly, while insects cannot.

Clinical signs of organophosphate poisoning follow directly from excess acetylcholine, remembered as **DUMBBELLS**: diarrhoea, urination, miosis, bronchospasm and bradycardia, excitation of skeletal muscle, lacrimation, lethargy, salivation. Treatment is atropine (blocks muscarinic effects) plus a reactivator.

**Ageing** is the loss of one alkyl group from the phosphorylated enzyme; once ageing has occurred, no reactivator works, so pralidoxime must be given early.

### Cholinesterase reactivator: pralidoxime chloride

Pralidoxime (2-PAM) is a quaternary pyridinium aldoxime. Its charged nitrogen is attracted to the anionic site, which places the strongly nucleophilic **oxime group** next to the phosphorus atom held on the serine. The oxime pulls the phosphate away, freeing the enzyme and forming a harmless phosphorylated oxime. Being quaternary, it works only in the periphery, not in the brain, and it is useless against carbamates or after ageing.

### Synthesis of neostigmine

1. **3-Dimethylaminophenol** is treated with **dimethylcarbamoyl chloride** in the presence of a base. The phenolic –OH becomes the **dimethylcarbamate ester**.
2. The product, 3-(dimethylcarbamoyloxy)-N,N-dimethylaniline, is **quaternised** by heating with **methyl bromide** (or dimethyl sulphate for the methylsulphate salt).
3. This gives **neostigmine bromide**, a quaternary carbamate that neither enters the brain nor is absorbed well from the gut, so the oral dose is far larger than the injected dose.

## 3.5 Cholinergic blocking (antimuscarinic) agents

### SAR of cholinolytic agents

The general antimuscarinic structure is an ester (or an alcohol) of a bulky aromatic/alicyclic acid with an aminoalcohol:

```text
    Ar1                     O
      \                     ‖
       C — (OH)  and/or  — C — O — CH2CH2 — N(R)2 or N⁺(R)3
      /
    Ar2 (or cycloalkyl)
```

1. **Two bulky rings** on the carbon next to the carbonyl are needed; one must usually be aromatic and one may be cyclohexyl or thienyl. Losing one ring (as in acetylcholine itself) returns agonist behaviour.
2. A **free hydroxyl or an ether oxygen** on that carbon strengthens binding by hydrogen bonding (atropine has it; drugs without it are weaker).
3. The **cationic head** is essential. Tertiary amines (atropine, dicyclomine, benztropine) cross into the brain and the eye and are absorbed orally; **quaternary** drugs (ipratropium, propantheline, glycopyrrolate, clidinium) act only peripherally, do not cause central effects and are poorly absorbed.
4. The distance between the cationic nitrogen and the ester carbonyl should again be about two or three carbons.
5. Quaternary compounds with bulky groups add some **ganglion and neuromuscular block** at high dose.

### Solanaceous alkaloids and analogues

| Drug | Notes and uses |
| --- | --- |
| Atropine sulphate | Racemic hyoscyamine (tropic acid + tropine); mydriasis and cycloplegia, antispasmodic, premedication to dry secretions, antidote in organophosphate poisoning and in bradycardia |
| Hyoscyamine sulphate | The natural (−) isomer, about twice as active as atropine |
| Scopolamine (hyoscine) hydrobromide | Has an epoxide bridge, more lipophilic; CNS depressant, used for motion sickness as a patch |
| Homatropine hydrobromide | Mandelic acid ester; shorter mydriasis, good for refraction testing |
| **Ipratropium bromide*** | Quaternary N-isopropyl derivative; inhaled bronchodilator in COPD, no central or systemic effects |

### Synthesis of ipratropium bromide

1. **Atropine** is demethylated at the ring nitrogen (von Braun reaction with cyanogen bromide, then hydrolysis) to give **noratropine**.
2. Noratropine is **alkylated with isopropyl bromide** to give **N-isopropylnoratropine**.
3. Treatment with **methyl bromide** quaternises the nitrogen, giving **ipratropium bromide**. The permanent charge is what keeps the inhaled drug in the airway.

### Synthetic cholinergic blocking agents

| Group | Drugs | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mydriatics / cycloplegics | Tropicamide, cyclopentolate hydrochloride | Short-acting eye drops for fundus examination |
| Antispasmodics (tertiary) | **Dicyclomine hydrochloride*** | Irritable bowel, colic (also a direct smooth-muscle relaxant) |
| Antispasmodics (quaternary) | Clidinium bromide, glycopyrrolate, methantheline bromide, propantheline bromide, isopropamide iodide, tridihexethyl chloride | Peptic ulcer and gut spasm; glycopyrrolate also as premedication |
| Antiparkinsonian (central) | Benztropine mesylate, orphenadrine citrate, biperiden hydrochloride, **procyclidine hydrochloride***, ethopropazine hydrochloride | Drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms and tremor of Parkinson's disease |

Typical side effects follow from blockade: dry mouth, blurred vision, photophobia, constipation, urinary retention, tachycardia, hot dry skin, and in overdose confusion and hallucinations ("dry as a bone, blind as a bat, red as a beet, mad as a hatter"). They are contraindicated in closed-angle glaucoma and prostatic enlargement.

### Synthesis of dicyclomine hydrochloride

1. **Benzyl cyanide** is treated with **1,5-dibromopentane** in the presence of sodium amide. Both bromides are displaced by the same carbon, building a ring: the product is **1-phenylcyclohexane-1-carbonitrile**.
2. The nitrile is **hydrolysed** with acid to **1-phenylcyclohexane-1-carboxylic acid**.
3. **Catalytic hydrogenation** of the benzene ring (ruthenium or nickel catalyst, pressure) gives **bicyclohexyl-1-carboxylic acid**.
4. The acid is converted to its **acid chloride** with thionyl chloride and esterified with **2-(diethylamino)ethanol**.
5. The product, 2-(diethylamino)ethyl bicyclohexyl-1-carboxylate, is isolated as the **hydrochloride (dicyclomine)**.

### Synthesis of procyclidine hydrochloride

1. **Acetophenone**, **formaldehyde** and **pyrrolidine hydrochloride** are heated together (Mannich reaction). The product is **3-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)propiophenone**.
2. This ketone is treated with **cyclohexylmagnesium bromide** (Grignard reagent) in dry ether; the Grignard adds to the carbonyl.
3. Hydrolysis of the addition complex with dilute acid gives the tertiary alcohol **1-cyclohexyl-1-phenyl-3-(pyrrolidin-1-yl)propan-1-ol**, isolated as the hydrochloride (procyclidine). Note how the final molecule carries the two bulky rings and the hydroxyl demanded by the SAR.

## Remember

1. Choline uptake is rate-limiting; ChAT makes acetylcholine; AChE destroys it in milliseconds at an anionic plus esteratic site.
2. Five-atom rule, quaternary trimethyl head, acetate ester: change the ester to a carbamate for resistance, add a β-methyl for muscarinic selectivity.
3. Carbamates (neostigmine, physostigmine) are slowly reversible; organophosphates are irreversible and can "age".
4. Pralidoxime pulls phosphorus off the enzyme, works only peripherally and only before ageing; atropine is the companion drug in poisoning.
5. Antimuscarinic SAR: two bulky rings, an –OH or ether oxygen, a cationic nitrogen two to three carbons away. Tertiary = central and oral; quaternary = peripheral only.
6. Syntheses to practise: carbachol (chloroethyl carbamate + trimethylamine), neostigmine (carbamate then quaternise), ipratropium (demethylate, isopropylate, quaternise), dicyclomine (cyclise, hydrolyse, hydrogenate, esterify), procyclidine (Mannich then Grignard).
