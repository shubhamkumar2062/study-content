# Unit 4: CNS drugs I — sedative-hypnotics, antipsychotics and anticonvulsants

This unit covers three groups that all act on the brain but by different chemistry: benzodiazepines and barbiturates (which open or prolong chloride entry through the GABA-A channel), phenothiazines and their relatives (which block dopamine D2 receptors), and anticonvulsants (which steady over-excitable membranes). Starred syntheses: **diazepam, barbital, chlorpromazine, phenytoin, ethosuximide and carbamazepine**.

## 4.1 Sedatives and hypnotics

A **sedative** calms and reduces excitement; a **hypnotic** produces sleep. The same drug often does both: sedative at a low dose, hypnotic at a higher one.

### Benzodiazepines

The nucleus is **1,4-benzodiazepine** — a benzene ring fused to a seven-membered ring containing nitrogen at positions 1 and 4, usually with a 5-phenyl group.

```text
        7          1  2
    (Cl) ╲   N ——— N ——— C=O         1 = N-CH3 in diazepam
          ╱ ╲      │                 2 = carbonyl (lactam)
   benzene  ring   │                 3 = CH2 (OH in oxazepam, lorazepam)
          ╲       C4 = N             5 = phenyl ring (2'-F in some drugs)
           ╲ C5 —— phenyl
```

**SAR of benzodiazepines**

1. An **electron-withdrawing group at position 7** (Cl, NO2, CF3) is essential for activity; nitro at 7 gives the most potent members (clonazepam, nitrazepam).
2. Substituents at **positions 6, 8 and 9 reduce** activity.
3. The **5-phenyl ring** is needed; substitution at the **2′ (ortho) position** with F or Cl increases potency, while para substitution lowers it.
4. A **carbonyl at position 2** (or an equivalent, such as the fused triazole ring of alprazolam) is required.
5. **Alkyl at N1** (methyl in diazepam) increases activity; the group is removed in the body to give the active nordazepam.
6. A **3-hydroxyl** (oxazepam, lorazepam) allows direct glucuronidation, so these drugs have no active metabolite and are safer in liver disease and in the elderly.
7. **Saturation of the 4,5 double bond** or opening of the ring destroys activity; reduction at 4,5 is seen in some prodrugs.

**Mechanism.** Benzodiazepines bind at a site between the α and γ subunits of the GABA-A receptor and increase the **frequency** of chloride channel opening in response to GABA. They need GABA to be present, which is why they are much safer in overdose than barbiturates. Flumazenil is the specific antagonist.

**Drugs**: chlordiazepoxide (the first, long acting), **diazepam*** (anxiety, status epilepticus, muscle spasm, alcohol withdrawal), oxazepam, chlorazepate (a prodrug of nordazepam), lorazepam (status epilepticus, premedication), alprazolam (panic disorder), zolpidem (an imidazopyridine, not a benzodiazepine chemically, but acting at the same site, selective for the α1 subunit and used only for sleep).

### Synthesis of diazepam

1. Start from **5-chloro-2-(methylamino)benzophenone**.
2. Acylate the amino group with **chloroacetyl chloride**; this gives the chloroacetamide.
3. Treat with **ammonia** (or hexamine followed by hydrolysis). Ammonia replaces the chlorine to give an aminoacetamide, which immediately attacks the ketone carbon.
4. Loss of water closes the seven-membered ring and forms the C=N bond at positions 4–5, giving **diazepam** (7-chloro-1-methyl-5-phenyl-1,3-dihydro-2H-1,4-benzodiazepin-2-one).

If the starting material is 2-amino-5-chlorobenzophenone (no N-methyl), the same route gives nordazepam, which can then be methylated with dimethyl sulphate and alkali to give diazepam.

### Barbiturates

Barbiturates are 5,5-disubstituted derivatives of **barbituric acid** (malonylurea), which is itself inactive.

**SAR of barbiturates**

1. **Both hydrogens at C5 must be replaced**; the 5,5-disubstituted compound is the active form. Mono-substituted compounds are inactive.
2. **Total carbon content** of the two 5-substituents should be about **6 to 10**: fewer carbons give weak activity, many more cause convulsions and poor absorption.
3. **Branched, unsaturated or alicyclic** 5-substituents raise potency and shorten the action (secobarbital, amobarbital).
4. **Replacing the C2 oxygen by sulphur** (thiobarbiturates: thiopental, thiamylal) greatly increases lipid solubility, giving an ultra-short, immediate action after injection, ended by redistribution to fat and muscle.
5. **N-methylation at N1** (methohexital, mephobarbital) raises lipid solubility and shortens the action; methylation at both nitrogens causes convulsions.
6. Barbiturates are **weak acids** (the imide hydrogen); sodium salts are water soluble and strongly alkaline, so injections are irritant.

| Class by duration | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long (6–8 h) | **Barbital***, phenobarbital | Epilepsy (phenobarbitone), sedation |
| Intermediate | Amobarbital, butabarbital | Hypnotic |
| Short | Pentobarbital, secobarbital | Hypnotic |
| Ultra-short | Thiopental, thiamylal, methohexital | Intravenous induction of anaesthesia |

**Mechanism**: barbiturates increase the **duration** of chloride channel opening and at higher doses open the channel without GABA — the reason overdose causes fatal respiratory depression. They are strong enzyme inducers and are now mostly replaced by benzodiazepines.

### Synthesis of barbital

1. **Diethyl malonate** is treated with **sodium ethoxide** and **ethyl bromide**; the acidic methylene hydrogen is removed and ethylation occurs. Repeating the step gives **diethyl diethylmalonate**.
2. This ester is condensed with **urea** in the presence of **sodium ethoxide**. Both ester groups react with the two nitrogen atoms of urea, ethanol is eliminated and the six-membered ring closes.
3. Acidification gives **barbital (5,5-diethylbarbituric acid)**. Using phenyl instead of one ethyl group gives phenobarbital by the same route.

### Miscellaneous sedatives

- **Amides and imides**: glutethimide (a piperidinedione, structurally close to the barbiturates).
- **Alcohols and carbamates**: meprobamate (a dicarbamate, also a muscle relaxant), ethchlorvynol.
- **Aldehydes and derivatives**: paraldehyde (a cyclic trimer of acetaldehyde, with a strong smell and an exhaled route of loss), triclofos sodium (a phosphate ester prodrug of trichloroethanol, the active metabolite of chloral hydrate).

## 4.2 Antipsychotics

Antipsychotics (neuroleptics) control the hallucinations and delusions of schizophrenia, mainly by blocking **dopamine D2** receptors in the mesolimbic pathway. Blockade in the nigrostriatal pathway causes extrapyramidal symptoms, and blockade in the tuberoinfundibular pathway raises prolactin. Atypical agents (clozapine, risperidone) also block 5-HT2A and cause fewer movement disorders.

### Phenothiazines

The nucleus is a tricyclic ring: two benzene rings joined by sulphur and nitrogen.

```text
      ring A        ring B
        ╲   S(5)    ╱            Position 2: electron-withdrawing substituent
         ╲ ╱   ╲  ╱              Position 10: N with the side chain
          ╲     ╱
            N(10)
             │
             3-carbon chain — amine
```

**SAR of phenothiazines**

1. The **side chain at N10 must be three carbons** long; a two-carbon chain gives antihistaminic rather than antipsychotic activity (promethazine).
2. The chain ends in a **basic nitrogen**: dimethylamino (promazine type), piperidine (thioridazine) or **piperazine**, which gives the most potent and most extrapyramidal members (trifluoperazine, prochlorperazine).
3. A **substituent at position 2** that withdraws electrons increases potency; the order is roughly CF3 > Cl > H. The substituent must be at 2, not 3, and makes the molecule asymmetric, which matters for the fit.
4. **Branching on the side chain** (as in a β-methyl group) lowers activity.
5. Substitution at other ring positions or on the sulphur generally reduces potency; replacing sulphur with other bridges gives the ring analogues below.

**Drugs**: promazine hydrochloride, **chlorpromazine hydrochloride*** (the first antipsychotic; also antiemetic, with strong sedative, α-blocking and antimuscarinic effects), triflupromazine, thioridazine hydrochloride (piperidine type, more antimuscarinic, can prolong the QT interval), piperacetazine hydrochloride, prochlorperazine maleate (used mainly as an antiemetic and in vertigo), trifluoperazine hydrochloride (piperazine type, highly potent).

**Ring analogues**: chlorprothixene and thiothixene (thioxanthenes, where N10 is replaced by a carbon joined to the side chain by a double bond, so cis/trans isomerism appears and only the cis form is active), loxapine succinate (a dibenzoxazepine), clozapine (a dibenzodiazepine, the first atypical drug; effective in resistant cases but causes agranulocytosis, so blood counts are needed).

**Butyrophenones**: haloperidol (potent D2 blocker, high risk of extrapyramidal effects), droperidol (used in anaesthesia), risperidone (a benzisoxazole, atypical, blocks D2 and 5-HT2A).

**Beta-amino ketones**: molindone hydrochloride. **Benzamides**: sulpiride (a selective D2 blocker, poorly absorbed, with less sedation).

### Synthesis of chlorpromazine

1. **2-Chlorophenothiazine** is prepared by heating **3-chlorodiphenylamine** with **sulphur** and a trace of **iodine**: the sulphur enters both ortho positions and closes the central ring.
2. The nitrogen of the phenothiazine is deprotonated with **sodamide** (sodium amide) in a solvent such as xylene.
3. The sodium salt is alkylated with **1-dimethylamino-3-chloropropane**; the nitrogen attacks the alkyl halide, giving the three-carbon side chain.
4. The base is purified and converted to **chlorpromazine hydrochloride**.

## 4.3 Anticonvulsants

An anticonvulsant stops the abnormal, synchronous discharge of neurons that causes a seizure.

### Mechanism of anticonvulsant action

1. **Blockade of voltage-gated sodium channels** in their inactivated state, so rapidly firing neurons cannot keep firing (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, lamotrigine).
2. **Blockade of T-type calcium channels** in thalamic neurons, which stops the 3 Hz spike-and-wave of absence seizures (ethosuximide, trimethadione).
3. **Enhancement of GABA** transmission, by prolonging chloride channel opening (barbiturates), increasing the frequency of opening (benzodiazepines), or raising GABA levels (valproate, vigabatrin).
4. **Reduction of excitatory glutamate** transmission (felbamate, topiramate).
5. Gabapentin binds the α2δ subunit of calcium channels and reduces transmitter release.

### SAR of anticonvulsants

Most of the classical drugs share one pattern: a ring containing a **–CO–NH–CO–** (ureide) or related fragment, carrying **two substituents on the carbon between the carbonyls**.

```text
        R1    R2
          \  /
           C
          /  \
     CO ——      —— X        X = NH (hydantoin, succinimide)
       \       /            X = O  (oxazolidinedione)
         N — H
```

1. At least **one aryl group** on that carbon gives activity against tonic-clonic seizures (phenytoin, mephenytoin).
2. **Only small alkyl groups** on that carbon give activity against absence seizures instead (ethosuximide, trimethadione).
3. **Larger alkyl or aryl groups** raise lipid solubility and add sedation.
4. The **imide NH** is weakly acidic and probably hydrogen-bonds to the channel protein; alkylating it (mephenytoin, methsuximide) gives a prodrug that is demethylated in the body.
5. Ring size and the atom X change the spectrum: hydantoin (5-ring, one nitrogen) → tonic-clonic; oxazolidinedione and succinimide → absence.

**Classes and drugs**

| Class | Drugs | Mainly used in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Barbiturates | Phenobarbitone, methabarbital | Tonic-clonic, status; cheap and still widely used |
| Hydantoins | **Phenytoin***, mephenytoin, ethotoin | Tonic-clonic and partial seizures |
| Oxazolidinediones | Trimethadione, paramethadione | Absence (now obsolete, toxic) |
| Succinimides | Phensuximide, methsuximide, **ethosuximide*** | Absence seizures (drug of choice) |
| Ureas and monoacylureas | Phenacemide, **carbamazepine*** | Partial seizures; carbamazepine also in trigeminal neuralgia and bipolar disorder |
| Benzodiazepines | Clonazepam, diazepam, lorazepam | Status epilepticus, myoclonic seizures |
| Miscellaneous | Primidone (converted to phenobarbitone), valproic acid (broad spectrum), gabapentin, felbamate | Various |

Points worth remembering: phenytoin shows **saturation kinetics**, so a small dose rise can cause toxicity (nystagmus, ataxia), and it causes gum hypertrophy and hirsutism; carbamazepine **induces its own metabolism**; valproate is hepatotoxic and strongly teratogenic (neural tube defects).

### Synthesis of phenytoin

1. **Benzaldehyde** is converted to **benzoin** by the benzoin condensation (thiamine or cyanide catalyst).
2. Benzoin is **oxidised to benzil** with concentrated nitric acid (or copper(II) sulphate in pyridine).
3. Benzil is refluxed with **urea** in **alcoholic sodium hydroxide**. The ureide nitrogen attacks one carbonyl, and a **benzilic acid type rearrangement** shifts a phenyl group so that both phenyls end on the same carbon; the five-membered ring closes.
4. The sodium salt that separates is dissolved in water and **acidified with dilute hydrochloric acid**, precipitating **phenytoin (5,5-diphenylhydantoin)**.

### Synthesis of ethosuximide

1. **Methyl ethyl ketone (butan-2-one)** is condensed with **ethyl cyanoacetate** in the presence of a base (Knoevenagel condensation) to give the unsaturated ester, ethyl 2-cyano-3-methylpent-2-enoate.
2. **Hydrogen cyanide (potassium cyanide and acid)** adds across the double bond, giving a compound with two nitrile groups and one ester.
3. **Hydrolysis with mineral acid** converts both nitriles and the ester into carboxylic acids, and loss of carbon dioxide gives **2-ethyl-2-methylsuccinic acid**.
4. Heating the acid with **ammonia or urea** closes the ring with loss of water, giving the cyclic imide **2-ethyl-2-methylsuccinimide (ethosuximide)**.

### Synthesis of carbamazepine

1. **Iminostilbene (5H-dibenz[b,f]azepine)** is the starting material, obtained from the dehydrogenation of iminodibenzyl.
2. The ring nitrogen is treated with **phosgene** to give the **carbamoyl chloride** at N5.
3. Reaction with **ammonia** replaces the chlorine by –NH2, giving **carbamazepine (5H-dibenz[b,f]azepine-5-carboxamide)**. The urea-like carboxamide is essential: removing it abolishes anticonvulsant activity.

## Remember

1. Benzodiazepines need an electron-withdrawing group at 7, a 5-phenyl ring (2′ substitution helps) and a 2-carbonyl; they raise the **frequency** of chloride channel opening and need GABA to work.
2. Barbiturates need both C5 hydrogens replaced, 6–10 carbons in total; sulphur at C2 or methyl at N1 shortens the action; they raise the **duration** of channel opening and can act without GABA.
3. Phenothiazines: three-carbon chain at N10, basic amine at the end (piperazine = most potent), electron-withdrawing group at position 2.
4. Extrapyramidal symptoms come from nigrostriatal D2 blockade; prolactin rise from the tuberoinfundibular pathway; clozapine and risperidone are the atypical examples in this syllabus.
5. Anticonvulsant spectrum follows the substituents: aryl → tonic-clonic, small alkyl → absence.
6. Syntheses to practise: diazepam (chloroacetyl chloride then ammonia, ring closure), barbital (dialkyl malonate + urea), chlorpromazine (phenothiazine + sodamide + dimethylaminopropyl chloride), phenytoin (benzil + urea with rearrangement), ethosuximide (Knoevenagel, cyanide addition, hydrolysis, imide), carbamazepine (iminostilbene + phosgene + ammonia).
