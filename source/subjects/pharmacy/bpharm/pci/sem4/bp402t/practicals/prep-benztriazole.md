# Preparation of benztriazole

**Aim:** To prepare benztriazole (1H-benzotriazole) from o-phenylenediamine and sodium nitrite in acetic acid, and to report the yield and melting point.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Nitrous acid reactions give off nitrous fumes: work in a fume cupboard and keep the temperature controlled.

## Principle

Sodium nitrite with acetic acid generates **nitrous acid** in situ. One amino group of the ortho-diamine is diazotised, and because the second amino group is held right next to it, the diazonium nitrogen is attacked immediately by that amine. The five-membered ring with three nitrogen atoms closes in the same step, so no separate coupling component is needed.

```text
o-C6H4(NH2)2  +  HNO2   →   benztriazole  +  2 H2O
     (diazotisation followed by immediate intramolecular ring closure)
```

## Requirements

o-Phenylenediamine 5 g, glacial acetic acid 6 ml, sodium nitrite 3.5 g, water, beaker 250 ml, thermometer, ice bath, Buchner funnel, melting point apparatus.

## Procedure

1. Dissolve 5 g of o-phenylenediamine in a mixture of 6 ml of glacial acetic acid and about 15 ml of water in a 250 ml beaker, warming gently if necessary.
2. Cool the solution to about 15 °C in an ice bath.
3. Dissolve 3.5 g of sodium nitrite in 10 ml of water and add this solution **slowly, in small portions**, with constant stirring, keeping the temperature below about 20 °C.
4. After the addition, allow the mixture to stand with stirring; the temperature rises on its own and the colour deepens as the ring closes. Warm on a water bath at about 60–70 °C for 15 minutes to complete the reaction.
5. Cool the mixture thoroughly in ice; benztriazole separates as a crystalline solid.
6. Filter under suction and wash with a little cold water.
7. Recrystallise from hot water (or aqueous ethanol), dry the crystals and record the weight and melting point.

**Worked example.** 5 g of o-phenylenediamine (molecular weight 108.14) is 0.0462 mol. One mole gives one mole of benztriazole (molecular weight 119.12), so the theoretical yield is 0.0462 × 119.12 = 5.50 g. If 3.6 g is obtained, the yield is (3.6/5.50) × 100 = **65%**.

## Precautions

1. Add the nitrite solution slowly and keep the mixture cool during the addition, or the diazonium intermediate decomposes and the yield falls.
2. Use a fume cupboard; nitrous fumes are irritant.
3. Do not use mineral acid in place of acetic acid in this preparation; the mild acid favours the internal ring closure.
4. Cool well before filtering, as benztriazole is appreciably soluble in warm water.

## Viva questions

1. **How is nitrous acid produced here?** Sodium nitrite reacts with acetic acid in the flask, so the unstable acid is formed just as it is needed.
2. **Why is no coupling component added?** The second amino group of the same molecule attacks the diazonium group, giving an intramolecular ring closure.
3. **How many nitrogen atoms are in the triazole ring, and where?** Three, at positions 1, 2 and 3 of the five-membered ring fused to benzene.
4. **Why is the temperature kept low during addition?** Diazonium salts decompose above about 20 °C, giving phenols and nitrogen gas.
5. **Give one use of benztriazole.** It is a corrosion inhibitor for copper and a useful synthetic intermediate; the triazole ring itself appears in drugs such as fluconazole and in benzotriazole-based coupling reagents.
6. **How would you confirm the product?** By its sharp melting point near 99 °C and by a mixed melting point with an authentic sample.
