# Assay of atropine sulphate

**Aim:** To determine the percentage purity of atropine sulphate by non-aqueous titration with perchloric acid.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your own lab manual, the current Indian Pharmacopoeia monograph and your teacher where they differ. Atropine is a potent drug: do not taste or handle it carelessly. Perchloric acid, acetic anhydride and glacial acetic acid are corrosive.

## Principle

Atropine sulphate is the sulphate salt of a weak organic base; its molecule contains **two atropine units** joined to one molecule of sulphuric acid. In water the base is too weak to be titrated sharply. In **anhydrous (glacial) acetic acid** the basicity is enhanced and the drug can be titrated with **0.1 M perchloric acid** using crystal violet as indicator.

Sulphuric acid behaves as a weak acid in glacial acetic acid and does not interfere, so no mercuric acetate is required here (unlike a hydrochloride). Both tertiary nitrogen atoms of the two atropine molecules take up one proton each.

**Each ml of 0.1 M perchloric acid is equivalent to 67.68 mg of anhydrous atropine sulphate**, that is one-tenth of a millimole of the salt (molecular weight 676.8). In glacial acetic acid the two atropine units are already protonated; what the perchloric acid titrates is the sulphate ion, which takes up one proton to become hydrogen sulphate (HSO4⁻). So one mole of perchloric acid corresponds to one mole of the salt. Check the factor against the current Indian Pharmacopoeia monograph.

## Requirements

Atropine sulphate sample, anhydrous (glacial) acetic acid, acetic anhydride, 0.1 M perchloric acid (standardised against potassium hydrogen phthalate), crystal violet indicator, dry conical flask, burette with guard tube, analytical balance, drying oven.

## Procedure

1. Dry the sample to constant weight as directed in the monograph, since the official salt is a monohydrate and the assay is reported on the dried basis.
2. Weigh accurately about 0.5 g of the dried sample into a dry conical flask.
3. Dissolve it in about 20 ml of anhydrous acetic acid, warming gently if necessary; cool. Where directed, add a few ml of acetic anhydride to remove traces of water.
4. Add 2 to 3 drops of crystal violet indicator.
5. Titrate with 0.1 M perchloric acid until the violet colour changes to **blue-green**, or find the end point potentiometrically.
6. Carry out a blank titration and subtract it.
7. Repeat for concordant readings.

Corrected volume V = (titre − blank), in ml.

Percentage purity = (V × molarity factor × 67.68 × 100) / (weight of dried sample in mg).

**Worked example.** Dried sample 0.5050 g (505.0 mg); titre 7.65 ml; blank 0.20 ml; acid exactly 0.1 M. Corrected volume = 7.45 ml. Atropine sulphate found = 7.45 × 67.68 = 504.2 mg. Percentage purity = (504.2/505.0) × 100 = **99.8%**.

## Result

The sample of atropine sulphate contains ___ % w/w of (C17H23NO3)2,H2SO4 calculated on the dried basis (the limit is given in the current monograph, normally not less than 98.5%).

## Precautions

1. All glassware and solvents must be dry; water weakens the end point because it competes as a base.
2. Standardise the perchloric acid on the day of use and correct for temperature, as glacial acetic acid has a large coefficient of expansion.
3. Dry the sample as directed before weighing, otherwise the water of crystallisation gives a low result.
4. Run a blank and subtract it.
5. Handle the alkaloid with care; it is toxic in small quantities.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is mercuric acetate not needed here?** The counter-ion is sulphate, and sulphuric acid is only weakly acidic in glacial acetic acid, so it does not interfere; a hydrochloride would need mercuric acetate.
2. **Why is the equivalence 67.68 mg (one whole mole of salt per mole of acid) and not half of it?** The atropine units are already protonated in the salt. In glacial acetic acid the acid titrates the sulphate ion, which takes only one proton (SO4²⁻ → HSO4⁻), so one mole of perchloric acid corresponds to one mole of atropine sulphate.
3. **What chemical type is atropine?** A tropane alkaloid, the ester of tropic acid with tropine; it is the racemic form of hyoscyamine.
4. **Give two therapeutic uses of atropine sulphate.** As a mydriatic and cycloplegic in the eye, as an antispasmodic and drying agent before anaesthesia, and as an antidote in organophosphate poisoning and severe bradycardia.
5. **Which test identifies atropine chemically?** The Vitali–Morin test: the alkaloid is treated with fuming nitric acid, evaporated, and the residue with alcoholic potassium hydroxide gives a violet colour.
6. **Why is the assay reported on the dried basis?** The official substance is a hydrate, and the water content varies, so the result is corrected to the dried material for comparability.
