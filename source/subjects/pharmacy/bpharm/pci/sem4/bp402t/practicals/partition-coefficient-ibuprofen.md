# Partition coefficient of ibuprofen (octanol–water)

**Aim:** To determine the partition coefficient of ibuprofen between n-octanol and water at room temperature by the shake-flask method, using ultraviolet spectrophotometry.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where they differ.

## Principle

At constant temperature a solute distributes between two immiscible liquids so that the ratio of its concentrations is constant (**Nernst distribution law**):

**P = C(octanol) / C(water)**, reported as **log P**.

Ibuprofen is a weak acid (pKa about 4.4) that is almost insoluble in water but very soluble in octanol, so its partition coefficient is large. The aqueous phase is therefore buffered or acidified to about pH 2 so that the drug stays unionized and a true P, not a pH-dependent distribution coefficient, is measured.

Because so little drug stays in water, titration is not sensitive enough: the aqueous concentration is measured by **ultraviolet absorbance** against a calibration curve, using the wavelength of maximum absorption found by scanning a standard solution (reported near 264 nm for ibuprofen in acidic aqueous alcohol).

## Requirements

Ibuprofen, n-octanol (water saturated), 0.01 M hydrochloric acid or pH 2 buffer (octanol saturated), methanol or ethanol for standards, stoppered bottles, mechanical shaker, centrifuge, volumetric flasks, pipettes, ultraviolet spectrophotometer with matched 1 cm quartz cells, thermometer.

## Procedure

**A. Calibration curve**

1. Prepare a stock solution of ibuprofen in a little alcohol and dilute with the pH 2 aqueous phase to give at least five standards covering a suitable range (for example 5 to 50 µg/ml).
2. Scan one standard between 200 and 350 nm and note the wavelength of maximum absorption.
3. Read the absorbance of each standard at that wavelength against a blank of the same solvent, and plot absorbance against concentration. Confirm that the plot is a straight line through the origin.

**B. Partition**

4. Pre-saturate the two phases by shaking n-octanol with the aqueous phase and letting them separate, so that no volume change occurs during the experiment.
5. Weigh accurately about 50 mg of ibuprofen into a stoppered bottle; add 25.0 ml of pre-saturated n-octanol and dissolve.
6. Add 25.0 ml of the pre-saturated aqueous phase.
7. Shake on a mechanical shaker for 30 minutes at room temperature; let the layers separate, centrifuging briefly if needed.
8. Withdraw the lower aqueous layer carefully, dilute if necessary with the same aqueous phase, and read the absorbance at the chosen wavelength.
9. Read the concentration from the calibration curve, allowing for any dilution. Note the temperature and repeat for concordant results.

## Observations and calculation

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Weight of ibuprofen taken (mg) | |
| Volume of octanol (ml) | 25.0 |
| Volume of water phase (ml) | 25.0 |
| Wavelength used (nm) | |
| Absorbance of aqueous layer | |
| Concentration in water from the curve (µg/ml) | Cw |

Amount in water = Cw × volume of the water phase. Amount in octanol = total amount taken − amount in water. Then:

**C(octanol) = amount in octanol / volume of octanol**, **P = C(octanol)/Cw**, and **log P = log10 P**.

**Worked example.** 50.0 mg taken; equal volumes of 25.0 ml. The aqueous layer reads 5.0 µg/ml, so the amount in water is 5.0 × 25 = 125 µg = 0.125 mg. Amount in octanol = 50.0 − 0.125 = 49.875 mg, giving C(octanol) = 49.875/25 = 1.995 mg/ml = 1995 µg/ml. P = 1995/5.0 = **399**, log P = **2.6** under these conditions. (Values obtained in a student laboratory are lower than the literature log P for ibuprofen, which is about 4, because small errors in the very low aqueous reading have a large effect; the experiment is meant to show the method and the size of the number.)

## Precautions

1. Pre-saturate both phases, otherwise the volumes change during shaking.
2. Keep the aqueous phase at about pH 2 so that the carboxylic acid remains unionized.
3. Use matched quartz cells and the same blank for all readings.
4. Let the layers separate fully and take the aqueous sample without carrying over droplets of octanol, which scatter light and raise the reading.
5. Work within the straight-line range of the calibration curve; dilute the sample if the absorbance is too high.
6. Keep the temperature constant and note it in the result.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is a spectrophotometric method used here rather than titration?** The aqueous concentration is extremely small, so a titration would need an impracticably sensitive end point, whereas ultraviolet absorbance measures micrograms per millilitre comfortably.
2. **Why must the aqueous phase be acidic?** Ibuprofen is a weak acid; at higher pH it ionises, dissolves in water and the measured value would be the distribution coefficient D, not P.
3. **What is the relation between P and D for a weak acid?** D falls as pH rises above the pKa; P refers only to the unionized species, so D is always less than or equal to P.
4. **Why is octanol the standard solvent?** Its mixture of a polar hydroxyl group and a long hydrocarbon chain resembles the membrane lipid environment, and a large body of data uses it.
5. **What does a high log P predict for a drug?** Good membrane crossing but poor water solubility, strong plasma protein binding and storage in fat; activity usually passes through a maximum rather than rising indefinitely.
6. **State one limitation of the shake-flask method.** It needs reasonably large amounts of drug, is slow, and gives poor precision when the concentration in one phase is very small, as in this experiment.
