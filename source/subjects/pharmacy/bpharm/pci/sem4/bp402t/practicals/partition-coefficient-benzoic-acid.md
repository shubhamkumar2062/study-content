# Partition coefficient of benzoic acid (octanol–water)

**Aim:** To determine the partition coefficient of benzoic acid between n-octanol and water at room temperature by the shake-flask method.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where they differ.

## Principle

When a solute is shaken with two immiscible liquids it distributes itself between them until, at constant temperature, the ratio of its concentrations is constant (**Nernst distribution law**):

**P = C(octanol) / C(water)**, usually reported as **log P**.

n-Octanol is the reference organic phase in medicinal chemistry because its long chain and hydroxyl group resemble a membrane lipid, so log P measured in this pair predicts how readily a drug will cross membranes.

Only the **unionized** molecule partitions into octanol. Benzoic acid is a weak acid (pKa about 4.2), so the water layer is kept acidic (dilute hydrochloric acid, about pH 2) to suppress ionisation and measure the true partition coefficient rather than the pH-dependent distribution coefficient.

The benzoic acid left in the water layer is estimated by titration with standard sodium hydroxide, and the amount in the octanol layer is found by difference.

## Requirements

Benzoic acid, n-octanol (water saturated), 0.01 M hydrochloric acid (water phase, octanol saturated), 0.05 M sodium hydroxide (standardised), phenolphthalein, separating funnel 250 ml or stoppered bottles, mechanical shaker, pipettes, burette, thermometer.

## Procedure

1. Prepare the two phases by shaking n-octanol with the aqueous acid for a few minutes and letting them separate, so that each phase is already saturated with the other and no volume change occurs during the experiment.
2. Weigh accurately about 0.5 g of benzoic acid and dissolve it in 50 ml of the pre-saturated 0.01 M hydrochloric acid in a stoppered bottle.
3. Pipette 25.0 ml of this solution into each of two bottles. Keep one as the **control** (it gives the total amount taken).
4. To the second bottle add 25.0 ml of pre-saturated n-octanol.
5. Shake on a mechanical shaker for 30 minutes at room temperature and then allow the layers to separate completely (centrifuge briefly if an emulsion forms).
6. Pipette 10.0 ml of the lower aqueous layer, add a few drops of phenolphthalein and titrate with 0.05 M sodium hydroxide to a permanent pink.
7. Titrate 10.0 ml of the control solution in the same way.
8. Note the room temperature and repeat for concordant readings.

## Observations and calculation

| Bottle | Volume titrated (ml) | 0.05 M NaOH used (ml) | Concentration (mol/L) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control (before partition) | 10.0 | | C0 |
| Aqueous layer (after partition) | 10.0 | | Cw |

Concentration (mol/L) = (volume of NaOH in ml × molarity) / volume of sample in ml.

Since equal volumes of the two phases were used,

**C(octanol) = C0 − Cw**, and **P = (C0 − Cw) / Cw**, then **log P**.

If unequal volumes are used, C(octanol) = (C0 − Cw) × Vwater / Voctanol.

**Worked example.** Control: 10.0 ml needs 8.20 ml of 0.05 M sodium hydroxide, so C0 = (8.20 × 0.05)/10.0 = 0.0410 M. Aqueous layer after partition: 10.0 ml needs 2.80 ml, so Cw = 0.0140 M. Equal volumes, so C(octanol) = 0.0410 − 0.0140 = 0.0270 M. P = 0.0270/0.0140 = **1.93**, log P = **0.29** under these conditions. (Values obtained in the laboratory vary with temperature, ionisation and the degree of dimerisation in the organic phase; the literature log P for benzoic acid in octanol–water is about 1.9.)

## Precautions

1. Pre-saturate each phase with the other, or the volumes change during shaking and the calculation is wrong.
2. Keep the temperature constant; the partition coefficient changes with temperature.
3. Keep the aqueous phase acidic so that the acid stays unionized.
4. Allow the layers to separate completely and pipette the aqueous layer without carrying over any octanol, which would use extra alkali.
5. Shake for a fixed, sufficient time so that equilibrium is reached.

## Viva questions

1. **State the distribution law and its conditions.** At constant temperature a solute distributes between two immiscible liquids so that the ratio of its concentrations is constant, provided the solutions are dilute, the solvents do not react, the temperature is constant and the solute has the same molecular form in both phases.
2. **Why is n-octanol used?** Its polarity and hydrogen bonding resemble a biological membrane, so log P in octanol–water correlates well with absorption and with activity.
3. **Why is the aqueous phase acidified?** To suppress ionisation of the weak acid, since only the unionized molecule partitions; otherwise a pH-dependent distribution coefficient is measured.
4. **What is the difference between P and D?** P applies to the unionized species only, while the distribution coefficient D is the ratio of the total concentrations at a stated pH.
5. **Why may the experimental value be higher than expected?** Benzoic acid partly dimerises through hydrogen bonding in the organic phase, which raises the apparent concentration there.
6. **How is log P used in drug design?** Activity usually passes through a maximum with log P; about 2 suits drugs that must enter the brain, while very high values give poor solubility and tissue trapping.
