# How to use this guide

Pharmacology I is a learning-by-classification subject. Almost every exam answer follows the same shape: definition → classification with examples → mechanism → actions → uses → adverse effects → contraindications or interactions. Study one unit at a time: read the notes, write each classification table once from memory, draw the key diagrams (dose–response curves, G-protein pathways, catecholamine synthesis, ANS organisation), then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly; no unit has been split or joined.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with classification, mechanism and a diagram.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start with a one-line definition, then a **classification table with two examples per class**. Examiners give a large share of marks for a clean classification.
2. For a drug essay use the fixed order: mechanism, pharmacological actions system by system, uses, adverse effects, contraindications, interactions.
3. Draw what can be drawn: ANS outflow, cholinergic and adrenergic synapse, catecholamine synthesis pathway, G-protein cycle, log dose–response curves with antagonists, first- vs zero-order plots, GABA-A receptor with its binding sites.
4. Write drug names in full and correctly (no brand names). Underline the drug of choice where one exists (adrenaline in anaphylaxis, ethosuximide in absence seizures, naloxone in opioid poisoning, atropine plus pralidoxime in organophosphate poisoning).
5. Poisoning questions (organophosphate, atropine, barbiturate, morphine, methanol) are favourites: give features, mechanism and stepwise treatment.
6. Comparisons score well in short answers: competitive vs non-competitive antagonism, depolarising vs non-depolarising blockers, BZD vs barbiturate, typical vs atypical antipsychotics, first- vs zero-order kinetics, physical vs psychological dependence.
7. Doses are given only where they are standard and well known; for everything else, give mechanism and use rather than a number.
