# Unit 3: Drugs acting on the peripheral nervous system

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) controls organs we do not move by will: heart, glands, smooth muscle of gut, bronchi, bladder, eye and blood vessels. Most drugs in this unit copy or block its two transmitters, acetylcholine and noradrenaline. The unit also covers drugs acting at the skeletal neuromuscular junction, local anaesthetics, and drugs for myasthenia gravis and glaucoma.

## 3.1 Organisation and function of the ANS

The ANS has two divisions. Each pathway has two neurons: a **preganglionic** neuron from the CNS to a ganglion and a **postganglionic** neuron from the ganglion to the organ.

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outflow | Thoracolumbar (T1–L2/L3) | Craniosacral (cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X; sacral S2–S4) |
| Ganglia | Near the spinal cord (paravertebral chain) | Near or in the organ |
| Preganglionic fibre | Short | Long |
| Postganglionic fibre | Long | Short |
| Transmitter at ganglion | Acetylcholine (nicotinic) | Acetylcholine (nicotinic) |
| Transmitter at organ | Noradrenaline (except sweat glands: ACh) | Acetylcholine (muscarinic) |
| General role | "Fight or flight": spends energy | "Rest and digest": conserves energy |

The **adrenal medulla** is a modified sympathetic ganglion: preganglionic fibres release ACh on nicotinic receptors and the medulla secretes adrenaline (mainly) into blood.

**Effects on major organs**

| Organ | Sympathetic (receptor) | Parasympathetic (muscarinic) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eye – iris | Dilates pupil (mydriasis), radial muscle α1 | Constricts pupil (miosis), circular muscle M3 |
| Eye – ciliary muscle | Relaxes for far vision (β2) | Contracts: accommodation for near vision |
| Heart | ↑ rate and force (β1) | ↓ rate, ↓ AV conduction (M2) |
| Blood vessels | Constrict skin and viscera (α1); dilate skeletal muscle (β2) | Little direct innervation |
| Bronchi | Relax (β2) | Constrict, ↑ secretions (M3) |
| GI tract | ↓ motility (α2, β2); sphincters contract (α1) | ↑ motility and secretion; sphincters relax |
| Bladder | Detrusor relaxes (β2/β3); sphincter contracts (α1) | Detrusor contracts; sphincter relaxes |
| Glands | Sweat (sympathetic cholinergic) | Salivary, lacrimal, bronchial secretions increase |
| Liver | Glycogenolysis (β2, α1) | — |
| Male sex organs | Ejaculation (α) | Erection |

## 3.2 Neurohumoral transmission and co-transmission

**Neurohumoral transmission** is the passage of a nerve impulse across a synapse or neuroeffector junction by a chemical messenger (neurotransmitter).

**Steps**

1. Synthesis of transmitter and storage in vesicles.
2. Nerve impulse (action potential) reaches the terminal.
3. Ca2+ enters through voltage-gated channels; vesicles fuse and release transmitter (exocytosis).
4. Transmitter binds postsynaptic receptors → excitatory (EPSP) or inhibitory (IPSP) potential.
5. Termination: breakdown by enzymes (acetylcholinesterase), reuptake (noradrenaline uptake-1) or diffusion.

**Criteria for a substance to be a neurotransmitter:** present in the nerve terminal with its synthesising enzymes, released on stimulation, produces the same effect as nerve stimulation when applied, and has a mechanism for termination; its effects are blocked by the same antagonists.

**Co-transmission.** Many neurons release more than one transmitter. Sympathetic nerves release noradrenaline with ATP and neuropeptide Y; parasympathetic nerves release ACh with vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) or nitric oxide. Co-transmitters may act on different receptors or modulate release.

**Classification of neurotransmitters**

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Esters | Acetylcholine |
| Monoamines | Noradrenaline, adrenaline, dopamine, serotonin (5-HT), histamine |
| Amino acids | GABA, glycine (inhibitory); glutamate, aspartate (excitatory) |
| Purines | ATP, adenosine |
| Peptides | Substance P, enkephalins, neuropeptide Y, VIP |
| Gases | Nitric oxide |

## 3.3 Cholinergic transmission

**Synthesis:** choline + acetyl-CoA → acetylcholine (choline acetyltransferase). Choline uptake into the terminal is the rate-limiting step (blocked by hemicholinium). ACh is stored in vesicles (vesamicol blocks storage). Release needs Ca2+ (botulinum toxin blocks release).

**Breakdown:** acetylcholinesterase (true cholinesterase, at synapses) splits ACh into choline and acetate within milliseconds. Butyrylcholinesterase (pseudocholinesterase) in plasma and liver splits succinylcholine and some esters.

**Cholinergic receptors**

| Receptor | Location | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Muscarinic M1 | Ganglia, CNS, gastric glands | Gq → IP3/DAG |
| M2 | Heart | Gi → ↓ cAMP, opens K+ channels → bradycardia |
| M3 | Smooth muscle, glands, eye, vascular endothelium (NO release) | Gq → IP3/DAG |
| Nicotinic NN | Autonomic ganglia, adrenal medulla, CNS | Ligand-gated Na+/K+ channel |
| Nicotinic NM | Skeletal neuromuscular junction | Ligand-gated Na+/K+ channel |

## 3.4 Parasympathomimetics (cholinergic agonists)

**Classification**

1. **Direct-acting (cholinergic agonists)**
   - Choline esters: acetylcholine, methacholine, carbachol, bethanechol.
   - Alkaloids: pilocarpine, muscarine, arecoline.
2. **Indirect-acting (anticholinesterases)**
   - Reversible: physostigmine, neostigmine, pyridostigmine, edrophonium, rivastigmine, donepezil, galantamine.
   - Irreversible: organophosphates (parathion, malathion, ecothiophate, nerve gases like sarin) and carbamate insecticides (carbaryl, propoxur).

**Actions (muscarinic effects):** bradycardia and fall in blood pressure (vasodilation via NO), increased secretions (salivation, sweating, lacrimation, bronchial), bronchoconstriction, increased GI motility, contraction of the bladder detrusor, miosis and spasm of accommodation, fall in intraocular pressure.

**Mnemonic for muscarinic excess (DUMBELS):** Diarrhoea, Urination, Miosis, Bronchospasm and Bradycardia, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation and Sweating.

**Uses**

| Drug | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Pilocarpine | Glaucoma (miotic); to break adhesions with alternating mydriatic; dry mouth |
| Bethanechol | Postoperative urinary retention and paralytic ileus (not broken down by cholinesterase, little nicotinic action) |
| Neostigmine | Myasthenia gravis; reversal of competitive neuromuscular block after surgery; postoperative ileus and urinary retention |
| Pyridostigmine | Myasthenia gravis (longer acting) |
| Edrophonium | Diagnosis of myasthenia gravis (very short action) |
| Physostigmine | Atropine and anticholinergic poisoning (crosses BBB, being a tertiary amine); glaucoma |
| Donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine | Alzheimer's disease |

Neostigmine is a quaternary compound, so it does not cross the BBB and has an additional direct action on NM receptors; physostigmine is tertiary and acts centrally.

**Organophosphate poisoning**

- Cause: insecticides (very common in rural India), nerve agents.
- Mechanism: irreversible phosphorylation of cholinesterase → ACh accumulates at all cholinergic sites.
- Features: muscarinic (miosis, salivation, sweating, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm, bradycardia, vomiting, diarrhoea), nicotinic (muscle fasciculations then weakness and paralysis, tachycardia, hypertension), central (confusion, convulsions, coma, respiratory depression).
- Treatment:
  1. Remove from exposure, wash skin, remove clothes; gastric lavage if swallowed.
  2. Maintain airway, suction, oxygen, ventilation.
  3. **Atropine** IV in repeated doses until signs of atropinisation (dry secretions, clear chest, adequate heart rate); it blocks muscarinic effects only.
  4. **Cholinesterase reactivators (oximes):** pralidoxime (2-PAM) given early, before "ageing" of the phosphorylated enzyme; reverses nicotinic effects at the neuromuscular junction. Not useful in carbamate poisoning.
  5. Diazepam for convulsions.

## 3.5 Parasympatholytics (anticholinergics, antimuscarinics)

**Classification**

1. **Natural alkaloids:** atropine, hyoscine (scopolamine).
2. **Semisynthetic / synthetic**
   - Mydriatics: homatropine, cyclopentolate, tropicamide.
   - Antisecretory / antispasmodic: glycopyrrolate, propantheline, dicyclomine, hyoscine butylbromide, oxybutynin, tolterodine.
   - Antiparkinsonian: trihexyphenidyl (benzhexol), benztropine, biperiden.
   - Bronchodilators: ipratropium, tiotropium (inhaled).
   - Antiulcer: pirenzepine (M1 selective).

**Actions of atropine (competitive antagonist at muscarinic receptors)**

- **CNS:** mild stimulation at usual doses; hyoscine causes sedation and amnesia; high doses cause excitement, hallucinations, delirium (central anticholinergic syndrome).
- **Eye:** mydriasis, cycloplegia (paralysis of accommodation), loss of light reflex; can raise intraocular pressure in narrow-angle glaucoma.
- **Heart:** tachycardia (small doses may first slow the heart); improves AV conduction.
- **Secretions:** dry mouth, reduced sweating (hot, dry skin), reduced bronchial and gastric secretions.
- **Smooth muscle:** relaxes gut, bronchi, bladder (urinary retention), biliary and ureteric tracts.
- **Toxicity (atropine poisoning):** "dry as a bone, red as a beet, hot as a hare, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter" — dry mouth, flushing, fever, blurred vision, delirium. Treatment: supportive care, physostigmine in severe cases, cooling.

**Uses**

| Use | Drug |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-anaesthetic medication (dry secretions, prevent vagal bradycardia) | Atropine, glycopyrrolate |
| Eye examination (mydriasis, cycloplegia) | Tropicamide (short), cyclopentolate, homatropine; atropine for children's refraction and iritis |
| Motion sickness | Hyoscine (transdermal patch) |
| Bronchial asthma and COPD | Ipratropium, tiotropium (inhaled) |
| Intestinal and renal colic, dysmenorrhoea | Dicyclomine, hyoscine butylbromide |
| Overactive bladder, urinary urgency | Oxybutynin, tolterodine |
| Parkinsonism and drug-induced extrapyramidal effects | Trihexyphenidyl, benztropine |
| Organophosphate poisoning; bradycardia and heart block | Atropine |

**Contraindications:** narrow-angle glaucoma, enlarged prostate (urinary retention), paralytic ileus; caution in the elderly and children.

## 3.6 Adrenergic transmission

**Synthesis:** tyrosine → (tyrosine hydroxylase, rate limiting) → DOPA → (dopa decarboxylase) → dopamine → (dopamine β-hydroxylase, inside vesicles) → noradrenaline → (phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase, adrenal medulla) → adrenaline.

```text
Tyrosine → L-DOPA → Dopamine → Noradrenaline → Adrenaline
       TH        DDC          DβH            PNMT (adrenal)
```

**Storage and release:** noradrenaline is taken into vesicles by VMAT (blocked by reserpine) and released by Ca2+-dependent exocytosis.

**Termination:** mainly **reuptake (uptake-1)** into the nerve terminal (blocked by cocaine and tricyclic antidepressants); also extraneuronal uptake-2; metabolism by **MAO** (inside nerve, mitochondria) and **COMT** (extraneuronal). The main urinary metabolite is vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), raised in phaeochromocytoma.

**Adrenergic receptors**

| Receptor | G-protein | Main location and effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| α1 | Gq | Vascular smooth muscle (vasoconstriction), radial muscle of iris (mydriasis), bladder sphincter, prostate |
| α2 | Gi | Presynaptic (↓ noradrenaline release), CNS (↓ sympathetic outflow), pancreas (↓ insulin) |
| β1 | Gs | Heart (↑ rate, force, conduction), kidney (↑ renin) |
| β2 | Gs | Bronchi (dilation), skeletal muscle vessels (dilation), uterus (relaxation), liver (glycogenolysis), tremor |
| β3 | Gs | Fat (lipolysis), bladder detrusor (relaxation) |

## 3.7 Sympathomimetics (adrenergic agonists)

**Classification by mechanism**

1. **Direct-acting:** adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, dobutamine, isoprenaline, phenylephrine, salbutamol, terbutaline, clonidine.
2. **Indirect-acting** (release stored noradrenaline): tyramine, amphetamine.
3. **Mixed action:** ephedrine (shows tachyphylaxis).

**Classification by clinical use**

| Use | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Pressor agents (shock, hypotension) | Noradrenaline, dopamine, phenylephrine, mephentermine |
| Cardiac stimulants | Adrenaline, isoprenaline, dobutamine |
| Bronchodilators (β2) | Salbutamol, terbutaline, salmeterol, formoterol |
| Nasal decongestants (α) | Oxymetazoline, xylometazoline, phenylephrine, pseudoephedrine |
| CNS stimulants | Amphetamine, methylphenidate |
| Uterine relaxants (β2) | Ritodrine, isoxsuprine |
| Anorectics | Fenfluramine-type drugs (most withdrawn) |

**Adrenaline (acts on α and β receptors)**

- **Heart:** ↑ rate, force and cardiac output (β1); may cause arrhythmias.
- **Blood vessels:** constricts skin and mucosal vessels (α1), dilates skeletal muscle vessels (β2).
- **Blood pressure:** systolic rises, diastolic may fall at low doses (β2 dilation), so pulse pressure widens. **Dale's vasomotor reversal:** after an α-blocker, adrenaline produces only a fall in BP because the β2 effect remains.
- **Bronchi:** strong relaxation (β2); also reduces mucosal oedema (α).
- **Metabolic:** hyperglycaemia (glycogenolysis), lipolysis, hypokalaemia.
- **Eye:** mydriasis on local use is weak; lowers intraocular pressure.
- **Uses:** **anaphylactic shock** (drug of choice, given intramuscularly as 1 in 1000 solution), cardiac arrest, with local anaesthetics to prolong action and reduce bleeding, local haemostatic.

**Noradrenaline** acts mainly on α1 and β1 (little β2): both systolic and diastolic pressure rise; reflex bradycardia. Used in septic shock. Extravasation can cause necrosis.

**Dopamine** — dose-dependent: low doses dilate renal and mesenteric vessels (D1); moderate doses stimulate β1 (inotropic); high doses stimulate α1 (vasoconstriction). Used in cardiogenic and septic shock.

**Dobutamine** — mainly β1 inotrope with little effect on heart rate; used in acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock.

**Isoprenaline** — non-selective β agonist: tachycardia, bronchodilation, fall in diastolic pressure; used occasionally in heart block.

**Salbutamol, terbutaline** — selective β2 agonists; inhaled for acute asthma relief. Side effects: tremor, palpitation, hypokalaemia. Salmeterol and formoterol are long-acting.

**Ephedrine** — direct and indirect, orally active, crosses BBB; used for hypotension during spinal anaesthesia.

**Phenylephrine** — selective α1: nasal decongestant, mydriatic without cycloplegia, raises BP.

**Clonidine** — central α2 agonist: lowers BP; also used in opioid withdrawal. Sudden withdrawal gives rebound hypertension.

## 3.8 Sympatholytics (adrenergic blockers)

### α-Adrenergic blockers

| Group | Drugs | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Non-selective, irreversible | Phenoxybenzamine | Long-lasting; phaeochromocytoma (preoperative) |
| Non-selective, reversible | Phentolamine, tolazoline | Phaeochromocytoma diagnosis and crisis; extravasation of noradrenaline |
| Selective α1 | Prazosin, terazosin, doxazosin | Hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) |
| Uroselective α1A | Tamsulosin, silodosin | BPH (little effect on BP) |
| α2 | Yohimbine | Research use |
| Ergot alkaloids | Ergotamine (partial agonist) | Migraine |

**Effects:** fall in BP, postural hypotension, reflex tachycardia, nasal stuffiness, failure of ejaculation. Prazosin causes a "first-dose effect" (sudden postural hypotension), so the first dose is small and taken at bedtime.

### β-Adrenergic blockers

| Group | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Non-selective (β1 + β2) | Propranolol, timolol, nadolol, sotalol, pindolol (with partial agonist activity) |
| Cardioselective (β1) | Atenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol, esmolol, nebivolol |
| With additional α-blocking action | Labetalol, carvedilol |

**Actions:** ↓ heart rate, force and cardiac output; ↓ renin release; ↓ BP over time; ↓ myocardial oxygen demand; antiarrhythmic; ↓ intraocular pressure (reduced aqueous formation); bronchoconstriction (β2 block) in asthmatics; mask warning signs of hypoglycaemia (tremor, palpitation).

**Uses:** hypertension, angina, arrhythmias, after myocardial infarction, chronic stable heart failure (carvedilol, metoprolol, bisoprolol), glaucoma (timolol eye drops), migraine prophylaxis, essential tremor, performance anxiety, thyrotoxicosis symptoms, phaeochromocytoma (after α-blockade).

**Adverse effects and contraindications:** bradycardia, heart block, worsening of acute heart failure, bronchospasm (contraindicated in asthma), cold extremities, fatigue, masking of hypoglycaemia, rebound angina on sudden withdrawal (taper the dose).

### Adrenergic neuron blockers and others

- Reserpine depletes noradrenaline stores (blocks VMAT); can cause depression.
- Guanethidine blocks release; methyldopa (prodrug → α-methylnoradrenaline, central α2 agonist) is used for hypertension in pregnancy.

## 3.9 Neuromuscular blocking agents and peripheral skeletal muscle relaxants

**Skeletal muscle relaxants** reduce muscle tone. Peripherally acting ones work at the neuromuscular junction or inside the muscle; centrally acting ones are covered in Unit 4.

**Classification of peripherally acting relaxants**

1. **Neuromuscular blockers**
   - **Non-depolarising (competitive):** d-tubocurarine, pancuronium, vecuronium, atracurium, cisatracurium, rocuronium.
   - **Depolarising:** succinylcholine (suxamethonium), decamethonium.
2. **Directly acting:** dantrolene (blocks Ca2+ release from sarcoplasmic reticulum via ryanodine receptor).
3. **Presynaptic release blockers:** botulinum toxin.

**Comparison**

| Feature | Non-depolarising (d-tubocurarine) | Depolarising (succinylcholine) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Competitive block of NM receptors | Persistent depolarisation then desensitisation (phase I, then phase II block) |
| Initial fasciculations | Absent | Present |
| Effect of neostigmine | Reverses block | Worsens phase I block |
| Onset / duration | Slower, longer (except rocuronium onset) | Fast onset (about 1 min), short (about 5 min) |
| Metabolism | Varies (atracurium by Hofmann elimination) | Plasma pseudocholinesterase |
| Special problems | Histamine release (tubocurarine: hypotension, bronchospasm) | Muscle pain, hyperkalaemia, raised intraocular pressure, malignant hyperthermia, prolonged apnoea in atypical pseudocholinesterase |

**Order of paralysis:** small fast muscles (eye, fingers) first, then limbs, neck and trunk, and finally the diaphragm; recovery is in reverse order. Consciousness and pain are not affected, so these drugs are always used with general anaesthesia.

**Uses:** muscle relaxation during surgery; endotracheal intubation (succinylcholine or rocuronium); electroconvulsive therapy (to prevent injury); mechanical ventilation.

**Dantrolene:** used for malignant hyperthermia (drug of choice) and spasticity.
**Botulinum toxin:** blepharospasm, spasticity, cosmetic use.

## 3.10 Local anaesthetic agents

**Local anaesthetics (LAs)** reversibly block nerve conduction when applied locally, producing loss of sensation in a limited area without loss of consciousness.

**Classification**

| Group | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Esters | Cocaine, procaine, chloroprocaine, tetracaine (amethocaine), benzocaine |
| Amides | Lignocaine (lidocaine), bupivacaine, ropivacaine, prilocaine, mepivacaine, dibucaine |
| By use | Surface: lignocaine, benzocaine, tetracaine; injectable: lignocaine, bupivacaine; spinal: bupivacaine (hyperbaric), lignocaine |

Esters are broken down by plasma pseudocholinesterase (short action; allergy via para-aminobenzoic acid); amides are metabolised in the liver (longer action, rarely allergenic).

**Mechanism:** LAs are weak bases. The un-ionised form crosses the nerve membrane; inside, the ionised form blocks **voltage-gated Na+ channels** from the inner side, so no action potential can start. Small, myelinated and pain fibres are blocked first; the order of loss is pain → temperature → touch → pressure, and motor function is lost last. In inflamed tissue (acidic pH) LAs work poorly because more drug is ionised and cannot enter the nerve.

**Why add adrenaline?** It constricts vessels, so it slows absorption, prolongs action, reduces systemic toxicity and gives a bloodless field. Avoid it at end-arteries (fingers, toes, penis, ear lobe) because of the risk of gangrene.

**Techniques:** surface (topical), infiltration, nerve block (field or conduction block), spinal (subarachnoid), epidural, intravenous regional (Bier's block).

**Adverse effects:** CNS (light-headedness, tremor, convulsions, then depression); cardiovascular (bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmia; bupivacaine is most cardiotoxic); allergy (esters); methaemoglobinaemia (prilocaine, benzocaine); headache after spinal anaesthesia.

**Lignocaine** is also a class IB antiarrhythmic for ventricular arrhythmias.
**Cocaine** blocks noradrenaline reuptake: vasoconstriction, euphoria, abuse potential; used only topically.

## 3.11 Drugs used in myasthenia gravis

**Myasthenia gravis** is an autoimmune disease: antibodies against nicotinic NM receptors reduce their number, causing muscle weakness and fatigability (ptosis, diplopia, difficulty in swallowing and breathing).

**Diagnosis:** edrophonium (Tensilon) test — brief improvement in strength; antibody test; nerve conduction studies.

**Treatment**

1. **Anticholinesterases:** neostigmine, pyridostigmine (mainstay) — more ACh available at the remaining receptors.
2. **Immunosuppressants:** prednisolone, azathioprine, mycophenolate, cyclosporine.
3. **Thymectomy** in suitable patients.
4. **Plasmapheresis** and **IV immunoglobulin** for crises.

**Myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis:** too little drug (myasthenic) and too much drug (cholinergic: weakness from persistent depolarisation, with muscarinic signs) both cause weakness. Edrophonium improves myasthenic crisis but worsens cholinergic crisis. Drugs that worsen myasthenia: aminoglycosides, neuromuscular blockers, some β-blockers.

## 3.12 Drugs used in glaucoma

**Glaucoma** is a group of eye diseases with raised intraocular pressure (IOP), damage to the optic nerve and loss of visual field. Aqueous humour is formed by the ciliary body and drains through the trabecular meshwork (canal of Schlemm) and the uveoscleral route.

- **Open-angle (chronic simple) glaucoma:** drainage angle open but outflow reduced; slow, painless.
- **Angle-closure (narrow-angle) glaucoma:** iris blocks the angle; acute painful attack; an emergency. Mydriatics (atropine) can precipitate it.

| Group | Drugs | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| β-blockers | Timolol, betaxolol | ↓ aqueous formation |
| Prostaglandin F2α analogues | Latanoprost, travoprost, bimatoprost | ↑ uveoscleral outflow (first line in open-angle; side effects: iris pigmentation, eyelash growth) |
| α2 agonists | Brimonidine, apraclonidine | ↓ formation, ↑ outflow |
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | Dorzolamide, brinzolamide (topical); acetazolamide (oral) | ↓ aqueous formation |
| Miotics (cholinergic) | Pilocarpine | Contract ciliary muscle and constrict pupil → open meshwork, ↑ outflow |
| Adrenergic agonists | Dipivefrine (adrenaline prodrug) | ↑ outflow |
| Osmotic agents | Mannitol (IV), glycerol (oral) | Draw fluid from the eye (acute attack) |

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma treatment:** IV mannitol and acetazolamide to lower IOP fast, topical pilocarpine and timolol, then laser iridotomy or surgery.

## Remember

- Sympathetic: thoracolumbar, short pre / long postganglionic, noradrenaline; parasympathetic: craniosacral, long pre / short post, ACh.
- M2 heart (Gi); M1, M3 Gq. NN ganglia, NM muscle.
- Organophosphate poisoning: atropine for muscarinic signs + pralidoxime early for nicotinic signs.
- Atropine: dry mouth, tachycardia, mydriasis, cycloplegia, urinary retention; contraindicated in narrow-angle glaucoma.
- Adrenaline is the drug of choice in anaphylaxis (IM, 1 in 1000). Dale's vasomotor reversal after α-blockade.
- Prazosin first-dose hypotension; tamsulosin for BPH; β-blockers contraindicated in asthma, taper on stopping.
- Succinylcholine: fasciculations, short action, pseudocholinesterase; competitive blockers reversed by neostigmine.
- LAs block Na+ channels from inside; esters vs amides; adrenaline prolongs action but not at end-arteries; bupivacaine most cardiotoxic.
- Myasthenia: pyridostigmine/neostigmine; edrophonium test. Glaucoma: timolol, latanoprost, pilocarpine, acetazolamide, mannitol.
