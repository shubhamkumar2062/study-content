# Unit 4: Drugs acting on the central nervous system (part 1)

Most CNS drugs act by changing the release, action or removal of a few key transmitters in the brain and spinal cord. This unit first describes those transmitters and then covers general anaesthetics and pre-anaesthetic medication, sedative–hypnotics, centrally acting muscle relaxants, antiepileptic drugs, and alcohols with disulfiram.

## 4.1 Neurohumoral transmission in the CNS

CNS transmission follows the same steps as in the periphery (synthesis, storage, release, receptor action, termination), but the brain uses many more transmitters and most neurons receive both excitatory and inhibitory inputs. The balance decides whether a neuron fires.

**Inhibitory and excitatory amino acids**

| Transmitter | Receptors | Role | Drugs acting on the system |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) — main inhibitory transmitter of the brain | GABA-A (ligand-gated Cl− channel); GABA-B (GPCR, Gi) | Opening of Cl− channels hyperpolarises neurons → inhibition | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, propofol, alcohol (GABA-A); baclofen (GABA-B agonist); vigabatrin (blocks GABA breakdown); tiagabine (blocks GABA uptake) |
| Glycine — main inhibitory transmitter of the spinal cord and brainstem | Glycine receptor (Cl− channel); also co-agonist at NMDA receptors | Inhibition of motor neurons | Strychnine blocks glycine receptors (convulsions); tetanus toxin blocks glycine release |
| Glutamate — main excitatory transmitter | Ionotropic: NMDA, AMPA, kainate; metabotropic (mGluR) | Learning, memory; excess causes excitotoxic neuronal death | Ketamine and memantine (NMDA blockers); lamotrigine reduces glutamate release |

**GABA synthesis:** glutamate → GABA by glutamic acid decarboxylase (needs pyridoxal phosphate). GABA is broken down by GABA-transaminase.

**Monoamines**

| Transmitter | Main pathways and roles | Drug relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dopamine | Nigrostriatal (movement), mesolimbic and mesocortical (emotion, reward, thought), tuberoinfundibular (inhibits prolactin), chemoreceptor trigger zone (vomiting) | Parkinson's disease (loss of nigrostriatal dopamine), schizophrenia (excess mesolimbic activity), antipsychotics (D2 block), levodopa, antiemetics |
| Serotonin (5-HT) | Raphe nuclei to cortex, limbic system and spinal cord; mood, sleep, appetite, pain, vomiting | SSRIs and other antidepressants, triptans (5-HT1B/1D), ondansetron (5-HT3 block), hallucinogens (LSD at 5-HT2A) |
| Noradrenaline | Locus coeruleus; arousal, attention, mood | Antidepressants, CNS stimulants, clonidine |
| Acetylcholine | Basal forebrain to cortex; memory and learning; striatal interneurons | Alzheimer's disease (cholinesterase inhibitors), anticholinergics in parkinsonism |
| Histamine | Hypothalamus; wakefulness | Sedation by H1 antihistamines |

**Other CNS mediators:** opioid peptides (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins), substance P, adenosine (caffeine blocks it), nitric oxide, endocannabinoids.

## 4.2 General anaesthetics

**General anaesthetics (GAs)** produce a reversible loss of consciousness and of all sensation, so that surgery can be done without pain. A complete anaesthetic state includes unconsciousness, analgesia, amnesia, muscle relaxation and loss of reflexes. In practice this is achieved by "balanced anaesthesia": a combination of drugs, each for one aim.

**Classification**

| Group | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Inhalational – gases | Nitrous oxide |
| Inhalational – volatile liquids | Ether (historical), halothane, isoflurane, desflurane, sevoflurane, enflurane |
| Intravenous – fast-acting inducing agents | Thiopentone sodium, propofol, etomidate |
| Intravenous – slower-acting | Ketamine (dissociative anaesthesia), benzodiazepines (midazolam, diazepam), opioids (fentanyl; neuroleptanalgesia with droperidol) |

**Mechanism.** Most GAs enhance the inhibitory GABA-A receptor (propofol, thiopentone, etomidate, volatile agents); ketamine and nitrous oxide block NMDA (glutamate) receptors. The older idea (Meyer–Overton) that potency matches lipid solubility still holds as a correlation.

**Stages of anaesthesia (Guedel, seen with ether)**

| Stage | Name | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Analgesia | Pain lost, consciousness kept, from start of inhalation to loss of consciousness |
| II | Delirium (excitement) | Unconscious but excited; irregular breathing, struggling, vomiting; dangerous stage to pass quickly |
| III | Surgical anaesthesia (planes 1–4) | Regular breathing, loss of reflexes, muscle relaxation; surgery done in plane 2–3 |
| IV | Medullary paralysis | Respiratory and vasomotor centres fail; death unless reversed |

Modern fast-acting agents pass stage II so quickly that the stages are rarely seen.

**Inhalational agents: important terms**

- **Minimum alveolar concentration (MAC):** the alveolar concentration at which 50% of patients do not move in response to a standard surgical incision. Lower MAC = more potent (halothane low MAC; nitrous oxide very high MAC, so it is weak).
- **Blood:gas partition coefficient:** a low value means low blood solubility, so faster induction and recovery (desflurane, sevoflurane, nitrous oxide); a high value means slow (ether).
- **Second gas effect and diffusion hypoxia** occur with nitrous oxide; give 100% oxygen for a few minutes at the end to avoid diffusion hypoxia.

**Individual agents**

| Agent | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| Nitrous oxide | Laughing gas; good analgesia, weak anaesthetic; used as carrier gas and in dental and labour analgesia (with oxygen); prolonged use causes megaloblastic anaemia (inactivates vitamin B12) |
| Ether | Irritant, inflammable, explosive; slow; no longer used in most places |
| Halothane | Potent, non-irritant, pleasant; sensitises heart to adrenaline (arrhythmias); hepatitis on repeated use; uterine relaxation; malignant hyperthermia |
| Isoflurane | Widely used; stable cardiac rhythm; pungent |
| Sevoflurane | Sweet smell, non-irritant, rapid induction and recovery; good for children; mask induction |
| Desflurane | Very rapid recovery; irritant, so not for induction |
| Thiopentone sodium | Ultra-short-acting barbiturate; induction within one arm–brain circulation time; short action due to redistribution; poor analgesic; laryngospasm; can precipitate porphyria; very irritant if injected outside the vein or into an artery |
| Propofol | Most used inducing agent; smooth rapid recovery, little hangover, antiemetic; pain on injection; hypotension |
| Etomidate | Cardiovascular stability (useful in heart disease); suppresses adrenal steroid synthesis |
| Ketamine | Dissociative anaesthesia (patient seems awake but feels no pain); strong analgesia; raises BP and heart rate; bronchodilator; emergence hallucinations (reduced by benzodiazepines); useful in shock, asthma and short procedures |

**Complications of GA:** respiratory depression, aspiration of vomitus, laryngospasm, cardiac arrhythmias, hypotension, hypothermia, malignant hyperthermia, postoperative nausea and vomiting, delirium.

## 4.3 Pre-anaesthetic medication

**Pre-anaesthetic medication** is the use of drugs before general anaesthesia to make anaesthesia safer and more pleasant.

| Aim | Drug group | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relieve anxiety, give sedation and amnesia | Benzodiazepines | Midazolam, diazepam, lorazepam |
| Analgesia (pre-existing pain, smoother induction) | Opioids | Morphine, pethidine, fentanyl |
| Dry secretions; prevent vagal bradycardia and laryngospasm | Anticholinergics | Atropine, glycopyrrolate, hyoscine |
| Prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting | Antiemetics | Ondansetron, metoclopramide, domperidone |
| Reduce gastric acidity and volume (prevent aspiration pneumonia) | H2 blockers, proton pump inhibitors | Ranitidine (formerly), famotidine, pantoprazole |
| Prevent allergic reactions | Antihistamines | Promethazine |

## 4.4 Sedatives and hypnotics

A **sedative** reduces anxiety and excitement and calms the patient without inducing sleep. A **hypnotic** produces drowsiness and sleep resembling natural sleep. Many drugs are sedative in small doses and hypnotic in larger doses; still larger doses cause anaesthesia, coma and death (barbiturates). Benzodiazepines do not usually go beyond sleep at high oral doses, which makes them much safer.

```text
Barbiturates:   sedation → hypnosis → anaesthesia → coma → death (steep curve)
Benzodiazepines: sedation → hypnosis → (rarely beyond, plateau)
```

**Classification**

| Group | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Benzodiazepines (BZDs) | Diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam, clonazepam, chlordiazepoxide, nitrazepam, flurazepam, midazolam, temazepam |
| Barbiturates | Long acting: phenobarbitone; short acting: butobarbitone, pentobarbitone; ultra-short: thiopentone |
| Non-BZD hypnotics ("Z drugs") | Zolpidem, zaleplon, zopiclone, eszopiclone |
| Melatonin receptor agonists | Ramelteon, melatonin |
| Orexin receptor antagonists | Suvorexant |
| Others | Chloral hydrate, triclofos, antihistamines (promethazine, diphenhydramine), sedative antidepressants |

**Benzodiazepines**

- **Mechanism:** bind a specific site on the GABA-A receptor (between α and γ subunits) and **increase the frequency of Cl− channel opening** in the presence of GABA; they need GABA to act.
- **Actions:** anxiolysis, sedation, hypnosis (shorter sleep latency, fewer awakenings, less REM), anticonvulsant, central muscle relaxation, anterograde amnesia.
- **Uses:** anxiety disorders, insomnia (short-term), status epilepticus (lorazepam or diazepam IV), febrile convulsions (rectal diazepam), alcohol withdrawal (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam), pre-anaesthetic and conscious sedation (midazolam), muscle spasm, panic disorder (alprazolam, clonazepam).
- **Adverse effects:** drowsiness, impaired psychomotor skill (danger when driving), confusion in the elderly, tolerance, dependence and withdrawal (anxiety, insomnia, seizures), additive depression with alcohol.
- **Antagonist:** **flumazenil** (competitive BZD-site antagonist) reverses BZD overdose and sedation.

**Barbiturates**

- **Mechanism:** bind a separate site on GABA-A and **increase the duration of Cl− channel opening**; at high doses they open the channel directly without GABA and also block glutamate (AMPA) receptors — hence the steep dose curve and deep depression.
- **Disadvantages:** low therapeutic index, respiratory depression, strong enzyme induction (drug interactions), tolerance and dependence, REM suppression and rebound, precipitate acute intermittent porphyria (contraindicated).
- **Current uses:** phenobarbitone in epilepsy and neonatal seizures and neonatal jaundice; thiopentone for induction of anaesthesia.
- **Barbiturate poisoning:** coma, respiratory depression, hypotension, shock. No antidote. Treatment: maintain airway and ventilation, gastric lavage or activated charcoal, support circulation, **alkalinisation of urine** with sodium bicarbonate (speeds phenobarbitone excretion), haemodialysis in severe cases.

**Comparison: BZD vs barbiturate**

| Feature | Benzodiazepines | Barbiturates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effect on GABA-A | ↑ frequency of opening; need GABA | ↑ duration; can act without GABA at high doses |
| Safety in overdose | Wide margin | Narrow; fatal respiratory depression |
| Enzyme induction | Negligible | Marked |
| REM suppression | Less | More |
| Antidote | Flumazenil | None |
| Abuse and dependence | Less | More |

**Zolpidem and zopiclone:** act at the BZD site (α1 subunit preferring); hypnotic with little anticonvulsant or muscle relaxant action; short-acting, less hangover; can be reversed by flumazenil.

## 4.5 Centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxants

These drugs reduce muscle tone by depressing spinal and supraspinal polysynaptic reflexes, without affecting the neuromuscular junction or consciousness (at usual doses).

| Group | Drugs | Mechanism / notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mephenesin congeners | Mephenesin, carisoprodol, chlorzoxazone, methocarbamol | Depress polysynaptic reflexes in spinal cord |
| Benzodiazepines | Diazepam | GABA-A facilitation |
| GABA-B agonist | Baclofen | Reduces excitatory transmitter release in spinal cord; spasticity in multiple sclerosis and spinal injury |
| Central α2 agonist | Tizanidine | Reduces release of excitatory transmitters |
| Others | Thiocolchicoside, tolperisone, cyclobenzaprine | Muscle spasm |

**Uses:** acute muscle spasm (sprains, backache, often combined with analgesics), spasticity (cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, spinal injury), tetanus (diazepam), orthopaedic procedures.
**Side effects:** drowsiness, weakness, dizziness; additive with alcohol.

**Central vs peripheral relaxants:** central drugs act on the spinal cord, are given orally, cause sedation and reduce tone in spasm; peripheral (neuromuscular) blockers act at the end-plate, are given IV, cause paralysis and need ventilation.

## 4.6 Antiepileptic (anticonvulsant) drugs

**Epilepsy** is a chronic disorder with recurrent unprovoked seizures due to abnormal, excessive, synchronous discharge of a group of brain neurons.

**Types of seizures**

| Type | Features | Drugs of choice (common) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generalised tonic–clonic (grand mal) | Loss of consciousness, tonic then clonic jerks, postictal sleep | Valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam |
| Absence (petit mal) | Brief loss of awareness, staring, mostly children; 3 Hz spike-wave EEG | Ethosuximide, valproate |
| Myoclonic | Brief shock-like jerks | Valproate, levetiracetam, clonazepam |
| Focal (partial) — simple or complex, with or without secondary generalisation | Starts in one area of the brain | Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, phenytoin |
| Infantile spasms | West syndrome | ACTH, vigabatrin |
| Status epilepticus | Continuous seizure or repeated seizures without recovery, an emergency | IV lorazepam or diazepam, then fosphenytoin/phenytoin, valproate or levetiracetam; then phenobarbitone or anaesthesia |

**Classification by mechanism**

| Mechanism | Drugs |
| --- | --- |
| Block voltage-gated Na+ channels (prolong inactivated state) | Phenytoin, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, valproate (partly), topiramate, lacosamide |
| Block T-type Ca2+ channels in thalamus | Ethosuximide, valproate (absence seizures) |
| Enhance GABA action | Benzodiazepines, phenobarbitone (GABA-A); vigabatrin (inhibits GABA-transaminase); tiagabine (blocks GABA uptake); valproate (↑ GABA) |
| Reduce glutamate action / release | Lamotrigine, topiramate (AMPA), felbamate (NMDA) |
| Bind synaptic vesicle protein SV2A | Levetiracetam, brivaracetam |
| Bind α2δ subunit of Ca2+ channels | Gabapentin, pregabalin |

**Important drugs**

- **Phenytoin:** Na+ channel blocker; follows saturation (zero-order) kinetics at higher levels, so small dose increases give large rises in plasma level; enzyme inducer. Adverse effects: gum hypertrophy, hirsutism, coarse facies, ataxia and nystagmus (toxicity), megaloblastic anaemia (folate deficiency), osteomalacia, fetal hydantoin syndrome. Also an antiarrhythmic.
- **Carbamazepine:** Na+ channel blocker; drug of choice for focal seizures and **trigeminal neuralgia**; also mood stabiliser. Autoinduction; enzyme inducer. Adverse effects: diplopia, ataxia, hyponatraemia, rashes including Stevens–Johnson syndrome (higher risk with HLA-B*1502, found in some Asian populations), aplastic anaemia, teratogenic.
- **Sodium valproate:** broad spectrum (generalised, absence, myoclonic, focal); also mania and migraine prophylaxis. Enzyme inhibitor. Adverse effects: weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity (risk in young children), pancreatitis, **neural tube defects** (avoid in pregnancy where possible).
- **Ethosuximide:** absence seizures only (T-type Ca2+ block).
- **Phenobarbitone:** cheap, effective for generalised tonic–clonic and neonatal seizures; sedation, behavioural problems in children.
- **Lamotrigine:** broad spectrum, mood stabiliser; serious rash risk (slow dose increase, especially with valproate).
- **Levetiracetam:** broad spectrum, few interactions, irritability.
- **Gabapentin, pregabalin:** add-on in focal seizures; neuropathic pain.

**Principles of treatment:** start with one drug at low dose and increase slowly; choose by seizure type; check adherence; monitor levels for phenytoin; withdraw slowly (sudden stopping can cause status epilepticus); counsel women about pregnancy and contraception (enzyme inducers reduce pill efficacy; folic acid supplements).

## 4.7 Alcohols

**Ethyl alcohol (ethanol)** is the most widely used CNS depressant.

**Pharmacokinetics:** absorbed rapidly from stomach and small intestine; distributed in total body water; about 90% metabolised in the liver; the rest excreted in breath, urine and sweat (basis of breath tests).

```text
Ethanol --(alcohol dehydrogenase)--> Acetaldehyde --(aldehyde dehydrogenase)--> Acetic acid → CO2 + H2O
                 (also microsomal ethanol-oxidising system, induced in chronic drinkers)
```

Ethanol follows **zero-order kinetics** at usual drinking levels: a fixed amount is removed per hour regardless of the blood level.

**Actions**

- **CNS:** progressive depression; first loss of inhibition (appears stimulant), then impaired judgment, slurred speech, ataxia, stupor, coma and respiratory depression.
- **CVS:** cutaneous vasodilation (flushing, feeling of warmth but more heat loss); chronic heavy use causes hypertension and cardiomyopathy.
- **GIT:** increases gastric secretion; high concentration causes gastritis.
- **Kidney:** diuresis (inhibits ADH).
- **Liver (chronic):** fatty liver, alcoholic hepatitis, cirrhosis.
- **Others:** hypoglycaemia, nutritional deficiency (thiamine → Wernicke's encephalopathy and Korsakoff psychosis), fetal alcohol syndrome, enzyme induction.

**Uses:** antiseptic (70% ethanol is most effective for skin), solvent, rubefacient, to prevent bedsores, in **methanol poisoning** (competes for alcohol dehydrogenase), nerve block (absolute alcohol injection for trigeminal neuralgia).

**Acute alcohol intoxication:** supportive care, maintain airway, IV glucose with thiamine, correct dehydration.

**Alcohol withdrawal:** tremor, sweating, anxiety, insomnia, seizures and delirium tremens; treated with long-acting benzodiazepines (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam) and thiamine.

**Methyl alcohol (methanol) poisoning**

- Source: adulterated or illicit liquor ("hooch tragedies"), industrial spirit.
- Metabolised by alcohol dehydrogenase to **formaldehyde** and **formic acid**, which cause severe metabolic acidosis and damage to the retina and optic nerve (blindness).
- Features: headache, vomiting, abdominal pain, breathlessness (acidosis), blurred vision, coma.
- Treatment: correct acidosis with IV sodium bicarbonate; **ethanol** or **fomepizole** to block alcohol dehydrogenase so methanol is excreted unchanged; haemodialysis; folinic acid to speed formate removal.

## 4.8 Disulfiram and drugs for alcohol dependence

**Disulfiram** irreversibly inhibits **aldehyde dehydrogenase**. If the patient drinks alcohol, acetaldehyde accumulates and produces the unpleasant **disulfiram–ethanol (acetaldehyde) reaction**: flushing, throbbing headache, palpitation, nausea, vomiting, sweating, hypotension, chest pain and anxiety. Fear of this reaction helps motivated patients stay abstinent (aversion therapy).

- Must be started only after at least a day without alcohol, with the patient's full knowledge and consent.
- Effect lasts days after stopping because new enzyme must be made.
- Interactions: enzyme inhibitor (raises levels of warfarin, phenytoin).
- **Disulfiram-like reaction** with other drugs taken with alcohol: metronidazole, tinidazole, cefoperazone, chlorpropamide, griseofulvin.

**Other drugs for alcohol dependence:** naltrexone (opioid antagonist, reduces craving and the pleasant effect), acamprosate (acts on glutamate/GABA systems, reduces craving), topiramate.

## Remember

- GABA (brain) and glycine (spinal cord) inhibit; glutamate excites via NMDA and AMPA.
- MAC measures potency of inhalational agents; low blood:gas coefficient = fast induction and recovery.
- Thiopentone short action by redistribution; propofol commonest inducer; ketamine dissociative, raises BP.
- Pre-anaesthetics: BZD, opioid, atropine/glycopyrrolate, antiemetic, H2 blocker or PPI.
- BZD ↑ frequency, barbiturate ↑ duration of Cl− opening; flumazenil reverses BZDs.
- Absence → ethosuximide/valproate; focal and trigeminal neuralgia → carbamazepine; status → IV lorazepam.
- Phenytoin: gum hypertrophy, zero-order kinetics at high levels; valproate: neural tube defects, hepatotoxicity.
- Ethanol → acetaldehyde → acetic acid; zero-order elimination. Methanol → formic acid → acidosis and blindness; treat with ethanol/fomepizole.
- Disulfiram blocks aldehyde dehydrogenase; metronidazole gives a disulfiram-like reaction.
