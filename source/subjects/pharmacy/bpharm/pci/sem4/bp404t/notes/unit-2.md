# Unit 2: Pharmacodynamics, adverse effects, interactions and new drug development

This unit explains how drugs produce their effects (receptors and signal transduction), how dose relates to response, what modifies drug action, how drugs harm and interact, and how a new drug travels from the laboratory to the pharmacy shelf.

## 2.1 Principles of drug action

Drugs do not give cells new functions; they only increase or decrease functions that already exist.

| Type of action | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stimulation | Increases activity of a cell or organ | Adrenaline on the heart; pilocarpine on salivary glands |
| Depression | Decreases activity | Barbiturates on the CNS; quinidine on the heart |
| Irritation | Non-specific damaging effect on cells | Counter-irritants; bitters increasing salivation |
| Replacement | Replaces a missing substance | Insulin in diabetes; levodopa in parkinsonism; iron |
| Cytotoxic action | Selective killing of microbes or cancer cells | Penicillin, chloroquine, anticancer drugs |

## 2.2 Mechanisms of drug action

Drugs act through four main protein targets, plus a few non-receptor mechanisms.

| Target | How the drug acts | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Receptors | Agonist activates, antagonist blocks | Salbutamol (β2 agonist), propranolol (β blocker) |
| Ion channels | Block or open/modulate channels | Lignocaine blocks Na+ channels; nifedipine blocks Ca2+ channels; benzodiazepines modulate GABA-A Cl− channel |
| Enzymes | Inhibit (reversibly or irreversibly) | Neostigmine (cholinesterase), aspirin (cyclo-oxygenase, irreversible), captopril (ACE), allopurinol (xanthine oxidase) |
| Transporters (carriers) | Block uptake or exchange | Imipramine (noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake), digoxin (Na+/K+-ATPase), omeprazole (H+/K+-ATPase), furosemide (Na+/K+/2Cl− co-transporter) |

**Non-receptor mechanisms**

- Physical action: osmotic purgatives (magnesium sulfate), osmotic diuretics (mannitol), adsorbents (activated charcoal), demulcents.
- Chemical action: antacids neutralise acid; chelating agents (dimercaprol, EDTA) bind metals.
- Formation of antibodies: vaccines.
- Placebo effect.

## 2.3 Receptor theories

| Theory | Main idea | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Occupation theory (Clark) | Response is proportional to the fraction of receptors occupied; maximum response when all are occupied | Cannot explain partial agonists or spare receptors |
| Modified occupation (Ariëns; Stephenson) | Adds intrinsic activity / efficacy: drugs differ in how well they activate the receptors they occupy | Still a static picture |
| Rate theory (Paton) | Response depends on the rate of drug–receptor association, not on the number occupied | Not supported for most receptors |
| Induced-fit theory (Koshland) | Binding changes the shape of the receptor, which triggers the response | Qualitative |
| Two-state (macromolecular perturbation) model | Receptor exists in resting (R) and active (R*) states in equilibrium; agonists favour R*, inverse agonists favour R, antagonists bind both equally | Best current explanation |

```text
          inverse agonist          agonist
               ↓                      ↓
     R (inactive)  ⇌  R* (active) → response
          ← antagonist binds both equally (no shift) →
```

## 2.4 Classification of receptors

Receptors are grouped by structure and the way they transduce the signal.

| Type | Time scale | Structure | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Ligand-gated ion channels (ionotropic) | Milliseconds | Several subunits around a central pore | Nicotinic ACh receptor, GABA-A, glutamate NMDA/AMPA, 5-HT3 |
| 2. G-protein-coupled receptors (metabotropic) | Seconds | Single chain crossing the membrane 7 times | Muscarinic, adrenergic, dopamine, opioid, histamine H1/H2, most 5-HT |
| 3. Enzyme-linked (kinase-linked) receptors | Minutes to hours | Single transmembrane helix, enzyme domain inside | Insulin receptor (tyrosine kinase), growth factor receptors, ANP (guanylyl cyclase) |
| JAK-STAT linked receptors (cytokine type) | Minutes to hours | Receptor lacks its own enzyme; recruits Janus kinase | Cytokines, growth hormone, prolactin, erythropoietin, interferons |
| 4. Nuclear receptors (regulate gene transcription) | Hours to days | Inside the cell (cytoplasm or nucleus) | Steroids (glucocorticoids, sex hormones), thyroid hormone, vitamin D, retinoids |

## 2.5 Signal transduction mechanisms

**G-protein-coupled receptors.** The G-protein has α, β and γ subunits. In the resting state α binds GDP. When an agonist binds, α exchanges GDP for GTP, separates from βγ and acts on an effector. α has GTPase activity and switches itself off.

| G-protein | Effector | Second messenger | Receptors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gs | Stimulates adenylyl cyclase | ↑ cAMP → protein kinase A | β1, β2, β3, D1, H2, V2, glucagon |
| Gi | Inhibits adenylyl cyclase; opens K+ channels | ↓ cAMP | α2, M2, D2, μ opioid, GABA-B |
| Gq | Activates phospholipase C | IP3 (releases Ca2+ from ER) + DAG (activates protein kinase C) | α1, M1, M3, H1, AT1, 5-HT2 |

```text
Agonist → GPCR → Gs-GTP → adenylyl cyclase → ATP → cAMP → protein kinase A → response
Agonist → GPCR → Gq-GTP → phospholipase C → PIP2 → IP3 (Ca2+ release) + DAG (PKC)
```

**Ion channel receptors.** Agonist binding opens the channel directly. Na+ entry depolarises (nicotinic); Cl− entry hyperpolarises (GABA-A, glycine).

**Tyrosine kinase receptors.** The ligand causes two receptor chains to dimerise and phosphorylate each other's tyrosine residues; adaptor proteins then start a kinase cascade (Ras–Raf–MAP kinase) that changes gene expression and growth.

**JAK-STAT receptors.** Cytokine binding dimerises the receptor, which activates Janus kinases (JAK); JAK phosphorylates STAT proteins (signal transducers and activators of transcription), which move to the nucleus and switch on genes.

**Nuclear receptors.** A lipid-soluble hormone enters the cell, binds its receptor, and the complex binds to hormone response elements on DNA, changing mRNA and protein synthesis. Effects take hours and outlast the drug.

## 2.6 Regulation of receptors

Receptor number and sensitivity change with use.

- **Down-regulation (desensitisation):** constant exposure to an agonist reduces receptor number or response. Example: tolerance to salbutamol with continuous use. Mechanisms: receptor phosphorylation, internalisation, uncoupling from the G-protein.
- **Up-regulation (supersensitivity):** long-term blockade or denervation increases receptor number. Example: stopping propranolol suddenly can cause rebound tachycardia and angina, because extra β receptors have formed.

## 2.7 Dose–response relationship

As the dose increases, the response increases until a maximum is reached. Plotting response against log dose gives an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve whose middle part is nearly straight.

```text
Response %
 100 |              ___________
     |            /
  50 |----------/  ← ED50
     |        /
   0 |______/_______________ log dose
```

- **Potency:** the amount of drug needed for a given effect (position of the curve on the dose axis). A curve further to the left = more potent. Expressed as ED50 or EC50.
- **Efficacy:** the maximum response a drug can produce (height of the curve). Efficacy matters more clinically. Furosemide has greater efficacy than thiazides; morphine than codeine.
- **Graded vs quantal response:** graded responses vary continuously in one subject (fall in blood pressure); quantal responses are all-or-none in a population (death, convulsion, sleep), plotted as % responding.

## 2.8 Therapeutic index

**Therapeutic index (TI) = LD50 / ED50** (in animals) or, in human use, TD50 / ED50.

- A high TI means a wide margin of safety (penicillin, diazepam).
- A low TI means the toxic dose is close to the effective dose; such drugs need careful dosing and often blood-level monitoring: **digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, theophylline, warfarin, aminoglycosides**.
- **Therapeutic window:** the range of plasma concentration between the minimum effective and the toxic level.
- **Margin of safety:** LD1 / ED99 is a stricter measure than TI.

## 2.9 Combined effects of drugs

| Effect | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Additive | Combined effect equals the sum (1 + 1 = 2) | Aspirin + paracetamol (analgesia); two antacids |
| Synergism / potentiation | Combined effect is greater than the sum (1 + 1 > 2), or one drug increases the effect of another that alone has no such effect (0 + 1 > 1) | Sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim; levodopa + carbidopa; alcohol + benzodiazepines |
| Antagonism | One drug reduces or cancels the effect of another | See below |

**Types of antagonism**

| Type | Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | Physical property | Activated charcoal adsorbs poisons |
| Chemical | Chemical reaction | Antacids neutralise HCl; dimercaprol chelates arsenic; protamine neutralises heparin |
| Physiological (functional) | Opposite effects through different receptors | Adrenaline (β2, bronchodilation) vs histamine (H1, bronchoconstriction) in anaphylaxis; insulin vs glucagon |
| Receptor (pharmacological) | Same receptor, competitive or non-competitive | Atropine vs acetylcholine; naloxone vs morphine |

## 2.10 Factors modifying drug action

1. **Body weight and size:** dose per kg or per m² surface area.
2. **Age:** newborns have immature liver enzymes and kidneys (grey baby syndrome with chloramphenicol); the elderly have reduced renal function, more sensitivity to CNS drugs, and take many drugs.
3. **Sex:** pregnancy alters kinetics; some drugs are teratogenic (thalidomide); drugs given in labour affect the newborn.
4. **Genetic factors (pharmacogenetics):** slow acetylators get isoniazid neuropathy; G6PD deficiency → haemolysis with primaquine, sulfonamides; atypical pseudocholinesterase → succinylcholine apnoea; malignant hyperthermia with halothane + succinylcholine.
5. **Route of administration:** magnesium sulfate is a purgative orally and a CNS depressant IV.
6. **Environmental factors and time of administration:** smoking induces enzymes; hypnotics act better at night.
7. **Psychological factors and placebo:** belief and expectation change response.
8. **Pathological states:** liver disease (reduced metabolism), kidney disease (reduced excretion; adjust doses of aminoglycosides), hypothyroidism (sensitive to morphine), GI disease (absorption).
9. **Other drugs:** interactions (2.12).
10. **Cumulation:** slowly eliminated drugs build up (digoxin, emetine).
11. **Tolerance:** natural (species or racial), acquired, cross-tolerance, tachyphylaxis.

## 2.11 Adverse drug reactions (ADR)

WHO defines an **adverse drug reaction** as a response to a drug that is noxious and unintended and occurs at doses normally used in humans for prevention, diagnosis or treatment.

**Classification (Rawlins and Thompson, extended)**

| Type | Nature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A — Augmented | Exaggerated normal action; dose related; predictable; common | Hypoglycaemia with insulin; bleeding with warfarin |
| B — Bizarre | Not dose related; unpredictable; often immune or genetic | Penicillin anaphylaxis; halothane hepatitis |
| C — Chronic | Long-term use | Cushing's syndrome with steroids |
| D — Delayed | Appears long after use | Teratogenesis, carcinogenesis |
| E — End of use | Withdrawal | Rebound hypertension after clonidine; withdrawal seizures after benzodiazepines |
| F — Failure | Unexpected failure of therapy | Oral contraceptive failure with enzyme inducers |

**Kinds of adverse effects**

- **Side effect:** unwanted but expected effect at therapeutic dose (dry mouth with atropine; sedation with antihistamines). A side effect can be useful elsewhere (antihistamine sedation as a hypnotic).
- **Secondary effect:** indirect result of the main action (superinfection after broad-spectrum antibiotics).
- **Toxic effect:** excessive effect due to overdose or accumulation (paracetamol liver damage).
- **Intolerance, idiosyncrasy** (see Unit 1).
- **Drug allergy:** four types (Gell and Coombs): I anaphylactic (IgE: penicillin anaphylaxis), II cytotoxic (haemolysis with methyldopa), III immune complex (serum sickness), IV delayed cell-mediated (contact dermatitis).
- **Photosensitivity:** tetracyclines, sulfonamides.
- **Drug dependence** and **withdrawal reactions.**
- **Teratogenicity:** thalidomide (phocomelia), warfarin, phenytoin (fetal hydantoin syndrome), valproate (neural tube defects), isotretinoin.
- **Carcinogenicity and mutagenicity:** alkylating agents, oestrogens.
- **Iatrogenic disease:** disease caused by treatment (drug-induced parkinsonism with antipsychotics; peptic ulcer with NSAIDs).

**Prevention:** avoid unnecessary drugs, take a drug and allergy history, use the lowest effective dose, monitor drugs with a narrow TI, watch high-risk groups (children, elderly, pregnant women, liver or kidney disease) and report suspected ADRs.

## 2.12 Drug interactions

A **drug interaction** occurs when the effect of one drug is changed by another drug, food or substance given earlier or at the same time.

**Pharmacokinetic interactions** — one drug changes the ADME of another.

| Step | Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absorption | Chelation or binding | Tetracyclines with calcium, iron, antacids; cholestyramine binds digoxin |
| Absorption | Changed GI motility | Metoclopramide speeds, atropine slows absorption of other drugs |
| Distribution | Displacement from protein binding | Aspirin or phenylbutazone displaces warfarin; sulfonamides displace tolbutamide (hypoglycaemia) |
| Metabolism | Enzyme induction | Rifampicin reduces oral contraceptive effect; phenobarbitone reduces warfarin effect |
| Metabolism | Enzyme inhibition | Cimetidine or erythromycin raises theophylline levels; ketoconazole raises terfenadine (arrhythmia); allopurinol raises 6-mercaptopurine |
| Excretion | Competition for tubular secretion | Probenecid prolongs penicillin; aspirin reduces methotrexate excretion (toxicity) |
| Excretion | Change in urine pH | Sodium bicarbonate increases salicylate excretion |

**Pharmacodynamic interactions** — at the site of action.

- Synergism: alcohol + benzodiazepines (CNS depression); sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim.
- Antagonism: naloxone reverses morphine; β-blockers reduce the bronchodilator effect of salbutamol.
- Changes in electrolytes: diuretic-induced hypokalaemia increases digoxin toxicity.

**Pharmaceutical (physical/chemical) incompatibility** happens outside the body, for example mixing drugs in one infusion bottle (penicillin and aminoglycosides in the same syringe).

**Beneficial interactions** are used deliberately: levodopa + carbidopa; amoxicillin + clavulanic acid; local anaesthetic + adrenaline.

## 2.13 Drug discovery and clinical evaluation of new drugs

Bringing a new drug to market takes many years and great cost, and only a small fraction of tested molecules succeed.

**1. Drug discovery phase**

- Identify a **target** (receptor, enzyme, gene) linked to a disease.
- Find **lead compounds**: random screening of natural or synthetic libraries, high-throughput screening, rational (structure-based) design using computer modelling, modification of known drugs, serendipity, biotechnology.
- **Lead optimisation:** change the structure to improve potency, selectivity, kinetics and safety (structure–activity relationship).

**2. Preclinical evaluation (animals and in vitro)**

- **Pharmacological screening:** primary and secondary actions, mechanism, safety pharmacology (heart, lungs, CNS).
- **Pharmacokinetics:** ADME in animals.
- **Toxicity studies:** acute (single dose, LD50 range), subacute and chronic (repeated dose), reproductive toxicity and teratogenicity, mutagenicity (Ames test), carcinogenicity, local toxicity.
- Must follow **Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)** and ethics committee (in India CPCSEA) rules for animal use.
- An application to the regulator (in India the CDSCO under the Drugs Controller General of India; in the USA an IND application) is made before human trials.

**3. Clinical trial phase**

| Phase | Who | Number (approx.) | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phase 0 (microdosing) | Healthy volunteers | Very few | Sub-therapeutic doses to study kinetics early (optional) |
| Phase I | Healthy volunteers (patients for toxic drugs like anticancer) | 20–100 | Safety, tolerability, kinetics, maximum tolerated dose; open label |
| Phase II | Patients with the disease | 100–500 | Efficacy, dose range, short-term side effects; often single-blind |
| Phase III | Large patient groups, many centres | Several hundred to thousands | Confirm efficacy and safety against placebo or standard drug; randomised, double-blind; data for marketing approval |
| Phase IV (post-marketing surveillance) | General population after approval | Thousands to millions | Rare and long-term ADRs, new uses, interactions |

Trials follow **Good Clinical Practice (GCP)**, need approval of an **ethics committee**, and **informed consent** of every participant. In India they are governed by the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019.

**Trial designs:** randomisation (to avoid selection bias), blinding (single-blind: subject does not know; double-blind: neither subject nor investigator knows), placebo control, crossover design.

## 2.14 Pharmacovigilance

**Pharmacovigilance** (WHO) is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem.

- **Aims:** improve patient safety, find rare or delayed ADRs, assess benefit–risk, give information to prescribers and regulators, and support rational use.
- **Methods:** spontaneous reporting (most common; yellow card in the UK), intensive monitoring in hospitals, case–control and cohort studies, record linkage.
- **India:** the **Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)**, run by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission at Ghaziabad as national coordination centre, with ADR monitoring centres in medical colleges and hospitals. Anyone, including pharmacists and patients, can report a suspected ADR on the suspected ADR reporting form.
- **Causality assessment:** WHO–UMC scale (certain, probable, possible, unlikely) or the Naranjo algorithm.
- **Outcomes:** label changes, restrictions or withdrawal of drugs (rofecoxib, cisapride, terfenadine were withdrawn for safety reasons).

## Remember

- Four protein targets: receptors, ion channels, enzymes, transporters.
- Receptor families: ion channel (ms), GPCR (s), kinase/JAK-STAT (min–h), nuclear (h–days).
- Gs ↑cAMP; Gi ↓cAMP; Gq → PLC → IP3 + DAG.
- Potency = position of curve; efficacy = maximum height. TI = LD50/ED50; narrow TI: digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, theophylline, warfarin.
- Type A ADR dose related and predictable; type B bizarre and immune or genetic.
- PK interactions: absorption, displacement, induction/inhibition, excretion; PD: synergism and antagonism.
- Trials: Phase I volunteers safety; II efficacy and dose; III large confirmatory; IV post-marketing.
- PvPI is run by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad.
