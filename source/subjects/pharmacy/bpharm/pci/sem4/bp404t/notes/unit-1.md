# Unit 1: Introduction to pharmacology and pharmacokinetics

Pharmacology answers two questions: what does the drug do to the body, and what does the body do to the drug. This unit sets up the vocabulary of the whole subject (drug, receptor, agonist, tolerance, dependence) and then follows a drug through the body: how it crosses membranes, gets absorbed, spreads, is changed by the liver and finally leaves.

## 1.1 Definition and scope of pharmacology

**Pharmacology** is the science that studies drugs and their interaction with living systems: their sources, properties, actions, mechanisms, fate in the body, uses and harmful effects.

A **drug** (WHO) is any substance or product used, or intended to be used, to modify or explore physiological systems or pathological states for the benefit of the person receiving it. Drugs are used to prevent, diagnose or treat disease.

**Branches of pharmacology**

| Branch | What it studies |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacodynamics | What the drug does to the body: actions and mechanism |
| Pharmacokinetics | What the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion (ADME) |
| Pharmacotherapeutics | Use of drugs to prevent and treat disease |
| Clinical pharmacology | Study of drugs in human beings, healthy and sick |
| Chemotherapy | Drugs that kill or stop microbes, parasites and cancer cells |
| Toxicology | Harmful effects of chemicals and drugs, their detection and treatment |
| Pharmacy | Preparation, compounding, dispensing and storage of medicines |
| Pharmacogenetics / pharmacogenomics | How genetic differences change drug response |
| Pharmacoepidemiology | Drug use and drug effects in large populations |
| Pharmacoeconomics | Costs and benefits of drug therapy |

**Scope.** Pharmacology links basic sciences (physiology, biochemistry, pathology) with clinical medicine. It guides the choice of drug and dose, predicts side effects and interactions, supports new drug discovery, and underlies pharmacovigilance and rational drug use.

## 1.3 Nature and sources of drugs

**Nature.** Drugs may be solids (tablets), liquids (syrups) or gases (anaesthetic gases). Chemically they may be alkaloids, glycosides, oils, gums, resins, proteins, peptides or synthetic molecules.

| Source | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Plants | Morphine (opium poppy), atropine (belladonna), digoxin (foxglove), quinine (cinchona), reserpine (Rauwolfia) |
| Animals | Insulin (formerly pig/cattle pancreas), heparin, thyroxine |
| Minerals | Ferrous sulfate, magnesium sulfate, iodine, lithium salts |
| Microorganisms | Penicillin (Penicillium), streptomycin (Streptomyces) |
| Synthetic | Paracetamol, aspirin, most modern drugs |
| Semi-synthetic | Ampicillin, heroin, dihydroergotamine |
| Biotechnology (recombinant DNA) | Human insulin, growth hormone, interferons, monoclonal antibodies |

## 1.4 Essential drugs concept

**Essential medicines** (WHO) are those that satisfy the priority health-care needs of the population. They are selected on the basis of disease burden, evidence of efficacy and safety, and comparative cost-effectiveness. They should be available at all times, in adequate amounts, in suitable dosage forms, with assured quality and at a price the community can afford.

- The WHO first published a Model List of Essential Medicines in 1977 and revises it every two years.
- India has its own **National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)**, prepared by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare and used for price control and public procurement.
- **Benefits:** rational prescribing, lower cost, better supply, easier training, better quality control.

## 1.5 Routes of drug administration

The route depends on the drug's properties (acid stability, solubility, irritancy), the site of action, the speed needed, the patient's condition and convenience.

**A. Local routes** — act where applied, little systemic effect.

- Topical: skin (ointment), mucous membranes (eye drops, nasal drops, lozenges, vaginal pessary).
- Deeper tissues: intra-articular, intrathecal (into CSF), infiltration of local anaesthetic.
- Arterial supply: close intra-arterial injection (for example in angiography or cancer chemotherapy).

**B. Systemic routes** — drug reaches the blood and is carried to the site of action.

| Route | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral | Safe, cheap, convenient, self-administration | Slow; first-pass metabolism; not for vomiting or unconscious patients; destroyed drugs (insulin) cannot be given |
| Sublingual / buccal | Rapid; avoids first-pass metabolism (glyceryl trinitrate) | Only small, lipid-soluble, potent drugs; taste |
| Rectal | Useful in vomiting or children (diazepam); partly avoids first pass | Irregular absorption; inconvenient |
| Cutaneous (transdermal patch) | Steady levels for days (nitroglycerin, fentanyl, nicotine) | Only potent lipid-soluble drugs; skin irritation |
| Inhalation | Rapid; direct to lungs (salbutamol, anaesthetic gases) | Needs devices and technique; irritant gases |
| Nasal | Some peptides (desmopressin) | Mucosal irritation |
| Intravenous (IV) | 100% bioavailability; immediate; large volumes; irritant drugs can be given diluted | Cannot be recalled; risk of infection, thrombophlebitis; needs skill and sterility |
| Intramuscular (IM) | Faster than oral; depot preparations possible | Painful; nerve injury; not with anticoagulants |
| Subcutaneous (SC) | Self-injection (insulin); slow steady absorption | Only non-irritant drugs; small volume |
| Intradermal | Tests and vaccines (BCG, sensitivity tests) | Very small volume |

**Special routes:** intra-arterial, intrathecal, intra-articular, intraperitoneal (mostly in animals), intracardiac (rarely, in emergencies).

## 1.6 Basic terms of drug response

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Receptor | A macromolecule (usually a protein) to which a drug binds to produce its effect |
| Affinity | Tendency of a drug to bind the receptor |
| Intrinsic activity (efficacy) | Ability of the bound drug to activate the receptor and produce a response (0 to 1) |
| Agonist | Has affinity and full intrinsic activity (1); produces maximal response (adrenaline on β receptors) |
| Partial agonist | Affinity, but intrinsic activity between 0 and 1; submaximal response, can block a full agonist (pentazocine, buprenorphine) |
| Antagonist | Affinity but zero intrinsic activity; blocks the agonist (atropine) |
| Inverse agonist | Affinity and negative intrinsic activity; produces the opposite effect of the agonist (β-carbolines at the benzodiazepine site) |

**Competitive versus non-competitive antagonism**

| Feature | Competitive (reversible) | Non-competitive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Same site as agonist | Different site (allosteric) or irreversible binding |
| Overcome by more agonist | Yes | No |
| Dose–response curve | Parallel shift to the right; same maximum | Flattened; maximum reduced |
| Potency of agonist | Decreased | May not change |
| Example | Atropine vs acetylcholine; propranolol vs adrenaline | Phenoxybenzamine (irreversible α-blocker) vs noradrenaline; papaverine |

**Spare receptors.** Many tissues have more receptors than needed for a maximal response. A full agonist may give a maximal effect after occupying only a small fraction of receptors. The remaining receptors are "spare". This explains why an irreversible antagonist first shifts the curve to the right (using up spare receptors) and only later lowers the maximum.

```text
Response
  100% |        _______ agonist alone
       |      /   _______ + competitive antagonist (shift right, same max)
       |     /   /
       |    /   /     _____ + non-competitive (max reduced)
       |   /   /    /
       |__/___/____/______________ log dose
```

**Other terms of altered response**

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tolerance | Larger dose needed for the same effect after repeated use | Morphine, nitrates, alcohol |
| Cross-tolerance | Tolerance to one drug gives tolerance to related drugs | Alcohol and barbiturates |
| Tachyphylaxis | Rapid tolerance after a few doses at short intervals; not overcome by raising the dose | Ephedrine, tyramine (depletion of noradrenaline stores) |
| Dependence | State in which the person needs the drug to function normally | Opioids, alcohol, benzodiazepines |
| Psychological dependence | Compulsive desire to take the drug for its pleasant effect | Cocaine, cannabis |
| Physical dependence | Body adapts; stopping causes a withdrawal syndrome | Opioids, alcohol, barbiturates |
| Addiction | Compulsive drug-seeking and use despite harm, with loss of control | Heroin, cocaine |
| Idiosyncrasy | Genetically determined abnormal reaction | Haemolysis with primaquine in G6PD deficiency; prolonged apnoea with succinylcholine (atypical pseudocholinesterase) |
| Allergy (hypersensitivity) | Immune-mediated reaction, not related to dose | Penicillin anaphylaxis |
| Intolerance | Usual effect at a dose that is low for most people | Tinnitus after small dose of quinine |

## 1.7 Pharmacokinetics: membrane transport

Every step of ADME needs the drug to cross cell membranes (a lipid bilayer with proteins).

| Mechanism | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive (simple) diffusion | Down a concentration gradient; no energy; no carrier; depends on lipid solubility and degree of ionisation; most drugs | Most lipid-soluble drugs |
| Filtration | Through aqueous pores; small water-soluble molecules | Water, urea, ions; glomerular filtration |
| Facilitated diffusion | Carrier-mediated, down the gradient, no energy, saturable | Glucose into cells |
| Active transport | Carrier, against gradient, needs ATP, saturable, can be inhibited | Levodopa, methyldopa, iron; P-glycoprotein efflux; probenecid–penicillin in kidney |
| Endocytosis / pinocytosis | Engulfing of large molecules | Vitamin B12 with intrinsic factor |

**Effect of pH (Henderson–Hasselbalch).** Only the un-ionised form crosses lipid membranes easily.

- For a weak acid: log (un-ionised / ionised) = pKa − pH.
- For a weak base: log (ionised / un-ionised) = pKa − pH.
- So weak acids (aspirin) are mostly un-ionised in the stomach; weak bases (morphine) are ionised in the stomach and absorbed better in the intestine.
- **Ion trapping:** acidic drugs collect in alkaline compartments and basic drugs in acidic ones. Making urine alkaline speeds the excretion of acidic drugs (salicylates, phenobarbitone) in poisoning.

## 1.8 Absorption

**Absorption** is the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the blood.

**Factors affecting absorption**

1. **Drug factors:** lipid/water solubility, particle size, dosage form (solution faster than tablet), degree of ionisation, molecular size, concentration.
2. **Formulation:** disintegration and dissolution time, excipients, enteric coating, sustained-release designs.
3. **Site factors:** area of absorbing surface (small intestine is largest), blood flow, gastric emptying (faster emptying → faster absorption for most drugs), GI motility.
4. **Food and other drugs:** food delays absorption of many drugs; calcium and antacids bind tetracyclines; vitamin C increases iron absorption.
5. **Disease:** achlorhydria, malabsorption, shock (reduced blood flow to muscles).

**Bioavailability** is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. IV bioavailability is 100%. It is measured by comparing the area under the plasma concentration–time curve (AUC):

F = (AUC oral / AUC IV) × 100

**First-pass (presystemic) metabolism.** A drug taken by mouth passes through the gut wall and liver before reaching the systemic circulation; much of it may be destroyed. Examples with high first pass: glyceryl trinitrate, lignocaine, propranolol, morphine, testosterone. Sublingual, transdermal and parenteral routes avoid it.

**Bioequivalence.** Two preparations of the same drug are bioequivalent when their rate and extent of absorption (Cmax, tmax, AUC) are not significantly different.

## 1.9 Distribution

After absorption the drug spreads into body fluids and tissues.

**Apparent volume of distribution (Vd)** = amount of drug in the body ÷ plasma concentration. It is a calculated volume, not a real space. A drug held in plasma (heparin, warfarin) has a small Vd; a drug taken up into tissues (digoxin, chloroquine) has a Vd far larger than body water.

**Factors affecting distribution:** lipid solubility, ionisation, plasma protein binding, tissue binding, blood flow, special barriers.

**Plasma protein binding**

- Acidic drugs bind mainly to **albumin** (warfarin, phenytoin, salicylates); basic drugs to **α1-acid glycoprotein** (lignocaine, propranolol).
- Only the **free** drug acts, is metabolised and is excreted; bound drug is a reservoir.
- Highly bound drugs have longer action and can be displaced by other drugs (aspirin displaces warfarin → bleeding risk; sulfonamides displace bilirubin → kernicterus in newborns).
- Hypoalbuminaemia (liver disease, nephrotic syndrome) raises the free fraction.

**Tissue storage:** fat (thiopentone, DDT), bone and teeth (tetracyclines, lead), liver (chloroquine), retina (chloroquine), thyroid (iodine).

**Barriers**

- **Blood–brain barrier:** tight junctions of brain capillary endothelium plus glial cells; only lipid-soluble drugs enter easily. Inflammation (meningitis) makes it leaky, so penicillin can reach the CSF.
- **Placental barrier:** lipid-soluble drugs cross freely; this matters for teratogenicity and for drugs given in labour.

**Redistribution.** A highly lipid-soluble drug first reaches organs with high blood flow (brain) and then moves to muscle and fat. Thiopentone given IV acts in seconds and its effect ends in minutes because it redistributes out of the brain, not because it is eliminated.

## 1.10 Metabolism (biotransformation)

**Biotransformation** is the chemical change of a drug inside the body, mainly to make it more water-soluble so that it can be excreted. The main site is the **liver** (microsomal enzymes of the smooth endoplasmic reticulum); others are gut wall, plasma, kidney, lungs and skin.

**Results of metabolism**

| Result | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Active drug → inactive metabolite | Most drugs (phenobarbitone, propranolol) |
| Active drug → active metabolite | Diazepam → desmethyldiazepam; codeine → morphine |
| Inactive prodrug → active drug | Levodopa → dopamine; enalapril → enalaprilat |
| Drug → toxic metabolite | Paracetamol → N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI) |

**Phase I (non-synthetic / functionalisation)** — adds or exposes a polar group (−OH, −NH2, −COOH).

- Oxidation (most common; by cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, especially CYP3A4, 2D6, 2C9, 2C19, 1A2).
- Reduction (chloramphenicol, halothane).
- Hydrolysis (esters: procaine, succinylcholine by plasma esterases; amides).
- Cyclisation and decyclisation.

**Phase II (synthetic / conjugation)** — joins the drug or its metabolite with an endogenous molecule; product is usually inactive and water-soluble.

| Conjugation | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Glucuronide conjugation (most common) | Morphine, paracetamol, chloramphenicol, bilirubin |
| Acetylation | Isoniazid, sulfonamides, hydralazine, dapsone (slow and fast acetylators) |
| Sulfate conjugation | Paracetamol, steroids |
| Methylation | Adrenaline, noradrenaline, histamine |
| Glycine conjugation | Salicylic acid, nicotinic acid |
| Glutathione conjugation | Paracetamol (NAPQI detoxified) |

Newborns have poor glucuronide conjugation, so chloramphenicol causes **grey baby syndrome**.

**Hofmann elimination:** spontaneous breakdown in plasma at body pH and temperature (atracurium), independent of liver and kidney.

## 1.11 Enzyme induction and inhibition

**Enzyme induction** — some drugs increase the synthesis of microsomal enzymes, so they speed the metabolism of themselves and other drugs. Onset takes days to weeks.

- **Inducers:** phenobarbitone, phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampicin, griseofulvin, chronic alcohol, smoking.
- **Consequences:** failure of oral contraceptives with rifampicin; reduced warfarin effect; tolerance (autoinduction by carbamazepine); toxicity when a prodrug forms more toxic metabolite (paracetamol in chronic alcoholics).
- **Uses:** phenobarbitone for neonatal jaundice (induces glucuronyl transferase).

**Enzyme inhibition** — some drugs reduce the metabolism of others; onset is quick.

- **Inhibitors:** cimetidine, erythromycin, clarithromycin, ketoconazole, ciprofloxacin, valproate, disulfiram, allopurinol, grapefruit juice.
- **Consequences:** toxicity of the other drug (ketoconazole raises terfenadine levels → arrhythmia; erythromycin with theophylline).

## 1.12 Excretion

**Routes:** kidney (main), bile and faeces, lungs (gases, alcohol), saliva, sweat, milk (important in breastfeeding), skin.

**Renal excretion = glomerular filtration + tubular secretion − tubular reabsorption.**

- **Glomerular filtration:** free (unbound) drug of small size is filtered.
- **Tubular secretion:** active, saturable; separate carriers for acids (penicillin, probenecid) and bases (quinine). Probenecid competes with penicillin and prolongs its action.
- **Tubular reabsorption:** passive, for lipid-soluble un-ionised drug; depends on urine pH. Alkaline urine increases excretion of weak acids (salicylate, phenobarbitone); acidic urine increases excretion of weak bases (amphetamine).

**Enterohepatic circulation.** Drugs conjugated in the liver pass into bile; gut bacteria split the conjugate and the free drug is reabsorbed. This prolongs action (oral contraceptives, morphine, doxycycline).

## 1.13 Kinetics of elimination

**Clearance (CL)** = rate of elimination ÷ plasma concentration. It is the volume of plasma cleared of drug per unit time.

**Plasma half-life (t½)** = time taken for the plasma concentration to fall to half. t½ = 0.693 × Vd / CL. After about 4–5 half-lives, a drug is almost fully eliminated; during regular dosing, steady state is also reached in about 4–5 half-lives.

| Feature | First-order kinetics | Zero-order kinetics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rate of elimination | Proportional to concentration (constant fraction per unit time) | Constant amount per unit time |
| Half-life | Constant | Not constant; increases with dose |
| Applies to | Most drugs at therapeutic doses | Alcohol, high doses of phenytoin, salicylates (enzymes saturated) |
| Plot | Straight line on log concentration vs time | Straight line on linear concentration vs time |

**Michaelis–Menten (mixed) kinetics:** a drug follows first order at low concentration and switches to zero order when the enzymes saturate (phenytoin), so a small dose increase can cause a large rise in plasma level.

```text
log C |\                          C |\
      | \  first order            |  \   zero order
      |  \ (straight on log)      |    \ (straight on linear)
      |___\______ time            |______\____ time
```

**Loading and maintenance dose.** A loading dose (Vd × target concentration) quickly reaches the desired level for drugs with long t½ (digoxin, amiodarone); maintenance dose (CL × target concentration) replaces what is eliminated.

## Remember

- Pharmacodynamics = drug on body; pharmacokinetics = body on drug (ADME).
- Competitive antagonist: parallel right shift, same maximum, overcome by more agonist; non-competitive: maximum reduced.
- Tachyphylaxis is quick and not overcome by dose; tolerance is slow and is.
- Only the free, un-ionised drug crosses membranes; weak acids are absorbed in the stomach, weak bases in the intestine.
- Bioavailability = AUC oral ÷ AUC IV; first-pass drugs: nitroglycerin, lignocaine, propranolol, morphine.
- Phase I adds a polar group (CYP450 oxidation); phase II conjugates (glucuronidation most common).
- Inducers: phenobarbitone, phenytoin, carbamazepine, rifampicin; inhibitors: cimetidine, erythromycin, ketoconazole, ciprofloxacin, valproate.
- Most drugs follow first-order kinetics; alcohol and high-dose phenytoin follow zero order; t½ = 0.693 Vd/CL.
