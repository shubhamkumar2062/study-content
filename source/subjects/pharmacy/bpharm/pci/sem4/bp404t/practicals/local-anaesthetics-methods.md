# Study of local anaesthetics by different methods

**Aim:** To study the action of local anaesthetics by surface, infiltration and nerve-block (conduction) methods.

> Shown by simulation or video as per the PCI note. Live use needs IAEC approval under CPCSEA rules and the 3Rs; frogs and guinea pigs are no longer used for routine teaching. Use the drug strengths in your lab manual.

## Principle

Local anaesthetics (lignocaine, procaine, bupivacaine, tetracaine) block voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve membranes, so nerve impulses cannot start or travel along the nerve. Sensation is lost in the area supplied, while the animal stays conscious. Their action can be tested in three ways:

| Method | Type of anaesthesia | Animal | Test of sensation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Corneal reflex method | Surface (topical) | Rabbit | Blink when the cornea is touched |
| Intradermal wheal method | Infiltration | Guinea pig | Skin twitch to a pin prick on the wheal |
| Foot withdrawal (plexus) method | Nerve block (conduction) | Frog | Withdrawal of the foot dipped in dilute acid |

Other methods: tail nerve block in mice or rats, and spinal (intrathecal) anaesthesia in rabbits.

## Requirements

Local anaesthetic solutions (for example lignocaine, procaine), normal saline as control, rabbit holder, cotton wisp, syringes with fine needles, electric clipper, marker pen, pin, frog board, beaker of dilute HCl, beaker of water, stopwatch; or simulation software.

## Procedure

### A. Surface anaesthesia – rabbit cornea
1. Test the corneal reflex in both eyes by touching the cornea from the side with a cotton wisp.
2. Put a few drops of the local anaesthetic into one eye and saline into the other.
3. Test the reflex every minute; note the time when the blink is lost (onset) and when it returns (duration).

### B. Infiltration anaesthesia – guinea pig intradermal wheal
1. Clip the hair on the back a day before.
2. Inject a small volume of the anaesthetic intradermally to raise a wheal; mark it. Inject saline on the other side as control.
3. Prick the wheal with a pin a fixed number of times (for example 6) at regular intervals (for example every 5 min for 30 min).
4. Count the pricks that give **no** skin twitch. More "no response" counts mean stronger anaesthesia.

### C. Nerve block – frog foot withdrawal
1. Pith the frog (brain only, so spinal reflexes remain) and suspend it on a stand.
2. Dip one foot in dilute HCl; note the time taken to withdraw it. Wash the foot in water at once.
3. Put the anaesthetic solution into the abdominal lymph sac so that it reaches the lumbar nerve plexus.
4. Repeat the acid test at fixed intervals; note when the foot is no longer withdrawn within a fixed cut-off time (onset of nerve block).

## Result

The local anaesthetic produced surface, infiltration and conduction anaesthesia; onset and duration were noted (specimen values) and saline had no effect.

## Precautions

1. Touch the cornea from the side so the animal does not blink at the approaching object.
2. Inject strictly intradermally, raising a clear wheal; deep injection gives false results.
3. Use the same pin pressure for every prick.
4. Wash the frog's foot in water immediately after each acid test.
5. Always run a saline control.

## Viva questions

1. **Mechanism of action of local anaesthetics?** They block voltage-gated sodium channels and stop nerve conduction.
2. **Which method tests surface anaesthesia?** The rabbit corneal reflex method.
3. **Which animal is used for the intradermal wheal method?** The guinea pig.
4. **Why is adrenaline added to some local anaesthetic injections?** It constricts blood vessels, slowing absorption so the action lasts longer and systemic toxicity is lower.
5. **Which nerve fibres are blocked first?** Small fibres carrying pain and temperature before large motor fibres.
6. **Name an ester and an amide local anaesthetic.** Procaine (ester) and lignocaine (amide).
7. **Why is only the brain destroyed in the frog?** The spinal reflex arc must work for the withdrawal reflex.
8. **Why is procaine poor as a surface anaesthetic?** It penetrates mucous membranes poorly.
