# Unit 3: Heterocyclic compounds — nomenclature, classification, pyrrole, furan and thiophene

**Heterocyclic compounds** are cyclic compounds in which the ring contains at least one atom other than carbon. The most common hetero atoms are nitrogen, oxygen and sulphur. More than half of all known drugs contain a heterocyclic ring, along with vitamins, nucleic acid bases, alkaloids, antibiotics and pigments such as haem and chlorophyll. This unit covers naming and classifying heterocycles and then the three five-membered aromatic rings with one hetero atom: pyrrole, furan and thiophene.

## 3.1 Classification of heterocyclic compounds

| Basis | Classes | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ring size | Three-, four-, five-, six-, seven-membered | oxirane, azetidine, pyrrole, pyridine, azepine |
| Number of hetero atoms | One, two or more | furan (1); imidazole (2); 1,2,4-triazole (3) |
| Kind of hetero atom | N, O, S or a mixture | pyrrole (N); oxazole (N, O); thiazole (N, S) |
| Degree of unsaturation | Aromatic; partly saturated; fully saturated (behave like ordinary amines, ethers, sulphides) | pyrrole; 3-pyrroline; pyrrolidine |
| Ring fusion | Monocyclic; fused with benzene; fused with another heterocycle | pyridine; quinoline, indole; purine, pteridine |
| Electron distribution (aromatic rings) | π-excessive (five-membered, electron-rich); π-deficient (six-membered with N, electron-poor) | pyrrole, furan, thiophene; pyridine, pyrimidine |

## 3.2 Nomenclature

Three systems are used.

### (a) Trivial (common) names

Most important heterocycles keep old names, which IUPAC accepts: pyrrole, furan, thiophene, pyridine, quinoline, isoquinoline, indole, purine, pyrimidine, imidazole. They must simply be learnt.

### (b) Hantzsch–Widman (systematic) names

Built from three parts: **prefix(es) for the hetero atoms + stem for ring size and saturation**.

**Prefixes** (in order of priority, highest first):

| Hetero atom | Prefix |
| --- | --- |
| Oxygen | oxa- |
| Sulphur | thia- |
| Nitrogen | aza- |

The final "a" is dropped before a vowel (oxa + ole = oxole).

**Stems (for rings with up to five members)**

| Ring size | Unsaturated (max. non-cumulative double bonds) | Saturated, containing N | Saturated, without N |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | -irine (N) / -irene | -iridine | -irane |
| 4 | -ete | -etidine | -etane |
| 5 | -ole | -olidine | -olane |

For six-membered rings containing nitrogen, the unsaturated stem is **-ine** (azine = pyridine, 1,3-diazine = pyrimidine) and the saturated one is **-inane** (azinane = piperidine).

**Examples**

| Structure | Hantzsch–Widman name | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Three-membered ring, one O, saturated | oxirane | ethylene oxide |
| Three-membered ring, one N, saturated | aziridine | ethyleneimine |
| Four-membered ring, one N, saturated | azetidine | — (the β-lactam ring of penicillins is azetidin-2-one) |
| Five-membered, one O, unsaturated | oxole | furan |
| Five-membered, one S, unsaturated | thiole | thiophene |
| Five-membered, one N, unsaturated | azole | pyrrole |
| Five-membered, one O, saturated | oxolane | tetrahydrofuran (THF) |
| Five-membered, N at 1 and 3 | 1,3-diazole | imidazole |
| Five-membered, O at 1 and N at 3 | 1,3-oxazole | oxazole |
| Five-membered, S at 1 and N at 3 | 1,3-thiazole | thiazole |

**Numbering rules**

1. Start at the hetero atom of highest priority (O > S > N) and give it number 1.
2. Go round the ring so that the other hetero atoms get the lowest possible numbers.
3. Substituents are then numbered by the same scheme (2-methylfuran, 3-bromothiophene).
4. Greek letters are also used for five-membered rings: positions next to the hetero atom (2 and 5) are **α**; positions 3 and 4 are **β**.

```text
            β     β
            4 ─── 3
           /       \
      α   5         2   α        X = NH (pyrrole), O (furan), S (thiophene)
           \       /
              X 1
```

### (c) Replacement nomenclature

The heterocycle is named as the carbocycle with "aza", "oxa" or "thia" showing which carbon is replaced. Quinoline = 1-azanaphthalene; isoquinoline = 2-azanaphthalene; acridine relates to anthracene.

### Fused ring names

Fused systems are named by joining a prefix (benzo-, pyrido-, imidazo-) to the base ring and giving letters (a, b, c…) for the bond of fusion: indole = benzo[b]pyrrole; quinoline = benzo[b]pyridine; isoquinoline = benzo[c]pyridine.

## 3.3 Aromaticity of pyrrole, furan and thiophene

Each of these is a flat five-membered ring. The four carbons are sp2 hybridised and each gives one p electron. The hetero atom is also sp2 and gives **two** electrons (one lone pair) into the p orbital perpendicular to the ring. This makes **6 π electrons** in a cyclic, planar, fully conjugated system — Hückel's rule (4n + 2, n = 1) is obeyed, so the rings are aromatic.

```text
   p orbitals above and below the ring plane
     ○   ○   ○   ○   ○       4 C × 1 e  +  heteroatom × 2 e  =  6 π electrons
     │   │   │   │   │
   ──C───C───C───C───X──     (in furan and thiophene the second lone pair of
     │   │   │   │   │        O or S lies in the ring plane and is not part
     ○   ○   ○   ○   ○        of the aromatic sextet)
```

Because the hetero atom pushes its lone pair into the ring, the ring carbons carry more electron density than benzene carbons. These rings are called **π-excessive** and react with electrophiles much faster than benzene.

**Relative aromaticity.** Aromatic character depends on how easily the hetero atom shares its lone pair. Oxygen is the most electronegative and holds its electrons tightly, so furan is the least aromatic. Sulphur is less electronegative and its larger orbitals overlap acceptably, so thiophene is the most aromatic of the three. Approximate resonance energies commonly quoted:

| Compound | Resonance energy (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| Benzene | 150 kJ/mol (36 kcal/mol) |
| Thiophene | 122 kJ/mol (29 kcal/mol) |
| Pyrrole | 88 kJ/mol (21 kcal/mol) |
| Furan | 67 kJ/mol (16 kcal/mol) |

**Order of aromaticity: benzene > thiophene > pyrrole > furan.**

Furan's low aromaticity shows in its chemistry: it adds reagents (it takes part in Diels–Alder reactions as a diene), opens easily in acid, and behaves partly like a diene ether.

**Relative reactivity towards electrophiles** is roughly the reverse: **pyrrole > furan > thiophene > benzene**. Nitrogen is the best electron donor, so the intermediate cation from pyrrole is best stabilised.

**Why substitution occurs at C-2 (α).** Attack at C-2 gives a cation stabilised by three resonance structures (positive charge on C-3, C-5 and the hetero atom). Attack at C-3 gives only two (charge on C-2 and the hetero atom). The more stable intermediate forms faster, so α-substitution dominates. If both α positions are occupied, substitution goes to β.

## 3.4 Pyrrole

Pyrrole (C4H5N) is a colourless liquid (bp about 130 °C) that darkens in air. It occurs in coal tar and bone oil. The pyrrole ring is part of porphyrins (haem, chlorophyll), vitamin B12 (corrin ring), bile pigments, and the amino acids proline and hydroxyproline (as the saturated pyrrolidine ring).

### Synthesis of pyrrole

1. **Paal–Knorr synthesis** — a 1,4-dicarbonyl compound is heated with ammonia (or a primary amine) to give a pyrrole. Hexane-2,5-dione (acetonylacetone) + NH3 → 2,5-dimethylpyrrole + 2 H2O; with methylamine the product is 1,2,5-trimethylpyrrole.

```text
  CH3–CO–CH2–CH2–CO–CH3  +  NH3   ──heat──►   2,5-dimethylpyrrole  +  2 H2O
     hexane-2,5-dione                         (ring closed by N between the two C=O carbons)
```

2. **From succinimide** — distilling succinimide with zinc dust reduces it to pyrrole.
3. **From ammonium mucate** — dry distillation of the ammonium salt of mucic acid (with glycerol) gives pyrrole.
4. **From furan (industrial)** — furan, ammonia and steam passed over heated alumina give pyrrole (the O is replaced by NH).
5. **Knorr pyrrole synthesis** — an α-aminoketone condenses with a β-ketoester (or other ketone with an active CH2) to give a substituted pyrrole. α-Aminoketones are unstable, so they are made in the flask by reducing an oximino ketone with zinc and acetic acid.
6. **Hantzsch pyrrole synthesis** — a β-ketoester, an α-haloketone and ammonia (or a primary amine) give a substituted pyrrole.

### Basicity and acidity of pyrrole

- Pyrrole is an **extremely weak base** (pKa of its conjugate acid about −3.8, compared with about 11 for pyrrolidine). The nitrogen lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet; using it to take a proton would destroy aromaticity. When pyrrole is protonated by strong acid, the proton actually adds to **C-2**, not N.
- Pyrrole is a **weak acid** (N–H pKa about 17). It reacts with potassium metal or solid KOH to give potassium pyrrolide, and with Grignard reagents to give pyrrylmagnesium halide. The anion is stabilised because the negative charge is spread over the aromatic ring.
- In strong acids pyrrole **polymerises** (protonated pyrrole is attacked by neutral pyrrole), giving a red resin. So strongly acidic reagents (conc. HNO3/H2SO4) cannot be used directly.

### Reactions of pyrrole

**Electrophilic substitution (mostly at C-2), under mild, non-acidic conditions:**

| Reaction | Reagent | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitration | Acetyl nitrate (HNO3 + acetic anhydride) at low temperature | 2-Nitropyrrole |
| Sulphonation | Pyridine–SO3 complex | Pyrrole-2-sulphonic acid |
| Halogenation | I2 in KI; SO2Cl2 | 2,3,4,5-Tetraiodopyrrole (iodol, once an antiseptic); tetrachloropyrrole — halogenation is hard to stop at one atom |
| Acylation | Acetic anhydride on heating (no catalyst needed) | 2-Acetylpyrrole |
| Formylation | DMF + POCl3 (Vilsmeier–Haack) | Pyrrole-2-carbaldehyde |
| Reimer–Tiemann | CHCl3 + KOH | Pyrrole-2-carbaldehyde, with some 3-chloropyridine from ring expansion |
| Mannich | HCHO + dimethylamine | 2-(Dimethylaminomethyl)pyrrole |
| Diazo coupling | Benzenediazonium chloride | 2-Phenylazopyrrole (coloured) |

**Reactions at nitrogen** — potassium pyrrolide reacts with CH3I to give N-methylpyrrole and with acyl chlorides to give N-acylpyrroles.

**Reduction**
- Zinc and acetic acid → 3-pyrroline (2,5-dihydropyrrole).
- H2 / Ni or Pt (heat) → pyrrolidine (a strong secondary amine base).

**Oxidation** — with chromium trioxide pyrrole gives maleimide.

**Ring expansion** — as noted, the Reimer–Tiemann conditions (dichlorocarbene) can give 3-chloropyridine.

### Medicinal uses of pyrrole and its derivatives

| Compound | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Haem (porphyrin of four pyrroles) | Oxygen transport in haemoglobin |
| Vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin, corrin ring) | Treatment of pernicious anaemia |
| Atorvastatin | Lowers cholesterol (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor) |
| Tolmetin, ketorolac | NSAIDs (ketorolac has a fused pyrrolizine ring) |
| Sunitinib | Anticancer (kinase inhibitor) |
| Povidone (polyvinylpyrrolidone) | Pharmaceutical binder; povidone-iodine antiseptic |
| Piracetam (pyrrolidone) | Nootropic |

## 3.5 Furan

Furan (C4H4O) is a colourless volatile liquid (bp about 31 °C). Its name comes from Latin *furfur* (bran), because its aldehyde, furfural, is obtained from bran.

### Synthesis of furan

1. **From pentosans (industrial)** — oat husks, corn cobs or bran (rich in pentosans) are heated with dilute H2SO4 or HCl. The pentoses formed lose water to give **furfural** (furan-2-carbaldehyde). Furfural is decarbonylated (CO removed) by passing its vapour with steam over a heated catalyst (for example ZnO–Cr2O3, or palladium) to give furan.

```text
 pentosans ──H3O+──► pentoses (C5H10O5) ──(−3 H2O)──► furfural ──(−CO, catalyst)──► furan
```

2. **From mucic acid** — dry distillation of mucic acid gives furan-2-carboxylic acid (pyromucic acid), which loses CO2 on heating to give furan.
3. **Paal–Knorr synthesis** — a 1,4-dicarbonyl compound is heated with a dehydrating agent (P2O5, H2SO4 or ZnCl2): hexane-2,5-dione → 2,5-dimethylfuran.
4. **Feist–Bénary synthesis** — an α-haloketone condenses with a β-ketoester in the presence of a base such as pyridine or ammonia, giving a substituted furan.

### Reactions of furan

Furan is very reactive and acid-sensitive, so mild reagents are used. Substitution occurs at C-2.

| Reaction | Reagent | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitration | Acetyl nitrate at low temperature | 2-Nitrofuran |
| Sulphonation | Pyridine–SO3 | Furan-2-sulphonic acid |
| Bromination | Bromine in dioxane at low temperature | 2-Bromofuran |
| Acylation | Acetic anhydride with a mild Lewis acid (BF3) | 2-Acetylfuran |
| Formylation | Vilsmeier–Haack (DMF/POCl3) | Furfural |

**Addition reactions** (reflect low aromaticity):

- **Diels–Alder reaction** — furan acts as a diene with maleic anhydride, giving an oxygen-bridged adduct.
- **Catalytic hydrogenation** (H2/Ni) → **tetrahydrofuran (THF)**, a common solvent.
- **Ring opening** — aqueous acid opens the ring to give 1,4-dicarbonyl compounds (the reverse of the Paal–Knorr reaction).

### Medicinal uses of furan and its derivatives

| Compound | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Nitrofurantoin | Urinary tract antiseptic |
| Nitrofurazone | Topical antibacterial for wounds and burns |
| Furazolidone | Antibacterial and antiprotozoal (diarrhoea, giardiasis) |
| Ranitidine | H2 receptor antagonist for peptic ulcer |
| Furosemide | Loop diuretic (contains a furfurylamino group) |
| Dantrolene | Skeletal muscle relaxant |
| Tetrahydrofuran | Solvent in drug synthesis |

## 3.6 Thiophene

Thiophene (C4H4S) is a colourless liquid (bp about 84 °C) with a smell like benzene. It occurs in coal-tar benzene, from which it is hard to separate by distillation because the boiling points are close. Victor Meyer discovered it when a sample of "benzene" gave a blue colour with isatin and conc. H2SO4 (the **indophenin test**), while pure benzene did not.

**Removal of thiophene from benzene**: shake the benzene with conc. H2SO4 at room temperature. Thiophene sulphonates much faster than benzene and passes into the acid layer as thiophene-2-sulphonic acid.

### Synthesis of thiophene

1. **Industrial** — n-butane (or butenes, butadiene) heated with sulphur at high temperature (around 560 °C).
2. **Volhard–Erdmann synthesis** — heating sodium succinate with phosphorus trisulphide (P2S3) gives thiophene.
3. **Paal–Knorr synthesis** — a 1,4-dicarbonyl compound heated with phosphorus pentasulphide (P2S5, also written P4S10): hexane-2,5-dione → 2,5-dimethylthiophene.
4. **From acetylene** — acetylene and hydrogen sulphide passed over heated alumina.
5. **Hinsberg synthesis** — a 1,2-dicarbonyl compound condenses with diethyl thiodiacetate in base, giving a thiophenedicarboxylic acid derivative.

### Reactions of thiophene

Thiophene is the most benzene-like of the three: it is stable to acids and can be sulphonated with conc. H2SO4. It still reacts faster than benzene, and at C-2.

| Reaction | Reagent | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitration | HNO3 in acetic anhydride | 2-Nitrothiophene |
| Sulphonation | Conc. H2SO4 at room temperature | Thiophene-2-sulphonic acid |
| Halogenation | Br2 or Cl2 (fast; can give polyhalo products) | 2-Bromothiophene, 2,5-dibromothiophene |
| Acylation | Acetic anhydride with H3PO4, or acetyl chloride with SnCl4 | 2-Acetylthiophene |
| Chloromethylation | HCHO + HCl | 2-Chloromethylthiophene |
| Mercuration | HgCl2 with sodium acetate | 2-Chloromercurithiophene |

**Other reactions**

- **Desulphurisation with Raney nickel** — removes sulphur and saturates the chain, giving an alkane (thiophene → n-butane). This is used to make chains of known length from thiophene derivatives.
- **Reduction** — thiophene poisons ordinary hydrogenation catalysts; with large amounts of palladium it gives tetrahydrothiophene (thiolane). Biotin contains this saturated ring.
- **Oxidation** — peracids oxidise the sulphur to the sulphoxide and sulphone (thiophene 1,1-dioxide), which are no longer aromatic.

### Medicinal uses of thiophene and its derivatives

| Compound | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Clopidogrel, ticlopidine, prasugrel (thienopyridines) | Antiplatelet drugs |
| Tenoxicam | NSAID (oxicam with thiophene in place of benzene) |
| Ticarcillin, cefalotin | β-Lactam antibiotics with a thiophene side chain |
| Olanzapine (thienobenzodiazepine) | Antipsychotic |
| Duloxetine | Antidepressant (SNRI) |
| Tiotropium | Inhaled bronchodilator for COPD |
| Biotin (tetrahydrothiophene ring) | Vitamin |

**Bioisosterism.** A thiophene ring often replaces a benzene ring in drug design ("thiophene is a bioisostere of benzene") because its size, shape and electronic nature are similar; tenoxicam vs piroxicam is an example.

## 3.7 Comparison of the three rings

| Property | Pyrrole | Furan | Thiophene |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hetero atom | NH | O | S |
| Aromaticity | Intermediate | Least | Most (closest to benzene) |
| Reactivity to electrophiles | Highest | Intermediate | Lowest of the three, but more than benzene |
| Stability in acid | Polymerises | Ring opens | Stable |
| Preferred site of substitution | C-2 | C-2 | C-2 |
| Diels–Alder as diene | Poor | Good | Very poor |
| Paal–Knorr reagent with 1,4-diketone | NH3 or RNH2 | Acid / P2O5 | P2S5 |
| Main natural or industrial source | Coal tar, bone oil | Furfural (bran) | Coal-tar benzene; butane + S |

## Remember

- Hantzsch–Widman: oxa > thia > aza; 5-membered unsaturated "-ole", saturated with N "-olidine", without N "-olane"; number from the highest-priority hetero atom.
- Pyrrole, furan, thiophene: 6 π electrons, the hetero atom lone pair is in the sextet → π-excessive, attacked at C-2.
- Aromaticity: benzene > thiophene > pyrrole > furan. Reactivity to electrophiles: pyrrole > furan > thiophene > benzene.
- Pyrrole is almost non-basic (lone pair in the sextet; protonates on C-2) and weakly acidic at N–H (forms K salt).
- Paal–Knorr: 1,4-diketone + NH3 (pyrrole), + acid (furan), + P2S5 (thiophene).
- Furan from furfural (bran + acid, then decarbonylation); thiophene detected by the indophenin test and removed from benzene with conc. H2SO4.
- Use mild reagents: acetyl nitrate for nitration, pyridine–SO3 for sulphonation (except thiophene, which tolerates conc. H2SO4).
- Drugs: atorvastatin, ketorolac (pyrrole); nitrofurantoin, furosemide, ranitidine (furan); clopidogrel, tenoxicam, olanzapine (thiophene).
