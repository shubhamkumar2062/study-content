# Unit 2: Geometrical and conformational isomerism, atropisomerism, stereospecific and stereoselective reactions

Unit 1 dealt with stereoisomers that arise from a stereocentre. This unit covers the other kinds: isomers caused by restricted rotation about a double bond or in a ring (geometrical isomers), shapes that interconvert by rotation about single bonds (conformations), chirality caused by hindered rotation in biphenyls (atropisomerism), and how the stereochemistry of reactions is described.

## 2.1 Geometrical isomerism

**Geometrical (cis–trans) isomers** are diastereomers that differ in the arrangement of groups on either side of a bond or ring about which free rotation is not possible.

**Why it happens.** In a C=C double bond the π bond is formed by sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the plane. Turning one carbon would break this overlap, which needs much more energy than is available at room temperature. So groups are locked on one side or the other.

**Conditions for geometrical isomerism**

1. There must be restricted rotation: a C=C, C=N or N=N double bond, or a ring.
2. **Each** carbon of the double bond must carry two **different** groups. Abc=Cab type: isomers possible. If either carbon carries two identical groups (CH2=CHCl, (CH3)2C=CHCH3), there is no geometrical isomerism.

**Types of compounds showing it**

| System | Example |
| --- | --- |
| C=C | 2-butene, maleic and fumaric acids, cinnamic acid, stilbene |
| C=N | oximes of aldehydes and unsymmetrical ketones (benzaldoxime), hydrazones, imines |
| N=N | azobenzene |
| Rings | cis- and trans-1,2-dimethylcyclopropane; cis- and trans-1,4-dimethylcyclohexane |

## 2.2 Nomenclature of geometrical isomers

### Cis–trans system

Used when the two carbons carry a pair of similar groups.

- **cis**: similar groups on the **same** side.
- **trans**: similar groups on **opposite** sides.

```text
   H3C       CH3            H3C       H
      \     /                  \     /
       C = C                    C = C
      /     \                  /     \
     H       H                H       CH3
   cis-2-butene              trans-2-butene

   HOOC      COOH            HOOC      H
      \     /                  \     /
       C = C                    C = C
      /     \                  /     \
     H       H                H       COOH
   maleic acid (cis)         fumaric acid (trans)
```

Limitation: cis/trans fails when all four groups are different (for example BrClC=CHF), because there is no "similar" pair.

### E–Z system

Works for every alkene.

1. Rank the two groups on **each** carbon separately by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog sequence rules (Unit 1).
2. If the two higher-priority groups are on the **same** side → **Z** (zusammen, together).
3. If on **opposite** sides → **E** (entgegen, opposite).

Example: 1-bromo-1-chloro-2-fluoroethene. On C-1, Br > Cl; on C-2, F > H. If Br and F are on the same side → (Z); opposite → (E).

Caution: cis is not always Z. In 2-chloro-2-butene type compounds, the priority order can make a "cis" arrangement of methyl groups E.

### Syn–anti system (oximes and C=N compounds)

The nitrogen of an oxime carries –OH and a lone pair.

- **Aldoximes** (RCH=NOH): **syn** when H and OH are on the same side; **anti** when they are on opposite sides. (Older books used the aryl group instead of H as the reference, so always state which convention you use.)
- **Ketoximes** (RR'C=NOH): the name names the group the OH is near, for example syn-phenyl methyl ketoxime means OH is on the side of the phenyl group.
- The E/Z system is now preferred for oximes as well.

## 2.3 Properties of geometrical isomers

| Property | cis isomer | trans isomer | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stability, heat of hydrogenation and combustion | Less stable; higher heats | More stable | Bulky groups crowd each other on the same side |
| Melting point | Lower | Higher | trans packs better in the crystal |
| Dipole moment | Higher (cis-1,2-dichloroethene about 1.9 D) | Zero or low (trans-1,2-dichloroethene 0 D) | Bond dipoles add in cis and cancel in trans |
| Boiling point | Usually higher | Usually lower | Higher polarity of cis |
| Solubility in water | Higher | Lower | Higher polarity |
| Density and refractive index | Usually higher | Usually lower | — |

Maleic acid melts at about 130–135 °C and is very soluble in water; fumaric acid melts near 287 °C (in a sealed tube) and is much less soluble.

## 2.4 Methods of determining the configuration of geometrical isomers

### A. Physical methods

1. **Dipole moment** — for symmetrical alkenes Xab=Cab, the isomer with the higher dipole moment is cis; trans has a zero or nearly zero value.
2. **Melting point and solubility** — the higher-melting, less soluble isomer is usually trans.
3. **Heat of combustion or hydrogenation** — the isomer releasing more heat (less stable) is usually cis.
4. **UV spectroscopy** — the trans isomer of a conjugated system such as stilbene is more planar, so it absorbs at a longer wavelength and with higher intensity than the cis isomer.
5. **IR spectroscopy** — trans-disubstituted alkenes show a strong C–H out-of-plane bending band near 965 cm⁻¹; cis-disubstituted alkenes absorb at lower frequency (roughly 675–730 cm⁻¹).
6. **NMR spectroscopy** — the coupling constant between the two vinylic hydrogens is larger for trans (about 12–18 Hz) than for cis (about 6–12 Hz).
7. **X-ray crystallography** — gives the full three-dimensional arrangement directly.

### B. Chemical methods

1. **Formation of a cyclic compound** — groups that react with each other to form a ring must be close together, which is possible only in the cis isomer. Maleic acid loses water on heating to give maleic anhydride; fumaric acid cannot form an anhydride directly (on strong heating it first converts to maleic anhydride). So maleic acid is cis.
2. **Formation from a ring compound** — opening a ring by a reaction that does not touch the double bond gives a cis product. Oxidation of benzene or quinone giving maleic acid supports its cis structure.
3. **Stereospecific addition reactions** — cold dilute alkaline KMnO4 adds two –OH groups to the same face (syn addition). Maleic acid gives meso-tartaric acid, while fumaric acid gives (±)-tartaric acid. Since the product's configuration is known, the starting alkene's configuration follows.
4. **Correlation with a compound of known configuration** — converting the alkene, without disturbing the double bond, into or from a compound whose configuration is already known.

### C. Configuration of oximes

- **Ketoximes — Beckmann rearrangement.** In acid (PCl5 or conc. H2SO4) the group **anti** to the –OH migrates to nitrogen (Unit 5). The amide formed shows which group was anti. For example, if acetophenone oxime gives acetanilide (CH3CONHC6H5), the phenyl group was anti to the OH.
- **Aldoximes — elimination test.** On treatment with acetic anhydride and then base, the isomer in which H and OH are trans to each other loses acetic acid easily and gives a nitrile (anti elimination); the other isomer gives back the oxime.

## 2.5 Conformational isomerism

**Conformations (conformers, rotamers)** are different spatial arrangements of a molecule produced by rotation about single (σ) bonds. They interconvert rapidly at room temperature and usually cannot be isolated. **Conformational analysis** studies their energies and populations.

**Ways of drawing them**

- **Sawhorse projection** — the C–C bond is drawn diagonally in perspective.
- **Newman projection** — looking straight down the C–C bond: the front carbon is a point where three bonds meet; the rear carbon is a circle with three bonds coming from its edge.

**Strains that raise energy**

| Strain | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Torsional strain | Repulsion between bonding electron pairs in eclipsed bonds |
| Steric (van der Waals) strain | Bulky groups forced closer than their van der Waals radii allow |
| Angle strain | Bond angles forced away from the ideal (109.5° for sp3 carbon) |

### Ethane

```text
 Newman projection, looking down the C1–C2 bond
 (front C = centre point, rear C = circle)

 STAGGERED: rear H's sit exactly between the front H's
            H–C–C–H dihedral angle 60°  → lowest energy
 ECLIPSED : rear H's sit directly behind the front H's
            H–C–C–H dihedral angle 0°   → highest energy

 0° ──► 60° ──► 120° ──► 180° ──► 240° ──► 300° ──► 360°
 ecl.   stag.   ecl.     stag.    ecl.     stag.    ecl.
```

- Rotating one carbon through 360° passes through three staggered and three eclipsed forms.
- The eclipsed form is about **12 kJ/mol (about 2.9 kcal/mol)** higher than the staggered form, entirely due to torsional strain. This barrier is small, so rotation is fast at room temperature; ethane is almost always found staggered.

### n-Butane (rotation about C2–C3)

| Conformation | CH3–CH3 dihedral angle | Relative energy (approx.) | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anti (staggered) | 180° | 0 (most stable) | Methyls as far apart as possible |
| Gauche (staggered) | 60° | about 3.8 kJ/mol | Steric strain between methyls |
| Eclipsed (CH3 eclipses H) | 120° | about 16 kJ/mol | Torsional + some steric strain |
| Fully eclipsed (syn) | 0° | about 19 kJ/mol or more (least stable) | Methyl eclipses methyl |

Stability order: **anti > gauche > eclipsed > fully eclipsed**. At room temperature most butane molecules are in the anti form, with a smaller fraction gauche.

```text
 Energy
   ▲      fully                         fully
   │     eclipsed                      eclipsed
   │       /\        eclipsed  eclipsed  /\
   │      /  \         /\        /\     /  \
   │     /    \ gauche/  \ anti /  \gauche  \
   │____/      \_/\__/    \_/\_/    \_/\_____\___► rotation 0° → 360°
```

### Cyclohexane

Cyclohexane is not flat. A flat hexagon would have 120° angles (angle strain) and all bonds eclipsed. Puckering removes both problems.

**Conformations and approximate energies above the chair**

| Conformation | Energy (approx.) | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chair | 0 (most stable) | All C–C–C angles about 111°, all neighbouring C–H bonds staggered — almost strain-free |
| Twist-boat (skew-boat) | about 23 kJ/mol | Partly relieves the boat's strains; a true energy minimum but rarely populated |
| Boat | about 29 kJ/mol | Four pairs of eclipsed bonds and a "flagpole" clash between the two hydrogens pointing up at C-1 and C-4 |
| Half-chair | about 45 kJ/mol | Highest point on the path from chair to twist-boat (transition state) |

At room temperature over 99.9% of cyclohexane molecules are in the chair form.

```text
 CHAIR: carbons 1 and 4 point to opposite sides of the mean plane
        (one "up", one "down") → all bonds staggered, no flagpole clash
 BOAT : carbons 1 and 4 both point to the same side
        → the two inner ("flagpole") H's on C-1 and C-4 clash,
          and the C2–C3 and C5–C6 bonds are eclipsed

 chair ⇌ half-chair ⇌ twist-boat ⇌ boat ⇌ twist-boat ⇌ half-chair ⇌ other chair
```

**Axial and equatorial bonds.** Each carbon of the chair has one **axial** bond (parallel to the ring axis, alternately up and down) and one **equatorial** bond (roughly along the "equator" of the ring). There are 6 axial and 6 equatorial hydrogens.

**Ring flipping.** The chair rapidly flips into the other chair; every axial bond becomes equatorial and vice versa.

**Monosubstituted cyclohexanes.** A substituent prefers the **equatorial** position. In the axial position it clashes with the two axial hydrogens on the same side, three carbons away (**1,3-diaxial interactions**, which are gauche-butane-like interactions). For methylcyclohexane about 95% of molecules have the methyl equatorial at room temperature. A very bulky group such as tert-butyl stays equatorial almost completely and "locks" the ring.

**Disubstituted cyclohexanes.** The most stable conformation is the one with the larger number of (and the bulkier) groups equatorial. trans-1,4-Dimethylcyclohexane can place both methyls equatorial and is more stable than the cis isomer, which must have one axial.

## 2.6 Stereoisomerism in biphenyl compounds (atropisomerism)

Biphenyl normally rotates freely about the single bond joining the two rings. If **bulky groups occupy the ortho positions** (2, 2', 6 and 6'), they bump into each other and rotation is blocked. The two rings are then held roughly perpendicular to each other.

**Atropisomers** are stereoisomers that can be isolated because rotation about a single bond is hindered (Greek *atropos*, "not turning"). The chirality is due to a **chiral axis**, not a stereocentre.

**Conditions for optical activity in biphenyls**

1. The ortho substituents must be large enough to prevent rotation at the working temperature (for example –NO2, –COOH, –Br, –I, –SO3H). Small groups such as –F or –OCH3 often allow slow rotation and the forms racemise.
2. **Each ring must be unsymmetrically substituted**: the two ortho groups on the same ring must be different (a ≠ b on one ring and c ≠ d on the other). If either ring carries two identical ortho groups, the molecule has a plane of symmetry and is optically inactive.

```text
       a           c          a ≠ b  and  c ≠ d
        \         /           rings held perpendicular
    ring A ──── ring B        → non-superimposable mirror images
        /         \
       b           d
 Example: 6,6'-dinitrobiphenyl-2,2'-dicarboxylic acid (6,6'-dinitrodiphenic acid)
 was resolved into optically active forms.
```

Atropisomers interconvert if heated enough to overcome the barrier; the higher the barrier, the more stable the separate forms. Related cases of axial chirality occur in suitably substituted allenes (abC=C=Cab) and spiranes. Atropisomerism is relevant to some modern drugs whose hindered biaryl bonds give separable isomers with different activity.

## 2.7 Stereospecific and stereoselective reactions

**Stereoselective reaction**: one stereoisomer of the product is formed **in preference** to others, when more than one could form. Selectivity can be partial.

- Dehydrohalogenation of 2-bromobutane with alcoholic KOH gives more trans-2-butene than cis-2-butene.
- Reduction of 4-tert-butylcyclohexanone with LiAlH4 gives mainly the trans alcohol (OH equatorial).
- Asymmetric synthesis (Unit 1) is enantioselective.

**Stereospecific reaction**: stereoisomeric starting materials give **stereoisomerically different** products — the mechanism decides the stereochemistry.

| Reaction | Starting isomer | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Br2 addition (anti addition, through a bromonium ion) | cis-2-butene | (±)-2,3-dibromobutane |
| | trans-2-butene | meso-2,3-dibromobutane |
| Cold dilute alkaline KMnO4 or OsO4 (syn addition) | cis-2-butene | meso-2,3-butanediol |
| | trans-2-butene | (±)-2,3-butanediol |
| | maleic acid | meso-tartaric acid |
| | fumaric acid | (±)-tartaric acid |
| SN2 substitution | (R) halide | (S) product (inversion) |
| E2 elimination | needs H and leaving group anti-periplanar; different diastereomers give different alkene isomers |
| Catalytic hydrogenation of alkynes (Lindlar catalyst) vs Na in liquid NH3 | same alkyne | cis-alkene vs trans-alkene |

**Relationship:** every stereospecific reaction is stereoselective, but a stereoselective reaction need not be stereospecific.

**Mechanism of anti addition of bromine (why cis gives racemate):**

```text
 cis-2-butene + Br2 → cyclic bromonium ion (Br bridges both carbons from one face)
 Br⁻ attacks from the opposite face, at either carbon (50:50)
 → (2R,3R) + (2S,3S) = racemic 2,3-dibromobutane
 The same steps on trans-2-butene give (2R,3S) = meso
```

## Remember

- Geometrical isomerism needs restricted rotation **and** two different groups on each doubly bonded carbon.
- cis/trans uses similar groups; E/Z uses CIP priority on each carbon; syn/anti for oximes.
- trans: higher melting point, more stable, lower dipole; cis: higher dipole, more soluble, forms cyclic anhydride (maleic acid).
- Determine configuration by dipole moment, melting point, IR (trans ~965 cm⁻¹), NMR J (trans larger), ring formation, syn addition products, Beckmann rearrangement (anti group migrates).
- Ethane: staggered more stable by about 12 kJ/mol. Butane: anti > gauche > eclipsed > fully eclipsed.
- Cyclohexane: chair > twist-boat > boat > half-chair; substituents prefer equatorial (1,3-diaxial strain).
- Biphenyl optical activity: bulky ortho groups and each ring unsymmetrically substituted; chiral axis, not a stereocentre.
- Stereospecific: product stereochemistry fixed by the starting isomer (Br2 to cis-2-butene → racemate, to trans → meso). Stereoselective: one product preferred.
