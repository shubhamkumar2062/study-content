# Unit 3: Plant tissue culture

Plant tissue culture is the growing of plant cells, tissues or organs on a sterile nutrient medium under controlled conditions in the laboratory. It rests on **totipotency** — the ability of a single living plant cell to divide and regenerate into a complete plant. For pharmacognosy it offers a way to produce plant drugs without depending on fields and seasons, to multiply rare plants, and to produce disease-free and improved plants. This unit covers its history, types of culture, nutritional needs, growth and maintenance, applications, and edible vaccines.

## 3.1 Historical development

| Year | Worker | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1838–1839 | Schleiden and Schwann | Cell theory: the cell is the basic unit of life, implying totipotency |
| 1902 | Gottlieb Haberlandt | First attempt to culture isolated plant cells; proposed totipotency — called the father of plant tissue culture |
| 1934 | Philip White | Continuous culture of excised tomato roots |
| 1939 | Gautheret, Nobécourt and White (independently) | First continuously growing callus cultures |
| 1955 | Miller and Skoog | Discovery of kinetin, the first cytokinin |
| 1957 | Skoog and Miller | Auxin–cytokinin ratio controls root and shoot formation |
| 1958 | F. C. Steward | Whole carrot plants regenerated from single cells (somatic embryos) — proof of totipotency |
| 1960 | Cocking | Enzymatic isolation of protoplasts |
| 1962 | Murashige and Skoog | MS medium, the most widely used culture medium |
| 1964 | Guha and Maheshwari (India) | Haploid plants from anther culture of *Datura innoxia* |
| 1983 | Commercial production | Shikonin from cell cultures of *Lithospermum erythrorhizon* (Japan), the first commercial plant cell culture product |

## 3.2 Basic terms

- **Explant** — the piece of plant tissue (leaf, stem, root, embryo, anther) placed on the medium to start a culture.
- **Callus** — an unorganised, undifferentiated mass of dividing cells formed from an explant on a medium containing auxin (and usually cytokinin).
- **Differentiation** — the formation of organised tissues and organs.
- **Dedifferentiation** — mature cells returning to a dividing, unorganised state.
- **Redifferentiation** — callus cells forming organs again.
- **Organogenesis** — formation of shoots or roots from callus or explant.
- **Somatic embryogenesis** — formation of embryos from body (non-sexual) cells.
- **Subculture** — transfer of a culture to fresh medium.

## 3.3 Types of cultures

| Type | Description | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Callus culture** | Explant on solid (agar) medium forms callus | Starting point for suspension cultures, organogenesis, secondary metabolites |
| **Suspension culture** | Cells or small clumps grown in liquid medium on a shaker or in a bioreactor | Large-scale production of metabolites; biotransformation |
| **Organ culture** | Isolated organs grown in vitro | Study of organ development; metabolite production |
| — Root culture | Excised roots in liquid medium | Alkaloids made in roots (tropane alkaloids) |
| — Shoot tip / meristem culture | Apical meristem (0.1–0.5 mm) cultured | Virus-free plants, micropropagation |
| — Embryo culture | Immature embryos grown | Rescue of hybrid embryos that would abort |
| **Anther and pollen culture** | Anthers or microspores grown | Haploid plants (n); doubled to homozygous lines for breeding |
| **Ovary and ovule culture** | Female organs grown | Haploids, in vitro fertilisation |
| **Protoplast culture** | Cells without walls (cell wall removed by cellulase and pectinase) | Somatic hybridisation by protoplast fusion; gene transfer |
| **Hairy root culture** | Roots transformed by *Agrobacterium rhizogenes*; grow fast without hormones and are genetically stable | Stable, high production of root metabolites |
| **Immobilised cell culture** | Cells trapped in gels such as calcium alginate | Continuous production; easy separation of product |

**Kinds of suspension cultures**
- **Batch culture:** cells grow in a fixed volume of medium until the nutrients run out.
- **Continuous culture:** fresh medium is added and an equal volume removed, so growth continues.
  - *Chemostat* — growth is controlled by a limiting nutrient.
  - *Turbidostat* — the cell density is kept constant.
- **Semi-continuous culture:** part of the culture is removed and replaced with fresh medium at intervals.

## 3.4 Nutritional requirements (culture medium)

A plant tissue culture medium must supply everything the cells cannot make for themselves, because cultured cells usually do not photosynthesise enough to feed themselves.

| Component | Examples and role |
| --- | --- |
| **Macronutrients** (mM amounts) | N (as nitrate and ammonium), P, K, Ca, Mg, S — for proteins, nucleic acids, membranes, enzymes |
| **Micronutrients** (µM amounts) | Fe (as Fe–EDTA so it stays available), Mn, Zn, B, Cu, Mo, Co, I |
| **Carbon source** | Sucrose, usually 2–3% — energy and carbon, since cultures are heterotrophic |
| **Vitamins** | Thiamine (essential), nicotinic acid, pyridoxine, myo-inositol |
| **Amino acids / nitrogen supplements** | Glycine, glutamine, casein hydrolysate |
| **Plant growth regulators** | Auxins (IAA, NAA, 2,4-D, IBA) and cytokinins (kinetin, BAP, zeatin); sometimes gibberellins and ABA |
| **Complex natural additives** | Coconut milk (coconut water), yeast extract, malt extract |
| **Gelling agent** | Agar (about 0.6–1%) or gellan gum for solid media; omitted in liquid media |
| **pH** | Adjusted to about 5.6–5.8 before autoclaving |
| **Others** | Activated charcoal (adsorbs toxic phenols), antioxidants such as ascorbic acid |

**Common media:** Murashige and Skoog (MS, high salt, most widely used), White's medium (low salt, root cultures), Gamborg's B5 (cell suspensions), Nitsch's medium (anther culture), Woody Plant Medium.

## 3.5 General technique, growth and maintenance

```text
Select healthy mother plant → explant
        ↓ surface sterilisation (70% ethanol briefly, then sodium hypochlorite
          or mercuric chloride; rinse in sterile water)
Inoculate explant on sterile medium (in laminar air-flow cabinet)
        ↓ incubate: about 25 ± 2 °C, controlled light (often 16 h light / 8 h dark)
Callus / shoots / embryos form
        ↓ subculture every 3–4 weeks
Suspension culture or regeneration of plantlets
        ↓ rooting → hardening (acclimatisation) → transfer to soil
```

**Asepsis (sterilisation)**
- Media and glassware are autoclaved at 121 °C and 15 lb/in² for about 15–20 minutes.
- Heat-sensitive components (some vitamins, hormones, antibiotics) are sterilised by membrane filtration (0.22 µm).
- Instruments are flame- or bead-sterilised.
- All work is done in a laminar air-flow cabinet.

### Growth measurement

| Method | Principle |
| --- | --- |
| Fresh weight | Cells are filtered and weighed; quick but includes water |
| Dry weight | Cells dried to constant weight; more accurate |
| Packed cell volume (PCV) | Suspension centrifuged in a graduated tube; volume of cells read |
| Cell count | Counted with a haemocytometer after separating clumps (chromic acid or pectinase) |
| Mitotic index | Percentage of cells dividing |
| Viability | Fluorescein diacetate (live cells fluoresce green); Evans blue (dead cells stain blue); TTC reduction to red formazan by living cells |

**Growth curve of a batch suspension culture (sigmoid):**

```text
Biomass
  |                         _________  stationary
  |                       /
  |                     /   linear / deceleration
  |                   /
  |                /     exponential (log) growth
  |  ____________/
  |  lag phase
  +------------------------------------------ Time
Secondary metabolites are often highest in the late linear and stationary phases.
```

### Maintenance of cultures

- Regular **subculture** to fresh medium before nutrients run out or toxic products build up.
- **Slow-growth storage:** low temperature (4–10 °C), reduced nutrients, or growth retardants. This lengthens the time between subcultures.
- **Cryopreservation:** storage at −196 °C in liquid nitrogen after treatment with cryoprotectants (DMSO, glycerol, sucrose). This stops metabolism, so cultures keep their genetic make-up for long periods.
- **Somaclonal variation** (genetic change in long-term cultures) is a problem. It is reduced by regular checks and cryopreservation.

## 3.6 Applications of plant tissue culture in pharmacognosy

**1. Production of secondary metabolites.** Valuable compounds are produced in fermenters independent of climate, season, pests and land.
- Examples: shikonin (*Lithospermum erythrorhizon*); ginsenosides (*Panax ginseng*); berberine (*Coptis japonica*); paclitaxel by plant cell fermentation of *Taxus* cells; rosmarinic acid (*Coleus blumei*).
- **Ways to raise yield:**
  - select high-yielding cell lines;
  - optimise the medium and hormones;
  - add precursors;
  - add elicitors (methyl jasmonate, fungal extracts) that switch on defence pathways;
  - immobilise the cells;
  - use hairy root cultures;
  - use two-phase systems that remove the product.

**2. Biotransformation.** Cultured cells convert a cheap compound into a more valuable one.
- Example: cell cultures of *Digitalis lanata* convert β-methyldigitoxin to β-methyldigoxin by 12β-hydroxylation, a step that is difficult chemically.

**3. Micropropagation (clonal propagation).** Thousands of identical plants from one elite plant, year-round and in a small space. Used for rare medicinal plants, plants hard to raise from seed, and high-yielding selections.

**4. Disease-free (virus-free) plants.** The apical meristem is usually free of virus, so meristem culture gives virus-free stock (potato, banana, sugarcane).

**5. Haploid production.** Anther/pollen culture gives haploids. Doubling their chromosomes with colchicine produces pure (homozygous) lines quickly for breeding.

**6. Somatic hybridisation.** Protoplast fusion combines species that cannot be crossed sexually.

**7. Embryo rescue.** Hybrid embryos that would abort in the seed are grown to plants.

**8. Germplasm conservation.** Slow-growth storage and cryopreservation keep rare and endangered medicinal plants.

**9. Genetic transformation.** Genes are introduced (by *Agrobacterium* or gene gun) to raise metabolite yield or resistance, and to make edible vaccines.

**10. Study of biosynthetic pathways** and of enzymes, using cultures fed with labelled precursors.

**Limitations:**
- Many cultures make little of the desired compound, because differentiated tissues are often needed.
- Cell lines are genetically unstable.
- Plant cells grow slowly.
- Large-scale fermentation is costly.
- Cells are sensitive to shear in bioreactors.

## 3.7 Edible vaccines

An **edible vaccine** is a vaccine produced in a genetically modified (transgenic) plant, so that eating the plant (or a product made from it) delivers the antigen and stimulates immunity. The idea was proposed in the early 1990s.

**Principle.** The gene coding for an antigenic protein of a pathogen is introduced into a plant. The plant makes the antigen in its edible tissue. When eaten, the antigen reaches the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches). This stimulates **mucosal immunity** (secretory IgA) as well as systemic immunity (IgG). Mucosal immunity is valuable because many pathogens enter through mucosal surfaces.

**Steps in making an edible vaccine**
1. Select the antigen gene of the pathogen.
2. Insert it into a plant expression vector with a suitable promoter.
3. Transform plant cells:
   - by *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*–mediated transfer, or
   - by the biolistic method (gene gun, DNA-coated gold or tungsten particles).
4. Regenerate whole transgenic plants by tissue culture.
5. Confirm that the antigen is expressed and test the immune response.

**Plants used:** potato, tomato, banana, lettuce, rice, maize, carrot, spinach. Banana and tomato can be eaten raw; potato must be cooked, which may destroy the antigen.

**Examples studied:**
- hepatitis B surface antigen (in potato and lettuce),
- heat-labile enterotoxin B subunit of *Escherichia coli* (in potato),
- cholera toxin B subunit,
- Norwalk virus capsid protein,
- rabies glycoprotein.

**Advantages:**
- Cheap to produce and easy to scale up by farming.
- No needles, syringes or trained staff; no pain or needle-borne infection.
- No cold chain needed for many forms (dried or processed plant material).
- Plant proteins are free of animal pathogens.
- Gives mucosal immunity.
- Well suited to developing countries.

**Limitations:**
- Dose is hard to control, since antigen content varies between plants and fruits.
- Proteins may be broken down in the stomach.
- Repeated oral antigen can cause oral tolerance instead of immunity.
- Allergy is possible.
- Antigen may be destroyed by cooking or ripening.
- Need to keep vaccine plants out of the food supply.
- Regulatory and public-acceptance issues with GM crops.

## Remember

- Totipotency: a single plant cell can form a whole plant; Haberlandt (1902) is the father of plant tissue culture.
- White (1934) root culture; Skoog and Miller (1957) auxin:cytokinin ratio; Murashige and Skoog (1962) MS medium; Guha and Maheshwari (1964) haploids from *Datura* anthers.
- Types: callus, suspension (batch, continuous, semi-continuous), organ, anther, protoplast, hairy root, immobilised.
- Medium: macro- and micronutrients, sucrose 2–3%, vitamins (thiamine), auxin + cytokinin, agar 0.6–1%, pH 5.6–5.8, autoclave 121 °C.
- High auxin → roots; high cytokinin → shoots; balance → callus.
- Growth measured by fresh/dry weight, PCV, cell count; viability by fluorescein diacetate or Evans blue.
- Shikonin was the first commercial plant cell culture product; *Digitalis lanata* cells hydroxylate β-methyldigitoxin to β-methyldigoxin.
- Edible vaccine = antigen expressed in a transgenic edible plant; gives mucosal (IgA) immunity; dose control is the main problem.
