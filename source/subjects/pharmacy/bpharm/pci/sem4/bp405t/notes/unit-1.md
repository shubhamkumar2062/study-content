# Unit 1: Introduction to pharmacognosy, classification and quality control of crude drugs

Pharmacognosy is the oldest branch of pharmacy. Before chemists could make medicines in flasks, every medicine came from a plant, an animal or a mineral. This unit explains what pharmacognosy is, where natural drugs come from, the ways crude drugs are grouped, how they are spoiled by adulteration, and how a pharmacist checks their identity and quality, including the measurements made under the microscope.

## 1.1 Definition, history, scope and development

**Pharmacognosy** comes from two Greek words: *pharmakon* (drug) and *gnosis* (knowledge). It is the study of drugs of natural origin — their biological source, cultivation, collection, preparation, identification, chemical constituents, evaluation, preservation and uses. The word was introduced by C. A. Seydler in 1815 (in his work *Analecta Pharmacognostica*).

A **crude drug** is a plant, animal or mineral material that is used as a medicine after only collection, drying and simple processing (it has not been purified into a single compound). Examples: senna leaf, cinchona bark, ginger rhizome, beeswax.

**Landmarks in history**

| Period / source | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Vedic India (Rigveda, Atharvaveda) | Earliest Indian references to medicinal plants; Atharvaveda is the root of Ayurveda |
| Charaka Samhita, Sushruta Samhita | Classical Ayurvedic texts describing hundreds of plant drugs and surgery |
| Shen Nung (China) | Pen Ts'ao, a herbal of Chinese medicinal plants |
| Ebers papyrus (ancient Egypt) | Lists plant drugs such as castor oil, aloe, opium |
| Hippocrates and Theophrastus (Greece) | Rational medicine; Theophrastus wrote on plants and their uses |
| Dioscorides (1st century AD) | *De Materia Medica*, a standard drug text for about 1,500 years |
| Galen (2nd century AD) | Preparations of plant drugs; the word "galenicals" comes from him |
| Avicenna (Ibn Sina) | *Canon of Medicine*, base of Unani medicine |
| Early 19th century | Isolation of pure active principles: morphine from opium, then quinine, strychnine, atropine and others |
| 20th–21st century | Antibiotics, plant tissue culture, biotechnology, marine drugs, herbal standardisation |

**Development of the subject.** Pharmacognosy began as "materia medica" — a description of whole drugs. It then became *descriptive pharmacognosy* (morphology and microscopy for identification), then *chemical pharmacognosy* or phytochemistry (isolating and identifying constituents), and today includes *biological/biotechnological pharmacognosy* (tissue culture, genetic improvement, biosynthesis) and *herbal drug standardisation*.

**Scope of pharmacognosy**

1. Identification and authentication of crude drugs and detection of adulteration.
2. Isolation of active constituents that become modern medicines (digoxin, morphine, vincristine, paclitaxel, artemisinin).
3. Starting materials for semi-synthetic drugs (diosgenin → steroid hormones; thebaine → semi-synthetic opioids).
4. Pharmaceutical aids from nature: gums, starch, fixed oils, waxes, gelatin, agar used as excipients.
5. Standardisation and quality control of herbal medicines and AYUSH products.
6. Cultivation and genetic improvement of medicinal plants, conservation of rare species.
7. Plant tissue culture and biotechnology for producing valuable compounds.
8. Search for new drugs from plants, microbes and marine organisms.
9. Nutraceuticals, cosmetics, flavours and perfumes (volatile oils).

## 1.2 Sources of drugs

| Source | What is used | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plants (the largest source) | Leaves, barks, roots, rhizomes, flowers, fruits, seeds, whole herbs, exudates | Senna and digitalis leaves, cinchona bark, rauwolfia root, ginger rhizome, clove buds, fennel fruit, nux vomica seed |
| Animals | Secretions, glands, tissues, products | Honey and beeswax (bee), wool fat (sheep), gelatin (animal collagen), cod liver oil (fish liver), pepsin (stomach of pig), insulin (formerly from pancreas) |
| Marine organisms | Algae, sponges, tunicates, molluscs, fish | Agar and alginates (algae), cytarabine (developed from sponge nucleosides), ziconotide (cone snail toxin), protamine (fish sperm) |
| Plant tissue culture | Cells grown in vitro in fermenters | Shikonin (from *Lithospermum erythrorhizon* cells, the first commercial product), paclitaxel by plant cell fermentation |
| Microorganisms | Fermentation products | Penicillin, streptomycin, enzymes such as streptokinase |
| Minerals | Inorganic natural materials | Kaolin, talc, bentonite, calamine |

## 1.3 Organised and unorganised drugs

**Organised drugs** are made of cells and tissues and have a definite structure, so they can be identified by morphology and microscopy. They are direct parts of a plant or animal: leaves (senna), barks (cinnamon), roots (ipecacuanha), rhizomes (ginger), flowers (clove), fruits (coriander), seeds (linseed), woods (sandalwood), whole plants (vinca).

**Unorganised drugs** have no cellular structure. They are products made from a plant by incision, drying or extraction, so they are identified mainly by physical and chemical tests.

| Type | How it is obtained | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dried latex | Milky juice from latex vessels, collected after incision and dried | Opium (unripe capsules of *Papaver somniferum*), papain from papaya fruit |
| Dried juice | Cell sap that flows from cut tissues, then evaporated | Aloe (juice from leaves) |
| Dried extract | Plant part boiled with water; extract concentrated and dried | Pale catechu (leaves and twigs of *Uncaria gambier*), black catechu (heartwood of *Acacia catechu*) |
| Gums | Exudates formed by breakdown of cell walls (gummosis) | Acacia, tragacanth, karaya (sterculia) |
| Mucilages | Polysaccharides present within cells; swell in water | Ispaghula seed coat, linseed |
| Oleoresins | Mixture of resin and volatile oil | Turpentine, ginger oleoresin, capsicum oleoresin |
| Oleo-gum-resins | Resin + volatile oil + gum | Asafoetida, myrrh, guggul |
| Balsams | Resins rich in benzoic and/or cinnamic acid | Benzoin, Tolu balsam, Peru balsam |
| Others | Waxes, fats, starches | Beeswax, wool fat, maize starch |

## 1.4 Classification of crude drugs

A classification is a way of arranging thousands of drugs so they can be studied. Each system has merits and demerits.

**1. Alphabetical classification.** Drugs are listed A to Z by Latin or English name. Used in pharmacopoeias (IP, BP, USP), formularies and dictionaries.
- Merit: quick to locate a drug.
- Demerit: no relation between neighbouring drugs; shows nothing about source, chemistry or action.

**2. Morphological classification.** Drugs are grouped by the plant part used.

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Leaves | Digitalis, senna, vasaka, belladonna, eucalyptus |
| Barks | Cinchona, cinnamon, cascara, arjuna |
| Roots and rhizomes | Rauwolfia, ipecacuanha, liquorice, ginger, turmeric |
| Flowers | Clove, saffron, chamomile |
| Fruits | Fennel, coriander, cardamom, senna pod |
| Seeds | Nux vomica, linseed, castor, isapgol |
| Woods | Sandalwood, quassia |
| Herbs (entire plant) | Vinca, ephedra, kalmegh |
| Unorganised | Aloe, opium, acacia, agar, beeswax |

Merit: useful for identifying an unknown drug in a laboratory. Demerit: drugs of different chemistry and action fall together.

**3. Taxonomical (biological) classification.** Drugs are arranged by the natural system of plant classification: Kingdom → Division → Class → Order → Family → Genus → Species. For example, family Solanaceae (belladonna, datura, hyoscyamus), Apocynaceae (rauwolfia, vinca), Apiaceae/Umbelliferae (coriander, fennel, ajowan), Leguminosae (senna, liquorice, acacia, tragacanth). Merit: shows botanical relationships, and related plants often have related chemistry. Demerit: does not tell the use of the drug.

**4. Chemical classification.** Drugs are grouped by their main active constituent: alkaloids (cinchona, nux vomica), glycosides (digitalis, senna), tannins (catechu, myrobalan), volatile oils (clove, peppermint), resins (benzoin, colophony), carbohydrates (acacia, agar), lipids (castor oil, beeswax), proteins (gelatin). Merit: best for phytochemical study and evaluation. Demerit: a drug containing two types of constituents is hard to place.

**5. Pharmacological (therapeutic) classification.** Drugs are grouped by their action.

| Action | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiotonic | Digitalis, strophanthus, thevetia |
| Purgative | Senna, aloe, cascara, castor oil |
| Antihypertensive | Rauwolfia |
| Antimalarial | Cinchona, artemisia |
| Anticancer | Vinca, podophyllum, taxus |
| CNS stimulant | Coffee, tea |
| Carminative | Fennel, cardamom, coriander |
| Astringent | Catechu, myrobalan |

Merit: useful for medical use. Demerit: one drug with several actions is hard to place, and plants of different chemistry fall together.

**6. Chemotaxonomic classification.** Plants are classified by combining taxonomy with the chemical constituents they make. Related plants tend to produce similar compounds, so chemistry is used as a taxonomic character. Examples: tropane alkaloids are typical of Solanaceae, isoquinoline alkaloids of Papaveraceae and Berberidaceae, sesquiterpene lactones of Asteraceae, glucosinolates (isothiocyanate glycosides) of Brassicaceae. It helps to find new sources of a compound in related species.

**7. Serotaxonomic classification.** Uses serology — the reaction between antigens and antibodies — to compare plant proteins. Proteins from one plant are injected into an animal to raise antibodies; the antiserum is then tested against proteins of other plants. A strong reaction means closely related proteins and therefore closely related plants. It settles doubtful relationships between taxa.

## 1.5 Quality control of drugs of natural origin

### Adulteration

**Adulteration** is the substitution of the original drug, partly or wholly, with an inferior, defective, spoiled or worthless material, or the addition of foreign matter, so that the quality of the drug is lowered. It may be deliberate (for profit) or accidental (confusion of names, careless collection, wrong storage).

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substitution with an inferior commercial variety | Cheaper related drug sold as the genuine one | Cassia bark for Ceylon cinnamon |
| Substitution with exhausted drug | Drug whose active matter has already been extracted | Exhausted clove or ginger (oil removed) mixed with genuine drug |
| Substitution with superficially similar but cheaper material | Looks alike but different plant | Ailanthus leaves in belladonna; safflower (*Carthamus*) florets in saffron |
| Artificially manufactured substitutes | Made to imitate the drug | Artificial coffee beans, compressed chicory with coffee |
| Addition of worthless heavy material | Increases weight | Sand, stones or earth in asafoetida; lead shot in opium |
| Addition of synthetic principles | Cheap chemical added to improve test values | Citral added to lemon oil |
| Use of vegetative parts of the same plant | Stems or other parts mixed with the official part | Stems in senna or stramonium leaves |
| Deterioration | Poor storage: moisture, insects, moulds, heat | Mouldy or insect-damaged drugs |

### Evaluation of crude drugs

Evaluation means confirming the **identity**, **quality** and **purity** of a drug. Five groups of methods are used.

**1. Organoleptic (morphological) evaluation** — using the senses and simple inspection: colour, odour, taste, size, shape, surface, texture and fracture. Examples: aromatic odour of clove and fennel; sweet taste of liquorice; bitter taste of quassia and chirata; fibrous fracture of cinchona bark; short fracture of rhubarb; the pentagonal mericarp of fennel. Quick and cheap, but needs experience and cannot detect chemical quality.

**2. Microscopic evaluation** — identification of tissues and cell contents in sections or powders.
- **Stomata types:** anomocytic (irregular-celled, ranunculaceous) — digitalis; anisocytic (unequal-celled, cruciferous) — belladonna, datura; diacytic (cross-celled, caryophyllaceous) — vasaka, peppermint; paracytic (parallel-celled, rubiaceous) — senna.
- **Trichomes:** covering (non-glandular) and glandular hairs (e.g., uniseriate multicellular warty hairs of datura, lignified unicellular hairs of senna).
- **Calcium oxalate crystals:** prisms (senna), rosettes or clusters (rhubarb, datura), acicular raphides (squill), microsphenoidal sandy crystals (belladonna).
- **Starch grains, aleurone grains, fibres, sclereids, vessels, oil glands.**
- **Histochemical tests** on sections:

| Reagent | Detects | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phloroglucinol + HCl | Lignin | Pink to red |
| Iodine solution | Starch | Blue |
| Sudan III | Fats, volatile oils | Red |
| Ruthenium red | Mucilage | Pink |
| Ferric chloride | Tannins | Blue-black or greenish-black |
| Dilute HCl / acetic acid | Calcium oxalate (dissolves in HCl, not in acetic acid); calcium carbonate dissolves with effervescence | — |

**3. Physical evaluation** — numerical values that check purity:

| Value | What it shows |
| --- | --- |
| Moisture content (loss on drying) | Excess water favours moulds and enzyme action |
| Total ash | Inorganic matter left after burning |
| Acid-insoluble ash | Silica, mainly sand and earth (contamination) |
| Water-soluble ash | Water-soluble inorganic salts; very low in exhausted drugs |
| Sulphated ash | Ash after treatment with sulphuric acid, for some drugs |
| Extractive values (water-, alcohol-, ether-soluble) | Amount of constituents extracted by a solvent; low in exhausted drugs |
| Foreign organic matter | Other plant parts or other plants mixed with the drug |
| Swelling index | Swelling of mucilage drugs such as isapgol |
| Foaming index | Foaming power of saponin drugs |
| Viscosity, melting point, refractive index, optical rotation, specific gravity | Purity of oils, waxes, gums |
| Fluorescence under UV light | Some drugs and adulterants fluoresce typically |

**4. Chemical evaluation** — chemical tests for identification (colour reactions, precipitation tests) and assays for the amount of active constituent (e.g., total alkaloids, volatile oil content). For oils and fats: acid value, saponification value, iodine value, ester value, acetyl value. Chemical methods are the most reliable for potency where the active principle is known.

**5. Biological evaluation (bioassay)** — used when the active principle is not known chemically or chemical methods are not sensitive enough. The effect on a living animal, tissue or microorganism is compared with a standard. Examples: digitalis (pigeon method), antibiotics (microbiological assay by zone of inhibition), bitter drugs (bitterness value), vitamins (growth response). Bioassays measure actual activity but are slow, costly and more variable.

## 1.6 Quantitative microscopy

Quantitative microscopy uses numbers measured under the microscope to identify a drug and to detect adulteration, especially in powdered leaf drugs where the outer shape is lost.

### Leaf constants

| Constant | Definition | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stomatal number** | Average number of stomata per square millimetre of epidermis | Varies with leaf age and environment, so less reliable |
| **Stomatal index (SI)** | Percentage of stomata out of all epidermal cells: SI = S × 100 / (E + S), where S = stomata and E = epidermal cells in the same unit area | Fairly constant for a species; separates related species (e.g., Indian and Alexandrian senna) |
| **Vein-islet number** | Average number of vein-islets per square millimetre of leaf surface, counted midway between midrib and margin | Constant for a species, independent of leaf size |
| **Vein-termination number** | Average number of veinlet endings (terminations) per square millimetre | Same as above |
| **Palisade ratio** | Average number of palisade cells beneath one upper epidermal cell | Constant within a species; useful for powdered leaves |

A **vein-islet** is the smallest area of green tissue completely surrounded by veinlets. A **vein-termination** is the free ending of a veinlet.

### Lycopodium spore method

Used for powdered drugs where particles cannot be counted directly but which contain a characteristic, countable element (e.g., starch grains in ginger, oil cells, pollen grains). Lycopodium spores (*Lycopodium clavatum*) are used as an internal standard because they are very uniform in size and shape, and **1 mg contains about 94,000 spores**.

```text
Weigh drug (M mg) + lycopodium (W mg) → mix with a suspending liquid → mount on slide
Count, in several fields:  N = characteristic particles of drug
                           S = lycopodium spores (same fields)

Number of particles per mg of drug = (N × W × 94,000) / (S × M)

% purity = (particles per mg in sample / particles per mg in standard drug) × 100
```

It is used to find the purity of powdered ginger, and the amount of an adulterant (e.g., foreign starch).

### Camera lucida and drawing to scale

A **camera lucida** is a drawing attachment fixed over the eyepiece. By means of a prism and mirror it superimposes the microscopic field on a sheet of paper beside the microscope, so the observer sees the object and the pencil tip together and traces the outline exactly.

**Drawing to scale:**
1. Place a **stage micrometer** (1 mm divided into 100 parts, each 0.01 mm = 10 µm) on the stage and trace its divisions through the camera lucida onto paper.
2. Use these traced divisions to make a scale on the paper.
3. Replace with the specimen (e.g., cleared leaf) and trace the outline of veins, epidermal cells or stomata.
4. Using the scale, mark a square of 1 mm² (or 4 mm²) on the drawing and count the vein-islets, stomata or cells inside it.

An **eyepiece micrometer** (a glass disc with an arbitrary scale) is calibrated against the stage micrometer to measure sizes of starch grains, crystals and fibres in micrometres.

## Remember

- Pharmacognosy = *pharmakon* (drug) + *gnosis* (knowledge); term introduced by Seydler, 1815.
- Organised drugs have cells (leaves, barks, roots); unorganised drugs do not (latex, juice, extract, gum, resin, balsam, wax).
- Seven classifications: alphabetical, morphological, taxonomical, chemical, pharmacological, chemotaxonomic, serotaxonomic — learn one merit, one demerit and one example each.
- Adulteration types: inferior variety, exhausted drug, look-alike plant, artificial substitute, heavy matter, synthetic additive, other parts of the same plant.
- Evaluation methods: organoleptic, microscopic, physical, chemical, biological.
- Stomatal index SI = S × 100/(E + S); vein-islet number, vein-termination number, palisade ratio are leaf constants.
- Lycopodium: about 94,000 spores per mg; particles per mg = N × W × 94,000/(S × M).
- Stage micrometer: 1 division = 10 µm; camera lucida traces the field on paper.
