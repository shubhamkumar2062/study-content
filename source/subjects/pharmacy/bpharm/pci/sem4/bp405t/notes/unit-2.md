# Unit 2: Cultivation, collection, processing and storage of drugs of natural origin

The quality of a crude drug is decided long before it reaches a laboratory. Where the plant grew, how it was propagated, when and how it was harvested, how it was dried and how it was stored all change the amount of active constituent. This unit covers cultivation and collection, the factors that affect cultivation, plant hormones, genetic methods for improving medicinal plants (polyploidy, mutation, hybridisation) and the conservation of medicinal plants.

## 2.1 Cultivation versus collection from the wild

Medicinal plants are obtained either from wild sources or from cultivated fields.

| Point | Wild sources | Cultivated sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality | Variable; mixed ages, chemical races and look-alike species | Uniform; selected high-yielding varieties |
| Purity | Risk of adulteration with other plants | Pure crop of one species |
| Supply | Irregular, depends on season and accessibility | Steady, planned supply |
| Cost of labour | Low input but costly collection from remote areas | Higher input but mechanised harvest possible |
| Effect on nature | Over-collection endangers species | Protects wild populations |
| Improvement | Not possible | Breeding, fertilisers, pest control, timely harvest |

So cultivation gives better and more uniform drugs, and also helps conserve rare plants. Examples of cultivated medicinal plants in India: senna, isapgol, ginger, turmeric, cardamom, opium poppy (under licence), mint, lemongrass, ashwagandha.

## 2.2 Methods of propagation

**A. Sexual propagation (by seeds).** Simple and cheap, and gives large numbers of plants, but seedlings may differ from the parent. Some seeds need pre-treatment to break dormancy:
- soaking in water or chemicals,
- scarification (scratching or treating a hard seed coat with acid),
- stratification (keeping moist seeds at low temperature),
- treatment with growth regulators such as gibberellic acid.

Examples raised from seeds: senna, isapgol, fennel, coriander, datura, castor.

**B. Asexual (vegetative) propagation.** Uses a vegetative part of the parent. The new plants are genetically identical to the parent (a clone), so a good variety is preserved.

| Method | How | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cuttings | Pieces of stem, root or leaf are planted to root | Stem cuttings of rauwolfia, roses |
| Layering | A branch is made to root while still attached to the parent, then cut off | Simple layering and air layering (gootee) of fruit trees |
| Grafting | Shoot (scion) of one plant is joined onto the rooted stock of another | Mango, citrus |
| Budding | A single bud is inserted under the bark of the stock | Roses, citrus |
| Underground parts | Rhizomes, bulbs, corms, tubers, suckers, runners | Ginger and turmeric (rhizomes), garlic (bulb), mint (runners/stolons) |
| Micropropagation | Plantlets raised from small explants in tissue culture | Many medicinal and ornamental plants (see Unit 3) |

## 2.3 Factors influencing cultivation of medicinal plants

**1. Altitude.** Some plants grow well only at particular heights. Tea, cinchona and many temperate herbs need hills; others such as senna and castor do well on plains.

**2. Temperature.** Each species has an optimum range. Frost destroys tropical plants, and very high temperature reduces growth. Temperature also changes the amount of volatile oil and other constituents.

**3. Rainfall and irrigation.** Water supply affects growth and yield. Excess water causes root rot and leaches nutrients; drought stunts plants. Irrigation (flood, sprinkler, drip) supplies water where rainfall is poor; drip irrigation saves water.

**4. Light and day length (photoperiod).**
- Light intensity affects photosynthesis and the formation of constituents.
- Some plants need full sun, others shade (cardamom grows under forest shade).
- Day length controls flowering: short-day, long-day and day-neutral plants.

**5. Soil.** Soil supplies water, air and nutrients.
- **Texture** (sand, silt, clay, loam) decides drainage and aeration. Most medicinal plants prefer a well-drained loam.
- **pH** affects nutrient availability. Most crops prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil.
- **Organic matter** and soil microbes improve fertility.

**6. Fertilisers and manures.**
- Organic manures: farmyard manure, compost, green manure, vermicompost.
- Inorganic fertilisers supply N, P and K.
- Nitrogen generally increases leafy growth and can raise alkaloid content, because alkaloids contain nitrogen.
- Biofertilisers such as *Rhizobium*, *Azotobacter* and mycorrhizal fungi add or mobilise nutrients.

**7. Pests and pest control.** Pests include weeds, insects, fungi, bacteria, viruses, nematodes, rodents and birds.

| Method | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Mechanical | Hand-picking, traps, fencing, weeding |
| Agricultural (cultural) | Crop rotation, resistant varieties, proper spacing, clean seed |
| Biological | Natural enemies (predatory insects, *Bacillus thuringiensis*), neem products |
| Chemical | Insecticides, fungicides, herbicides, rodenticides (used carefully; residues must stay within limits) |

**8. Genetic factors.** The variety or chemical race planted decides the potential yield of active constituent. Improved varieties are produced by selection and breeding (2.6).

**9. Time and stage of harvest.** The active constituent varies with age of the plant, season and even time of day, so harvest is timed to the peak (2.4).

## 2.4 Collection and harvesting

**General rules for the time of collection**

| Part | Usual time |
| --- | --- |
| Leaves and herbs | When photosynthesis is most active, usually just before or during flowering |
| Flowers | Before they are fully open (clove is collected as unopened buds) |
| Fruits | When fully grown; some ripe, others just before ripening |
| Seeds | When fully mature |
| Barks | In spring, when cambium is active and the bark separates easily |
| Roots, rhizomes | After the aerial parts have died down (autumn), when reserves are stored |
| Latex, gums, resins | By incision at the right season, e.g. opium latex from unripe capsules |

**Methods of harvesting**
- **Hand-picking:** for leaves, flowers and fruits needing selection, such as tea leaves and clove buds.
- **Mechanical:** harvesters, diggers and combines for large-scale crops.
- Barks are peeled by cutting the stem in rings and longitudinal strips.
- Roots are dug and washed free of soil.

## 2.5 Processing and storage

**1. Garbling.** Removal of unwanted matter: soil, stones, other plant parts (e.g., stems from leaves), other plants and dead insects. It is the last step before packing.

**2. Drying.** Removes water so that enzymes stop acting and moulds and bacteria cannot grow. It also reduces weight for transport.

| Type | Details | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural (sun) drying | Spread in sun; cheap but slow and weather-dependent | Seeds, barks, many roots |
| Shade drying | Avoids loss of colour and of volatile oil | Aromatic leaves and flowers |
| Artificial drying | Tray dryers, tunnel dryers, hot-air ovens, vacuum dryers, spray dryers (for extracts and latex such as papain), freeze drying (for heat-sensitive material) | Digitalis leaves, papain |

Drying temperature is chosen to suit the drug. Drugs with volatile oil are dried at low temperature to avoid oil loss; leaves containing enzyme-sensitive glycosides such as digitalis are dried quickly so the enzymes do not break down the glycosides.

**3. Special processing (curing or fermentation).** Some drugs need controlled enzyme action to develop their flavour or constituents.
- **Vanilla pods** are cured (sweating and slow drying) so that glucovanillin is hydrolysed to vanillin.
- **Black tea** leaves are fermented (oxidised).
- **Cocoa beans** are fermented.
- Gentian root is slowly dried to develop its colour and flavour.

**4. Size reduction and packing.** Drugs are cut, crushed or powdered as needed and packed in bags, boxes, bales or airtight containers that protect them from moisture, light and pests.

**5. Storage.** Crude drugs deteriorate through:
- moisture (moulds, enzyme action),
- heat (loss of volatile oil, softening),
- light (loss of colour, oxidation),
- air (oxidation and rancidity of oils),
- insects, mites and rodents.

Good storage means:
- a cool, dry, well-ventilated store with protection from light,
- airtight containers for hygroscopic drugs (squill, aloe) and for aromatic drugs,
- drying agents or moisture-proof packs where needed,
- inspecting stocks regularly and using the oldest stock first,
- controlling insects by approved fumigation, and keeping the store clean.

## 2.6 Plant hormones and their applications

**Plant hormones (phytohormones)** are organic substances produced in small amounts in one part of a plant that move to another part and regulate growth and development. Synthetic compounds with similar effects are called plant growth regulators.

| Hormone | Examples | Main effects | Applications |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Auxins** | Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, natural); indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), 2,4-D (synthetic) | Cell elongation, apical dominance, root initiation, phototropism | Rooting of cuttings (IBA, NAA); fruit set without seeds (parthenocarpy); 2,4-D as a weedkiller for broad-leaved weeds; callus formation in tissue culture |
| **Gibberellins** | Gibberellic acid (GA3) | Stem elongation, seed germination, breaking dormancy, bolting, flowering in some plants | Better germination; larger fruits (grapes); increased growth and yield of some crops |
| **Cytokinins** | Kinetin, zeatin (natural), benzylaminopurine (BAP) | Cell division, shoot formation, delay of ageing (senescence) of leaves | Shoot regeneration in tissue culture; keeping cut leaves and flowers fresh |
| **Abscisic acid (ABA)** | Abscisic acid | Growth inhibitor; seed and bud dormancy; stomatal closure under water stress | Inducing dormancy for storage; improving stress (drought) tolerance |
| **Ethylene** | Ethylene gas; ethephon releases it | Fruit ripening, leaf and fruit fall, breaking dormancy | Uniform ripening of banana, mango; promoting latex flow in rubber trees |

Other regulators include brassinosteroids (growth and stress tolerance) and jasmonates (defence signals; methyl jasmonate is used as an elicitor to increase secondary metabolites in plant cell cultures).

**Auxin–cytokinin balance (tissue culture):**

```text
High auxin : low cytokinin      → roots form
Balanced auxin and cytokinin     → callus (undifferentiated mass) grows
Low auxin : high cytokinin      → shoots form
```

## 2.7 Polyploidy, mutation and hybridisation

Genetic improvement aims at plants with more active constituent, better growth, disease resistance and uniformity.

### Polyploidy

**Polyploidy** is the condition in which a cell has more than two complete sets of chromosomes, e.g., triploid (3n) or tetraploid (4n), instead of the normal diploid (2n).
- **Autopolyploidy** — chromosome sets from the same species.
- **Allopolyploidy** — sets from two different species (after hybridisation).

**Induction:** chiefly by **colchicine**, an alkaloid from *Colchicum autumnale*. Colchicine stops spindle formation during cell division, so the chromosomes double but the cell does not divide. Seeds, seedlings or growing buds are treated with dilute colchicine solution. Other agents include heat or cold shocks and some other chemicals.

**Effects:**
- Polyploid plants are usually larger, with bigger cells, leaves, flowers and fruits (gigas effect).
- They often contain more secondary metabolites. Tetraploid forms of *Datura* and other tropane-containing plants have been reported with higher alkaloid content.
- Triploids are usually seedless (seedless watermelon, banana).

### Mutation

**Mutation** is a sudden, heritable change in the genetic material (a gene or a chromosome).
- **Spontaneous mutations** occur naturally but rarely.
- **Induced mutations** are produced by mutagens.

| Mutagen type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Physical | X-rays, gamma rays, ultraviolet light, neutrons |
| Chemical | Ethyl methane sulphonate (EMS), nitrous acid, sodium azide, nitroso compounds |

Useful mutants are selected and multiplied. Examples in medicinal plants include poppy lines with changed alkaloid profile (rich in thebaine with little morphine), and mutants of peppermint resistant to wilt disease. Most mutations are harmful or neutral, so large numbers must be screened.

### Hybridisation

**Hybridisation** is the crossing of two genetically different plants so that useful characters of both are combined in the offspring (hybrid). Hybrids often show **hybrid vigour (heterosis)** — better growth and yield than either parent.

**Steps:**
1. Select parents with the desired characters.
2. Emasculate the female parent (remove the anthers before they shed pollen).
3. Bag the flower to prevent unwanted pollination.
4. Pollinate with pollen from the chosen male parent and bag again.
5. Collect the hybrid seed; grow and select the best plants over several generations.

Types: intraspecific (within a species), interspecific (between species of a genus) and intergeneric. Examples: peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*) is itself a natural hybrid of water mint and spearmint; hybrids of cinchona species were raised to combine high quinine content with hardiness.

## 2.8 Conservation of medicinal plants

**Why conservation is needed.** Many medicinal plants are collected from the wild faster than they can regrow. Other threats are:
- loss of forests and habitats,
- grazing and fires,
- climate change,
- destructive harvesting (uprooting whole plants for roots or bark).

Threatened Indian examples include:
- *Nardostachys jatamansi* (jatamansi),
- *Saussurea costus* (kuth),
- *Taxus wallichiana* (Himalayan yew),
- *Podophyllum hexandrum* (Indian podophyllum),
- *Rauvolfia serpentina* (sarpagandha).

**In-situ conservation** (in the natural habitat):
- national parks and wildlife sanctuaries,
- biosphere reserves,
- sacred groves,
- medicinal plant conservation areas.

**Ex-situ conservation** (outside the natural habitat):
- botanical gardens and herbal gardens,
- seed banks and field gene banks,
- tissue culture banks and slow-growth storage,
- cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C),
- DNA banks.

**Other measures:**
- cultivation of threatened species,
- sustainable harvesting rules (collect only part of a population; leave roots),
- benefit-sharing with local communities.

In India this work is supported by the National Medicinal Plants Board and the Biological Diversity Act, 2002. International trade in endangered species is controlled by CITES.

## Remember

- Cultivation gives uniform, pure, high-yielding drugs and protects wild plants.
- Propagation: seeds (sexual) or cuttings, layering, grafting, budding, rhizomes, bulbs, runners, micropropagation (asexual, true to type).
- Factors: altitude, temperature, rainfall, light, soil, fertilisers, pests, genetics, harvest time.
- Leaves at flowering, barks in spring, roots in autumn, flowers before opening.
- Garbling = removal of extraneous matter; drying stops enzymes and microbes; curing develops vanillin in vanilla.
- Auxins root cuttings; gibberellins elongate stems; cytokinins divide cells and form shoots; ABA causes dormancy; ethylene ripens fruit.
- Colchicine doubles chromosomes (polyploidy); X-rays, gamma rays and EMS cause mutations; hybridisation gives heterosis.
- In-situ (parks, reserves) and ex-situ (gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation) conservation.
