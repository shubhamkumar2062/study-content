# % NaCl in a solution by the critical solution temperature of phenol–water

**Aim:** To determine the critical solution temperature (CST) of the phenol–water system and to find the percentage of sodium chloride in an unknown solution from the rise in CST.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Phenol is corrosive and burns skin: weigh it with gloves, avoid contact, and wash any spill immediately with plenty of water.

## Principle

Phenol and water are partially miscible at room temperature. On heating, mutual solubility increases until, above the **upper CST (about 66.8 °C)**, they mix in all proportions. The temperature at which a turbid mixture of a given composition just becomes clear (or turns turbid on cooling) is its **miscibility temperature**. Plotting miscibility temperature against % phenol gives a dome-shaped curve whose maximum is the CST.

A solute soluble in only one component (NaCl in water) **raises the CST**, and the rise is proportional to its concentration. Measuring the CST with standard NaCl solutions in place of water gives a calibration line; the CST obtained with the unknown solution then gives its NaCl content.

## Requirements

Phenol, distilled water, sodium chloride, unknown NaCl solution, boiling tubes, thermometer (0–100 °C), stirrer, water bath (large beaker on a burner or hot plate), burette, balance.

## Procedure

**Part A — CST of phenol–water**

1. Weigh 5 g of phenol into a boiling tube.
2. Add 2 ml water from a burette. Place the tube in a water bath with a thermometer and stirrer.
3. Heat slowly with stirring until the turbid mixture just becomes clear (T1). Remove from the bath and let it cool with stirring; note the temperature at which turbidity reappears (T2). Take the mean as the miscibility temperature.
4. Add more water in steps (for example to give 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 ml total), repeating step 3 each time.
5. Calculate % phenol (w/w) for each mixture (water density taken as 1 g/ml) and plot miscibility temperature against % phenol. The maximum is the CST.

**Part B — calibration with NaCl and unknown**

6. Prepare 0.5%, 1.0%, 1.5% and 2.0% w/v NaCl solutions.
7. For each solution, mix 5 g phenol with the volume of NaCl solution that gave the CST composition in Part A (near the top of the curve) and find its miscibility temperature.
8. Plot miscibility temperature against % NaCl (a straight line).
9. Repeat with the unknown solution and read its % NaCl from the line.

**Worked example.** The calibration line rises from 67 °C (water) by about 4 °C for each 1% NaCl. If the unknown gives 71.8 °C, % NaCl = (71.8 − 67)/4 = **1.2% w/v** (use your own calibration line, not these figures).

## Precautions

1. Heat and cool slowly with constant stirring.
2. Read the temperature at the first appearance or disappearance of turbidity.
3. Keep the thermometer bulb in the liquid, not touching the glass.
4. Avoid loss of water by evaporation; use a cork loosely fitted.

## Viva questions

1. **Define CST.** The temperature above (or below) which two partially miscible liquids become miscible in all proportions.
2. **Give an example of a lower CST.** Triethylamine–water.
3. **Why does NaCl raise the CST?** It dissolves only in water, reducing the mutual solubility of phenol and water.
4. **What lowers the CST?** A substance soluble in both liquids, such as succinic acid or soap.
5. **What are conjugate solutions?** The two layers of partially miscible liquids in equilibrium at a given temperature.
6. **Give an application of CST.** Determining purity of phenol and the concentration of salts; formulating cresol-with-soap disinfectants.
