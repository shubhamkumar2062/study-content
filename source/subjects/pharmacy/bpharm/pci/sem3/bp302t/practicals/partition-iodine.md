# Partition coefficient of iodine between carbon tetrachloride and water

**Aim:** To determine the partition coefficient of iodine between carbon tetrachloride (CCl4) and water at room temperature.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. CCl4 is toxic: handle it in a fume hood with gloves and goggles and collect waste separately.

## Principle

Iodine is non-polar and is far more soluble in CCl4 than in water, and it exists as I2 molecules in both. By the distribution law:

**K = C(CCl4) / C(water)** = constant at constant temperature.

The iodine in each layer is titrated with standard sodium thiosulfate using starch as indicator:

I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6

## Requirements

Saturated solution of iodine in CCl4, distilled water, 0.1 N and 0.01 N sodium thiosulfate (standardised), starch solution, potassium iodide, stoppered bottles, pipettes, burettes, conical flasks.

## Procedure

1. Take three stoppered bottles. Add 20, 15 and 10 ml of the iodine–CCl4 solution, making each up to 20 ml with plain CCl4 where needed.
2. Add 100 ml of distilled water to each bottle.
3. Shake for about 30 minutes and allow the layers to separate (CCl4 is the lower layer).
4. Pipette 5 ml of the lower CCl4 layer into a flask containing about 10 ml of 10% KI solution (KI helps transfer iodine to the aqueous phase during titration); titrate with 0.1 N thiosulfate, adding starch near the end, until the blue colour disappears.
5. Pipette 50 ml of the upper aqueous layer, add a little KI and titrate with 0.01 N thiosulfate using starch.
6. Repeat for concordant readings.

**Worked example.** CCl4 layer: 5 ml needs 4.25 ml of 0.1 N → 0.085 N. Water layer: 50 ml needs 5.0 ml of 0.01 N → 0.001 N. K = 0.085/0.001 = **85**.

## Precautions

1. Shake thoroughly and keep the temperature constant.
2. Withdraw the lower layer without contamination from the aqueous layer (blow gently while passing the pipette through).
3. Add starch only near the end point.
4. Keep bottles stoppered to avoid loss of iodine.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is K for iodine much greater than 1?** Iodine is non-polar and dissolves far better in non-polar CCl4.
2. **Why is K constant here, unlike benzoic acid in benzene?** Iodine exists as the same I2 molecule in both phases.
3. **Why add KI before titration?** It dissolves iodine as triiodide, helping the transfer from CCl4 during titration.
4. **What happens if KI is present in the water layer during distribution?** The I3− complex forms and apparent water solubility rises; this is used to find the stability constant of I3−.
5. **Why add starch near the end point?** Starch–iodine complex at high iodine concentration may not release iodine easily, giving an error.
6. **Name a limitation of the distribution law.** It applies only to dilute solutions at constant temperature with the same molecular species in both phases.
