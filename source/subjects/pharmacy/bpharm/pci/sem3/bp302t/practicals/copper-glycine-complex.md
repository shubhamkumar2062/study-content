# Stability constant of the copper–glycine complex by pH titration

**Aim:** To determine the stability constants and the metal–ligand (acceptor–donor) ratio of the copper(II)–glycine complex by the pH titration method.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles and a lab coat; copper salts are harmful if swallowed.

## Principle

Glycine (H2N–CH2–COOH) binds Cu²⁺ through its amino nitrogen and carboxylate oxygen, forming five-membered chelate rings. Each glycine that binds releases a proton:

Cu²⁺ + HGly ⇌ Cu(Gly)⁺ + H⁺  Cu(Gly)⁺ + HGly ⇌ Cu(Gly)2 + H⁺

Glycine is titrated with NaOH twice — alone and in the presence of Cu²⁺. Because copper frees protons, the titration curve with copper lies **at lower pH** than the curve without it. From the difference in NaOH at each pH, the **average number of glycine molecules bound per copper ion (n̄)** is calculated and plotted against **pA** (−log of free glycinate concentration). This **formation curve** gives:

- log K1 = pA at n̄ = 0.5
- log K2 = pA at n̄ = 1.5
- Overall stability constant β = K1 × K2

n̄ rises to a limit of **2**, showing the complex Cu(Gly)2 (one copper : two glycine).

## Requirements

Glycine, copper sulfate (CuSO4·5H2O), 0.1 N NaOH (carbonate-free, standardised), pH meter with buffers, beakers, magnetic stirrer, burette, pipettes, volumetric flasks.

## Procedure

1. Calibrate the pH meter.
2. **Titration A (ligand alone):** pipette 25 ml of 0.01 M glycine (with a little HCl if your manual directs, to start from the fully protonated form) into a beaker. Titrate with 0.1 N NaOH in 0.2–0.5 ml portions, recording the pH after each addition, up to about pH 11.
3. **Titration B (ligand + metal):** pipette 25 ml of the same glycine solution and add 25 ml of 0.0025 M CuSO4 (or the amount that keeps glycine in excess). Make up both titrations to the same total volume with water. Titrate with 0.1 N NaOH in the same way.
4. Plot pH against volume of NaOH for both titrations on the same graph.

**Steps of calculation (at chosen pH values)**

1. From the two curves, read the volume of NaOH needed to reach the same pH (VA without copper, VB with copper). The difference (VB − VA) × N = moles of protons released by complexation = moles of glycine bound.
2. n̄ = moles of glycine bound / total moles of copper.
3. Free glycinate concentration [A] is calculated from the total glycine not bound and the pH, using the pKa of the –NH3+ group of glycine: [A] = (glycine not bound) × Ka / (Ka + [H+]) (approximately).
4. pA = −log [A]. Plot n̄ against pA.

```text

   |               /
1.5|.............../  <- pA here = log K2
 1 |            /
0.5|......... /    <- pA here = log K1
   |________/____________________
            pA (decreasing to the right as ligand increases)
```

**Worked example (reading the curve).** If n̄ = 0.5 at pA = 8.1 and n̄ = 1.5 at pA = 6.9, then log K1 = 8.1, log K2 = 6.9, log β = 15.0. n̄ levels off at 2, so the ratio Cu : glycine = 1 : 2. (Use your own data; these figures only show the method.)

## Precautions

1. Use carbonate-free NaOH and calibrate the pH meter.
2. Keep the same total volume and temperature in both titrations.
3. Stir gently and allow the pH to settle after each addition.
4. Do not let copper hydroxide precipitate (avoid excess copper; stop before strongly alkaline pH if a precipitate forms).

## Viva questions

1. **Why does the curve with copper lie at lower pH?** Complex formation releases protons from glycine.
2. **What is n̄?** The average number of ligand molecules bound per metal ion.
3. **What is a chelate?** A ring complex formed when one ligand binds a metal through two or more donor atoms.
4. **What is the coordination of Cu in Cu(Gly)2?** Two glycines, each through N and O, giving four donor atoms (square planar arrangement).
5. **Name another method for stoichiometry.** Job's method of continuous variation.
6. **Relation between stepwise and overall constants?** β2 = K1 × K2.
