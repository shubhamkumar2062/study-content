# Critical micelle concentration of a surfactant

**Aim:** To determine the critical micelle concentration (CMC) of a surfactant (for example sodium lauryl sulfate) by the surface tension (drop count) method.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles and a lab coat.

## Principle

As surfactant is added to water, its molecules first collect at the air–water surface and the surface tension falls steadily. When the surface is saturated, further molecules form **micelles** in the bulk. The concentration at which micelles first form is the **CMC**. Above it, the surface tension stays almost constant because the concentration of free (monomer) surfactant hardly increases.

A plot of surface tension against **log concentration** shows a falling line followed by an almost flat line; the point where they meet is the CMC.

Surface tension is measured with a stalagmometer (drop count method), using water as reference: γ = γw × (nw × ρ) / (n × ρw). For dilute surfactant solutions the density can be taken as that of water, so γ ≈ γw × nw / n.

The CMC can also be found from the change in slope of a conductivity–concentration plot (ionic surfactants) or from the sudden increase in solubility of a dye.

## Requirements

Sodium lauryl sulfate, distilled water, volumetric flasks, stalagmometer, beakers, pipettes, thermometer (or a conductivity meter for the alternative method).

## Procedure

1. Prepare a stock solution of SLS (for example 0.05 M, 1.44 g in 100 ml; molecular weight about 288).
2. By dilution prepare a series, for example 0.001, 0.002, 0.004, 0.006, 0.008, 0.010, 0.015 and 0.020 M.
3. Count the drops of water for the stalagmometer volume (nw), three times.
4. Starting from the most dilute solution, count the drops for each solution three times (rinse the stalagmometer with the next solution before use).
5. Calculate surface tension for each and plot γ against log c.

Draw the best straight line through the falling points and through the flat points; read log c at the intersection; CMC = antilog of this value.

**Worked example.** The two lines meet at log c = −2.09. CMC = 10^(−2.09) = **8.1 × 10⁻³ M** (sodium lauryl sulfate in water at room temperature is commonly reported near 8 × 10⁻³ M).

## Precautions

1. Go from dilute to concentrated solutions to reduce carry-over.
2. Keep the temperature constant; CMC depends on temperature.
3. Avoid foaming when filling the stalagmometer.
4. Use freshly prepared solutions.

## Viva questions

1. **Define CMC.** The concentration of surfactant above which micelles form.
2. **Why does surface tension stay constant above CMC?** Extra surfactant forms micelles; the monomer concentration at the surface hardly changes.
3. **Name other properties that change at CMC.** Conductivity, osmotic pressure, solubilising power, turbidity (light scattering).
4. **How do electrolytes affect the CMC of ionic surfactants?** They lower it by reducing repulsion between heads.
5. **What is the Krafft point?** Temperature above which an ionic surfactant's solubility rises sharply and micelles form.
6. **Why is CMC important in pharmacy?** Solubilisation of poorly soluble drugs starts only above the CMC.
