# Unit 4: Complexation and protein binding

Many drugs do not stay as free single molecules in a formulation or in the body. They join with metal ions, with other small molecules, with cyclodextrins or with plasma proteins. This joining (complexation) can raise or lower solubility, protect a drug from breakdown, slow its absorption, or hold it in the blood for longer. Questions on the classification of complexes, methods of analysis and protein binding are asked almost every year.

## 4.1 Introduction

A **complex** (coordination compound) is a species formed when two or more molecules or ions associate. One part donates electrons (the **donor or ligand**); the other accepts them (the **acceptor**, often a metal ion or an electron-poor organic molecule).

The forces holding a complex together can be:

- **Coordinate covalent bonds** — a ligand donates a lone pair to a metal ion (metal complexes).
- **Weaker forces** — hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, dipole forces, charge-transfer (donor–acceptor) interactions, hydrophobic interactions (organic molecular complexes, inclusion compounds).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Ligand | Molecule or ion that donates electrons to the central atom |
| Coordination number | Number of donor atoms bound to the central metal ion |
| Monodentate ligand | One donor atom (NH3, H2O, Cl−) |
| Multidentate (chelating) ligand | Two or more donor atoms (glycine 2, EDTA 6) |
| Chelate | Ring structure formed when one ligand binds the metal through two or more atoms |
| Stability constant (K) | Equilibrium constant for complex formation; larger K means a more stable complex |

## 4.2 Classification of complexes

Complexes are divided into three main classes according to the type of bonding and the species involved.

```text
COMPLEXES
├── I. Metal ion complexes
│      a. Inorganic type
│      b. Chelates
│      c. Olefin type
│      d. Aromatic type (π, σ and "sandwich" complexes)
├── II. Organic molecular complexes
│      a. Quinhydrone type
│      b. Picric acid type
│      c. Caffeine and other drug complexes
│      d. Polymer type
└── III. Inclusion / occlusion compounds
       a. Channel lattice type
       b. Layer type
       c. Clathrates (cage type)
       d. Monomolecular inclusion compounds (cyclodextrins)
       e. Macromolecular inclusion compounds (molecular sieves)
```

### I. Metal ion complexes

**a. Inorganic type.** A metal ion (Lewis acid) accepts lone pairs from simple ligands (Lewis bases). Example: copper sulfate with ammonia gives the deep blue tetrammine copper(II) ion, [Cu(NH3)4]²⁺; [Ag(NH3)2]⁺; potassium ferrocyanide K4[Fe(CN)6].

**b. Chelates.** A ligand with two or more donor groups binds one metal ion, forming a stable ring (usually 5- or 6-membered). Chelates are more stable than complexes of similar monodentate ligands (the **chelate effect**, mainly because of a gain in entropy).

| Chelating agent | Metal | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EDTA (6 donor atoms) | Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, heavy metals | Complexometric titrations, water softening, preservative aid (removes metal catalysts of oxidation), calcium disodium EDTA for lead poisoning |
| Dimercaprol (BAL) | Arsenic, mercury, lead | Antidote |
| Penicillamine | Copper | Wilson's disease |
| Deferoxamine | Iron | Iron overload |
| Glycine | Cu²⁺ | Copper glycinate (practical 11) |
| 8-Hydroxyquinoline (oxine) | Many metals | Analysis; antibacterial action linked to chelation |

Chlorophyll (Mg) and haemoglobin (Fe) are natural chelates.

**c. Olefin type.** Alkenes donate π electrons to metals (e.g. platinum and silver complexes with ethylene).

**d. Aromatic type.** Aromatic rings form π complexes and σ complexes with metal ions; "sandwich" compounds such as ferrocene have a metal held between two aromatic rings.

### II. Organic molecular complexes

Formed between organic molecules held by weak forces such as hydrogen bonding, dipole forces and **charge transfer** (an electron-rich donor gives partial electron density to an electron-poor acceptor).

**a. Quinhydrone type.** Equimolar quinone and hydroquinone form a dark green crystalline complex (used in the quinhydrone electrode).

**b. Picric acid type.** Picric acid (an electron-poor acceptor) forms coloured complexes (picrates) with aromatic hydrocarbons and bases; used to identify alkaloids and amines (e.g. butesin picrate is a local anaesthetic).

**c. Caffeine and other drug complexes.** Caffeine forms complexes with many drugs, mainly through dipole forces and hydrogen bonding between its polarised carbonyl groups and acidic hydrogens of the other molecule.

- Caffeine with **sodium benzoate** or sodium salicylate — increases the water solubility of caffeine (caffeine and sodium benzoate injection).
- Caffeine with **benzocaine** — the complex slows the hydrolysis of benzocaine.
- Caffeine with **PABA** — studied in practical 10.
- Theophylline with ethylenediamine (aminophylline) — more water-soluble form of theophylline.

**d. Polymer type.** Polymers with electron-donating groups bind drugs:

- Polyethylene glycol, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, povidone) and carboxymethylcellulose complex with phenols, tannins and other drugs.
- **Povidone-iodine** — iodine held by PVP; releases iodine slowly, less irritant than iodine solution.
- Complexes with plastic containers and closures can remove preservatives or drug from solution.

### III. Inclusion (occlusion) compounds

One molecule (the **guest**) is trapped inside a cavity or channel of another (the **host**) without chemical bonding. The fit depends on size and shape.

| Type | Host | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Channel lattice | Molecules crystallise leaving long channels | Deoxycholic acid holding fatty acids (choleic acids); urea and thiourea channels holding straight-chain hydrocarbons |
| Layer type | Layered crystals that take guests between layers | Montmorillonite (bentonite) clay, graphite |
| Clathrates (cage) | Crystal lattice with closed cages | Hydroquinone trapping gases such as methanol, SO2, argon; gas hydrates of water |
| Monomolecular | A single host molecule with a cavity | **Cyclodextrins** |
| Macromolecular | Large porous structures | Molecular sieves (zeolites), dextrins, silica gels |

**Cyclodextrins** are cyclic oligosaccharides of glucose units: **α (6), β (7) and γ (8) units**. They are shaped like a hollow truncated cone with a **hydrophobic cavity** and a **hydrophilic outer surface**.

```text
        wide rim  ( -OH groups, water-loving outside )
       \_______________/
        \  [ drug ]   /    <- non-polar drug (or its non-polar part) sits in the cavity
         \___________/
        narrow rim
```

Uses: raise solubility and dissolution of poorly soluble drugs, protect drugs from light, oxidation and hydrolysis, reduce irritation and bitter taste, convert liquids or volatile substances into stable powders.

## 4.3 Applications of complexation

1. **Solubility** — caffeine–sodium benzoate, cyclodextrin complexes, iodine dissolved in potassium iodide solution as the soluble triiodide complex.
2. **Stability** — benzocaine–caffeine, cyclodextrin protection; EDTA binds traces of metal ions that catalyse oxidation.
3. **Absorption** — complexes can raise or lower it: tetracyclines form insoluble chelates with Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ and Fe²⁺, so they should not be taken with milk, antacids or iron tablets.
4. **Antidotes and chelation therapy** — EDTA, dimercaprol, penicillamine, deferoxamine.
5. **Analysis** — complexometric (EDTA) titrations, colorimetric tests (e.g. Fe³⁺ with thiocyanate), metal indicators.
6. **Taste masking and sustained release** — drug–resin and polymer complexes.
7. **Antiseptics with slow release** — povidone-iodine.

## 4.4 Methods of analysis of complexes

The aim is to find the **stoichiometry** (ratio of ligand to substrate) and the **stability constant**.

### a. Method of continuous variation (Job's method)

A property that is additive and changes on complexation (for example absorbance, dielectric constant, refractive index) is measured for a series of mixtures in which the **total moles of the two components are constant** but their mole fraction varies. The difference between the measured value and the value expected without complexation is plotted against mole fraction.

```text
 Δ property
   |          /\
   |        /    \          The peak is at the mole fraction of the complex.
   |      /        \        Peak at 0.5      -> 1:1 complex
   |    /            \      Peak at 0.33 metal (0.67 ligand) -> 1:2 complex (ML2)
   |__/________________\___
     0      0.5        1.0   mole fraction of metal
```

### b. pH titration method (Bjerrum method)

Used when complex formation releases or uses H+ — for example **copper with glycine**. Glycine is titrated with alkali alone and then in the presence of Cu²⁺. Because Cu²⁺ binds glycinate and frees protons, the curve with copper lies **below** (lower pH than) the curve without copper. From the extra alkali needed, the **average number of ligands bound per metal ion (n̄)** is calculated at each point and plotted against pA (−log of free ligand concentration) to give a **formation curve**.

- log K1 = pA at n̄ = 0.5
- log K2 = pA at n̄ = 1.5
- Overall stability constant β = K1 × K2 (for ML2)

### c. Distribution method

The distribution of a substance (e.g. iodine) between water and an organic solvent is measured with and without a complexing agent (e.g. KI in the water). The complexing agent increases the apparent concentration in water. From the change in the distribution ratio, the stability constant is calculated (e.g. I2 + I⁻ ⇌ I3⁻).

### d. Solubility method (Higuchi and Connors)

Excess of the poorly soluble drug (substrate) is shaken with solutions containing increasing concentrations of the ligand until equilibrium; the total drug dissolved is assayed and plotted against ligand concentration (a **phase solubility diagram**).

```text
 Total drug
 dissolved     A_P (curves up)
   |          /
   |        /  A_L (straight line: soluble 1:1 complex)
   |      / /
   |    / /--- A_N (curves down)
   |  /_/
   | S0 ______   B_S: rises, plateau, then falls (complex of limited solubility)
   |__________________ ligand concentration
```

- **Type A** — complex is soluble (AL linear, AP positive deviation, AN negative deviation).
- **Type B** — complex has limited solubility (BS: rises then plateaus then falls; BI: insoluble complex).

For a linear AL diagram (1:1 complex):

K(1:1) = slope / [S0 (1 − slope)]

S0 = solubility of the drug with no ligand (intercept).

**Worked example.** S0 = 0.002 M, slope = 0.40. K = 0.40 / [0.002 × (1 − 0.40)] = 0.40 / 0.0012 = **333 M⁻¹**.

This method is used in practical 10 (PABA–caffeine).

### e. Spectroscopy and other methods

- **UV–visible spectroscopy:** a charge-transfer complex often has a new absorption band; the Benesi–Hildebrand equation gives K from absorbance at several ligand concentrations.
- **NMR, IR** — shifts in signals of groups involved in binding.
- **Polarography, conductance, potentiometry with ion-selective electrodes, calorimetry**.

## 4.5 Protein binding

Many drugs bind reversibly to plasma and tissue proteins:

- **Albumin** — the main binding protein; binds mostly acidic and neutral drugs (warfarin, phenytoin, salicylates, sulfonamides, NSAIDs) and also bilirubin.
- **α1-acid glycoprotein** — binds basic drugs (propranolol, lidocaine, imipramine).
- **Lipoproteins** — lipophilic drugs; **globulins** — some hormones (thyroxine, cortisol).

```text
  Drug (free) + Protein  ⇌  Drug–Protein complex
      |                         (inactive, too big to leave blood,
      v                          not filtered or metabolised; acts as a store)
  crosses membranes, acts on receptors,
  is metabolised and excreted
```

**Only the free (unbound) drug** is active, diffuses into tissues, is filtered by the kidney and is metabolised. The bound drug is a **reservoir** that releases free drug as it is removed.

### Consequences of protein binding

| Effect | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Longer duration of action | Bound drug is released slowly (e.g. long-acting sulfonamides) |
| Smaller volume of distribution | Highly bound drugs stay in plasma |
| Delayed elimination | Only free drug is filtered by glomeruli |
| Drug interactions (displacement) | A second drug competing for the same site raises the free level of the first: phenylbutazone or aspirin displacing warfarin → bleeding |
| Toxicity in newborns | Sulfonamides displace bilirubin from albumin → kernicterus |
| Effect of disease | Low albumin (liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition) raises the free fraction |
| Saturation | At high drug levels binding sites fill, so the free fraction jumps |

### Quantitative treatment of protein binding

Let r = moles of drug bound per mole of protein, ν = number of binding sites per protein molecule, K = association constant, [Df] = free drug concentration.

r = ν K [Df] / (1 + K [Df])

**Klotz (double reciprocal) plot:** 1/r = 1/(ν K [Df]) + 1/ν — plot 1/r against 1/[Df]; intercept = 1/ν, slope = 1/(νK).

**Scatchard plot:** r/[Df] = ν K − r K — plot r/[Df] against r; slope = −K, x-intercept = ν. A curved Scatchard plot shows more than one class of binding site.

**Worked example (Klotz).** A plot of 1/r against 1/[Df] gives intercept 0.5 and slope 2 × 10⁻⁵ M.
ν = 1/0.5 = **2 sites**; K = 1/(ν × slope) = 1/(2 × 2 × 10⁻⁵) = **2.5 × 10⁴ M⁻¹**.

**Worked example (percent bound).** Total plasma drug 50 µg/ml, free drug 5 µg/ml. Bound = 45 µg/ml → **90% bound**, fraction unbound fu = 0.1.

### Methods of studying protein binding

1. **Equilibrium dialysis** — protein solution and buffer separated by a semipermeable membrane; only free drug crosses; at equilibrium free drug is equal on both sides (most widely used reference method).
2. **Ultrafiltration** — pressure or centrifugation forces water and free drug through a membrane that holds back protein; the filtrate gives free drug. Fast.
3. **Gel filtration (molecular sieve, e.g. Sephadex)** — large protein–drug complexes elute first, free drug later.
4. **Dynamic dialysis** — the rate of loss of drug from a dialysis bag is followed with and without protein.
5. **Spectroscopy and fluorescence** — binding changes absorbance or fluorescence; electrophoresis and ultracentrifugation also used.

### Factors affecting protein binding

- **Drug:** physicochemical properties (lipophilicity, ionisation), concentration, affinity.
- **Protein:** amount (age, disease, pregnancy), number of sites.
- **Interactions:** competing drugs, endogenous substances (fatty acids, bilirubin).
- **Patient:** age (newborns and elderly have lower albumin), disease (uraemia, liver disease).

## 4.6 Complexation and drug action

- **Absorption** can be reduced (tetracycline–calcium) or improved (cyclodextrin complexes of poorly soluble drugs).
- **Distribution and duration** are controlled by protein binding.
- **Activity** may depend on chelation (oxine and some antibacterials act by binding essential metal ions); some metal complexes are drugs themselves (cisplatin as an anticancer agent).
- **Toxicity** is reduced by chelating agents that remove toxic metals.
- **Drug–receptor binding** follows the same equilibrium principles (association constant, number of sites) as other complexes.

## 4.7 Crystalline structures of complexes

The arrangement of ligands around a metal ion depends mainly on its **coordination number**:

| Coordination number | Geometry | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Linear | [Ag(NH3)2]⁺ |
| 4 | Tetrahedral | [Zn(NH3)4]²⁺ |
| 4 | Square planar | [Cu(NH3)4]²⁺, [Ni(CN)4]²⁻, cisplatin |
| 6 | Octahedral | [Co(NH3)6]³⁺, [Fe(CN)6]⁴⁻ |

```text
  Square planar          Octahedral
       L                     L
       |                     |
  L -- M -- L           L -- M -- L    (+ one ligand above, one below the plane)
       |                     |
       L                     L
```

Square-planar and octahedral complexes can show **cis–trans isomerism** (cis-platin is the active anticancer isomer). Octahedral complexes with chelating ligands can show **optical isomerism**.

Organic molecular complexes often crystallise with donor and acceptor molecules stacked alternately face to face (charge-transfer stacks), as in quinhydrone. Inclusion compounds owe their structure to the host lattice — channels, layers or cages — as described in Section 4.2.

## 4.8 Thermodynamic treatment of stability constants

For M + L ⇌ ML: **K = [ML] / ([M][L])**

For stepwise formation of ML2: K1 = [ML]/([M][L]); K2 = [ML2]/([ML][L]); **overall β2 = K1 × K2**.

**Free energy of complexation**

ΔG° = − 2.303 RT log K

A large K means a large negative ΔG° — the complex forms readily.

**Worked example.** K = 1000 M⁻¹ at 25 °C (298 K):
ΔG° = − 2.303 × 1.987 × 298 × 3 = **− 4091 cal/mol ≈ − 4.1 kcal/mol**.

**Enthalpy and entropy:** ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. ΔH° is found from the change of K with temperature (van't Hoff equation):

log (K2/K1) = ΔH° (T2 − T1) / (2.303 R T1 T2)

- A negative ΔH° (heat released) shows bond formation; such complexes become less stable as temperature rises.
- A positive ΔS° often explains the **chelate effect**: one chelating ligand displaces several water molecules, increasing the number of free particles (disorder).

## Remember

- Three classes: metal ion complexes, organic molecular complexes, inclusion compounds.
- Chelate = ring formed by a multidentate ligand; EDTA is hexadentate; chelates are extra-stable (entropy).
- Cyclodextrins: α 6, β 7, γ 8 glucose units; hydrophobic cavity, hydrophilic outside.
- Job's method: peak at the mole fraction of the complex; pH titration (Cu–glycine): log K1 at n̄ = 0.5, log K2 at n̄ = 1.5.
- Solubility method: K(1:1) = slope / [S0(1 − slope)]; AL, AP, AN, BS, BI diagrams.
- Only free drug acts; albumin binds acids, α1-acid glycoprotein binds bases; displacement causes interactions (warfarin) and kernicterus (sulfonamides).
- Klotz: 1/r = 1/(νK[Df]) + 1/ν; Scatchard: r/[Df] = νK − rK. Equilibrium dialysis is the standard method.
- ΔG° = −2.303RT log K; β2 = K1K2.
