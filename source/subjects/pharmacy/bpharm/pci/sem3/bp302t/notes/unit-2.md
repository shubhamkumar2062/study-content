# Unit 2: States of matter and properties of matter

Every dosage form is a solid, liquid or gas, or a mixture of them. Whether a powder stays dry, a suppository melts in the body, an inhaler sprays correctly or a drug dissolves well depends on the state of matter and how it changes. The second half of the unit covers measurable properties of drug molecules (refractive index, optical rotation, dielectric constant, dipole moment, dissociation constant) that are used to identify drugs and design formulations.

## 2.1 States of matter

Matter exists as **solid, liquid and gas**. The state depends on the balance between **intermolecular forces** (holding molecules together) and **thermal energy** (pushing them apart).

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Fixed | Takes shape of container | Fills container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Variable |
| Arrangement | Closely packed, ordered (crystals) | Close but disordered | Far apart, random |
| Movement | Vibration only | Slide past each other | Fast, random |
| Compressibility | Very low | Very low | High |

**Intermediate states:** liquid crystals (between solid and liquid) and the glassy state (a "frozen liquid"). Some texts add **plasma** (ionised gas) as a fourth state.

**Intermolecular forces (binding forces)**

| Force | Between | Relative strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ion–ion | Ions | Strongest |
| Hydrogen bond | H on N, O or F and a lone pair | Strong |
| Ion–dipole | Ion and polar molecule | Strong |
| Dipole–dipole (Keesom) | Two polar molecules | Medium |
| Dipole–induced dipole (Debye) | Polar and non-polar molecule | Weak |
| Induced dipole–induced dipole (London) | Non-polar molecules | Weakest |

The last three together are called **van der Waals forces**.

## 2.2 Changes in the state of matter and latent heats

| Change | Name | Heat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid → liquid | Melting (fusion) | Absorbed |
| Liquid → solid | Freezing | Released |
| Liquid → gas | Vaporisation | Absorbed |
| Gas → liquid | Condensation | Released |
| Solid → gas | Sublimation | Absorbed |
| Gas → solid | Deposition | Released |

**Latent heat** is the heat absorbed or given out when a substance changes state **at constant temperature**. It is "hidden" because the thermometer does not move while it is absorbed.

- Latent heat of fusion of ice: about 80 cal/g (334 J/g).
- Latent heat of vaporisation of water at 100 °C: about 540 cal/g (2260 J/g).

The large latent heat of vaporisation of water explains why steam burns are severe, why steam is an efficient sterilising medium, and why sweating cools the body.

## 2.3 Vapour pressure and the Clausius–Clapeyron equation

**Vapour pressure** is the pressure exerted by a vapour in equilibrium with its liquid (or solid) in a closed container at a given temperature. It rises steeply with temperature. A liquid **boils** when its vapour pressure equals the external pressure; at lower external pressure (in a vacuum or at high altitude) it boils at a lower temperature — used in vacuum drying and evaporation of heat-sensitive extracts.

**Clausius–Clapeyron equation**

log (P2 / P1) = ΔHv (T2 − T1) / (2.303 R T1 T2)

**Worked example.** Water boils at 100 °C (373 K) at 760 mm Hg; ΔHv ≈ 9720 cal/mol. Vapour pressure at 90 °C (363 K):

log (P2/760) = 9720 × (363 − 373) / (2.303 × 1.987 × 373 × 363) = − 0.157

P2 = 760 × 0.697 ≈ **530 mm Hg**

## 2.4 Sublimation, critical point and the phase rule

**Sublimation** — a solid passes directly to vapour. Happens when the vapour pressure of the solid reaches the external pressure below its melting point. Examples: camphor, iodine, menthol, naphthalene, ammonium chloride, solid CO2 ("dry ice"). Used in **freeze-drying (lyophilisation)**: a frozen product is placed under vacuum and the ice sublimes, leaving a dry porous cake (antibiotics, vaccines, proteins).

**Critical temperature (Tc)** — the temperature above which a gas cannot be liquefied however much pressure is applied. **Critical pressure (Pc)** — pressure needed to liquefy the gas at Tc. Water: Tc ≈ 374 °C, Pc ≈ 218 atm. CO2: Tc ≈ 31 °C, Pc ≈ 73 atm. Above both, the substance is a **supercritical fluid** (supercritical CO2 is used to extract plant actives and make fine drug particles).

**Phase rule (Gibbs)**

F = C − P + 2

F = degrees of freedom (variables such as temperature, pressure, concentration that can be changed independently), C = number of components, P = number of phases.

**Phase diagram of water (one component)**

```text
Pressure
   ^     B
   |      \        LIQUID           A (critical point)
   |       \                     _/
   | SOLID  \                 __/       OA = vapour pressure curve (liquid ⇌ vapour)
   |         \            __/           OB = fusion curve (solid ⇌ liquid);
   |          \        __/                   leans slightly left for water
   |           \    __/                 OC = sublimation curve (solid ⇌ vapour)
   |            O_/      VAPOUR         O  = triple point: 0.01 °C, 4.58 mm Hg (F = 0)
   |         __/
   |    C __/
   +------------------------------------> Temperature
```

At the triple point three phases coexist, so F = 1 − 3 + 2 = 0 (no variable can be changed). On a line, F = 1; in an area, F = 2.

## 2.5 Eutectic mixtures

When two solids that are miscible as liquids but not as solids are mixed, the mixture melts below the melting point of either. The lowest such melting mixture is the **eutectic mixture**, and its melting point is the **eutectic point**.

```text
Temp
 ^  mp of A                        mp of B
 |   \                              /
 |    \     liquid (one phase)     /
 |     \                          /
 |      \  solid A + liquid      /  solid B + liquid
 |       \______________________/
 |              E (eutectic point: lowest melting)
 |          solid A + solid B
 +--------------------------------> composition (100% A ... 100% B)
```

**Pharmaceutical examples**

- **Camphor, menthol, thymol, phenol, chloral hydrate** — pairs liquefy when triturated together (a problem in powders; prevented by adding an inert adsorbent such as light magnesium oxide or kaolin, or by dispensing separately).
- **Lidocaine + prilocaine** form a eutectic oil used in a topical anaesthetic cream.
- **Solid dispersions** — a poorly soluble drug and a water-soluble carrier (e.g. urea, PEG) melted together and cooled give very fine drug crystals that dissolve faster.

## 2.6 Gases

**Ideal gas laws**

| Law | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| Boyle | PV = constant at constant T |
| Charles | V/T = constant at constant P |
| Gay-Lussac | P/T = constant at constant V |
| Avogadro | Equal volumes at the same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules |
| Combined | PV = nRT |

R = 0.08205 L atm/mol K = 8.314 J/mol K = 1.987 cal/mol K. One mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at 0 °C and 1 atm.

**Worked example.** Volume of 2 mol of an ideal gas at 27 °C (300 K) and 1 atm: V = nRT/P = 2 × 0.08205 × 300 / 1 = 49.2 L.

**Kinetic molecular theory** assumes gas molecules are point masses in constant random motion, with no attraction between them and perfectly elastic collisions. **Real gases** deviate at high pressure and low temperature because molecules have volume and do attract. The **van der Waals equation** corrects for both:

(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT

a corrects for attraction between molecules; b corrects for the volume of the molecules.

## 2.7 Aerosols and inhalers

An **aerosol** is a preparation packed under pressure that releases the product as a fine mist, spray, foam or stream when a valve is pressed.

**Components:** container, valve and actuator, **propellant**, and the product concentrate (drug with solvents or suspending agents).

**Propellants**

| Type | Examples | Behaviour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Liquefied gases | Hydrofluoroalkanes (HFA 134a, HFA 227), hydrocarbons (propane, butane); older chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, phased out because they damage the ozone layer) | Liquid in the can; vapour above it gives constant pressure until the can is empty |
| Compressed gases | Nitrogen, carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide | Pressure falls as product is used; used for foams and topical sprays |

The principle: the propellant's vapour pressure inside the can is higher than atmospheric pressure, so opening the valve pushes the contents out, and the liquid propellant flashes into vapour, breaking the product into droplets.

**Inhalers**

- **Metered-dose inhaler (MDI)** — pressurised; each press releases a fixed dose.
- **Dry powder inhaler (DPI)** — no propellant; the patient's breath disperses micronised powder.
- **Nebuliser** — turns a drug solution into a mist using compressed air or ultrasound.

Particles of roughly **1–5 µm** reach the lower airways; larger ones hit the throat, and very small ones may be breathed out.

## 2.8 Relative humidity

**Relative humidity (RH)** = (partial pressure of water vapour in the air ÷ vapour pressure of water at saturation at the same temperature) × 100.

- **Hygroscopic** substances take up moisture from air; **deliquescent** ones take up so much that they dissolve in it (e.g. calcium chloride).
- **Critical relative humidity (CRH)** — the RH above which a water-soluble solid starts absorbing moisture steeply.
- **Efflorescent** substances lose water of crystallisation to dry air.

**Uses in pharmacy:** storage conditions, choice of desiccants (silica gel) and moisture-proof packaging, controlling humidity in tablet and capsule manufacture (hard gelatin capsules become brittle when too dry and soft when too humid), and stability studies (40 °C / 75% RH accelerated condition).

## 2.9 Liquid state and liquid complexes

In liquids the molecules are close together and attract each other but can move past one another. Properties such as viscosity, surface tension and vapour pressure reflect the strength of these attractions. Some liquids are **associated** — their molecules are linked by hydrogen bonds into clusters (water, alcohols, carboxylic acids) — giving higher boiling points than expected from molecular weight. Molecules of different liquids can also bind to each other in the liquid state (e.g. chloroform and acetone through hydrogen bonding), forming loose **molecular complexes** in solution; this explains negative deviation from Raoult's law (Unit 1) and is treated further under complexation (Unit 4).

## 2.10 Liquid crystals

A **liquid crystal (mesophase)** is a state between a crystalline solid and an isotropic liquid. Its molecules (usually long rod-shaped or amphiphilic) flow like a liquid but keep some orderly arrangement, so they are **anisotropic** and show **birefringence** under polarised light.

| Type | How formed | Arrangement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Smectic** ("soap-like") | Thermotropic | Molecules in layers, long axes parallel; layers slide over one another; most solid-like |
| **Nematic** ("thread-like") | Thermotropic | Molecules parallel but not in layers; flow more freely |
| **Cholesteric** | Thermotropic | Nematic-like sheets, each twisted slightly relative to the next (a helix); colour changes with temperature |
| **Lyotropic** | Amphiphile + solvent (concentration-dependent) | Surfactant–water systems forming lamellar, hexagonal or cubic phases |

**Thermotropic** liquid crystals appear when a solid is heated; **lyotropic** ones appear when a solvent (usually water) is added to amphiphiles.

**Applications:** stabilising emulsions and creams (liquid-crystalline layers around droplets), sustained drug delivery from lyotropic gels, cholesteric films for skin-temperature mapping (thermography), and display screens. Cell membranes behave like lyotropic liquid crystals.

## 2.11 Solids: crystalline, amorphous and the glassy state

**Crystalline solids** have atoms or molecules arranged in a repeating three-dimensional pattern (a **lattice**). The smallest repeating unit is the **unit cell**. There are **7 crystal systems** — cubic, tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, hexagonal and rhombohedral (trigonal) — and **14 Bravais lattices**. Crystals may be held by ionic, covalent, molecular (van der Waals / hydrogen bond) or metallic forces.

| Property | Crystalline | Amorphous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Order | Long-range | Short-range only |
| Melting | Sharp melting point | Softens over a range |
| Optical / mechanical | Usually anisotropic | Isotropic |
| Energy | Lower (stable) | Higher (metastable) |
| Solubility, dissolution | Lower | Higher |
| Stability | Better | May crystallise on storage |
| Examples | Sodium chloride, sucrose crystals | Glass, many polymers, spray-dried or freeze-dried drugs |

**Glassy state.** When a liquid is cooled quickly it may not crystallise but become a rigid, disordered **glass**. The **glass transition temperature (Tg)** is the temperature below which the material is hard and brittle and above which it becomes soft and rubbery (molecular mobility increases). Tg matters for amorphous drugs (they stay stable well below Tg), freeze-dried products, and polymer film coatings (plasticisers lower Tg so films are flexible).

**Crystal habit** is the outer shape (needles, plates, prisms); it affects flow, compression and syringeability but not the internal lattice.

## 2.12 Polymorphism

**Polymorphism** is the ability of one chemical substance to exist in more than one crystalline form. The forms have the same chemistry but different lattices, so they differ in melting point, solubility, dissolution rate, density, hardness and stability.

- **Enantiotropic** — one form changes reversibly into another at a definite transition temperature below both melting points (e.g. sulfur).
- **Monotropic** — only one form is stable at all temperatures; the metastable form can change to it but not back.
- **Metastable forms** are more soluble and dissolve faster but may convert to the stable form during storage.
- **Pseudopolymorphs** — solvates and hydrates (solvent built into the lattice). Anhydrous forms usually dissolve faster than hydrates (anhydrous ampicillin vs ampicillin trihydrate).

**Pharmaceutical examples**

| Drug / material | Point |
| --- | --- |
| Chloramphenicol palmitate | The metastable form B gives much higher blood levels than the stable form A |
| Theobroma oil (cocoa butter) | Several polymorphs; overheating above about 36 °C gives metastable forms that set poorly, so suppositories are made with gentle warming |
| Ritonavir | A less soluble polymorph appeared after launch and forced reformulation |

**Methods to detect polymorphs:** X-ray powder diffraction (most definite), differential scanning calorimetry and thermal analysis, infrared spectroscopy, hot-stage microscopy, solubility and dissolution studies.

## 2.13 Physicochemical properties of drug molecules

### Refractive index

**Refractive index (n)** = speed of light in vacuum ÷ speed of light in the substance = sin i ÷ sin r. It depends on temperature and wavelength (usually stated for the sodium D line at 20 °C or 25 °C). Measured with the **Abbe refractometer** using the critical angle.

**Molar refraction** Rm = [(n² − 1)/(n² + 2)] × (M/ρ) is additive for atoms and bonds and helps confirm structure.

**Uses:** identification and purity of oils and liquids (pharmacopoeial tests), measuring concentration (sugar solutions), and detecting adulteration.

### Optical rotation

Substances with a chiral centre rotate the plane of **plane-polarised light**. **Dextrorotatory (+)** — clockwise; **laevorotatory (−)** — anticlockwise. Measured with a **polarimeter** (light source, polariser, sample tube, analyser).

**Specific rotation**

[α] (at temperature t, wavelength λ) = 100 α / (l × c)

α = observed rotation in degrees, l = tube length in **decimetres**, c = grams of solute per 100 ml.

**Worked example.** A 5 g/100 ml solution in a 2 dm tube rotates light by +2.6°. [α] = 100 × 2.6 / (2 × 5) = **+26°**.

**Uses:** identity and purity of chiral drugs (e.g. dextrose, sucrose, alkaloids), measuring concentration, following reactions such as the inversion of sucrose, and distinguishing enantiomers that differ in activity. **Optical rotatory dispersion** (rotation vs wavelength) and **circular dichroism** help study structure.

### Dielectric constant

When a substance is placed between the plates of a capacitor, the capacitance rises. **Dielectric constant (ε) = Cx / C0**, the capacitance with the substance ÷ the capacitance in vacuum. It measures how well a solvent separates charges.

| Solvent (approx., 25 °C) | ε |
| --- | --- |
| Water | 78.5 |
| Glycerin | about 42 |
| Ethanol | about 24 |
| Chloroform | about 4.8 |
| Benzene | about 2.3 |

**Measurement:** with a capacitance cell in a bridge or resonance circuit, comparing the cell filled with sample to the same cell in air or vacuum.

**Uses:** classifying solvents (polar above about 50, semipolar about 20–50, non-polar below about 20), choosing cosolvent mixtures that match the dielectric need of a drug, predicting solubility and the stability of ionic reactions.

### Dipole moment

A molecule with separated centres of positive and negative charge has a **permanent dipole moment**:

µ = q × d (charge × distance between charges)

Unit: **debye (D)**, 1 D = 10⁻¹⁸ esu cm = 3.34 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. Water ≈ 1.84 D; symmetrical molecules such as CO2, CCl4 and benzene have zero net dipole moment.

An electric field can also create an **induced dipole** in a molecule by shifting its electrons; the ease of this is the **polarisability**. The **Debye equation** links the total polarisation (from dielectric constant and density) with polarisability and µ; measuring dielectric constant at several temperatures gives µ.

**Uses:** finding molecular shape and symmetry (cis/trans isomers, ortho/meta/para disubstituted benzenes — para isomers of identical groups have zero dipole moment), predicting solvent polarity and solubility, and interactions with receptors.

### Dissociation constant

Weak acids and bases ionise only partly. For HA ⇌ H+ + A−:

Ka = [H+][A−] / [HA]    pKa = − log Ka

For a weak base, Kb and pKb; for a conjugate pair pKa + pKb = pKw = 14 at 25 °C.

**Worked example.** Acetic acid Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵. pKa = − log (1.75 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.76.

**Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** (weak acid): pH = pKa + log [A−]/[HA]. When the acid is half-neutralised, [A−] = [HA], so **pH = pKa** (practical 2).

**% ionised (weak acid)** = 100 / [1 + 10^(pKa − pH)]. Aspirin (pKa about 3.5) at pH 1.5 is only about 1% ionised, so it is mostly absorbable in the stomach.

**Methods of determining pKa:** potentiometric (pH) titration and half-neutralisation, UV spectrophotometry (ionised and un-ionised forms absorb differently), conductometry, and the solubility–pH method.

**Uses:** predicting absorption (pH–partition), choosing salt forms and solution pH, solubility and stability, and buffer design.

## Remember

- Latent heat: fusion of ice 80 cal/g, vaporisation of water 540 cal/g; absorbed at constant temperature.
- Clausius–Clapeyron: log(P2/P1) = ΔHv(T2 − T1)/(2.303RT1T2). Phase rule F = C − P + 2; triple point of water 0.01 °C, 4.58 mm Hg.
- Eutectic: lowest-melting mixture (camphor + menthol liquefy; lidocaine + prilocaine cream).
- Propellants: liquefied (HFA, hydrocarbons) or compressed (N2, CO2); lung deposition best at 1–5 µm.
- RH = actual ÷ saturated vapour pressure × 100; CRH is where a soluble solid begins to soak up water.
- Liquid crystals: smectic (layers), nematic (parallel threads), cholesteric (twisted); thermotropic vs lyotropic.
- Amorphous and metastable polymorphs dissolve faster but are less stable; chloramphenicol palmitate form B, cocoa butter forms.
- [α] = 100α/(l c) with l in dm; ε water 78.5; µ in debye (water 1.84 D); half-neutralisation pH = pKa.
