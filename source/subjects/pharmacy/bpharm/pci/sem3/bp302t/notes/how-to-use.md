# How to use this guide

Physical Pharmaceutics I mixes theory with numericals. Study one unit a day: read the notes, redo every worked example with a calculator without looking, draw the diagrams once from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with diagrams, equations and a worked numerical.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Topics that keep coming back |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Solubility | Factors affecting solubility, Raoult's law and deviations, CST of phenol–water, distribution law with limitations and applications |
| 2 States of matter | Polymorphism, liquid crystals, eutectic mixtures, aerosols, dielectric constant, dipole moment, optical rotation, pKa determination |
| 3 Surface phenomena | Surface tension measurement, spreading coefficient, surfactant classification, HLB, solubilisation, Freundlich and Langmuir isotherms |
| 4 Complexation | Classification of complexes, methods of analysis, protein binding and its significance |
| 5 pH and buffers | Buffer equation, buffer capacity, pH determination, isotonicity methods with numericals |

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start every definition-type answer with a one-line definition and the equation; then explain each symbol with its unit.
2. Numericals: write the formula first, substitute with units, box the final answer with its unit (dyne/cm, M⁻¹, g NaCl per 100 ml). Method marks are given even if the arithmetic slips.
3. Draw the graph that goes with the topic: CST dome, phase diagram, eutectic diagram, surface tension vs log concentration (CMC), Job's plot, phase solubility diagram, buffer capacity curve.
4. Always add 3–4 pharmaceutical applications at the end of an essay — examiners in pharmacy look for the link to formulation.
5. Learn the standard numbers: water surface tension 72.8 dyne/cm, dielectric constant 78.5, blood freezing point −0.52 °C, 0.9% NaCl isotonic, phenol–water CST about 66.8 °C, βmax = 0.576C.
6. For classification answers (complexes, surfactants, liquid crystals), use a table or tree with one example per class.
7. Practise the five numerical types until they are automatic: buffer pH, buffer capacity, NaCl to add for isotonicity, HLB of a blend, and surface tension by drop count or capillary rise.
