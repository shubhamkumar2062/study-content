# Unit 1: Solubility of drugs

A drug must be in solution before it can be absorbed, so solubility decides how a medicine is formulated, how fast it acts and how much reaches the blood. This unit explains how solubility is expressed, why some solutes dissolve and others do not, how temperature, pH and other factors change it, and how two liquids mix or share a solute between them.

## 1.1 Solubility expressions

**Solubility** is the concentration of solute in a **saturated solution** at a stated temperature, when the dissolved solute is in equilibrium with undissolved solid.

- **Saturated solution** — holds the maximum solute the solvent can keep at that temperature.
- **Unsaturated solution** — holds less than the maximum.
- **Supersaturated solution** — holds more than the equilibrium amount; it is unstable and crystallises on scratching or seeding.

**Descriptive terms of the pharmacopoeia** (parts of solvent needed to dissolve 1 part of solute):

| Term | Parts of solvent for 1 part of solute |
| --- | --- |
| Very soluble | Less than 1 |
| Freely soluble | 1 to 10 |
| Soluble | 10 to 30 |
| Sparingly soluble | 30 to 100 |
| Slightly soluble | 100 to 1,000 |
| Very slightly soluble | 1,000 to 10,000 |
| Practically insoluble / insoluble | More than 10,000 |

**Quantitative expressions**

| Expression | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Molarity (M) | Moles of solute in 1 litre of solution |
| Molality (m) | Moles of solute in 1 kg of solvent (does not change with temperature) |
| Normality (N) | Gram equivalents of solute in 1 litre of solution |
| Mole fraction (X) | Moles of one component ÷ total moles of all components |
| % w/w, % w/v, % v/v | Grams per 100 g, grams per 100 ml, ml per 100 ml |
| mg% | Milligrams per 100 ml (used for blood levels) |
| ppm | Parts per million (trace impurities, water hardness) |

## 1.2 Mechanisms of solute–solvent interactions

The old rule "**like dissolves like**" holds because a solute dissolves when its attraction to the solvent is similar to, or stronger than, the attractions it gives up (solute–solute and solvent–solvent).

**Three steps of dissolving (think of it as a three-step energy account):**

```text
Step 1: remove one solute molecule from its crystal      (needs energy: breaks solute-solute bonds)
Step 2: make a hole in the solvent the size of the solute (needs energy: breaks solvent-solvent bonds)
Step 3: put the solute into the hole                      (releases energy: solute-solvent bonds form)

Dissolves well when the energy released in step 3 covers steps 1 + 2.
```

**Classes of solvents**

| Class | How it dissolves things | Examples | Dissolves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Polar | High dielectric constant lowers the attraction between ions; forms hydrogen bonds; acid–base reactions with the solute | Water, glycerin | Salts, sugars, polar drugs |
| Semipolar | Can induce a degree of polarity in non-polar molecules; acts as a bridge | Acetone, ethanol, propylene glycol | Helps water and oils mix (cosolvents) |
| Non-polar | Weak van der Waals (London) forces only; cannot break ionic bonds | Hexane, benzene, mineral oil, CCl4 | Oils, fats, alkaloid bases, iodine |

Why water is a good solvent for ionic drugs: its dielectric constant (about 78.5 at 25 °C) weakens the attraction between oppositely charged ions; it forms hydrogen bonds with –OH, –NH2, –COOH and similar groups; and its molecules surround (hydrate) each ion.

## 1.3 Ideal solubility and solubility parameters

**Ideal solution of a solid in a liquid.** If the solute and solvent attract each other just as they attract themselves, solubility depends only on the solid's melting point and its heat of fusion:

log X2(ideal) = − (ΔHf / 2.303 R) × (T0 − T) / (T0 × T)

where X2 = mole fraction solubility, ΔHf = molar heat of fusion, T0 = melting point (K), T = temperature of the solution (K), R = 1.987 cal/mol K.

So, for ideal solutions: **higher melting point or higher heat of fusion → lower solubility.**

**Worked example.** Naphthalene melts at 80 °C (353 K) and its heat of fusion is about 4500 cal/mol. Ideal solubility at 20 °C (293 K):

log X2 = − 4500 / (2.303 × 1.987) × (353 − 293) / (353 × 293) = − 983.4 × 0.000580 = − 0.570

X2 = 0.27 (mole fraction). Naphthalene in benzene (a nearly ideal pair) dissolves to almost this value.

**Regular solutions and the solubility parameter.** Most real pairs are not ideal. Hildebrand defined a **solubility parameter (δ)** for each liquid:

δ = [(ΔHv − RT) / V]^½

ΔHv = heat of vaporisation, V = molar volume. The term inside is the **cohesive energy density** — the energy needed to pull the molecules of 1 cm³ apart. The real (regular) solubility is:

− log X2 = − log X2(ideal) + [V2 φ1² (δ1 − δ2)²] / (2.303 RT)

φ1 is the volume fraction of solvent. **The closer δ of solvent and solute, the higher the solubility.** When δ1 = δ2 the second term is zero and solubility is ideal. This is used to choose solvents and cosolvent blends.

## 1.4 Solvation and association

- **Solvation** — the solute surrounds itself with solvent molecules through solute–solvent attraction. In water it is called **hydration** (e.g. hydrated Na+ and Cl− ions). Solvation increases solubility.
- **Association** — solute molecules join together (solute–solute attraction), for example benzoic acid forming hydrogen-bonded **dimers** in benzene, or surfactants forming micelles. Association changes apparent molecular weight and lowers the effective number of free molecules; it also affects the distribution law (Section 1.10).

## 1.5 Quantitative approach to factors influencing solubility

| Factor | Effect | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | Endothermic dissolving (ΔH positive): solubility rises with temperature. Exothermic (ΔH negative): falls | Most drugs dissolve more when warmed; calcium hydroxide less |
| Nature of solute and solvent | Similar polarity → more soluble | Iodine in CCl4 vs water |
| Particle size | Very small particles (below about 1 µm) are slightly more soluble | Micronised griseofulvin |
| Crystal form | Metastable polymorphs and amorphous forms are more soluble than the stable form | Chloramphenicol palmitate form B |
| pH | Weak acids dissolve more at high pH, weak bases at low pH | Sodium salt of phenobarbital |
| Cosolvents | Water-miscible solvents raise solubility of non-polar drugs | Ethanol, propylene glycol, glycerin, PEG 400 in injections |
| Common ion / salts | Common ion lowers solubility of a sparingly soluble salt; other salts can salt-in or salt-out | NaCl reduces solubility of non-electrolytes in water (salting-out) |
| Complexation and surfactants | Can raise solubility | Caffeine with sodium benzoate; vitamin A in polysorbate micelles |

**Temperature (quantitative).** For a solid, ln of solubility plotted against 1/T gives a straight line (van't Hoff relation):

log (S2/S1) = ΔHsol (T2 − T1) / (2.303 R T1 T2)

**Particle size (Ostwald–Freundlich equation).**

log (S/S0) = 2γV / (2.303 RT r)

S = solubility of fine particles of radius r, S0 = solubility of large particles, γ = interfacial tension, V = molar volume. The effect is seen only for very small particles.

**pH — weak acids and weak bases.** Only the un-ionised form has a limited solubility S0 (intrinsic solubility); the ionised form is very soluble. The total solubility S is:

- Weak acid: S = S0 [1 + 10^(pH − pKa)]
- Weak base: S = S0 [1 + 10^(pKa − pH)]

Rearranged, the **pH below which a weak acid precipitates (pHp)**:

pHp = pKa + log [(S − S0) / S0]

**Worked example.** A 1% w/v solution of sodium phenobarbital (molecular weight 254) is 10/254 = 0.0394 M. Phenobarbital has pKa 7.41 and intrinsic solubility 0.005 M. Lowest pH at which it stays in solution:

pHp = 7.41 + log [(0.0394 − 0.005) / 0.005] = 7.41 + log 6.88 = 7.41 + 0.84 = **8.25**

Below pH 8.25 the free acid precipitates. This is why such injections are alkaline and must not be mixed with acidic infusions.

For a weak base the matching equation is pHp = pKw − pKb + log [S0 / (S − S0)]; above this pH the free base precipitates.

## 1.6 Diffusion principles in biological systems

**Diffusion** is the movement of molecules from a region of high concentration to low concentration because of random motion.

**Fick's first law**

J = dM / (S dt) = − D (dC/dx)

J = flux (amount crossing unit area per unit time), D = diffusion coefficient (cm²/s), dC/dx = concentration gradient. The minus sign shows movement down the gradient.

**Passive diffusion across a membrane.** For a drug crossing a membrane of thickness h:

dM/dt = (D K S / h) (Cd − Cr)

- K = partition coefficient of drug between membrane and water.
- Cd and Cr = concentration on the donor (gut) and receiver (blood) side. Blood removes the drug quickly ("sink condition"), so Cr ≈ 0.
- P = DK/h is the **permeability coefficient**.

```text
   GIT lumen (donor)      |  lipid membrane  |   blood (receiver)
   high C, unionised  --> |   dissolves in   | --> carried away (sink)
   drug                   |   lipid (needs K)|
```

**Key ideas for drug absorption**

1. Only **un-ionised, lipid-soluble** drug crosses the lipid membrane readily (pH–partition hypothesis). Aspirin (weak acid) is mostly un-ionised in the stomach; weak bases are mostly un-ionised in the intestine.
2. A higher partition coefficient helps up to a point; an extremely lipophilic drug stays in the membrane and dissolves poorly in gut fluids.
3. Other processes: **facilitated diffusion** (carrier, down the gradient, no energy — e.g. vitamin B12 with intrinsic factor), **active transport** (carrier, against the gradient, needs energy), **pinocytosis**, and passage of small polar molecules through **aqueous pores**.
4. Diffusion also governs drug release from matrices, dialysis, ultrafiltration and transport through skin.

## 1.7 Solubility of gases in liquids

**Henry's law.** At constant temperature, the amount of gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid:

C2 = σ p

C2 = concentration of dissolved gas, p = partial pressure, σ = solubility coefficient.

**Factors**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Pressure | More pressure → more gas dissolves (opening a soda releases CO2) |
| Temperature | Higher temperature → less gas dissolves (gases are expelled by boiling) |
| Salts (salting-out) | Electrolytes in water reduce gas solubility |
| Chemical reaction | Gases that react with the solvent (HCl, NH3, CO2 in water) dissolve far more than Henry's law predicts |

**Pharmaceutical relevance:** carbonated preparations and effervescent solutions; aerosol propellants dissolved under pressure; boiling water for injections and flushing with nitrogen to remove oxygen from oxidisable products (e.g. ascorbic acid injections); oxygen and anaesthetic gas solubility in blood.

The **Bunsen absorption coefficient (α)** is the volume of gas, reduced to 0 °C and 1 atm, dissolved by one volume of liquid when the gas pressure is 1 atm.

## 1.8 Solubility of liquids in liquids — binary and ideal solutions, Raoult's law

Pairs of liquids are of three kinds:

1. **Completely miscible** — alcohol and water, glycerin and water.
2. **Partially miscible** — phenol and water (two layers at room temperature).
3. **Practically immiscible** — water and liquid paraffin, water and CCl4.

A **binary solution** contains two components. An **ideal solution** is one in which the attraction between unlike molecules equals that between like ones. It shows:

- No heat change on mixing (ΔHmix = 0).
- No volume change on mixing (ΔVmix = 0).
- Obeys **Raoult's law** at all concentrations.

**Raoult's law.** The partial vapour pressure of each component equals its vapour pressure when pure multiplied by its mole fraction in the solution:

pA = pA° × XA    pB = pB° × XB    P (total) = pA + pB

**Worked example.** Two liquids A and B form an ideal solution. Pure A has vapour pressure 100 mm Hg and pure B 40 mm Hg at the working temperature. For XA = 0.3 and XB = 0.7:

pA = 100 × 0.3 = 30 mm Hg; pB = 40 × 0.7 = 28 mm Hg; P = 58 mm Hg.

Mole fraction of A in the vapour = 30/58 = 0.52. The vapour is richer in the more volatile liquid — the basis of fractional distillation.

```text
Vapour pressure vs mole fraction (ideal pair)

P  |pA°
   | \                 /pB° ... total P is a straight line between pA° and pB°
   |  \  partial A    /
   |   \           /  partial B
   |____\______/_______
   XA=1        XB=1
```

## 1.9 Real solutions, partially miscible liquids and critical solution temperature

**Real solutions** deviate from Raoult's law.

| Deviation | Cause | Vapour pressure | Example | Distillation result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Negative | A–B attraction **stronger** than A–A and B–B (e.g. hydrogen bonding) | Lower than ideal; heat given out on mixing | Chloroform–acetone, HCl–water | Maximum-boiling azeotrope |
| Positive | A–B attraction **weaker** than A–A and B–B | Higher than ideal; heat absorbed, volume may expand | Ethanol–water, benzene–ethanol | Minimum-boiling azeotrope |

An **azeotrope** boils at constant temperature with vapour of the same composition as the liquid, so it cannot be separated by simple distillation.

**Partially miscible liquids — phenol and water.** At room temperature, mixing phenol and water gives two layers: a water-rich layer containing some phenol and a phenol-rich layer containing some water (**conjugate solutions**). As temperature rises, each layer dissolves more of the other, and their compositions approach each other. Above the **critical solution temperature (CST, upper consolute temperature)** the two liquids mix in all proportions. For phenol–water the upper CST is about **66.8 °C**.

```text
Temperature
   ^
66.8 °C ......*......   <- CST: top of the dome
   |        /   \
   |       / two \       Outside the dome: one clear phase
   |      / layers\
   |_____/_________\____> % phenol
        water-rich  phenol-rich
        layer        layer
   A tie-line across the dome joins the two conjugate compositions.
```

**Types of CST**

| Type | Behaviour | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upper CST | Miscible above it | Phenol–water |
| Lower CST | Miscible below it | Triethylamine–water |
| Both (closed loop) | Miscible below the lower and above the upper | Nicotine–water |

**Effect of impurities on CST (and its uses)**

- A substance soluble in **only one** liquid (e.g. NaCl or KCl in water) **raises** the upper CST of phenol–water.
- A substance soluble in **both** liquids (e.g. succinic acid, sodium oleate) **lowers** it.
- The rise is proportional to the impurity concentration, so CST can be used to **find the % of NaCl** in a solution (practical 5) and to check purity (e.g. of phenol).
- Soaps lower the CST of cresol–water and give clear "solution of cresol with soap" preparations used as disinfectants.

## 1.10 Distribution law — limitations and applications

**Nernst distribution law.** When a solute is added to two immiscible liquids in contact, it distributes between them so that, at equilibrium and constant temperature, the ratio of its concentrations is constant:

K = C1 / C2 (C1 and C2 = concentrations in the two phases)

K is the **partition (distribution) coefficient**. In pharmacy it is usually written for oil and water: K = Coil / Cwater (log P when the oil is n-octanol).

**Corrections when the solute changes form**

- **Association in one phase** (benzoic acid forms dimers in benzene): Cwater / √Cbenzene = constant. In general for n molecules associating, C1 / (C2)^(1/n) = constant.
- **Dissociation in water** (a weak acid partly ionised): K = Coil / [Cwater (1 − α)], where α is the degree of ionisation; only the un-ionised form partitions.

**Limitations**

1. Temperature must be constant.
2. The two liquids must be immiscible and not react with the solute or with each other.
3. Solutions should be dilute.
4. The solute must exist in the same molecular form in both phases (else apply the corrections above).
5. Equilibrium must be reached.

**Worked example — extraction.** 1 g of a drug is in 100 ml of water; K (oil/water) = 4. If it is shaken once with 100 ml of oil, the amount left in water is:

w1 = w × Vw / (Vw + K Vo) = 1 × 100 / (100 + 4 × 100) = 0.20 g (80% extracted)

If the same 100 ml oil is used as two 50 ml portions:

w2 = 1 × [100 / (100 + 4 × 50)]² = (1/3)² = 0.11 g (89% extracted)

**Rule: several small extractions remove more than one large one.**

**Applications of the distribution law**

1. Solvent extraction of drugs and alkaloids from plant material and during analysis.
2. Partition chromatography.
3. Predicting drug absorption and distribution (log P): moderately lipophilic drugs cross membranes best.
4. Preservative action in emulsions — a preservative that partitions mostly into the oil leaves too little in the water phase where microbes grow.
5. Release of drugs from ointment and suppository bases.
6. Determining association or dissociation of a solute, and molecular weight in solution.

## Remember

- IP terms: "sparingly soluble" = 30–100 parts of solvent; "practically insoluble" = more than 10,000.
- Ideal solubility depends only on melting point and heat of fusion; closer solubility parameters mean better solubility.
- Weak acid: S = S0[1 + 10^(pH − pKa)]; precipitation pH: pHp = pKa + log[(S − S0)/S0].
- Fick's first law J = −D dC/dx; permeability P = DK/h; only un-ionised drug crosses lipid membranes easily.
- Henry's law C = σp; gases are less soluble when hot.
- Ideal solution: obeys Raoult's law, no heat or volume change; negative deviation → maximum-boiling azeotrope.
- Phenol–water upper CST about 66.8 °C; NaCl raises it, soap or succinic acid lowers it.
- K = C1/C2 at constant temperature; correct for association (√C) and dissociation (1 − α); many small extractions beat one large one.
