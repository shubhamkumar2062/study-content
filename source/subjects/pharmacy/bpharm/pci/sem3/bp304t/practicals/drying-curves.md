# Drying curves of calcium carbonate and starch

**Aim:** To construct the drying curve and the rate of drying curve for wet calcium carbonate and wet starch, and to find the critical moisture content.

> Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Use tongs for hot trays.

## Principle

When a wet solid is dried under constant conditions (temperature, humidity and air velocity), its moisture falls in a typical pattern:

- an initial adjustment period,
- a **constant rate period**, when the surface is fully wet,
- the **critical moisture content**, where the rate starts to fall,
- one or two **falling rate periods**, until the solid reaches its **equilibrium moisture content (EMC)**.

Calcium carbonate is a non-porous, non-hygroscopic solid, so most water is unbound and it shows a long constant rate period. Starch holds water inside its granules (bound water), so it shows a shorter constant rate period and a long falling rate period.

## Requirements

Calcium carbonate and starch powders, water, two shallow flat trays (or Petri dishes) of known area, hot air oven (about 60–70 °C) or tray dryer, balance, stopwatch, desiccator, spatula.

## Procedure

1. Weigh each empty, dry tray (W0) and measure its area (A).
2. Mix about 50 g calcium carbonate with enough water (e.g. 25 ml) to form a uniform paste; spread evenly in a tray. Do the same with starch in the second tray.
3. Weigh each tray with wet material (initial weight).
4. Place both in the oven at a fixed temperature (e.g. 60 °C).
5. Remove and weigh quickly every 10 minutes (every 15–20 minutes later on) until two successive weights are nearly constant.
6. Finally dry a small portion to constant weight at 105 °C (or use the final constant weight) to obtain the dry solid weight (Ws).

## Observations and calculations

1. Water present = (tray + material) − W0 − Ws.
2. Moisture content X = water present ÷ Ws.
3. Rate of drying R = (Ws/A) × (ΔX/Δt), or simply loss in weight ÷ (A × time interval).
4. Plot **drying curve:** X against time.
5. Plot **rate of drying curve:** R against average X for each interval.
6. Read the critical moisture content where the constant rate line ends, and the EMC where the rate becomes zero.

**Worked example (illustration only):** tray area 0.02 m²; weight lost in 10 minutes in the constant rate period = 2.0 g. Rate = 2.0 ÷ (0.02 × 10) = 10 g/m²·min.

## Result

1. Drying curves and rate of drying curves were drawn for both materials.
2. Critical moisture content: calcium carbonate ___ ; starch ___ g water/g dry solid.
3. Calcium carbonate showed a longer constant rate period; starch showed a longer falling rate period because of bound water.

## Precautions

1. Spread the material in a uniform thin layer.
2. Keep oven temperature constant; open the door briefly.
3. Weigh quickly so the material does not gain moisture from room air.
4. Use the same balance throughout.

## Viva questions

1. **What is the constant rate period?** The stage in which the surface stays fully wet and evaporation proceeds like that of free water, at a steady rate.
2. **Define critical moisture content.** The moisture content at which the constant rate ends and the rate starts falling.
3. **Why does starch show a long falling rate period?** It holds bound water inside its granules that must diffuse out slowly.
4. **What controls the rate in the second falling rate period?** Diffusion of moisture from the inside of the solid to the surface.
5. **What use is the rate curve?** To estimate drying time, choose a dryer and conditions, and scale up.
