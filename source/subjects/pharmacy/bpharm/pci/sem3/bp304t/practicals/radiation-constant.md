# Radiation constant of brass, iron, unpainted and painted glass

**Aim:** To determine the radiation constant (rate of heat loss by radiation) of brass, iron, unpainted glass and painted (black) glass, and to compare them.

> Quantities and set-ups vary between colleges. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Handle hot water and hot vessels with tongs and heat-resistant gloves.

## Principle

Every body above absolute zero emits radiant energy. By the **Stefan–Boltzmann law**, the rate of emission is Q/t = ε σ A T⁴, where ε is the emissivity of the surface. Dark, rough surfaces have high emissivity; polished metals have low emissivity.

In the simple laboratory method, equal containers (or tubes) made of different materials are filled with the same volume of hot water and allowed to cool in still air. The fall in temperature with time is recorded. The heat lost per unit time per unit area per degree of excess temperature gives a comparative **radiation (cooling) constant** of each surface. Surfaces that radiate well cool faster.

By Newton's law of cooling, for small temperature differences: −dT/dt = K (T − Ts), where Ts is the surroundings temperature and K the cooling constant, which depends on the surface.

## Requirements

Containers of the same size and shape made of brass, iron and glass; one glass container painted dull black outside; thermometers (0–110 °C); stopwatch; hot water (about 80–90 °C); measuring cylinder; insulated stands or corks; vernier calipers or scale.

## Procedure

1. Measure the outer diameter and height of each container and calculate its outer surface area (A).
2. Note the room temperature (Ts).
3. Fill each container with the same volume of hot water at the same starting temperature (about 80 °C). Close with a cork carrying a thermometer.
4. Place all containers on insulating stands, away from draughts, at the same distance from one another.
5. Start the stopwatch and record the temperature of each every minute (or every 2 minutes) for 20–30 minutes.
6. Weigh or note the mass of water (m) in each and the mass and specific heat of each container.

1. Plot temperature against time for each surface (cooling curves).
2. From the slope (dT/dt) at a chosen temperature, find the rate of heat loss:
   Q/t = (m × c water + mass of vessel × c vessel) × dT/dt
3. Radiation (cooling) constant = (Q/t) ÷ [A × (T − Ts)]   in W/m²·K
4. Alternatively, plot ln (T − Ts) against time; the slope gives the cooling constant K.

**Worked example (illustration only):** water 200 g (c = 4.18 J/g·K), negligible vessel heat capacity, dT/dt = 0.8 K/min = 0.0133 K/s, A = 0.03 m², T − Ts = 50 K.
Q/t = 200 × 4.18 × 0.0133 ≈ 11.1 W. Constant = 11.1 ÷ (0.03 × 50) ≈ 7.4 W/m²·K.

## Result

The radiation constants of the surfaces were: brass ___, iron ___, unpainted glass ___, painted glass ___ W/m²·K. The painted (black) glass lost heat fastest and polished brass slowest.

## Precautions

1. Use identical containers and the same volume and starting temperature of water.
2. Avoid draughts and keep containers apart so they do not heat one another.
3. Read thermometers at eye level and at exact times.
4. Stir gently before each reading for a uniform temperature.

## Viva questions

1. **State the Stefan–Boltzmann law.** Energy radiated per unit time is proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature: Q/t = εσAT⁴.
2. **What is a black body?** A body that absorbs all radiation falling on it and is the perfect emitter (ε = 1).
3. **What is emissivity?** The ratio of energy radiated by a surface to that by a black body at the same temperature.
4. **Why does painted glass cool faster?** A dull black surface has a higher emissivity, so it radiates more heat.
5. **State Kirchhoff's law.** At the same temperature, emissivity of a body equals its absorptivity.
6. **Why are steam pipes lagged and shiny?** Lagging reduces conduction and convection; shiny surfaces have low emissivity and lose less heat by radiation.
