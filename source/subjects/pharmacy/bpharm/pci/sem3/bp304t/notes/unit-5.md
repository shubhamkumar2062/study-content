# Unit 5: Materials of plant construction, corrosion and material handling

Every vessel, pipe, mill and dryer in a pharmaceutical plant is made of some material. A poor choice can **contaminate the product**, corrode and fail, or cost far too much. This unit covers how materials are chosen, the main metals and non-metals used, why metals corrode and how to prevent it, and the basics of moving materials around the plant.

## 5.1 Factors affecting the selection of materials

### Chemical factors

1. **Contamination of the product:** the material must not react with, dissolve in, or leach into the product. Even traces of metals (iron, copper) can catalyse oxidation of drugs, change colour or cause toxicity. This is the most important factor in pharmacy.
2. **Effect of the product on the material (corrosion):** acids, alkalis, salts and solvents attack many metals. The material must resist the chemicals it meets, including cleaning agents.

### Physical factors

1. **Strength:** must withstand pressure, vacuum and the weight of the contents (tensile strength, hardness).
2. **Mechanical properties:** ductility (can be drawn into wire), malleability (can be beaten into sheets), toughness (resists shock), elasticity.
3. **Thermal properties:** good heat conduction for heat exchangers and jackets; low conduction for insulation; ability to stand heating and cooling cycles (sterilisation) without cracking.
4. **Ease of fabrication:** can be cast, welded, machined and polished.
5. **Cleaning:** smooth, non-porous, crevice-free surfaces that can be easily cleaned and sterilised (GMP requirement).
6. **Weight:** light materials for portable equipment.
7. **Transparency:** glass where the process must be seen.

### Economic factors

Initial cost, cost of fabrication and maintenance, expected life, and availability. A costly but long-lasting material (stainless steel) is often cheaper overall than a cheap one that must be replaced often.

## 5.3 Ferrous metals

Ferrous metals contain iron as the main element.

### Cast iron

Iron with about 2–4% carbon. **Properties:** cheap, easily cast into complex shapes, good compressive strength, resists concentrated sulfuric acid and alkalis reasonably, but **brittle** (poor tensile strength and shock resistance) and rusts in moist air and dilute acids.

**Uses:** bases of machines, heavy supports, steam jackets, pipes for water, mortars in some mills. Not for direct product contact in most pharmaceutical work, because iron discolours and contaminates.

### Carbon (mild) steel

Iron with a small amount of carbon (below about 2%, mild steel much less). **Properties:** strong, ductile, easily welded and machined, cheap; but corrodes easily in moist air, acids and many salts.

**Uses:** structural frames, storage tanks for non-corrosive materials, outer jackets, supports, often coated or lined with glass, rubber or paint when it touches corrosive substances.

### Stainless steel

Alloy steel containing at least about **11–12% chromium**. The chromium forms a thin, invisible, self-healing film of **chromium oxide** on the surface (**passive layer**), which prevents further corrosion.

| Grade | Approximate composition | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 304 ("18/8") | 18% chromium, 8% nickel | General pharmaceutical equipment; good corrosion resistance |
| 316 | Similar, plus 2–3% molybdenum | Better resistance to chlorides and pitting; preferred for product-contact parts and injectables |
| 304L / 316L | Low-carbon versions | Weldable without intergranular corrosion |

**Properties:** strong, tough, resists most chemicals, smooth, easily cleaned and sterilised, does not contaminate the product, good appearance. Attacked by hydrochloric acid and chlorides (pitting) and by hot concentrated sulfuric acid.

**Uses:** almost all product-contact equipment: mixers, tanks, dryers, tablet machine parts, pipes, filters, centrifuges, vessels for injections.

## 5.4 Non-ferrous metals

| Metal | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aluminium** | Light, good conductor of heat, forms a protective oxide film, resists many organic acids; attacked by alkalis and mineral acids | Collapsible tubes, foils for strip and blister packs, containers, heat-exchanger parts |
| **Copper** | Excellent heat conductor, ductile; attacked by ammonia, oxidising acids; traces catalyse oxidation of oils and vitamins | Steam pipes, heat exchangers, coils, older evaporating pans (not for direct contact with many drugs) |
| **Brass** (copper + zinc) and **bronze** (copper + tin) | Easily machined, stronger than copper | Valves, fittings, sieve wire, small parts |
| **Lead** | Soft, resists sulfuric acid, very heavy, toxic, poor strength | Lining of vessels for sulfuric acid (rarely now) |
| **Nickel** | Strong, resists alkalis and many chemicals | Handling caustic alkalis; plating; alloying |
| **Monel** (nickel + copper) | Strong, corrosion-resistant to many acids and alkalis | Pumps, valves, equipment for corrosive liquids |
| **Tin** | Soft, non-toxic, resists food acids | Coating steel (tin plate), collapsible tubes (older) |
| **Titanium** | Light, strong, excellent corrosion resistance (chlorides) | Special corrosive duties; costly |

## 5.5 Inorganic non-metals

| Material | Properties | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Glass** (especially borosilicate) | Transparent, inert to most chemicals (not HF and hot strong alkali), smooth, easily cleaned; brittle and poor at heat shock (borosilicate is better) | Laboratory apparatus, pipelines in pilot plants, containers, sight glasses |
| **Glass-lined (enamelled) steel** | Steel vessel coated inside with a fused glass layer: strength of steel + inertness of glass; lining chips if struck | Reaction vessels for acids, storage tanks |
| **Ceramics, porcelain, stoneware** | Hard, resist acids, heat-resistant; brittle, heavy | Mortars, pestles, ball mill linings and balls, filter candles, acid storage |
| **Cement and concrete** | Cheap, strong in compression; attacked by acids | Floors, foundations, water tanks |

## 5.6 Organic non-metals

### Plastics

| Type | Behaviour on heating | Examples | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thermoplastics** | Soften on heating and harden on cooling repeatedly | Polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, polystyrene, PTFE (Teflon), nylon | Containers, tubing, pipes, filter media, gaskets (PTFE), linings |
| **Thermosetting plastics** | Set permanently on heating; do not soften again | Phenol-formaldehyde (Bakelite), epoxy resins, urea-formaldehyde | Closures, handles, coatings, laminates |

**Properties:** light, resist corrosion and many chemicals, cheap, easily moulded, poor heat conductors; but low strength, may soften with heat, may absorb drugs or leach additives (plasticisers), some are attacked by organic solvents. **PTFE** is very inert and stands high temperature; it is used for gaskets, seals, valve linings and coatings.

### Rubber

**Natural rubber** is elastic and resists dilute acids and alkalis but swells in oils and solvents and ages in air. **Synthetic rubbers** (neoprene, butyl, nitrile, silicone) resist oils, heat and ageing better. **Hard rubber (ebonite)** is used for linings.

**Uses:** linings of tanks and pipes for acids, gaskets, tubing, stoppers and closures, conveyor belts.

### Wood

Cheap and easy to work, but porous, absorbs liquids, cannot be sterilised and harbours microbes. Used only for tanks for some non-sterile liquids (old practice), pallets and building parts, not for product contact under GMP.

## 5.7 Corrosion

**Corrosion** is the gradual destruction of a metal by chemical or electrochemical reaction with its environment. Rusting of iron is the familiar example.

**Effects in a pharmaceutical plant:** contamination of products with metal ions, loss of strength and leaks, rough surfaces that are hard to clean, shutdowns and replacement cost.

### Theories of corrosion

**1. Electrochemical (wet) theory** — the main theory. When a metal is in contact with water or an electrolyte, small **galvanic cells** form on its surface between areas that differ (different metals, impurities, strain, oxygen concentration).

- At the **anode**, metal dissolves (is oxidised): Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
- Electrons flow through the metal to the **cathode**, where they are used up:
  - in neutral or alkaline water with oxygen: O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (**oxygen absorption**)
  - in acid: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H2 (**hydrogen evolution**)
- Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ combine: Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)2, which is further oxidised to hydrated ferric oxide, Fe2O3·xH2O (**rust**).

```text
         water drop (electrolyte, O2 dissolved)
        ┌─────────────────────────────┐
        │   O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻    │ ← cathode (edge, more O2)
        │        Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻      │ ← anode (centre, less O2)
 ═══════╧═════════ iron ══════════════╧════════
              e⁻ flow through the metal →
```

**2. Chemical (dry) theory** — direct reaction of the metal with dry gases (oxygen, chlorine, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide) at high temperature, forming a film of oxide or other compound. If the film is porous (iron) corrosion continues; if it is dense and adherent (aluminium, chromium), it protects the metal.

**3. Acid theory** — older explanation of rusting: carbon dioxide and water form carbonic acid, which attacks iron to form iron bicarbonate, later oxidised to rust. It explains why iron rusts faster in acidic surroundings.

### Galvanic series

Metals arranged in order of their tendency to corrode in a given environment (e.g. sea water). When two different metals touch in an electrolyte, the **more active (anodic) metal corrodes** and protects the more noble one.

```text
 More active (anodic, corrodes)
   magnesium → zinc → aluminium → iron/steel → lead → tin
   → brass → copper → nickel → stainless steel (passive)
   → silver → titanium → gold → platinum
 More noble (cathodic, protected)
```

### Types of corrosion

| Type | What happens | Example / remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Uniform (general)** | Even thinning of the whole surface | Rusting of a bare steel tank; use coatings, allowance for thickness |
| **Galvanic (bimetallic)** | Two different metals in contact in an electrolyte; the more active one corrodes | Steel bolt in copper plate; avoid dissimilar metals or insulate them |
| **Pitting** | Small deep holes at weak points of the protective film | Stainless steel with chloride solutions; use 316 grade |
| **Crevice (concentration cell)** | Attack inside narrow gaps (under gaskets, bolts, deposits) where oxygen is low | Avoid crevices, weld instead of bolting, keep surfaces clean |
| **Intergranular** | Attack along grain boundaries, often after welding (chromium carbide forms at boundaries) | Use low-carbon (L grade) stainless steel |
| **Stress corrosion cracking** | Cracks from the combined action of tensile stress and a corrosive medium | Stress-relieve after fabrication; choose resistant alloy |
| **Erosion corrosion** | Fast-moving fluid or suspended particles remove the protective film | Pump impellers, pipe bends; reduce velocity, harder materials |
| **Selective leaching (dezincification)** | One element of an alloy dissolves out (zinc from brass) | Use inhibited brass or bronze |
| **Fretting** | Wear and corrosion at surfaces rubbing slightly under load | Lubricate, avoid vibration |
| **Hydrogen embrittlement** | Hydrogen enters steel and makes it brittle | Avoid acid cleaning of high-strength steel |

### Prevention of corrosion

1. **Proper selection of material:** stainless steel, glass, plastics or rubber for corrosive service; avoid joining dissimilar metals.
2. **Proper design:** avoid crevices, sharp corners and pockets where liquid stays; allow drainage; weld instead of bolting; avoid stress; give extra thickness (corrosion allowance).
3. **Protective coatings:**
   - **Metallic:** *galvanising* (coating iron with zinc, which corrodes first and protects iron), *tinning* (coating with tin), *electroplating* (chromium, nickel), *cladding* (bonding a thin corrosion-resistant metal sheet to a cheap base metal).
   - **Non-metallic:** paints, varnishes, lacquers, enamels, glass lining, rubber and plastic linings, epoxy coatings.
   - **Chemical conversion coatings:** phosphating, chromate coating, anodising of aluminium (thickening its oxide film).
4. **Cathodic protection:** make the metal to be protected the cathode.
   - **Sacrificial anode method:** a more active metal (zinc, magnesium) is connected to the steel structure; it corrodes instead (used for buried pipes, tanks).
   - **Impressed current method:** a direct current from an external source is applied, with an inert anode, so the structure becomes the cathode.
5. **Corrosion inhibitors:** chemicals added in small amounts to the liquid. *Anodic inhibitors* (chromates, phosphates) form a film at the anode; *cathodic inhibitors* (salts of zinc, arsenic compounds) slow the cathodic reaction; *adsorption inhibitors* (amines) cover the surface.
6. **Modifying the environment:** remove oxygen from water (deaeration), lower humidity (dehumidification), neutralise acids, control temperature.
7. **Passivation:** treating stainless steel with nitric acid (or citric acid) to strengthen its chromium oxide film after fabrication or cleaning.

## 5.8 Basics of material handling systems

**Material handling** is the movement, storage, protection and control of materials throughout manufacturing, from receiving raw materials to dispatching finished goods.

### Objectives

1. Reduce cost and labour.
2. Reduce damage, spillage and contamination of materials (cross-contamination control is a GMP need).
3. Increase speed and productivity; reduce waiting time.
4. Improve safety and working conditions (less lifting, less dust exposure).
5. Make the best use of floor space and storage.

### Principles

- Move materials the **shortest distance**, in a straight line, as few times as possible.
- Use **gravity** wherever possible (chutes, gravity-fed hoppers).
- Use **unit loads** (pallets, drums, IBCs) instead of small packets.
- Use **mechanical and automatic** handling to cut labour.
- Choose **closed systems** for powders and potent drugs to avoid dust and cross-contamination.
- Standardise containers and equipment; plan layout to avoid backtracking.

### Equipment for solids

| Equipment | Description | Pharmaceutical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Belt conveyor** | Endless belt (rubber, PVC, fabric) over rollers | Moving cartons, bags, containers along packing lines |
| **Screw conveyor** | Helical screw turning in a trough or tube | Powders and granules over short distances, feeding hoppers |
| **Bucket elevator** | Buckets on a chain or belt moving vertically | Lifting granules and powders to a height |
| **Pneumatic conveyor** | Powder carried in a stream of air through closed pipes (vacuum or pressure) | Dust-free transfer of powders to blenders and tablet presses |
| **Chutes and gravity feed** | Inclined or vertical pipes | Feeding powders from upper floor to lower (gravity plant layout) |
| **Roller conveyor** | Free or driven rollers | Moving boxes and drums |
| **Vibratory conveyor** | Vibrating trough | Gentle feeding of tablets and capsules |
| **Hand trucks, trolleys, forklifts, pallet trucks** | Mobile equipment | Moving drums, pallets in stores |
| **Cranes and hoists** | Lift heavy loads | Lifting bins, heavy machine parts; IBC lifting |
| **Intermediate bulk containers (IBCs)** | Closed bins moved between stations | Blending in the bin and feeding directly to compression |

### Equipment for liquids and gases

- **Pipes and fittings:** stainless steel sanitary piping with smooth welds and clamps; valves (gate, globe, ball, diaphragm, butterfly) to control flow.
- **Pumps:**
  - *Centrifugal pumps:* an impeller throws liquid outward; common for thin liquids such as water and solvents.
  - *Reciprocating pumps* (piston, plunger, diaphragm): give high pressure and accurate metering.
  - *Rotary pumps* (gear, lobe, screw): good for viscous liquids such as syrups and creams.
  - *Peristaltic pumps:* liquid squeezed through flexible tubing; no contact with moving parts, so used for sterile filling and accurate dosing.
- **Fans, blowers and compressors:** move air and gases (dryers, pneumatic conveyors, compressed air for machines).

## Remember

- Selection of material: chemical (product contamination, corrosion), physical (strength, heat, cleaning, fabrication), economic.
- Stainless steel: at least about 11–12% Cr; 304 = 18/8; 316 adds molybdenum for chloride resistance; L grades for welding.
- Glass-lined steel = strength of steel + inertness of glass. PTFE = most inert plastic.
- Electrochemical theory: anode Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (or 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H2).
- Types: uniform, galvanic, pitting, crevice, intergranular, stress cracking, erosion, dezincification.
- Prevention: material, design, coatings (galvanising, lining), cathodic protection (sacrificial zinc/magnesium or impressed current), inhibitors, environment control, passivation.
- Material handling: shortest path, gravity, unit loads, closed systems; screw and pneumatic conveyors for powders; peristaltic pumps for sterile liquids.
