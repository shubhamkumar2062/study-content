# Unit 4: Filtration and centrifugation

Both operations separate solids from liquids (or one liquid from another). **Filtration** pushes the liquid through a porous medium that holds the solids back. **Centrifugation** spins the mixture so that the heavier part is thrown outward. Exam questions here are mostly "describe the principle, construction and working of …", so learn the diagram of each machine.

## 4.1 Filtration

**Filtration** is the separation of solid particles from a fluid (liquid or gas) by passing the fluid through a porous medium that retains the solids.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Slurry | The suspension fed to the filter |
| Filter medium | The porous barrier (cloth, paper, membrane) |
| Filter cake | The layer of solids built up on the medium |
| Filtrate | The clear liquid that passes through |
| Clarification | Filtration where solids are below about 1% and the liquid is the product |
| Cake filtration | Filtration where a large amount of solid is collected (solid may be the product) |

### Objectives and applications

1. **Clarification** of liquids: syrups, elixirs, tinctures, oils, mouthwashes.
2. **Sterile filtration** of heat-sensitive injections, eye drops and culture media (0.22 µm membranes).
3. **Collecting solids**: crystals, precipitates (e.g. in production of antibiotics and chemicals).
4. **Removing particles** from water for injection, solvents and air (HEPA filters in aseptic areas).
5. **Separating mycelia** from fermentation broth (antibiotic production).
6. **Quality control**: particulate matter in injections; microbial limit tests.

### Mechanisms of filtration

1. **Straining (sieving):** particles larger than the pores are held back on the surface (membrane filters).
2. **Impingement:** particles in the moving stream hit the fibres of the medium and stick.
3. **Entanglement:** particles get trapped in the tortuous path of a fibrous medium.
4. **Attractive forces:** electrostatic and van der Waals forces hold particles on the medium.

Surface filters work mainly by straining; **depth filters** (fibrous pads, sintered glass) trap particles inside their thickness.

### Theories of filtration

The filtrate flows through the fine channels between particles of the cake and medium. The flow is laminar, so the laws of flow through capillaries apply.

**Poiseuille's equation** (flow through a capillary):

V/t = π r⁴ ΔP / (8 η L)

where V/t is the rate of flow, r the radius of the capillary, ΔP the pressure difference, η the viscosity and L the length of the capillary.

**Darcy's equation** (flow through a porous bed):

**V/t = K A ΔP / (η L)**

where K is the **permeability** of the bed, A the area and L the thickness of the cake and medium.

**Kozeny–Carman equation** relates the rate to the structure of the bed:

V/t = (A ΔP / η S² L) × ε³ / [K (1 − ε)²]

where ε is the **porosity** of the cake, S the specific surface of the particles and K the Kozeny constant (about 5). It shows that small particles (large S) and low porosity sharply reduce the rate.

### Factors influencing filtration (from Darcy's equation)

| Factor | Effect on rate | Practical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Area of filter (A) | Rate ∝ area | Use pleated cartridges, many leaves or plates |
| Pressure difference (ΔP) | Rate ∝ ΔP (for incompressible cakes) | Apply pressure or vacuum; too much compresses soft cakes |
| Viscosity of filtrate (η) | Rate ∝ 1/η | Warm the liquid (oils, syrups) to lower viscosity |
| Thickness of cake (L) | Rate ∝ 1/L | Remove cake regularly; continuous filters keep it thin |
| Particle size and porosity | Fine particles and low porosity slow filtration | Use filter aids; flocculate the slurry |
| Compressibility of cake | Soft cakes close their pores under pressure | Use filter aids, moderate pressure |
| Temperature | Raises rate by lowering viscosity | Avoid for volatile or thermolabile liquids |

### Filter media

The medium must hold back solids, let liquid pass easily, be strong, chemically inert, not shed fibres, easy to clean or cheap enough to discard, and suitable for sterilisation where needed.

| Medium | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Woven fabrics | Cotton, wool, silk, nylon, polyester, polypropylene | Filter presses; nylon and polyester resist chemicals and microbes |
| Paper | Filter paper of different grades | Laboratory and small scale |
| Non-woven fabrics | Felts, pads of fibres | Depth filtration |
| Wire mesh | Stainless steel, Monel | Strong, for coarse particles, easy to clean |
| Sintered and porous solids | Sintered glass, sintered metal, porcelain, kieselguhr candles | Rigid, can be sterilised |
| Membranes | Cellulose esters, nylon, PTFE, PVDF, polycarbonate | Precise pore size; sterile filtration |
| Asbestos pads | Seitz filter (old) | Sheds fibres; now replaced |

### Filter aids

**Filter aids** are inert, porous, incompressible powders added to a slurry that would otherwise form a slimy, compressible cake that blocks the medium. They form a **porous, rigid cake** that keeps the channels open and traps fine particles.

**Examples:** diatomaceous earth (kieselguhr), perlite, cellulose fibres, asbestos (old), talc, charcoal, bentonite.

**Methods of use:**

- **Pre-coat:** a thin layer of filter aid is first filtered onto the medium to protect it and give a clear filtrate from the start.
- **Body mix (admix):** filter aid is mixed into the slurry so that it becomes part of the cake and keeps it porous.

**Properties of a good filter aid:** chemically inert, insoluble, low density, porous and incompressible, free of impurities, and able to give a clear filtrate. A demerit is that it may adsorb the drug and it is lost if the solid is the product.

### Plate and frame filter press

**Principle:** filtration under **pressure** through filter cloths; the solids collect as a cake in the hollow frames.

**Construction:** alternate **plates and frames** hung on side rails and pressed together by a screw or hydraulic ram.

- **Plate:** solid square plate with a **ridged or grooved surface** on both sides, covered with filter cloth; outlet channel at the bottom for the filtrate.
- **Frame:** hollow square frame that provides space for the cake; has an inlet channel for the slurry.
- Holes in the corners of plates and frames line up to form continuous channels for slurry, filtrate and wash water.

```text
   slurry in ═══╦═══════╦═══════╦═══════
                ▼       ▼       ▼
  ┌──┐┃┌──────┐┃┌──────┐┃┌──────┐┃┌──┐
  │P ││││frame │││frame │││frame │││P │   P = plates (grooved)
  │L ││││ cake │││ cake │││ cake │││L │   ┃ = filter cloth
  │A ││││      │││      │││      │││A │
  └┬─┘┃└──────┘┃└──────┘┃└──────┘┃└┬─┘
   ▼  filtrate runs down plate grooves  ▼ → filtrate out
```

**Working:**

1. The press is closed tightly with cloths between plates and frames.
2. Slurry is pumped in through the slurry channel and enters every frame.
3. Filtrate passes through the cloths, runs down the grooves of the plates and leaves through the outlets.
4. Solids build up in the frames until they are full (filtrate flow slows).
5. **Washing:** wash water can be passed through the cake (in a washing press, through special washing plates so it crosses the whole cake).
6. The press is opened, the cakes removed, cloths cleaned, and the press reassembled.

**Uses:** collecting precipitates and crystals; clarifying large volumes of liquids; production of sterile filtrate when used with sterilising pads (in older practice).

**Merits:** large filtering area in a small floor space; flexible (plates can be added or removed); simple; can be used with high pressure; easy washing of the cake; filtered product hardly exposed to air.

**Demerits:** batch process; labour-intensive and slow to dismantle; filter cloths wear quickly; may leak at joints; not economical for small amounts of solid.

### Filter leaf

**Principle:** a single flat filter unit (leaf) is placed in the slurry; **vacuum** applied inside (or pressure outside) draws liquid through the cloth.

**Construction:** a frame enclosing a **drainage screen** (coarse wire mesh or grooved plate), covered on both faces with filter cloth. An outlet pipe connects the inside of the leaf to a vacuum pump. Several leaves can be mounted together in a closed vessel to give a larger area (leaf filter).

```text
          to vacuum ↑
               │
     ┌─────────┴─────────┐
     │ ┃▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒┃ │ ← cloth both sides,
     │ ┃▒ drainage  ▒▒┃ │   mesh inside
     │ ┃▒  screen   ▒▒┃ │
     └───────────────────┘
       immersed in slurry
```

**Working:** the leaf is dipped into the slurry and vacuum applied; filtrate passes through the cloth into the drainage screen and out; solids form a cake on the outside of the leaf. The leaf may then be transferred to wash water and back-blown with air to drop the cake.

**Uses:** small and medium-scale filtration; slurries with low solid content.

**Merits:** simple and cheap; cake can be washed in place; little labour; the slurry can be filtered in its own vessel. **Demerits:** batch process; only moderate pressure difference with vacuum; cake may fall off if vacuum fails.

### Rotary drum filter

**Principle:** a **continuous vacuum filter**. A rotating drum covered with cloth dips into slurry; vacuum inside pulls liquid through, leaving a cake on the drum, which is then washed, dried and removed in one revolution.

**Construction:** a hollow metal **drum** (1–3 m diameter, perforated surface covered with filter cloth), divided internally into **sectors**. Each sector is connected through a **rotary valve** to vacuum or compressed air. The drum dips partly into a **slurry trough** with an agitator. Sprays for wash water and a **doctor knife** (or strings, or a pre-coat cutting blade) for cake removal.

```text
            wash sprays
           ↓    ↓
         ╱‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾╲    drying zone
        │  ← drum   │ ╲
        │   rotates │  ╲ doctor knife → cake out
         ╲_________╱
     ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  slurry trough (pick-up zone)
        vacuum inside via rotary valve
```

**Working (zones in one revolution):**

1. **Pick-up (filtration) zone:** sector under the slurry; vacuum draws filtrate through; cake forms.
2. **Drainage zone:** sector leaves the slurry; remaining liquid is drawn out.
3. **Washing zone:** wash water sprayed onto the cake and drawn through.
4. **Drying zone:** air is sucked through to dry the cake.
5. **Cake removal (discharge) zone:** vacuum cut off and a little compressed air blown through to loosen the cake; the knife scrapes it off.

**Uses:** large-scale continuous filtration of slurries with high solid content: separating mycelia from antibiotic fermentation broth, collecting calcium carbonate, magnesium carbonate and other precipitates.

**Merits:** continuous and automatic; large capacity; labour saving; cake thickness and washing easily controlled; pre-coat version handles fine slimy solids.

**Demerits:** expensive and complex; only vacuum, so pressure difference is limited to less than one atmosphere; not for hot or volatile liquids (they boil under vacuum); cake may crack and wash poorly; not for small batches.

### Meta filter (edge filter)

**Principle:** filtration through narrow channels formed between **stacked metal rings**. Usually a bed of filter aid (kieselguhr) builds up on the stack and does the actual fine filtration.

**Construction:** a grooved (fluted) rod carries a pack of thin **stainless steel rings**. Each ring has semicircular projections on one face, so when stacked they leave very narrow gaps (channels) between them. The pack is mounted inside a closed vessel. Liquid flows from outside the pack, through the gaps, into the grooves of the rod and out at the top.

```text
   filtrate out ↑
        ┌──┴──┐
        │ ▬▬▬ │  ← stacked metal rings
        │ ▬▬▬ │     (narrow gaps between them)
        │ ▬▬▬ │  ← fluted rod inside
        │ ▬▬▬ │
        └─────┘  in a vessel filled with slurry
```

**Working:** slurry (with kieselguhr if needed) is pumped into the vessel under pressure; a layer of filter aid forms on the rings; clear liquid passes through the channels and leaves through the rod. The pack is cleaned by **back-flushing** (reversing the flow).

**Uses:** clarification of syrups, elixirs, injections and oils; filtering liquids with small amounts of solid.

**Merits:** strong and long-lasting; no cloth to replace; stainless steel can be sterilised and resists corrosion; low running cost; easy cleaning by back-flushing; can be used at high pressure.

**Demerits:** mainly for clarification (low solid content); needs filter aid for fine particles; initial cost high.

### Cartridge filter

**Principle:** filtration through a **replaceable cylindrical cartridge** (surface or depth type) held in a closed housing under pressure.

**Construction:** a stainless steel **housing** with inlet and outlet; inside, one or more cartridges. A cartridge is a cylinder of pleated membrane or paper (large area in small space), or a depth element of wound fibres or porous polymer, around a perforated core, with end caps and O-ring seals.

**Working:** liquid enters the housing, passes from the outside of the cartridge to its core and leaves through the outlet; particles are held on or in the cartridge. When the pressure drop rises, the cartridge is replaced.

**Uses:** prefiltration and sterile filtration of parenteral solutions, ophthalmic products, water for injection, air and gas filtration (vent filters).

**Merits:** large area in small volume; quick replacement; no cleaning; low contamination; available in many pore sizes and materials; sterilisable. **Demerits:** disposable cartridges are costly; clog with heavily loaded liquids (need prefilters); not for collecting solids.

### Membrane filters

**Principle:** **sieving (screen) filtration** through thin polymer films with uniform, very small pores (about 0.1–10 µm). A 0.22 µm (or 0.2 µm) membrane retains bacteria, so it is used for sterilisation.

**Construction:** a thin membrane (about 0.1 mm thick) of cellulose acetate, cellulose nitrate, mixed esters, nylon, PTFE or PVDF, about 80% porous. It is held on a support screen in a **filter holder**: a small syringe holder (Swinnex type), an in-line disc holder, or a pleated cartridge for large volumes.

**Working:** liquid is forced through under positive pressure (or vacuum); particles and microorganisms larger than the pores remain on the surface. A **prefilter** (glass-fibre depth layer) is often used in front to prevent clogging.

**Integrity testing:** the **bubble point test** checks a wetted membrane: gas pressure is raised until bubbles pass through the largest pore. A bubble point below the specified value means a damaged or wrong membrane. Other tests: diffusion test, bacterial challenge test.

**Uses:** sterilisation of heat-labile injections, eye drops, antibiotic solutions, culture media; particle counting; microbiological testing (membrane filtration method for sterility testing and microbial counts).

**Merits:** precise pore size; no shedding of fibres; low adsorption of drug; can be sterilised by autoclave; disposable, so no cross-contamination; fast filtration due to high porosity.

**Demerits:** clog easily (need prefilters); fragile; some membranes are attacked by certain solvents; costly; small particle-holding capacity.

### Seitz filter

**Principle:** **depth filtration** through a compressed **asbestos (and cellulose) pad** under pressure or vacuum; bacteria are trapped within the pad. Historically used for sterile filtration.

**Construction:** two metal parts: an upper **cylinder** (funnel) and a lower **base** with a perforated metal support, between which the asbestos pad is clamped by screws or wing nuts. The outlet leads to a sterile receiving flask under vacuum.

```text
        liquid
     ┌────────┐  ← upper metal cylinder
     │        │
  ═══╧════════╧═══  ← asbestos pad (clamped)
     │▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓│  ← perforated support
      ╲      ╱
        ║  → to sterile flask (vacuum)
```

**Working:** the assembly with a fresh pad is sterilised; the liquid is poured into the upper part and drawn through the pad by vacuum (or pushed by pressure); the sterile filtrate collects below. The pad is discarded after use.

**Uses:** sterile filtration of heat-sensitive solutions in older practice; clarification.

**Merits:** high dirt-holding capacity; cheap pads; simple.

**Demerits:** asbestos fibres may shed into the product and are a health hazard; pads adsorb drug (loss of potent drugs); may release alkali, changing pH; pores not uniform. For these reasons Seitz asbestos filters have been replaced by membrane filters.

## 4.2 Centrifugation

**Centrifugation** is the separation of particles or immiscible liquids by **centrifugal force** produced by rotating the mixture at high speed. It works much faster than settling under gravity.

### Principle

A body rotating in a circle of radius r with angular velocity ω experiences a centrifugal force:

**F = m ω² r**

The **relative centrifugal force (RCF)** compares it with gravity:

RCF = ω² r / g ≈ 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × N²

where r is in cm and N is the speed in revolutions per minute. A centrifuge giving 10,000 × g makes particles settle 10,000 times faster than under gravity.

The settling velocity in a centrifuge follows Stokes' law with ω²r in place of g:

v = d² (ρs − ρl) ω² r / 18 η

So separation is faster for larger particles, a greater density difference, higher speed and radius, and lower viscosity.

### Objectives and applications

1. Separating crystals from mother liquor (e.g. in drug manufacture).
2. Clarifying liquids: removing fine solids from syrups, oils and extracts.
3. Separating immiscible liquids and breaking emulsions (e.g. cream from milk).
4. Separating cells, bacteria and mycelia from fermentation broth; collecting insulin and enzyme precipitates.
5. Separating blood into plasma and cells; fractionation of plasma proteins.
6. Evaluating the stability of emulsions and suspensions (accelerated creaming test).
7. Particle size analysis by centrifugal sedimentation; separating viruses, proteins, nucleic acids (ultracentrifuge).

### Classification

| Type | Basis | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filtration (perforated) | Liquid passes through a perforated wall with cloth | Perforated basket centrifuge |
| Sedimentation (solid wall) | Heavier phase settles on the wall | Non-perforated basket, tubular (super) centrifuge |
| By operation | Batch, semi-continuous, continuous | Basket (batch), knife-discharge (semi-continuous) |

### Perforated basket centrifuge (basket centrifuge)

**Principle:** **centrifugal filtration**. Centrifugal force drives the liquid through a filter cloth lining a perforated basket; the solids remain as a cake.

**Construction:** a **cylindrical basket** of stainless steel with **perforated walls**, lined with a filter cloth or screen, mounted on a vertical shaft driven by a motor (top or bottom drive). It rotates inside a stationary **casing** that collects the liquid and has an outlet.

```text
         feed ↓
   ┌─────────────────────┐ ← outer casing
   │ ┌─┬─────────────┬─┐ │
   │ │:│   cake  │   │:│ │ ← perforated basket + cloth
   │ │:│         │   │:│ │
   │ │:│      shaft  │:│ │
   │ └─┴─────────────┴─┘ │
   └──────────┬──────────┘
              ▼ filtrate out
```

**Working:** the basket is started; slurry is fed in; the liquid is thrown outward through the cloth and perforations into the casing and out; solids build up as a cake on the cloth. The cake can be washed by spraying, then spun dry. The machine is stopped and the cake removed by hand or a scraper.

**Uses:** separating crystals from mother liquor (e.g. drugs, sugar, salts); removing moisture from crystals before drying; separating fibrous material.

**Merits:** compact; quick; cake is washed and spun nearly dry, which shortens drying; gives a drier cake than most filters.

**Demerits:** batch process; cake removal is laborious; heavy machine with vibration if unbalanced; not suitable for very fine particles that block the cloth.

### Non-perforated basket centrifuge

**Principle:** **centrifugal sedimentation**. The basket wall is solid; solids are thrown to the wall and settle there, while the clear liquid forms an inner layer that is removed.

**Construction:** similar to the perforated basket, but with a **solid (imperforate) wall**. A **skimming tube** is used to draw off the clear liquid from the surface.

**Working:** slurry is fed into the rotating basket; solids form a layer on the wall; the clear liquid is removed by the skimming tube or by stopping and decanting; finally the solids are scraped out.

**Uses:** when solids are fine or soft and would block a filter cloth; clarifying liquids with small amounts of solid; separating precipitates.

**Merits:** no filter cloth to clog or wash; handles fine and slimy solids. **Demerits:** batch; the solid cake contains more liquid than a perforated basket cake; skimming needs skill.

### Semi-continuous centrifuge (knife-discharge or peeler centrifuge)

**Principle:** filtration or sedimentation in a basket that keeps **running at full speed** while the steps of feeding, washing, drying and cake removal follow one another automatically.

**Construction:** a basket (usually perforated) on a **horizontal shaft**; feed pipe; wash pipe; a hydraulically operated **knife or plough** that moves into the cake to cut it out and drop it into a chute.

**Working:** the basket runs at constant speed; slurry is fed until the cake reaches set thickness; feed stops; cake is washed and spun; the knife moves in and peels the cake off into the chute; the cycle repeats automatically.

**Uses:** large-scale separation of crystalline products, starch, antibiotics.

**Merits:** high output; automatic, little labour; no time lost stopping and starting the basket. **Demerits:** the knife breaks crystals; a thin layer (heel) of cake is left on the cloth; expensive.

### Super centrifuge (tubular bowl centrifuge)

**Principle:** a **long, narrow bowl** rotating at very high speed (often above 15,000 rpm) gives a very large centrifugal force. Liquids of different density separate into layers, the heavier one outermost; fine solids deposit on the wall.

**Construction:** a long **tubular bowl** of small diameter (about 10–15 cm), suspended from a drive at the top and steadied at the bottom. Feed enters at the **bottom** through a nozzle. At the top there are **two outlets** at different radii: one for the heavy liquid (outer) and one for the light liquid (inner). A dam ring sets the boundary between the layers.

```text
   light liquid out ←┐ ┌→ heavy liquid out
               ┌─────┴─┴─────┐
               │ │ light │ H │ │ ← tubular bowl spins
               │ │ phase │ e │ │   at very high speed
               │ │       │ a │ │
               │ │       │ v │ │
               └─────┬───────┘
                     ↑ feed (bottom)
```

**Working:** feed enters at the bottom and is spun up to bowl speed; as it travels up the bowl the heavy liquid moves to the wall and the light liquid stays near the centre; at the top they leave separately. Fine solids stay on the wall and are removed after stopping.

**Uses:** separating immiscible liquids (oil and water), breaking emulsions, clarifying oils and syrups, separating blood plasma, collecting bacteria and fine precipitates, separating cream from milk.

**Merits:** very high separating power; small size; continuous for liquid separation; can separate very fine particles.

**Demerits:** small capacity; solids must be removed by stopping and opening the bowl; expensive and needs careful balancing and maintenance.

## Remember

- Darcy: V/t = K A ΔP / (η L). Rate rises with area and pressure; falls with viscosity and cake thickness.
- Kozeny–Carman adds porosity (ε³/(1 − ε)²) and specific surface.
- Filter aids (kieselguhr, perlite): pre-coat or body mix; keep the cake porous.
- Plate and frame = pressure, batch, large area. Rotary drum = vacuum, continuous, 5 zones.
- Meta filter = stacked steel rings + kieselguhr, back-flush. Membrane 0.22 µm = sterile filtration; bubble point test.
- Seitz = asbestos pad; replaced because of fibre shedding and drug adsorption.
- F = mω²r; RCF ≈ 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ r N² (r in cm, N in rpm).
- Perforated basket = filtration; non-perforated = sedimentation; peeler = semi-continuous; super centrifuge = tubular, liquid–liquid.
