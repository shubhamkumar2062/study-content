# Sterility testing of a pharmaceutical product

**Aim:** To test a given parenteral or ophthalmic product for sterility by the membrane filtration and direct inoculation methods.

> Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where details differ. The test must be carried out under strict aseptic conditions, or a false positive result will follow.

## Principle

A sterility test shows whether viable organisms can be recovered from a product when it is incubated in media that support a wide range of organisms. **Fluid thioglycollate medium** (incubated at 30–35 °C) supports anaerobes and aerobes; **soyabean-casein digest medium** (incubated at 20–25 °C) supports aerobes, yeasts and moulds. Incubation runs for **not less than 14 days**. Because only a sample is tested, a pass supports but does not prove the sterility of the whole batch; it is one part of a validated process.

## Requirements

Test product (ampoules, vials or eye drops), fluid thioglycollate medium, soyabean-casein digest medium, sterile membrane filtration assembly with **0.45 µm** membranes, sterile rinsing fluid, sterile syringes and needles, forceps, 70% alcohol swabs, laminar air flow unit or isolator, incubators at 30–35 °C and 20–25 °C.

## Procedure

**A. Preparation**

1. Confirm by the growth promotion test that each batch of medium supports growth of the specified organisms, and that the media were sterile on incubation.
2. Disinfect the laminar air flow unit and all surfaces; wear sterile gloves and gown.
3. Disinfect the neck of each ampoule or the closure of each vial with 70% alcohol and let it dry.

**B. Membrane filtration method (preferred)**

4. Assemble the sterile filtration unit with a 0.45 µm membrane and wet it with sterile rinsing fluid.
5. Withdraw the contents of the required number of containers aseptically and transfer them to the funnel; apply suction so the liquid passes through, leaving any organisms on the membrane.
6. Rinse the membrane with sterile rinsing fluid (usually three rinses) to wash out any antibiotic or preservative that would stop growth.
7. Cut the membrane aseptically into two halves (or use two units): place one half in fluid thioglycollate medium and the other in soyabean-casein digest medium.

**C. Direct inoculation method**

8. For small volumes or products that cannot be filtered, transfer a measured volume of the product directly into each medium, keeping the volume of product small compared with the medium so that any antimicrobial substance is diluted out.

**D. Controls and incubation**

9. Keep uninoculated containers of each medium as **negative controls**.
10. Incubate thioglycollate tubes at **30–35 °C** and soyabean-casein digest tubes at **20–25 °C** for **14 days**.
11. Examine on alternate days and on the fourteenth day for turbidity or any sign of growth.

## Precautions

1. Work only in a laminar air flow unit or isolator with full aseptic technique; the commonest cause of a positive result in a student laboratory is contamination during the test.
2. Rinse the membrane thoroughly when the product contains an antibiotic or a preservative.
3. Handle positive controls separately and after the test samples.
4. Do not shake the tubes during incubation, as this disturbs the anaerobic layer in thioglycollate medium.
5. Record observations without opening the containers.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is 0.45 µm used instead of 0.22 µm here?** The membrane only has to retain organisms for culture, and the larger pore size filters viscous products more easily; 0.22 µm is the size used when a filter is relied on to sterilise a product.
2. **Why two different media and temperatures?** Thioglycollate at 30–35 °C recovers anaerobes and aerobic bacteria, while soyabean-casein digest at 20–25 °C recovers fungi and yeasts as well as aerobes.
3. **What is the growth promotion test?** A check that the medium can support small numbers of specified organisms, proving a negative result is meaningful.
4. **What is done if growth appears?** The result is investigated; the test may be repeated only if the growth can be shown to have come from the testing itself rather than the product.
5. **Why can a sterility test never prove a whole batch is sterile?** Only a small sample is tested and the test is destructive; sterility assurance comes from validation of the process.
