# Simple staining of bacteria

**Aim:** To prepare a bacterial smear and stain it with a single basic dye to study the shape, size and arrangement of the cells.

> Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where details differ. Stains mark skin and clothes; handle them over the sink on a staining rack.

## Principle

The bacterial cell surface carries a **negative charge**, so it attracts the coloured positive ion of a **basic dye** such as methylene blue, crystal violet or carbol fuchsin. One dye is used, so every cell takes the same colour; the stain therefore shows only **morphology** — shape, size and arrangement — and not any difference between organisms.

## Requirements

Young (18–24 hour) broth or slant culture, clean grease-free slides, inoculating loop, burner, staining rack, methylene blue (or crystal violet or carbol fuchsin), wash bottle of distilled water, blotting paper, immersion oil, microscope.

## Procedure

1. **Clean the slide** with alcohol and wipe dry; grease stops the smear from spreading.
2. **Make the smear.** For a slant culture, place a small drop of saline or water on the slide, add a little growth with a sterile loop and spread it into a thin film about the size of a coin. For a broth culture, spread one or two loopfuls directly.
3. **Air dry** the smear completely at room temperature; do not heat it while wet.
4. **Heat fix** by passing the slide, smear upwards, through the flame two or three times. The slide should feel warm, not hot. This kills the cells, makes them stick to the glass and helps them take the stain.
5. **Stain:** flood the smear with methylene blue for about 1 minute (crystal violet needs about 30–60 seconds, carbol fuchsin about 30 seconds).
6. **Wash** gently with a thin stream of water until no more colour runs off.
7. **Blot dry** between blotting paper; do not rub.
8. Examine under low power, then under the **oil immersion objective (100x)** with a drop of immersion oil.

## Observations

| Feature | What to look for | Observed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Cocci (round), bacilli (rods), vibrio (comma), spirilla (spiral) | |
| Arrangement | Single, pairs, chains, clusters, tetrads | |
| Size | Compared with the field, roughly 0.5–5 µm | |
| Colour | Uniform colour of the dye used | |

Typical results: *Staphylococcus aureus* appears as blue cocci in grape-like clusters; *Escherichia coli* as blue short rods, single or in pairs; *Bacillus subtilis* as long rods, sometimes in chains.

## Precautions

1. Make the smear thin; a thick smear makes the cells pile up and look distorted.
2. Dry the smear fully before fixing, or the cells will burst.
3. Do not over-heat during fixing; excess heat distorts the cells.
4. Wash gently, directing the water above the smear, so the film is not washed away.
5. Use a young culture; old cells stain unevenly and may look misshapen.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is a basic dye used?** The bacterial surface is negatively charged and binds the positively charged coloured part of a basic dye.
2. **What is the purpose of heat fixing?** It kills the organisms, sticks them to the slide and improves staining.
3. **What does a simple stain not tell you?** It cannot distinguish one organism from another, since every cell takes the same colour; it shows only shape and arrangement.
4. **What is negative staining?** An acidic dye such as nigrosin or India ink stains the background instead of the cell, so the unstained cell and its capsule stand out.
5. **Why is immersion oil used?** It has nearly the same refractive index as glass, so less light is lost by refraction and the resolving power of the 100x objective is realised.
