# Microbiological assay of an antibiotic by the cup-plate method

**Aim:** To determine the potency of a given antibiotic sample by the cup-plate (agar diffusion) method, comparing it with a standard preparation.

> Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where details differ.

## Principle

An antibiotic placed in a cup cut in seeded agar diffuses outwards, and its concentration falls as it spreads. Growth of the test organism is prevented up to the distance where the concentration is still above the inhibitory level, giving a clear **zone of inhibition**. Within a working range, the **diameter of the zone is proportional to the logarithm of the concentration**. By measuring zones for known concentrations of a **standard** and for the **test** solution under identical conditions, the potency of the test sample is calculated.

## Requirements

Standard antibiotic solution of known potency, test antibiotic sample, sterile nutrient agar (base and seed layers), culture of the specified test organism (for example *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Bacillus subtilis* as directed by the monograph), sterile Petri dishes, sterile cork borer (6–8 mm), sterile pipettes, phosphate buffer of the stated pH, laminar air flow unit, incubator, zone reader or vernier calliper.

## Procedure

1. Prepare the dilutions. From the standard, prepare two doses, for example S1 (low) and S2 (high), differing by a fixed ratio such as 1:2. Prepare the test sample at the same two expected levels, T1 and T2, in the same buffer.
2. Pour about 15 ml of molten nutrient agar into each Petri dish as a **base layer** and let it set on a level surface.
3. Cool a second portion of agar to about 45 °C, add the standardised inoculum of the test organism, mix without forming bubbles, and pour it over the base as a thin **seed layer**. Let it set.
4. With a sterile borer, cut four cups in each plate at equal distances from the centre and from each other, and remove the agar plugs with a sterile needle.
5. Fill the cups with equal volumes (for example 0.1 ml) of S1, S2, T1 and T2 in a **balanced arrangement**, so that each solution occupies each position equally often across the set of plates.
6. Keep the plates at room temperature (or 4 °C) for about an hour so the solutions diffuse before growth begins.
7. Incubate at 37 °C for 18–24 hours.
8. Measure the diameter of each zone of inhibition with a zone reader or calliper, taking two readings at right angles and averaging them.

**By standard curve:** plot mean zone diameter against log concentration for the standard; the points fall on a straight line. Read the log concentration of the test from its mean zone diameter and take the antilog.

**By the two-dose formula:**

log of potency ratio = ( (T1 + T2) − (S1 + S2) ) ÷ ( (S2 + T2) − (S1 + T1) ) × log of the dose ratio

Potency of the sample = antilog of the above × potency of the standard.

*Worked example:* S1 = 16.0 mm, S2 = 20.0 mm, T1 = 16.8 mm, T2 = 20.8 mm, dose ratio 2 (log 2 = 0.301).

- (T1 + T2) − (S1 + S2) = 37.6 − 36.0 = 1.6
- (S2 + T2) − (S1 + T1) = 40.8 − 32.8 = 8.0
- log ratio = (1.6 ÷ 8.0) × 0.301 = 0.0602; antilog = 1.15

The sample is about 1.15 times as potent as the assumed value, so if it was assumed to be 1000 units per ml, the found potency is about 1150 units per ml.

## Precautions

1. Keep the layer of seeded agar of the same thickness in every plate; thickness changes the zone size.
2. Use a standardised inoculum; a heavier inoculum gives smaller zones.
3. Cool the agar to about 45 °C before adding the inoculum.
4. Fill all cups to the same level, without spilling on the surface.
5. Allow a diffusion period before incubation, and keep temperature and time identical for all plates.
6. Use the balanced (rotated) arrangement of solutions to cancel out plate-to-plate differences.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is zone diameter related to the logarithm of concentration?** Diffusion from the cup gives a concentration gradient that falls steeply with distance, so a geometric increase in dose produces an arithmetic increase in zone diameter.
2. **Why is a standard preparation needed?** Biological activity is compared, not measured absolutely; the standard defines the unit of potency.
3. **What is the advantage of a microbiological assay over a chemical assay?** It measures the actual antimicrobial activity, including that of mixtures and of substances with no convenient chemical method.
4. **Name the alternative method and its advantage.** The turbidimetric method, which gives a result in 3–4 hours instead of 18–24 hours.
5. **What happens if the plates are not level?** The agar layer varies in thickness, so zone diameters vary and the assay is invalid.
