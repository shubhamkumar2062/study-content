# Gram staining

**Aim:** To stain a given bacterial culture by Gram's method and to classify it as Gram-positive or Gram-negative.

> Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where details differ. The decolourising step decides the result, so time it carefully.

## Principle

Crystal violet enters every cell and is fixed by iodine as a large **crystal violet–iodine complex**. Alcohol then dehydrates the thick peptidoglycan of a **Gram-positive** wall, closing its pores so the complex is trapped, and the cell stays purple. In a **Gram-negative** cell alcohol dissolves the lipid-rich outer membrane and the thin peptidoglycan cannot hold the complex, so the cell loses the colour and is then stained pink by safranin.

## Requirements

18–24 hour cultures (for example *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Escherichia coli*), clean slides, loop, burner, staining rack, crystal violet solution, Gram's iodine, 95% ethanol (or acetone–alcohol), safranin, wash bottle, blotting paper, immersion oil, microscope.

## Procedure

1. Prepare a thin smear, air dry it and heat fix it as for simple staining.
2. Flood with **crystal violet** and leave for **1 minute**. Wash briefly with water.
3. Flood with **Gram's iodine** (the mordant) and leave for **1 minute**. Wash briefly.
4. **Decolourise** by letting 95% ethanol run down the tilted slide, drop by drop, for only a few seconds, until no more violet colour comes away. Wash at once with water to stop the action.
5. Counterstain with **safranin** for 30–60 seconds. Wash with water.
6. Blot dry and examine under the oil immersion objective.

```text
  Crystal violet  →  Iodine  →  Alcohol  →  Safranin
      purple          purple     Gram +ve stays purple     purple
      purple          purple     Gram −ve goes colourless  pink
```

## Precautions

1. Use a young culture. Old Gram-positive cells lose the ability to hold the dye and may appear Gram-negative.
2. The smear must be thin; thick areas decolourise unevenly.
3. Do not over-decolourise. This is the commonest cause of a false Gram-negative result; stop as soon as the running alcohol is clear.
4. Wash immediately after the alcohol step.
5. Always run a known Gram-positive and a known Gram-negative control alongside an unknown.

## Viva questions

1. **Who developed this stain and when?** Christian Gram, in 1884.
2. **What is the role of iodine here?** It acts as a mordant, forming a large complex with crystal violet inside the cell so the dye is not easily washed out.
3. **Which step is critical and why?** Decolourising with alcohol, because the difference between the two groups depends entirely on how much colour is removed.
4. **Why does a Gram-positive cell retain the colour?** Its thick multilayered peptidoglycan with teichoic acid dehydrates and traps the complex; the Gram-negative wall is thin and its outer lipid membrane is dissolved away.
5. **Name two organisms of each type.** Gram-positive: *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Bacillus subtilis*. Gram-negative: *Escherichia coli*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
6. **Why does the Gram reaction matter clinically?** It guides the first choice of antibiotic before the full report is available.
