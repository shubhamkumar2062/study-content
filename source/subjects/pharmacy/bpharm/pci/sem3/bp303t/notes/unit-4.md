# Unit 4: Aseptic areas, clean rooms and microbiological assays

Sterile products are only as good as the room and the people who make them. The first half of this unit is about building and running an aseptic area. The second half is about using microorganisms as measuring instruments: microbiological assay of antibiotics, vitamins and amino acids, and how a newly found antibiotic is assessed.

## 4.1 Why an aseptic area is needed

Many sterile products cannot be sterilised in their final container (heat-labile injections, eye drops with heat-sensitive drugs, biological products). They are made by **aseptic processing**: each part is sterilised separately and then combined without letting any organism in. The room, the air, the surfaces, the equipment and the staff must all be controlled, because once contamination enters there is no later step to remove it.

## 4.2 Design of an aseptic area

**General principles**

1. **Separate the area** from the rest of the factory. Entry only through **air locks** and changing rooms arranged as a cascade (outer → changing → aseptic room).
2. **Smooth, hard, non-shedding surfaces.** Walls, floors and ceilings coated with washable epoxy or similar; **coved (curved) junctions** at wall-floor and wall-ceiling joints so dust cannot collect; no cracks, no open drains in the critical area.
3. **No unnecessary fittings:** flush-fitted lights sealed into the ceiling, no ledges, no sliding doors with recesses, pipes and ducts enclosed. Furniture kept to a minimum and made of stainless steel.
4. **Air supply:** filtered through **HEPA filters** (high efficiency particulate air; retain at least 99.97% of particles of 0.3 µm), with many air changes per hour (commonly 20 or more in clean rooms).
5. **Positive air pressure** in the aseptic room compared with the rooms around it, so air always leaks outwards. A differential of about 10–15 pascals between grades is usual, with gauges fitted on the wall.
6. **Temperature and humidity control** (about 20–22 °C, 45–55% relative humidity) for comfort inside full gowning and to limit growth.
7. **Pass-through hatches** (double-door, interlocked) to move material in without opening the room.
8. **Unidirectional flow of people and materials**, from clean to less clean, never crossing back.
9. **Ultraviolet lamps** may be fitted for surface and air treatment when the room is empty; they do not replace filtration.

```text
  Outside  →  Change 1  →  Change 2 (sterile gown)  →  Aseptic room
  (ordinary)   air lock      air lock                   LAF unit
   pressure  →   +10 Pa    →    +25 Pa      →   +35 Pa (highest)
                                            air flows outward at every step
```

## 4.3 Laminar air flow (LAF) equipment

**Principle.** Air is drawn in, passed through a pre-filter and then a **HEPA filter**, and delivered as a stream of parallel layers (laminar, unidirectional) at a steady velocity of about **0.45 m/s (90 ft/min, ±20%)**. Because the layers do not mix, particles are swept out of the work zone instead of swirling about.

| Type | Air direction | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Horizontal LAF | Filtered air blows from the back of the cabinet towards the worker | Filling, media preparation, plating — protects the product |
| Vertical LAF | Filtered air comes down from the ceiling of the cabinet onto the bench | Protects product and limits spread of powders |
| Biological safety cabinet | Vertical flow plus exhaust HEPA filter and inward air curtain | Protects both the product and the worker; needed for pathogens and cytotoxics |

**Correct use:** switch on 15–30 minutes before work; disinfect the work surface; keep articles at least 15 cm inside the front edge; never put anything between the filter and the sterile item (it creates a "shadow" of unfiltered air); work with hands downstream of the sterile item; no talking, coughing or rapid movements; do not block the grilles.

**Checks:** HEPA filter integrity (DOP or PAO aerosol challenge test), air velocity, particle counts, airflow pattern (smoke test), pressure drop across the filter.

## 4.4 Sources of contamination and how they are prevented

| Source | Examples | Prevention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **People** (the biggest source) | Skin scales, hair, saliva, clothing fibres, cosmetics, jewellery, illness | Minimum number of staff, training, full sterile gowning (coverall, hood, mask, goggles, gloves, overshoes), hand disinfection, no talking, exclusion of staff with skin or respiratory infection |
| **Air** | Dust, droplets, spores from outside and from the corridors | HEPA filtration, many air changes, positive pressure, air locks |
| **Water** | Tap water, condensate, badly maintained purified water loops (*Pseudomonas*) | Water for injection, circulating hot loops, regular sanitisation and monitoring |
| **Raw materials and excipients** | Natural gums, starch, talc, sugar can carry spores | Supplier control, microbial limit testing, sterilisation of components |
| **Packaging and containers** | Vials, ampoules, closures, cartons | Washing, depyrogenation, sterilisation; cartons kept out of the aseptic area |
| **Equipment and surfaces** | Filling machines, trolleys, benches, pipes | Sterilisation in place, validated cleaning, smooth surfaces, no dead legs in pipework |
| **The building itself** | Cracks, drains, false ceilings, pests | Sealed surfaces, no drains in critical zones, pest control, regular maintenance |

**Environmental monitoring** proves the controls are working: settle (exposure) plates, air sampling by slit-to-agar or sieve samplers, contact (RODAC) plates for surfaces, swabs for irregular surfaces, finger-dab plates from gloves, and non-viable particle counters. **Media fill (process simulation)** runs sterile nutrient medium through the whole filling line to show that the process itself does not contaminate the product.

## 4.5 Classification of clean areas

Clean rooms are graded by the number of airborne particles allowed, measured both **at rest** (equipment running, no staff) and **in operation**.

| Grade | Where it is used | Air pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Critical zone: filling, stoppering, open ampoules and vials, aseptic connections | Unidirectional (laminar) air, about 0.36–0.54 m/s |
| **B** | Background room around a grade A zone in aseptic processing | Turbulent, high air change rate |
| **C** | Preparation of solutions to be filtered, filling of products to be terminally sterilised | Turbulent |
| **D** | Handling of components after washing, less critical steps | Turbulent |

Older systems describe the same idea by particle count per cubic foot or cubic metre, for example "Class 100" (ISO 5) air for the critical zone, with grades 10 000 and 100 000 for the rooms around it. Each grade has its own limits for **viable** counts too (settle plates, air samples, contact plates and glove prints), with the strictest limits in grade A, where the target is effectively no growth.

## 4.6 Microbiological assay — principle

A **microbiological assay** measures the **potency** (biological activity) of a substance by its effect on a suitable microorganism, comparing the unknown with a **standard preparation** of known potency under identical conditions.

**When it is used**

- For antibiotics, where the activity, not just the chemical amount, matters, and where mixtures or degradation products exist.
- For vitamins and amino acids present in very small amounts in complex materials (feeds, extracts), where chemical assay is difficult.
- As an official method in the pharmacopoeias.

**Requirements:** a pure, standard-sensitivity **test organism**; a validated standard preparation; a suitable medium and buffer; strict control of pH, temperature, incubation time and inoculum size; and a statistical design with replication.

## 4.7 Methods of microbiological assay

**A. Cylinder (cup) plate or agar diffusion method**

```text
   ┌──────────────── Petri plate with seeded agar ──────────────┐
   │        S1(o)            T1(o)                              │
   │                                                            │
   │   T2(o)        (zones of inhibition around each cup)  S2(o)│
   └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
       S = standard dilutions, T = test dilutions, placed alternately
```

1. Melted agar seeded with the test organism is poured into plates as a base and seed layer.
2. Cups are made with a sterile borer, or steel cylinders are placed on the surface, or discs soaked in the solutions are laid down.
3. Standard and test dilutions are added alternately in a balanced design (commonly 2 doses of standard and 2 of test, "2 + 2").
4. Plates are refrigerated for a short diffusion period, then incubated (often 37 °C, 18–24 h).
5. The **diameter of the zone of inhibition** is measured. Zone diameter is proportional to the **logarithm of the concentration**.
6. Potency is read from a standard curve (zone diameter against log dose) or calculated by the official formula from the mean zone diameters.

**B. Turbidimetric (tube) method**

1. Tubes of liquid medium receive graded doses of standard or test solution and the same inoculum.
2. All tubes are incubated in a water bath at a set temperature (commonly 37 °C) for a short, fixed time (about 3–4 hours).
3. Growth is stopped (for example by adding formaldehyde) and **turbidity** is measured in a colorimeter at about 530 nm.
4. Less growth means more antibiotic; a dose–response line of absorbance against log dose gives the potency.

| | Cylinder plate | Turbidimetric |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Measures | Zone diameter | Turbidity (growth) |
| Time | 18–24 h | 3–4 h |
| Equipment | Plates, borer, zone reader | Water bath, colorimeter |
| Sensitivity to coloured or turbid samples | Low | High (interference) |
| Suitable for | Most antibiotics, routine work | Clear solutions, fast results |

**C. Other methods**

- **Metabolic response (acid production) assay:** growth of lactobacilli in a vitamin-free medium produces lactic acid, which is titrated; used for B vitamins and amino acids.
- **Gravimetric assay:** dry weight of the organism after growth is measured.
- **End-point dilution (MIC) type assay** for some substances.

## 4.8 Standardisation of antibiotics

- Potency is expressed in **units or micrograms per mg** against an international or pharmacopoeial standard (for example penicillin potency in International Units).
- The assay organism is chosen for sensitivity to that antibiotic: for example *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Bacillus subtilis* for many antibiotics, *Bacillus pumilus*, *Micrococcus luteus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae* or *Bordetella bronchiseptica* for others; the pharmacopoeial monograph names the organism, the medium and the pH for each antibiotic.
- The buffer and pH are fixed because the activity of many antibiotics changes with pH.
- Doses are arranged in a **balanced design (2 + 2 or 3 + 3)**, with several replicates, and the result is calculated with its confidence limits; the assay is accepted only if the limits fall within the stated range.

## 4.9 Standardisation of vitamins and amino acids

The principle is **growth-dependence**: a test organism that cannot make the vitamin or amino acid grows only in proportion to how much is supplied.

| Substance | Common test organism |
| --- | --- |
| Vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin) | *Lactobacillus leichmannii* |
| Riboflavin (B2) | *Lactobacillus casei* |
| Niacin, pantothenic acid, folic acid, biotin | Lactobacilli and related organisms named in the monograph |
| Amino acids (lysine, methionine, tryptophan) | Auxotrophic strains such as *Leuconostoc mesenteroides*, *Streptococcus faecalis* |

**Procedure in outline:** a basal medium is prepared complete in every nutrient **except** the substance being assayed. Graded amounts of standard, and of the unknown extract, are added to tubes; each is seeded with a washed inoculum and incubated (for example 37 °C for 16–72 h). Growth is measured by **turbidity** or by **titrating the acid produced**. A standard curve of response against concentration gives the amount in the sample.

**Precautions:** glassware must be free of traces of the nutrient; the sample often needs extraction or hydrolysis to free the bound vitamin; the inoculum must be washed so it carries no nutrient with it; and tubes must be handled identically.

## 4.10 Assessment of a new antibiotic

A newly discovered antibiotic goes through a planned series of steps.

1. **Screening and isolation.** Soil or other samples are screened (crowded plate, agar overlay or giant colony methods) for organisms that inhibit test bacteria; the producing organism is isolated and identified.
2. **Production and purification.** Fermentation conditions are improved; the active substance is extracted, purified and its structure, molecular weight and stability are determined.
3. **Spectrum of activity.** MIC and MBC are measured against a panel of Gram-positive, Gram-negative, acid-fast, anaerobic and fungal organisms, and compared with existing drugs; the time–kill curve shows whether it is cidal or static, and whether killing depends on concentration or on time.
4. **Mode of action and resistance.** Which target it acts on (cell wall, membrane, protein synthesis, nucleic acid, folate pathway); how fast resistance develops on repeated subculture; whether it is destroyed by enzymes such as beta-lactamases; and whether it is synergistic or antagonistic with other drugs.
5. **Effect of body factors.** Activity in the presence of serum, blood, pus and at body pH; protein binding; stability in gastric acid.
6. **Toxicity and safety.** Acute and chronic toxicity in animals, therapeutic index, irritation, allergy; effect on normal flora.
7. **Pharmacokinetics in animals and then humans:** absorption, distribution to the site of infection, metabolism, route of excretion and half-life, which decide dose and dosing interval.
8. **Activity in experimental infection,** then clinical trials in phases, comparing cure rates with existing treatment.
9. **Formulation and stability:** a suitable dosage form, assay method, shelf life and storage conditions.
10. **Standardisation:** a reference standard and an official assay (usually microbiological) are established, and the product is registered.

A useful new antibiotic should be active at low concentration, act on targets that are absent from human cells, resist common resistance enzymes, reach the infected tissue, cause few side effects, and be stable and affordable to make.

## Remember

- Aseptic area: air locks, coved corners, HEPA-filtered air, positive pressure, unidirectional flow of people and materials.
- LAF velocity about 0.45 m/s; HEPA filters retain at least 99.97% of 0.3 µm particles; never place anything between the filter and the sterile item.
- People are the largest source of contamination; media fill proves the process.
- Clean area grades: A critical zone (unidirectional air), B background, C and D supporting rooms.
- Cylinder plate assay: zone diameter against log dose. Turbidimetric assay: turbidity, faster but hit by colour.
- Vitamin B12 is assayed with *Lactobacillus leichmannii*; riboflavin with *Lactobacillus casei*.
- A new antibiotic is judged on spectrum, MIC/MBC, mode of action, resistance, toxicity, pharmacokinetics and clinical results.
