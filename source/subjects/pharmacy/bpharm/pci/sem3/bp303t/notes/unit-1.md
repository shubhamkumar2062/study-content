# Unit 1: Introduction to microbiology, bacteria and their cultivation, microscopy

Microbiology is the study of living things too small to see clearly with the naked eye: bacteria, fungi, viruses, protozoa and microscopic algae. For a pharmacist it is a working subject, not just theory. Every injection must be sterile, every syrup must resist spoilage, antibiotics are made by microbes and are measured with microbes, and vaccines are grown in cells and eggs.

## 1.1 History of microbiology

| Year | Scientist | Contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1676 | Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Made simple lenses and first described "animalcules" (bacteria and protozoa); called the father of microbiology (and of bacteriology) |
| 1796 | Edward Jenner | Vaccinated against smallpox using cowpox material |
| 1860s | Louis Pasteur | Swan-neck flask experiment disproved spontaneous generation; showed fermentation is caused by microbes; developed pasteurisation; vaccines for anthrax and rabies |
| 1867 | Joseph Lister | Antiseptic surgery using carbolic acid (phenol) |
| 1876–1884 | Robert Koch | Showed *Bacillus anthracis* causes anthrax; pure culture on solid media; discovered the tubercle bacillus (1882); Koch's postulates |
| 1884 | Christian Gram | Gram staining |
| 1887 | Julius Petri | Petri dish |
| 1892 | Dmitri Iwanowski | Tobacco mosaic agent passed through bacteria-proof filters (start of virology) |
| 1910 | Paul Ehrlich | Salvarsan for syphilis; idea of the "magic bullet" (chemotherapy) |
| 1928 | Alexander Fleming | Discovered penicillin from *Penicillium notatum* |
| 1940s | Florey and Chain | Purified penicillin for clinical use; Waksman discovered streptomycin (1943–44) |

**Koch's postulates** (proof that a microbe causes a disease):

1. The organism is present in every case of the disease and absent from healthy hosts.
2. It can be isolated and grown in pure culture.
3. The pure culture causes the same disease when put into a healthy susceptible animal.
4. The same organism is re-isolated from that animal.

*Exceptions:* organisms that cannot be grown on artificial media (for example *Mycobacterium leprae*, *Treponema pallidum*), carriers who show no disease, and diseases caused by more than one organism.

## 1.2 Branches, scope and importance

**Branches by organism:** bacteriology, mycology (fungi), virology, protozoology, phycology (algae), immunology.

**Branches by application:** medical, pharmaceutical, industrial, food and dairy, agricultural and soil, environmental and water, genetic and molecular microbiology.

**Scope and importance in pharmacy**

- **Production of drugs:** antibiotics (penicillin from *Penicillium*, streptomycin from *Streptomyces*), vitamins (B12, riboflavin), enzymes (streptokinase, amylase), steroids by microbial transformation, vaccines, toxoids, insulin and other recombinant proteins.
- **Quality control:** sterility testing of injections and eye drops, microbial limit tests on oral and topical products, pyrogen and endotoxin control.
- **Assay:** potency of antibiotics and some vitamins and amino acids is measured by microbiological assay.
- **Sterilisation and aseptic manufacture:** design of clean rooms, validation of autoclaves and filters.
- **Preservation:** choosing and testing preservatives so that multi-dose products stay safe.
- **Diagnosis and infection control** in hospitals and community pharmacy.

## 1.3 Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell (bacteria) | Eukaryotic cell (fungi, protozoa, algae, animals) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Size | 0.5–5 µm | usually 10–100 µm |
| Nucleus | No nuclear membrane; nucleoid | True nucleus with membrane and nucleolus |
| Chromosome | Single circular DNA, no histones | Several linear chromosomes with histones |
| Cell division | Binary fission | Mitosis and meiosis |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes present |
| Ribosomes | 70S (50S + 30S) | 80S in cytoplasm (70S in mitochondria) |
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan (except mycoplasma) | Cellulose (plants), chitin (fungi), absent in animal cells |
| Sterols in membrane | Absent (except mycoplasma) | Present |
| Respiratory enzymes | On cell membrane | In mitochondria |
| Flagella | Simple, one protein fibre (flagellin) | Complex, 9 + 2 microtubules |

*Why it matters:* many antibiotics act on structures found only in prokaryotes — peptidoglycan (penicillins), 70S ribosomes (aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides) — so they harm bacteria and spare human cells.

## 1.4 Ultra-structure of the bacterial cell

```text
              capsule (slime layer)
        _____________________________
       /  cell wall                   \        pili / fimbriae
      |   cell (plasma) membrane       |~~~~
      |     cytoplasm   o o  ribosomes |
      |      (nucleoid DNA)   plasmid  |============  flagellum
      |     mesosome   inclusion       |
       \_____________________________/
```

| Structure | Description | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capsule / slime layer | Polysaccharide (or polypeptide, e.g. *B. anthracis*) layer outside the wall | Protects from phagocytosis; virulence factor; adhesion |
| Flagella | Long thin protein appendages | Motility |
| Pili (fimbriae) | Short hair-like protein projections | Attachment; sex pilus transfers DNA in conjugation |
| Cell wall | Rigid peptidoglycan (murein) layer | Shape, protection from osmotic bursting; site of penicillin action |
| Cell membrane | Phospholipid bilayer with proteins, no sterols | Selective permeability, respiration, transport |
| Mesosome | Infolding of membrane | Thought to help in division and respiration |
| Cytoplasm | Gel with enzymes, ribosomes | Metabolism |
| Nucleoid | Single circular DNA | Genetic material |
| Plasmid | Small extra circular DNA | Antibiotic resistance, toxin genes; used in genetic engineering |
| Ribosomes | 70S | Protein synthesis |
| Inclusions | Volutin (metachromatic granules), glycogen, poly-β-hydroxybutyrate | Food reserves |
| Endospore | Thick resistant resting body formed inside some bacilli (*Bacillus*, *Clostridium*) | Survival; resistant to heat, drying, chemicals because of dipicolinic acid and calcium, and low water |

**Gram-positive vs Gram-negative cell wall**

| Feature | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peptidoglycan | Thick, many layers (20–80 nm) | Thin, 1–2 layers |
| Teichoic acid | Present | Absent |
| Outer membrane with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) | Absent | Present; LPS = endotoxin (pyrogen) |
| Periplasmic space | Small or absent | Present |
| Gram stain colour | Purple/violet | Pink/red |
| Penicillin sensitivity | Usually high | Usually lower |
| Examples | *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Bacillus* | *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Pseudomonas* |

## 1.5 Morphological classification of bacteria

1. **Cocci (spherical)** — arrangement depends on the plane of division:
   - Diplococci (pairs): *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria*
   - Streptococci (chains): *Streptococcus pyogenes*
   - Staphylococci (grape-like clusters): *Staphylococcus aureus*
   - Tetrads (groups of four) and sarcinae (cubes of eight): *Micrococcus*, *Sarcina*
2. **Bacilli (rods):** *E. coli*, *Bacillus*; coccobacilli (very short rods); streptobacilli (chains of rods).
3. **Vibrios (comma-shaped):** *Vibrio cholerae*.
4. **Spirilla (rigid spirals with flagella):** *Spirillum*.
5. **Spirochaetes (flexible spirals, axial filaments):** *Treponema*, *Leptospira*, *Borrelia*.
6. **Actinomycetes:** branching filamentous bacteria (*Streptomyces*, *Nocardia*) — important antibiotic producers.
7. **Others:** mycoplasma (no cell wall, smallest free-living), rickettsiae and chlamydiae (obligate intracellular).

**By flagella:** atrichous (none), monotrichous (one at one end, *Vibrio*), lophotrichous (tuft at one end), amphitrichous (one at each end), peritrichous (all over, *E. coli*, *Salmonella*).

## 1.6 Nutritional requirements of bacteria

Bacteria need water, a carbon source, an energy source, nitrogen, minerals and sometimes growth factors.

**Nutritional types**

| Type | Energy from | Carbon from | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Photoautotroph | Light | CO2 | Cyanobacteria |
| Photoheterotroph | Light | Organic compounds | Purple non-sulphur bacteria |
| Chemoautotroph (chemolithotroph) | Oxidation of inorganic compounds | CO2 | Nitrifying bacteria |
| Chemoheterotroph | Organic compounds | Organic compounds | Almost all medically important bacteria |

**Elements:** C, H, O, N, S, P (macro); K, Mg, Ca, Fe; trace elements Zn, Cu, Mn, Co, Mo. **Growth factors:** vitamins, amino acids, purines and pyrimidines that some bacteria cannot make (fastidious organisms such as *Haemophilus* need blood factors).

## 1.7 Raw materials used in culture media

| Ingredient | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Water | Solvent; distilled or deionised |
| Peptone | Partly digested protein; source of nitrogen, amino acids, peptides |
| Meat (beef) extract | Amino acids, vitamins, minerals |
| Yeast extract | Rich in B vitamins and growth factors |
| Agar | Polysaccharide from red seaweed; solidifies medium (1.5–2% for solid, about 0.5% for semi-solid); melts near 100 °C and sets near 40 °C; not digested by most bacteria |
| Gelatin | Older solidifying agent; melts at body temperature and is digested by many bacteria |
| Carbohydrates (glucose, lactose, sucrose) | Energy and carbon; used in fermentation tests |
| Sodium chloride and buffers | Osmotic balance, pH control |
| Blood or serum | Enrichment for fastidious organisms |
| Indicators (phenol red, neutral red, bromothymol blue) | Show acid production or pH change |
| Selective agents (bile salts, dyes, antibiotics) | Suppress unwanted organisms |

**Types of culture media**

| Basis | Types and examples |
| --- | --- |
| Consistency | Liquid (nutrient broth), semi-solid (motility medium), solid (nutrient agar) |
| Composition | Synthetic/defined (exact chemicals known), complex/non-synthetic (peptone, extracts) |
| Purpose | Basal (nutrient agar), enriched (blood agar, chocolate agar), enrichment (selenite F broth for *Salmonella*), selective (MacConkey for Gram-negative enterics, Sabouraud agar for fungi), differential (MacConkey: lactose fermenters pink; blood agar: haemolysis), transport (Stuart's, Cary–Blair), anaerobic (Robertson's cooked meat, thioglycollate broth) |

## 1.8 Physical parameters for growth

- **Temperature:** psychrophiles (optimum below about 15–20 °C), mesophiles (20–45 °C; most pathogens grow best at 37 °C), thermophiles (optimum about 50–60 °C or higher).
- **pH:** most bacteria grow best at pH 6.5–7.5; fungi prefer acidic pH (about 5–6); acidophiles such as lactobacilli tolerate low pH.
- **Oxygen:** obligate aerobes (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*), obligate anaerobes (*Clostridium*), facultative anaerobes (*E. coli*, *Staphylococcus*), microaerophiles (*Campylobacter*), aerotolerant anaerobes (*Streptococcus*).
- **Carbon dioxide:** some (capnophiles such as *Neisseria*) need 5–10% CO2.
- **Moisture and osmotic pressure:** water is essential; high salt or sugar stops most bacteria (basis of preserving with brine and syrup); halophiles need salt.
- **Light:** most bacteria grow in the dark; UV light kills.

## 1.9 Bacterial growth curve

When bacteria are put into fresh broth and counted at intervals, a plot of log number of cells against time has four phases.

```text
 log number
 of cells        ______ stationary ______
                /                         \
               /  log (exponential)        \  decline (death)
              /                             \
 ____________/ lag                           \
 ------------------------------------------------>  time
```

| Phase | What happens | Practical importance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lag | No increase in number; cells grow in size, make enzymes and adapt | Length depends on inoculum and medium |
| Log (exponential) | Cells divide at a constant fastest rate; generation time is shortest (about 20 minutes for *E. coli* in good conditions) | Cells most uniform and most sensitive to antibiotics; used for assays and research |
| Stationary | Growth rate = death rate because nutrients run out and toxic products build up | Spores and many antibiotics (secondary metabolites) are formed here |
| Decline (death) | Death exceeds division; number of viable cells falls exponentially | Involution (abnormal) forms appear |

**Generation (doubling) time:** g = t ÷ n, where n = (log N − log N0) ÷ log 2.

## 1.10 Isolation of pure culture

A **pure culture** contains only one species grown from a single cell. Methods:

1. **Streak plate:** a loop of mixed culture is streaked over the agar surface in successive sectors (quadrant or multiple streaking), thinning the cells so that separate colonies grow at the end.
2. **Pour plate:** serial dilutions are mixed with molten agar cooled to about 45 °C and poured; colonies grow within and on the agar.
3. **Spread plate:** 0.1 ml of a dilution is spread on the agar surface with a bent glass rod (L-shaped spreader).
4. **Serial dilution in broth** until a tube receives a single cell.
5. **Selective and enrichment media** to favour the wanted organism.
6. **Micromanipulator:** a single cell is picked under the microscope.
7. **Special methods:** heating to 80 °C to select spore-formers; animal inoculation for some pathogens.

## 1.11 Preservation of pure cultures

| Method | Details | Keeps for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Periodic subculture | Transfer to fresh slants and store | Weeks |
| Refrigeration | Slants at 4 °C | 1–3 months |
| Paraffin (mineral oil) overlay | Sterile liquid paraffin poured over the slant cuts oxygen and drying | Months to years |
| Freezing | Deep freezer −20 °C or −70 °C, with glycerol as cryoprotectant | Years |
| Cryopreservation | Liquid nitrogen at −196 °C with glycerol or DMSO | Many years |
| Lyophilisation (freeze-drying) | Culture in skim milk or serum is frozen and water removed under vacuum; ampoules sealed | Many years; preferred by culture collections |
| Drying on soil, sand or silica gel | For spore-formers and fungi | Years |

## 1.12 Cultivation of anaerobes

Anaerobes need oxygen removed or kept away.

1. **Reducing media:** thioglycollate broth, Robertson's cooked meat medium (meat particles contain reducing substances); media are boiled before use to drive off dissolved oxygen.
2. **McIntosh–Fildes anaerobic jar:** a metal jar where hydrogen is let in; a palladium catalyst makes hydrogen combine with remaining oxygen to form water. An indicator (methylene blue, colourless when anaerobic) confirms the condition.
3. **Gas-generating envelopes (GasPak system):** adding water releases hydrogen and CO2 inside a sealed jar; a catalyst removes oxygen.
4. **Deep agar (shake) culture** and agar covered with paraffin.
5. **Anaerobic chambers (glove boxes)** filled with nitrogen, hydrogen and CO2.
6. **Chemical absorption:** alkaline pyrogallol absorbs oxygen.

## 1.13 Quantitative measurement of bacterial growth

**A. Total count (living + dead cells)**

- **Direct microscopic count** in a counting chamber (Petroff–Hausser or haemocytometer). Quick but cannot tell live from dead.
- **Turbidity (optical density)** by colorimeter or spectrophotometer, often at 600 nm. More cells give more cloudiness.
- **Dry weight or wet weight** of cells, nitrogen content.
- **Electronic counters** (Coulter counter).

**B. Viable count (living cells only)**

- **Pour plate and spread plate counts:** dilutions are plated, colonies counted after incubation (count plates with about 30–300 colonies). Result is in colony-forming units (CFU).
  - CFU per ml = number of colonies ÷ (volume plated in ml × dilution).
  - *Example:* 156 colonies from 1 ml of a 10⁻⁵ dilution → 156 × 10⁵ = 1.56 × 10⁷ CFU/ml.
- **Membrane filtration:** a known volume is filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane; the membrane is placed on medium and colonies counted. Good for water with few organisms.
- **Most probable number (MPN):** sets of tubes receive decreasing volumes; the pattern of positive tubes is read from MPN tables. Used for coliforms in water.

## 1.14 Microscopy

**Resolving power** is the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate. For a light microscope it is about 0.2 µm, limited by the wavelength of light. Using oil immersion increases numerical aperture.

**Bright-field microscope:** specimen appears dark on a bright background; usually needs staining, which kills the cells.

**Dark-field microscopy**

- *Principle:* a special condenser with an opaque central stop gives a hollow cone of light. Direct light does not enter the objective; only light scattered by the specimen does.
- *Result:* bright objects on a dark background.
- *Uses:* living unstained thin organisms such as spirochaetes (*Treponema pallidum*, *Leptospira*), motility.

**Phase-contrast microscopy**

- *Principle:* living cells are transparent, but different parts have slightly different refractive index and thickness, which shifts the phase of light passing through them. An annular diaphragm in the condenser and a phase plate in the objective convert these invisible phase differences (about a quarter wavelength) into differences of brightness.
- *Result:* internal structures of unstained living cells are seen in contrast.
- *Uses:* living cells, motility, spores, inclusions, cell division, cell culture.

**Electron microscopy**

- Uses a beam of electrons (very short wavelength) instead of light, focused by electromagnetic lenses, in a vacuum. Resolution reaches about 0.2–0.5 nm — roughly a thousand times better than the light microscope.
- **Transmission EM (TEM):** electrons pass through ultra-thin sections stained with heavy metals (osmium, uranium, lead); shows internal structures and viruses. Negative staining (phosphotungstic acid) shows virus shapes.
- **Scanning EM (SEM):** electrons scan a metal-coated surface; gives 3-D images of surfaces.
- *Limitations:* specimens must be dead, dried and in vacuum; expensive; preparation may create artefacts.

| Microscope | Source | Resolution | Specimen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bright field | Visible light | ~0.2 µm | Stained, dead |
| Dark field | Visible light (scattered) | ~0.2 µm | Living, unstained |
| Phase contrast | Visible light (phase shift) | ~0.2 µm | Living, unstained |
| TEM | Electron beam | ~0.2–0.5 nm | Thin sections, viruses |
| SEM | Electron beam | ~10 nm | Surfaces |

## Remember

- Leeuwenhoek saw bacteria first; Pasteur disproved spontaneous generation; Koch gave postulates and pure culture.
- Prokaryotes: no nucleus, 70S ribosomes, peptidoglycan wall, no sterols.
- Gram-positive = thick peptidoglycan + teichoic acid; Gram-negative = thin peptidoglycan + outer membrane with LPS (endotoxin).
- Agar 1.5–2% for solid media; melts ~100 °C, sets ~40 °C.
- Growth curve: lag → log → stationary → decline. Log phase = uniform, most sensitive cells.
- Total count counts dead cells too; viable count (CFU, MPN, membrane filter) counts only living cells.
- Anaerobes: McIntosh–Fildes jar, GasPak, thioglycollate, cooked meat medium.
- Dark field for spirochaetes; phase contrast for living unstained cells; EM for viruses and ultra-structure.
