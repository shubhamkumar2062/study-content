# Unit 2: Identification of bacteria and sterilisation

This unit has two parts. First, how a laboratory recognises a bacterium (stains and biochemical tests). Second, sterilisation — the most examined topic of the whole subject, because every parenteral and ophthalmic product depends on it.

## 2.1 Staining — why and how

Bacteria are almost transparent, so they are stained to see shape, arrangement and structure.

**Smear preparation (common to all stains)**

1. Clean, grease-free slide. Place a small drop of saline (skip if using broth culture).
2. Mix a little growth with the loop and spread into a thin film.
3. **Air dry.**
4. **Heat fix** by passing the slide through the flame 2–3 times (smear side up). This kills cells and makes them stick to the slide.

**Basic dyes** (positively charged coloured part — crystal violet, methylene blue, safranin, basic fuchsin) bind to the negatively charged bacterial cell. **Acidic dyes** (nigrosin, India ink) are repelled by the cell and stain the background (negative staining).

## 2.2 Simple staining

One basic dye is used. Methylene blue (about 1 minute) or crystal violet (about 30–60 seconds) is applied to the fixed smear, washed off, dried and examined under oil immersion. All cells take the same colour, so it shows only **shape, size and arrangement**.

## 2.3 Gram staining (differential stain, Christian Gram, 1884)

| Step | Reagent | Time (approx.) | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Primary stain | Crystal violet | 1 min | Purple | Purple |
| 2. Mordant | Gram's iodine | 1 min | Purple (CV–iodine complex) | Purple |
| 3. Decolouriser | 95% ethanol or acetone–alcohol | a few seconds, until no more colour runs | Stays purple | Becomes colourless |
| 4. Counterstain | Safranin | 30–60 s | Purple | Pink/red |

**Mechanism.** Crystal violet and iodine form a large CV–I complex inside the cell. In Gram-positive cells the thick peptidoglycan is dehydrated by alcohol, its pores shrink and the complex is trapped. In Gram-negative cells alcohol dissolves the lipid-rich outer membrane, the thin peptidoglycan cannot hold the complex, and it washes out; the cell then takes up safranin.

**Errors:** over-decolourising makes Gram-positive look negative; old cultures, too-thick smears and cells damaged by antibiotics may give false results. Use young (18–24 h) cultures.

**Importance:** first step in identification; guides early antibiotic choice.

## 2.4 Acid-fast staining (Ziehl–Neelsen)

*Mycobacterium* has a waxy wall rich in **mycolic acid**, so it resists ordinary stains. Once stained with hot carbol fuchsin, it resists decolourising by acid — hence "acid-fast".

| Step | Reagent | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Strong carbol fuchsin | Flood and **heat** until steam rises (do not boil or dry) for about 5 min; heat and phenol help the dye enter the waxy wall |
| 2 | Wash | Water |
| 3 | Decolourise | 20% sulphuric acid (or 3% acid-alcohol) until the smear is faint pink |
| 4 | Counterstain | Methylene blue (or malachite green) about 1 min |

**Result:** acid-fast bacilli (*M. tuberculosis*, *M. leprae* — the latter with 5% acid) are **red/pink**; non-acid-fast cells and background are **blue**.

**Other special stains** (for awareness): endospore stain (Schaeffer–Fulton, malachite green with heat, safranin counterstain — spores green, cells red), capsule stain (negative staining with India ink), flagella stain, Albert's stain for volutin granules of *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*.

## 2.5 Biochemical tests — IMViC

IMViC separates *E. coli* (faecal coliform) from *Enterobacter aerogenes* (often from soil and plants). The letter "i" is added only for pronunciation.

| Test | Medium / reagent | Principle | Positive result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I**ndole | Tryptone (peptone) water, 37 °C, 24–48 h; Kovac's reagent (p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde) | Tryptophanase splits tryptophan into indole, pyruvate and ammonia; indole reacts with the aldehyde | Red/pink ring at the top |
| **M**ethyl red (MR) | Glucose phosphate (MR-VP) broth, 37 °C, 48 h or more; methyl red indicator | Mixed-acid fermentation drops pH to 4.4 or below | Red colour (yellow = negative) |
| **V**oges–Proskauer (VP) | MR-VP broth; Barritt's reagent (α-naphthol + 40% KOH), shake | Butanediol fermentation gives acetoin (acetylmethylcarbinol), which oxidises to diacetyl and reacts with guanidine of peptone | Pink-red in 15–30 min |
| **C**itrate | Simmons citrate agar slant (bromothymol blue) | Organism uses citrate as sole carbon source; alkaline products raise pH | Growth and blue colour (green = negative) |

| Organism | I | M | V | C |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| *Escherichia coli* | + | + | − | − |
| *Enterobacter aerogenes* | − | − | + | + |

**Other common tests:** catalase (H2O2 → bubbles; staphylococci +, streptococci −), oxidase (purple with tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine; *Pseudomonas* +), coagulase (*S. aureus* +), urease (pink in Christensen's urea agar; *Proteus* +), sugar fermentation (acid and gas in Durham's tube), H2S production (triple sugar iron agar), nitrate reduction, starch and gelatin hydrolysis.

## 2.6 Sterilisation — definitions

- **Sterilisation:** a process that kills or removes **all** living microorganisms, **including bacterial spores**. Sterility is absolute — a thing is sterile or not.
- **Disinfection:** killing or removal of disease-causing organisms on non-living objects; spores may survive.
- **Antisepsis:** applying an antimicrobial to living tissue (skin, wounds).
- **Sterility assurance level (SAL):** probability that one unit is non-sterile after the process. For terminally sterilised products the target is **10⁻⁶** (one chance in a million).
- **D-value (decimal reduction time):** time at a stated temperature (or dose) to kill 90% of the population (one log reduction).
- **Z-value:** temperature rise needed to reduce the D-value by 90% (tenfold). About 10 °C for moist-heat spores.
- **F-value:** equivalent time at a reference temperature; **F0** = equivalent minutes at 121 °C (z = 10 °C) delivered by a moist-heat cycle.

## 2.7 Moist heat sterilisation

**Principle:** steam under pressure gives a higher temperature than boiling; when it condenses on a cooler article it releases a large amount of latent heat and moisture. Microbes die by **coagulation and denaturation of proteins and enzymes**. Moist heat works at lower temperature and shorter time than dry heat.

**Autoclave (steam under pressure) — the standard method**

| Temperature | Pressure (above atmospheric) | Holding time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **121 °C** | **15 psi (about 1 kg/cm², 103 kPa)** | **15 min** (IP standard) |
| 115–116 °C | about 10 psi | 30 min |
| 126 °C | about 20 psi | 10 min |
| 134 °C | about 30 psi | 3 min |

Holding time starts only after the whole load reaches the set temperature.

```text
        safety valve   pressure gauge
             |            |
     ┌───────┴────────────┴───────┐  <- lid with gasket and clamps
     │   perforated tray          │
     │   [ flasks ] [ media ]     │   steam fills the chamber
     │ ~~~~~~~~ water ~~~~~~~~~~~~│   after air is driven out
     │   heating element          │
     └──────────── drain ─────────┘
```

**Procedure (simple vertical autoclave):** add water to the mark, load articles loosely (wrapped, plugged, caps loosened), close lid, heat with the air-escape valve open until steam flows steadily (air removed), close valve, let pressure rise to 15 psi, hold 15 min, switch off, let pressure fall to zero by itself, then open.

**Why remove air?** An air–steam mixture is cooler than pure steam at the same pressure, and air pockets stop steam reaching the load.

| Merits | Demerits |
| --- | --- |
| Reliable, kills spores | Not for oils, fats, powders (steam cannot penetrate) |
| Short time, cheap, non-toxic | Not for heat-sensitive or moisture-sensitive drugs |
| Good penetration of fabrics and aqueous liquids | Can corrode sharp instruments; wet packs |
| Easy to monitor | Sealed containers may burst; plastic may deform |

**Uses:** culture media, aqueous injections (in sealed ampoules/vials), surgical dressings, gowns, rubber closures, glassware, discarded cultures.

**Other moist heat methods (not true sterilisation unless stated)**

- **Below 100 °C:** pasteurisation of milk — holder method 63 °C for 30 min, flash (HTST) 72 °C for 15 s; vaccine baths (about 60 °C, 1 h) for non-sporing bacterial vaccines; inspissation (serum and egg media, 80–85 °C on three days).
- **At 100 °C:** boiling for 10–30 min (kills vegetative cells, not all spores); **Tyndallisation** (intermittent sterilisation) — steaming at 100 °C for 20–30 min on three successive days; spores that germinate between heatings are killed next day. Used for sugar- and gelatin-containing media.
- **Above 100 °C:** autoclave (sterilisation).
- **Heating with a bactericide:** an old method for small-volume aqueous injections that could not stand autoclaving — heating at 98–100 °C for 30 min with a preservative such as chlorocresol or phenylmercuric nitrate. Now largely replaced.

## 2.8 Dry heat sterilisation

**Principle:** hot air kills by **oxidation** of cell parts and protein denaturation. Dry heat penetrates slowly and is less efficient than steam, so higher temperatures and longer times are needed.

| Temperature | Time |
| --- | --- |
| **160 °C** | **2 hours** (IP standard: not less than 2 h at 160 °C) |
| 170 °C | 1 hour |
| 180 °C | 30 minutes |
| about 250 °C or higher | about 30 min or more — depyrogenation (destroys endotoxin) |

**Hot air oven:** double-walled insulated cabinet, electric heating, thermostat, perforated shelves and a **fan for forced air circulation** so that temperature is even. Load must allow air to circulate; glassware must be dry; cool slowly inside the oven to avoid cracking.

| Merits | Demerits |
| --- | --- |
| Suitable for powders, oils, fats, glassware, metal instruments | Long time, high temperature |
| No corrosion of sharp instruments | Not for rubber, plastics, fabrics, most medicaments |
| Can depyrogenate glassware | Slow, uneven heating if overloaded |

**Uses:** glassware (Petri dishes, pipettes, flasks, syringes), metal instruments, powders (talc, zinc oxide), oils, liquid paraffin, Vaseline, oily injections.

**Other dry heat methods:** red-heat flaming of loops and forceps; flaming glass mouths; incineration of soiled dressings and animal carcasses.

## 2.9 Radiation sterilisation

**Ionising radiation (gamma rays, electron beams, X-rays)**

- Gamma rays from **cobalt-60** (or caesium-137) have high penetration; electron beams from accelerators penetrate less but act fast.
- *Principle:* ionisation produces free radicals that break DNA strands and damage cell components.
- *Usual dose:* **25 kGy (2.5 Mrad)**.
- *Uses:* single-use plastic syringes, catheters, surgical sutures, gloves, dressings, some powders and ointments, packaged products (it works through the final packing).
- *Merits:* cold sterilisation (little heat), high penetration, continuous process, sterilised in final package.
- *Demerits:* expensive plant, safety shielding, may discolour glass and degrade some plastics and drugs.

**Non-ionising radiation (ultraviolet)**

- UV around 260 nm is strongly absorbed by DNA; germicidal lamps emit at **254 nm**. It forms **thymine (pyrimidine) dimers** that block replication.
- Very poor penetration (stopped by glass, dust, thin films), so used only for **air and surfaces**: aseptic rooms, laminar flow cabinets, operation theatres.
- Harmful to eyes and skin; lamps lose output with age.

## 2.10 Mechanical method — filtration

Filtration **removes** organisms rather than killing them. Used for heat-labile liquids and for air.

**Types of filters**

| Type | Examples | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Depth filters | Seitz (asbestos) pads, sintered glass, unglazed porcelain (Chamberland) and diatomaceous earth (Berkefeld) candles | Random fibrous/porous matrix traps particles by adsorption and entrapment; may shed fibres |
| Membrane (screen) filters | Cellulose acetate or nitrate, nylon, PVDF, polysulfone | Uniform pores of defined size act as a sieve |

**Sterilising-grade filter:** pore size **0.22 µm (or 0.2 µm)**, checked by retention of *Brevundimonas diminuta*. Pyrogens and viruses are not reliably removed. A 0.45 µm membrane is used in sterility testing and water analysis (it retains organisms but is not claimed sterilising).

**Integrity tests:** bubble point test (pressure needed to push air through the wetted membrane rises with smaller pores), diffusion (forward-flow) test, pressure-hold test — done before and after use.

**Uses:** heat-labile solutions (some antibiotics, serum, enzymes, vitamins, eye drops, insulin-type biological products), culture media additives, sterilising air (HEPA filters), gases.

**Merits:** no heat; removes dead cells too; fast for small volumes. **Demerits:** no protection against viruses and pyrogens; filter may adsorb drug; product must then be filled aseptically; risk of filter failure.

## 2.11 Chemical and gaseous sterilisation

**Ethylene oxide (EtO)**

- Colourless gas; highly penetrating; kills by **alkylation** of –SH, –OH, –NH2, –COOH groups of proteins and nucleic acids.
- Flammable and explosive with air, so it is mixed with CO2 or other inert gases.
- Process factors: gas concentration, temperature (often about 50–60 °C), relative humidity (about 40–60% — some moisture is essential), exposure time (several hours). After exposure, a long **aeration** period removes toxic residues.
- *Uses:* heat-sensitive plastics, catheters, heart–lung machines, sutures, prepacked disposables.
- *Demerits:* toxic, mutagenic and possibly carcinogenic; residues; slow; reacts with some drugs.

**Formaldehyde:** gas or vapour (heating formalin, or with potassium permanganate) for fumigation of rooms, aseptic areas and animal houses; low-temperature steam with formaldehyde for heat-sensitive equipment. Irritant, poor penetration.

**Hydrogen peroxide vapour / gas plasma, peracetic acid:** low-temperature sterilisation of isolators and devices; break down to harmless products.

**β-Propiolactone:** more active than formaldehyde, used for vaccines and tissue grafts; possible carcinogen.

**Liquid chemical sterilants:** 2% alkaline **glutaraldehyde** (kills spores after several hours' contact) for endoscopes and other heat-sensitive instruments.

## 2.12 Evaluation of the efficiency of sterilisation methods

Sterility cannot be proved by testing a few units, so the process itself is **validated and monitored**.

1. **Physical indicators:** thermometers, thermocouples placed in the coolest part of the load, pressure gauges, time–temperature chart recorders; radiation dosimeters.
2. **Chemical indicators:** substances that change colour or melt at set conditions:
   - Browne's tubes (colour change from red through amber to green at the right time–temperature).
   - Autoclave indicator tape (stripes darken) — shows exposure only.
   - **Bowie–Dick test** — a test sheet in a standard pack, run each day in pre-vacuum autoclaves to check air removal (uniform colour change = good).
   - Melting-point indicators (substances that melt at set temperatures).
   - EtO and radiation-sensitive indicators (radiation dots change colour).
3. **Biological indicators (BIs):** standardised preparations of highly resistant **bacterial spores** on paper strips or in ampoules. After the cycle they are cultured; no growth shows the process worked.

| Method | Biological indicator organism |
| --- | --- |
| Moist heat (autoclave) | *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* (formerly *Bacillus stearothermophilus*) spores |
| Dry heat | *Bacillus atrophaeus* (formerly *B. subtilis* var. *niger*) spores |
| Ethylene oxide | *Bacillus atrophaeus* spores |
| Ionising radiation | *Bacillus pumilus* spores |
| Filtration | *Brevundimonas diminuta* (formerly *Pseudomonas diminuta*) challenge |

4. **Sterility test** on finished product (see Unit 3) — a final check with statistical limits.
5. **Validation:** heat-distribution and heat-penetration studies, F0 calculation, filter integrity tests, dose mapping for radiation.

## 2.13 Equipment employed in large-scale sterilisation

- **Industrial autoclaves:** large horizontal, jacketed steam sterilisers, often with **pre-vacuum** (air removed by vacuum pump before steam enters), double doors (load from the non-sterile side, unload in the clean side), programmable controllers and chart recorders. Water-spray and steam–air mixture autoclaves for sealed bags and syringes.
- **Hot air ovens and dry-heat tunnels:** forced-air convection ovens; **continuous depyrogenation tunnels** carry washed vials and ampoules on a conveyor through a hot zone (about 300 °C) with HEPA-filtered air and then a cooling zone.
- **Ethylene oxide chambers:** gas-tight chambers with humidity and temperature control, vacuum pump, gas dosing and aeration rooms.
- **Gamma irradiation plants:** cobalt-60 source in a shielded concrete cell with a water pool for storage; product boxes move on a conveyor around the source. **Electron beam accelerators** for fast throughput.
- **Filtration assemblies:** stainless steel cartridge housings with membrane cartridges, pressure vessels, inline integrity testers.
- **Steam-in-place (SIP)** systems for tanks and pipelines.

## 2.14 Sterility indicators — summary

| Type | What it shows | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical | That the machine reached its settings | Thermocouple, pressure gauge, recorder |
| Chemical | That an item was exposed (and roughly to what) | Indicator tape, Browne's tube, Bowie–Dick sheet |
| Biological | That the process actually killed resistant spores | *G. stearothermophilus* strips for autoclave |

Biological indicators are the most direct proof; chemical indicators are quick but only show exposure.

## Remember

- Gram stain: crystal violet → iodine → alcohol → safranin. Positive purple, negative pink.
- Ziehl–Neelsen: hot carbol fuchsin → 20% H2SO4 → methylene blue. AFB red on blue.
- IMViC: *E. coli* + + − −; *Enterobacter aerogenes* − − + +.
- Autoclave **121 °C, 15 psi, 15 min**; hot air oven **160 °C, 2 h**; depyrogenation about 250 °C.
- Moist heat coagulates proteins; dry heat oxidises.
- Gamma dose 25 kGy; UV 254–260 nm forms thymine dimers, surface only.
- Sterilising filter 0.22 µm, tested with *Brevundimonas diminuta*; bubble point test for integrity.
- EtO alkylates; needs humidity; leaves toxic residues, needs aeration.
- BI: autoclave — *G. stearothermophilus*; dry heat and EtO — *B. atrophaeus*; radiation — *B. pumilus*.
- SAL 10⁻⁶; D-value = time for 90% kill.
