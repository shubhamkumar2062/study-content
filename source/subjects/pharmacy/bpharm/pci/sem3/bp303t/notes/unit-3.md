# Unit 3: Fungi, viruses, disinfectants and sterility testing

This unit covers two more kinds of microbes (fungi and viruses), the chemicals used to kill microbes outside the body, how their strength is measured, and finally the pharmacopoeial sterility test.

## 3.1 Fungi — general features

Fungi are **eukaryotic**, non-photosynthetic (no chlorophyll), heterotrophic organisms with a rigid cell wall of **chitin** and glucans. Their membrane contains **ergosterol**, the target of amphotericin B and the azole antifungals. They feed by absorption and grow best in moist, slightly acidic conditions (pH about 5–6) at 25–30 °C.

**Morphological forms**

| Form | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yeasts | Single round or oval cells that reproduce by budding | *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Cryptococcus* |
| Yeast-like | Buds stay attached in chains (pseudohyphae) | *Candida albicans* |
| Moulds (filamentous) | Branching tubes called **hyphae** forming a mat, the **mycelium**; hyphae may be septate or aseptate | *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*, *Rhizopus* |
| Dimorphic | Yeast form at 37 °C in tissue, mould form at 25 °C in culture | *Histoplasma capsulatum* |

Vegetative hyphae grow into the medium and absorb food; aerial hyphae carry the spore-bearing structures.

## 3.2 Classification of fungi

| Class | Hyphae | Sexual spore | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zygomycetes | Aseptate | Zygospore | *Rhizopus*, *Mucor* |
| Ascomycetes | Septate | Ascospore, formed in a sac (ascus) | *Saccharomyces*, *Claviceps purpurea* (ergot) |
| Basidiomycetes | Septate | Basidiospore, formed on a club (basidium) | Mushrooms, *Cryptococcus neoformans* |
| Deuteromycetes (Fungi Imperfecti) | Septate | No sexual stage known | *Candida*, many dermatophytes |

Medically, fungal infections are grouped as superficial, cutaneous (ringworm), subcutaneous, systemic and opportunistic (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*).

## 3.3 Reproduction of fungi

**Asexual methods**

- **Budding** in yeasts; **fission** in *Schizosaccharomyces*; **fragmentation** of hyphae.
- **Sporangiospores** formed inside a sac (sporangium) on a sporangiophore — *Rhizopus*, *Mucor*.
- **Conidia** formed free at the tip of a conidiophore — brush-like in *Penicillium*, on a swollen vesicle in *Aspergillus*.
- **Arthrospores** (hypha breaks into cells), **chlamydospores** (thick-walled resting spores, seen in *Candida albicans*), **blastospores** (buds).

**Sexual reproduction:** two compatible nuclei come together (plasmogamy), fuse (karyogamy) and then divide by meiosis to give zygospores, ascospores or basidiospores.

## 3.4 Cultivation of fungi

- **Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA):** high glucose with peptone at about pH 5.6; the acid pH holds back bacteria. Chloramphenicol and cycloheximide may be added to make it more selective.
- Other media: potato dextrose agar, Czapek-Dox agar, corn meal agar (shows chlamydospores of *Candida*).
- Incubate at 25–30 °C for several days to weeks; keep a second set at 37 °C for dimorphic fungi.
- Examination: lactophenol cotton blue mount, slide culture for intact spore heads, 10% KOH mount for skin and nail scrapings.

**Why pharmacists care:** fungi give penicillin, cephalosporin C, griseofulvin, citric acid and ergot alkaloids; they also spoil syrups, creams and tablets, and *Candida albicans* and *Aspergillus brasiliensis* are standard test organisms in pharmacopoeial tests.

## 3.5 Viruses — general features and structure

Viruses are **acellular, obligate intracellular parasites**. They carry **only one kind of nucleic acid** (DNA or RNA), have no ribosomes or energy-producing system, and multiply only inside a living cell. They measure about 20–300 nm, pass through bacteria-proof filters, are seen only with the electron microscope, and are not affected by antibiotics.

```text
        envelope (lipid, taken from host membrane)
       /    spikes (glycoproteins used for attachment)
   ___/__|___
  /  ______  \
 |  | capsid | |    capsid = protein coat of many capsomeres
 |  |  DNA   | |    nucleocapsid = capsid + nucleic acid
 |  |  or RNA| |
  \ |______| /
   \________/
```

- **Nucleic acid:** DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, linear, circular or segmented (influenza).
- **Capsid:** protein shell built of subunits called **capsomeres**; protects the genome and helps attachment.
- **Envelope:** lipid layer present in some viruses (influenza, herpes, HIV). Enveloped viruses are easily destroyed by alcohol, detergents and lipid solvents; naked viruses (polio, adenovirus) are much tougher.
- **Symmetry:** helical (tobacco mosaic virus), icosahedral or cubical (polio, adenovirus), complex (poxvirus; bacteriophage with head and tail).

## 3.6 Classification of viruses

1. **By nucleic acid:** DNA viruses (pox, herpes, adeno, hepatitis B, papilloma) and RNA viruses (polio, influenza, measles, rabies, dengue, HIV).
2. **By host:** animal, plant, insect and bacterial viruses (**bacteriophages**).
3. **By symmetry and by presence of an envelope**, as described above.
4. **Baltimore scheme:** seven groups based on genome type and how messenger RNA is produced, including the retroviruses that carry reverse transcriptase.
5. **ICTV scheme:** order → family (names end in *-viridae*) → genus → species.

## 3.7 Replication of viruses

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Adsorption | Surface proteins of the virus bind to specific receptors; this decides which cells can be infected |
| 2. Penetration | Entry by endocytosis, by fusion of the envelope with the cell membrane, or by injection of nucleic acid (phage) |
| 3. Uncoating | The capsid is removed and the nucleic acid is freed |
| 4. Biosynthesis (eclipse phase) | Early enzymes, copies of the genome and structural proteins are made with the host's machinery; no complete virus can be found in the cell during this phase |
| 5. Assembly (maturation) | Genome and capsid proteins come together to make new virions |
| 6. Release | The cell bursts (naked viruses) or new virions bud out through the membrane, collecting the envelope |

**Bacteriophage cycles.** In the **lytic cycle** the phage multiplies and bursts the bacterium. In the **lysogenic cycle** a temperate phage joins the bacterial chromosome as a **prophage**, is copied along with it, and can later be induced (for example by ultraviolet light) into the lytic cycle. Lysogeny can give the host cell new properties, such as toxin production in diphtheria.

## 3.8 Cultivation of viruses

1. **Animal inoculation** (mice, guinea pigs, rabbits) — used for isolation and for testing pathogenicity; now limited for ethical reasons.
2. **Embryonated hen's eggs**, 8–11 days old:
   - chorioallantoic membrane — pox and herpes viruses give visible pocks;
   - allantoic cavity — influenza and mumps, used to produce influenza vaccine;
   - amniotic cavity — first isolation of influenza;
   - yolk sac — some viruses, chlamydiae and rickettsiae.
3. **Cell (tissue) culture** — the usual method today. Growth is recognised by the **cytopathic effect** (rounding, clumping, giant cells, inclusion bodies), by haemadsorption, by plaque formation or by immunofluorescence.

**Counting viruses:** plaque assay (each plaque comes from one infectious particle, so results are in plaque-forming units), haemagglutination titre, and the dose that infects half the cultures.

## 3.9 Disinfectants and antiseptics — the words first

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Disinfectant | Chemical that destroys harmful organisms on non-living surfaces; may not destroy spores |
| Antiseptic | Chemical mild enough to be applied to living tissue to prevent infection |
| Sanitiser | Lowers microbial numbers to a level accepted as safe (utensils, food areas) |
| Bactericidal, fungicidal, virucidal, sporicidal | Kills that group of organisms |
| Bacteriostatic, fungistatic | Only stops growth; growth restarts when the agent is removed |
| Preservative | Added to a product to stop growth of organisms that get in during use |

## 3.10 Classes of disinfectants and how they act

| Class | Examples | How it acts | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phenols | Phenol, cresol, chloroxylenol, chlorocresol, hexachlorophene | Damage the cell membrane so contents leak out; at higher strength they denature proteins | Floors and drains, skin antiseptics, preservatives in multi-dose products |
| Alcohols | Ethanol 60–80% (usually 70%), isopropyl alcohol | Denature proteins (water must be present) and dissolve membrane lipids | Skin before injection, surfaces, hand rubs; does not destroy spores |
| Chlorine compounds | Sodium hypochlorite, bleaching powder, chloramine-T | Hypochlorous acid oxidises proteins and sulphydryl groups | Water treatment, surfaces, spill clean-up; weakened by organic matter |
| Iodine compounds | Tincture of iodine, povidone–iodine | Iodinate and oxidise proteins | Skin preparation before surgery, wound antiseptic |
| Aldehydes | Formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde 2% alkaline | Alkylate amino and sulphydryl groups of proteins and nucleic acids | Fumigation of rooms; glutaraldehyde for endoscopes and heat-sensitive instruments |
| Oxidising agents | Hydrogen peroxide, peracetic acid, potassium permanganate | Release oxygen and free radicals that oxidise cell components | Wound cleaning, isolators, equipment surfaces |
| Surface-active agents | Quaternary ammonium compounds such as benzalkonium chloride, cetrimide | Disrupt the cell membrane and denature proteins | Skin and mucosa antiseptics, preservatives in eye drops, cleaning |
| Heavy metal salts | Silver nitrate, mercurials, copper sulphate | Combine with sulphydryl groups and inactivate enzymes (oligodynamic action) | Silver for burns and eye prophylaxis; copper against algae |
| Dyes | Crystal violet, brilliant green, acriflavine | Interfere with nucleic acid and enzyme function | Skin lesions, selective media |
| Acids and alkalis | Benzoic, sorbic, propionic acid; lime | Undissociated acid enters the cell and lowers internal pH | Preservatives in foods and liquid orals |
| Gases | Ethylene oxide, formaldehyde, hydrogen peroxide vapour | Alkylation or oxidation | Sterilising heat-sensitive devices (see Unit 2) |

**Ideal disinfectant:** broad spectrum, fast, active in the presence of organic matter, stable, non-corrosive, non-staining, non-toxic to skin, cheap, soluble in water, no bad smell, and leaving no harmful residue. No single agent meets all of these, so the choice depends on the job.

## 3.11 Factors affecting disinfection

1. **Concentration and the dilution coefficient (η):** activity falls as the agent is diluted. For phenol η is about 6, so a small dilution causes a large loss of activity; for mercuric chloride it is near 1.
2. **Contact time:** killing follows roughly first-order kinetics, so a stated time must be allowed.
3. **Temperature:** activity rises with temperature; the temperature coefficient θ10 shows the change in activity for a 10 °C rise.
4. **pH:** affects both the agent (phenols and organic acids work better when undissociated at acid pH; glutaraldehyde needs alkaline pH) and the charge on the cell surface.
5. **Type and number of organisms:** spores are most resistant, then *Mycobacterium* (waxy wall), then Gram-negative bacteria (outer membrane), then Gram-positive bacteria, then enveloped viruses. A larger load needs longer.
6. **Organic matter:** blood, pus, serum, faeces and soap residues inactivate hypochlorites and quaternary compounds, so surfaces are cleaned first.
7. **Nature of the surface:** porous, cracked or greasy surfaces protect organisms; biofilms are very resistant.
8. **Formulation and incompatibility:** anionic soaps inactivate cationic agents; some plastics and rubber adsorb preservatives; hard water inactivates some agents.

## 3.12 Evaluation of disinfectants

**Phenol coefficient (Rideal–Walker test).** The test disinfectant and phenol are each diluted serially and mixed with a standard culture of *Salmonella typhi*. Subcultures are taken at 2.5, 5, 7.5 and 10 minutes.

Phenol coefficient = highest dilution of the test agent that kills in 7.5 min but not in 5 min ÷ the dilution of phenol doing the same.

A value above 1 means the agent is stronger than phenol at that dilution. *Limitations:* no organic matter, only one organism, fixed temperature, and it suits only phenol-like agents.

**Chick–Martin test.** Same idea, but dried yeast or faecal suspension is added to imitate organic matter, so the result is closer to real use; values are usually lower than the Rideal–Walker figure.

**Other methods**

- **Capacity (Kelsey–Sykes) test:** the disinfectant receives repeated additions of culture, showing how long an in-use dilution stays effective.
- **In-use test:** a sample of disinfectant actually in use in the ward or plant is diluted and cultured to see whether live organisms survive in it.
- **Suspension and carrier tests:** organisms free in suspension, or dried on steel or glass carriers, are exposed and then cultured.
- **Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC):** lowest concentration that stops visible growth in broth or on agar.

## 3.13 Testing bacteriostatic and bactericidal activity

**Bacteriostatic (MIC) by broth dilution.** Tubes of broth hold doubling dilutions of the agent (for example 128, 64, 32 … µg/ml). Each is seeded with a standard inoculum (commonly about 10⁵ CFU/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h. The lowest concentration with no visible turbidity is the **MIC**. A growth control and a sterility control must be included.

**Bactericidal (MBC).** Tubes from the MIC series that show no growth are subcultured onto fresh agar free of the agent. The lowest concentration that kills (usually taken as 99.9% kill, so that very few colonies grow) is the **minimum bactericidal concentration**. If MBC is close to MIC the agent is cidal; if MBC is much higher, it is mainly static.

**Agar diffusion (cup-plate or disc) method.** Seeded agar plates receive the agent in cups or on discs; the diameter of the clear zone after incubation shows activity. Convenient but affected by diffusion rate.

**Time–kill (death rate) curve.** Samples are taken at intervals, diluted and plated; log viable count is plotted against time to show how fast the agent kills.

```text
 log CFU/ml
   8 |o
     |  o                control (grows)
   6 |    o        o---o---o---o
     |      o   o
   4 |        x                 x = test agent (killing)
   2 |            x
     |                x
   0 +-----------------------------> time (minutes)
```

## 3.14 Sterility testing of pharmaceutical products

**Purpose.** To check that parenterals, ophthalmic preparations, implants, sutures, irrigation fluids and surgical dressings show no microbial growth under test conditions. The test is destructive and only a sample is tested, so it can never prove that every unit is sterile — it supports, but does not replace, process validation and good manufacturing practice.

**Conditions.** Work is done in a clean room or isolator with laminar airflow, by trained staff in sterile clothing, using full aseptic technique, so that an organism that grows really came from the product.

**Media (IP, BP and USP)**

| Medium | Detects | Incubation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fluid thioglycollate medium | Anaerobes and aerobes | 30–35 °C |
| Soyabean–casein digest (tryptone soya) broth | Aerobes, including fungi and yeasts | 20–25 °C |

Incubation runs for **not less than 14 days**, and the containers are inspected at intervals and at the end.

**Methods**

1. **Membrane filtration (preferred).** The product is dissolved or diluted as required and filtered through a sterile **0.45 µm** membrane that retains organisms. The membrane is rinsed with sterile diluent to wash away any antimicrobial substance, then cut in two (or two filters are used) and placed in the two media. It suits large volumes, oils after suitable treatment, and products containing a preservative or antibiotic.
2. **Direct inoculation.** A measured volume of the product is transferred straight into each medium, keeping the product-to-medium ratio low enough for any antimicrobial to be diluted out. Used for small volumes and for products that cannot be filtered.

**Controls and checks**

- **Negative (blank) control:** media alone are incubated to show they were sterile.
- **Growth promotion test:** each batch of medium is challenged with small numbers of specified organisms (such as *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Bacillus subtilis*, *Clostridium sporogenes*, *Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus brasiliensis*) and must support their growth.
- **Method suitability (validation) test:** the whole procedure is carried out with the product plus a small inoculum, to prove the product itself does not stop growth.
- **Positive control** tubes are handled separately so they cannot contaminate the test.

**Number of containers.** The pharmacopoeia gives tables of how many containers to test against the batch size; larger batches need more containers.

**Interpretation.** No growth in any container means the batch passes. If growth appears, the result is investigated: if it can be shown to come from the testing (a fault in technique or environment, or the same organism in the environmental monitoring records), the test is repeated with the same number of units; otherwise the batch fails.

**Special points for different dosage forms**

- **Liquids (injections, infusions):** usually membrane filtration; the contents of ampoules are withdrawn with a sterile needle after disinfecting the neck.
- **Solids and powders:** dissolved in a suitable sterile diluent, then filtered.
- **Oily preparations:** dissolved in a sterile oil-miscible solvent such as isopropyl myristate, or filtered after warming.
- **Ophthalmic and other semisolids:** dispersed with a suitable sterile solvent and emulsifying agent, then filtered or inoculated.
- **Devices, sutures and dressings:** immersed whole in the media, or flushed with media if they have a lumen (catheters, giving sets).

## Remember

- Fungi: eukaryotic, chitin wall, ergosterol membrane, grow on Sabouraud agar at 25–30 °C, pH about 5.6.
- Asexual spores: sporangiospores (*Rhizopus*), conidia (*Penicillium*, *Aspergillus*), chlamydospores (*Candida*).
- Viruses: one nucleic acid, capsid of capsomeres, some enveloped; replication = adsorption, penetration, uncoating, biosynthesis, assembly, release.
- Viruses are grown in animals, embryonated eggs or cell culture; growth is seen as cytopathic effect or pocks or plaques.
- Phenol coefficient uses *Salmonella typhi*; Chick–Martin adds organic matter.
- Resistance order: spores > *Mycobacterium* > Gram-negative > Gram-positive > enveloped viruses.
- MIC = lowest concentration stopping visible growth; MBC = lowest concentration that kills.
- Sterility test: 0.45 µm membrane, thioglycollate at 30–35 °C and soyabean–casein digest at 20–25 °C, 14 days.
