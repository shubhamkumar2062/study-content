# Unit 5: Spoilage, preservation and animal cell culture

A product can leave the factory perfectly made and still fail in the shop or at the bedside, because microorganisms get into it and change it. This unit deals with that spoilage, with the preservatives used to prevent it, and finally with growing animal cells in the laboratory.

## 5.1 What spoilage means

**Microbial spoilage** is any change in a pharmaceutical product caused by the growth or activity of microorganisms. It can make the product unsafe (infection), ineffective (drug destroyed) or unacceptable (bad look, smell or taste). Even without visible change, a contaminated product can carry pathogens.

**Why it matters in pharmacy**

- Direct harm to the patient, worst in injections, eye drops and products for broken skin or for patients with low immunity.
- Loss of drug: some organisms destroy penicillins (beta-lactamase), hydrolyse esters such as aspirin, break down steroids, or use the sugar in syrups.
- Breakdown of the dosage form: emulsions crack, suspensions cake, gums lose viscosity, creams separate.
- Loss of money and reputation: recalls, failed batches, legal action.

## 5.2 Types of spoilage

| Type | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Physical (aesthetic)** | Change in colour, smell, taste, turbidity, surface growth, gas production, separation of phases | Mould mat on a syrup surface; cloudy eye drops; sour smell in a cream |
| **Chemical** | Microbial enzymes break down the drug, excipient or preservative | Hydrolysis of esters; destruction of penicillin; use of cellulose and gums; oxidation of fats (rancidity) |
| **Therapeutic** | Loss of potency, or the product itself causes infection | Sub-potent antibiotic; contaminated eye drops causing a corneal infection |

Spoilage may be **gross** (obvious to the eye) or **hidden**, where counts rise and chemistry changes with nothing visible.

## 5.3 Factors affecting microbial spoilage

1. **Water content (water activity, aw).** Microbes need free water. Most bacteria need aw above about 0.9, most yeasts above about 0.88, and moulds grow down to about 0.7. Syrups and viscous liquids with much dissolved sugar have low aw and resist spoilage; dry tablets and powders are safe while dry, so they spoil only if they pick up moisture.
2. **Nutrients.** Sugars, gums, starch, proteins, surfactants and some flavours are food for microbes; purified water with a trace of organic matter supports *Pseudomonas*.
3. **pH.** Most bacteria prefer about pH 6–8; yeasts and moulds tolerate acid (pH 4–6). Syrups of pH 3–4 usually spoil with fungi rather than bacteria.
4. **Temperature of storage.** Warm storage speeds growth; refrigeration slows it but does not stop psychrophiles.
5. **Oxygen (redox potential).** Aerobes grow at the surface and in shallow layers; anaerobes grow deep in viscous products and in sealed tubes.
6. **Packaging and container.** Wide-mouthed jars, cracked closures, droppers touched by the patient, and multi-dose containers allow organisms in. Good closures, collapsible tubes and single-dose packs reduce it.
7. **Formulation.** Alcohol, high sugar or salt content, surfactants and the preservative itself all affect survival; emulsions give an oil–water interface and a protected water phase.
8. **Degree and type of contamination:** the number and kind of organisms present at the start, and whether spores are involved.
9. **Storage and use conditions:** humidity, repeated opening, contaminated spoons and fingers, hospital use by many hands.

## 5.4 Sources and types of microbial contaminants

**Sources**

| Source | Typical organisms |
| --- | --- |
| Water (the commonest source) | *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and other Gram-negative rods, *Burkholderia* |
| Raw materials of natural origin: gums, starch, talc, kaolin, gelatin, sugars, herbs | Spore-formers (*Bacillus*, *Clostridium*), moulds, coliforms |
| Air and dust | Mould and *Bacillus* spores, *Micrococcus* |
| Equipment, pipes, filling lines, dead legs, biofilms | *Pseudomonas*, moulds |
| Packaging materials, closures, cartons | Spores, fungi |
| Personnel: hands, hair, skin scales, breath | *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus*, *Candida* |
| Patient during use | Skin and mouth flora, *Staphylococcus*, *E. coli*, *Pseudomonas* |

**Types of contaminants:** Gram-negative rods (often in aqueous products), Gram-positive cocci (from staff), spore-forming bacilli (from raw materials, resistant), fungi — moulds such as *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium*, *Rhizopus* and yeasts such as *Candida* — and occasionally viruses in biological raw materials.

**Objectionable organisms** are those that must be absent from the product because of where it is used: *Escherichia coli* in oral products, *Salmonella* in oral products, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus* in topical and ophthalmic products.

## 5.5 Assessment of microbial contamination and spoilage

**Visual and sensory examination:** colour, cloudiness, films, gas, smell, change in viscosity, separation, swollen containers, cracked emulsions.

**Microbial limit tests (as in the pharmacopoeias)**

1. **Total aerobic microbial count (TAMC)** and **total combined yeasts and moulds count (TYMC)** by plate count (pour plate or spread plate) or membrane filtration, after preparing a suitable dilution of the product and neutralising any preservative.
2. **Tests for specified organisms** using enrichment and selective media, for example *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *P. aeruginosa*, *S. aureus*, and *Clostridia* or *Candida albicans* where the monograph requires it.
3. **Method suitability:** the product plus a small inoculum must give the expected recovery, proving that the preservative or drug has been neutralised.

**Chemical and physical checks:** assay of the active ingredient, pH, viscosity, particle size, and content of the preservative itself.

**For sterile products**, the sterility test of Unit 3, plus bacterial endotoxin (LAL) or pyrogen testing.

**Neutralisers** used during testing include polysorbate 80 and lecithin for quaternary compounds and parabens, sodium thiosulphate for mercurials and halogens, and simple high dilution.

## 5.6 Preservation with antimicrobial agents

A **preservative** is added to stop growth of organisms that enter during manufacture and, more importantly, during repeated use by the patient. It is not a substitute for good manufacture, and it cannot rescue a grossly contaminated batch.

**Properties of an ideal preservative:** wide spectrum against bacteria, yeasts and moulds; active at low concentration at the product's pH; soluble enough in the water phase; stable over the shelf life and at the temperature used; non-toxic, non-irritant and non-sensitising at the concentration used; free of colour, odour and taste; compatible with the drug, excipients and container; and cheap.

**Common preservatives**

| Group | Examples | Useful notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Parabens (esters of p-hydroxybenzoic acid) | Methyl, propyl paraben, often together | Mainly antifungal; work over a wide pH range; adsorbed by polysorbates and some plastics |
| Organic acids | Benzoic acid and sodium benzoate, sorbic acid, propionic acid | Active only in the undissociated form, so they need an acidic product (about pH 4 or below) |
| Phenolics | Phenol, chlorocresol, cresol, chlorbutol | Used in injections and some topicals; chlorocresol is common in creams |
| Alcohols | Ethanol, benzyl alcohol, phenylethyl alcohol, propylene glycol | Benzyl alcohol in multi-dose injections; high alcohol content preserves elixirs |
| Quaternary ammonium compounds | Benzalkonium chloride, cetrimide | Common in eye drops; inactivated by soaps and some surfactants |
| Mercurials | Phenylmercuric nitrate or acetate, thiomersal | Traditional eye drop and vaccine preservatives; use restricted because of toxicity |
| Others | Chlorhexidine, chlorbutol, sodium propionate | Chlorhexidine in some topicals and solutions |

**Points for practice**

- Preservatives act mainly in the **water phase**, so in an emulsion the concentration available there matters, not the total added; oil-soluble preservatives partition into the oil and are wasted.
- **Adsorption and partition losses** to plastics, rubber closures, suspended solids and surfactant micelles reduce the free concentration.
- pH changes activity sharply for the acid preservatives.
- Combinations (for example methyl plus propyl paraben) broaden the spectrum.
- Oral liquids for children, injections for the spine or the eye, and single-dose injections have special restrictions on which preservatives may be used.

## 5.7 Evaluation of microbial stability of formulations

**Preservative efficacy test (challenge test)** — the standard way to prove a preservative system works.

1. The finished product in its own container is **inoculated separately** with standard cultures, typically *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Escherichia coli*, *Candida albicans* and *Aspergillus brasiliensis*, at about 10⁵–10⁶ organisms per ml or per g.
2. Inoculated containers are stored at 20–25 °C, protected from light.
3. Samples are withdrawn at set intervals (for example 7, 14 and 28 days) and the viable count is determined by plate count after neutralising the preservative.
4. The log reduction at each interval is compared with the criteria in the pharmacopoeia, which differ by dosage form — injections and ophthalmics must show a fast and large fall, oral liquids and topicals a smaller one — and **no increase** is allowed at the later intervals.

```text
 log CFU/ml
   6 |o  inoculum
     |  \
   4 |    o
     |       o
   2 |           o              preservative passing the test
     |                  o
   0 +----|----|----|----|----> days   0    7    14    28
```

**Other stability work**

- **Accelerated and real-time stability studies** that also measure the **content of the preservative**, because a preservative can be lost by adsorption, partition or chemical breakdown.
- **In-use testing:** the container is opened and sampled the way a patient would use it, to check it stays within limits until the end of its in-use shelf life.
- **Repeat microbial limit tests** at intervals during the shelf life.
- **Container and closure integrity tests**, since a leaking pack lets organisms in whatever the preservative does.

## 5.8 Animal cell culture — basics

**Cell culture** means growing animal cells outside the body in a controlled medium. **Tissue culture** is the wider term that also covers pieces of tissue and whole organs.

**What cells need**

| Requirement | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Medium | A balanced salt solution with glucose, amino acids, vitamins and buffer; standard media include MEM, DMEM and RPMI 1640 |
| Serum | Usually 5–10% foetal bovine serum, which supplies growth factors, hormones and attachment proteins; serum-free media are used where defined conditions are needed |
| pH | About 7.2–7.4, held by a bicarbonate buffer with 5% carbon dioxide in the incubator, or by HEPES; phenol red shows the pH by its colour |
| Temperature | 37 °C for mammalian cells |
| Sterility | Strict aseptic work in a laminar flow or biological safety cabinet; antibiotics such as penicillin and streptomycin may be added |
| Surface | Most cells are anchorage-dependent and need a treated plastic or glass surface; some lines grow in suspension |
| Gas and humidity | Humidified incubator with controlled CO2 |

## 5.9 General procedure for cell culture

1. **Collect tissue** aseptically and wash it in balanced salt solution with antibiotics.
2. **Disaggregate** into single cells, mechanically (mincing, sieving) or with enzymes such as **trypsin**, collagenase or dispase, sometimes with a chelating agent (EDTA).
3. **Prepare the suspension:** centrifuge, discard the enzyme, resuspend in complete medium and count the cells (haemocytometer with **trypan blue**, which stains only dead cells, to find viability).
4. **Seed** the cells into flasks, dishes or multiwell plates at a suitable density.
5. **Incubate** at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a humid incubator; examine daily under an inverted microscope.
6. **Feed** by changing the medium every 2–3 days as the colour turns yellow.
7. **Subculture (passage)** when the cells reach confluence: wash, detach with trypsin–EDTA, neutralise with serum-containing medium, dilute and reseed at a chosen split ratio. Each passage is counted.
8. **Preserve** a stock by freezing in medium with 10% dimethyl sulphoxide or glycerol, cooling slowly, and storing in liquid nitrogen; revive by rapid thawing at 37 °C and diluting out the cryoprotectant.
9. **Check regularly** for contamination with bacteria, fungi and especially **mycoplasma** (which causes no turbidity and needs a special test), and confirm the identity of the line.

## 5.10 Types of cell culture

| Type | Description | Features and examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary culture** | Cells taken straight from tissue and grown for the first time, before the first subculture | Closest to the living tissue, mixed cell types, limited life, used for virus isolation and vaccine production (primary monkey kidney, chick embryo fibroblast) |
| **Secondary / established (finite) cell line** | Cells subcultured from a primary culture; a diploid cell strain keeps the normal chromosome number | Survives a limited number of passages (often around 50) before senescence; used for vaccines (human diploid fibroblasts such as WI-38 and MRC-5) |
| **Continuous (transformed) cell line** | Cells that have gained the ability to divide indefinitely, from a tumour or by transformation with chemicals, radiation or viruses | Immortal, often aneuploid, grow fast and are easy to keep; HeLa, Vero, CHO, BHK-21, HEK 293 |

```text
 Tissue → disaggregation → PRIMARY culture
                               │ first subculture
                               ▼
                     CELL LINE (finite, diploid)  → senescence
                               │ transformation / tumour origin
                               ▼
                  CONTINUOUS CELL LINE (immortal, aneuploid)
```

## 5.11 Applications of cell culture

**In the pharmaceutical industry**

- **Vaccine production:** polio, rabies, measles, mumps, rubella, hepatitis A and varicella vaccines are grown in cell cultures such as Vero and human diploid cells.
- **Recombinant proteins:** CHO and related lines make erythropoietin, clotting factors, interferons and monoclonal antibodies, where human-type glycosylation is needed.
- **Monoclonal antibodies** from hybridoma cultures.
- **Toxicity and safety testing:** cytotoxicity assays replace or reduce animal tests; eye and skin irritation models.
- **Screening and testing of drugs:** anticancer screening, receptor studies, transport studies with intestinal cell monolayers for predicting absorption, and metabolism studies with liver cells.
- **Potency and sterility-related testing:** virus titration, interferon assays, detection of toxins.

**In research and medicine**

- Virus isolation and identification from clinical samples.
- Study of cell growth, differentiation, gene expression and the action of drugs on cells.
- Chromosome studies (karyotyping) and prenatal diagnosis from amniotic fluid cells.
- Gene transfer, genetic engineering and stem cell work; tissue engineering and skin grafts for burns.

**Advantages:** controlled and reproducible conditions, less use of animals, small amounts of material needed, and a direct view of the effect on cells. **Limitations:** strict aseptic technique and costly media and facilities; cells behave differently from tissue in the body; continuous lines can change with passage; and contamination with mycoplasma or with another cell line is a real risk.

## Remember

- Spoilage types: physical (look and smell), chemical (drug destroyed), therapeutic (unsafe or sub-potent).
- Water activity decides a lot: most bacteria need aw above about 0.9; moulds grow down to about 0.7.
- Water is the commonest source of contamination, with *Pseudomonas* the usual organism.
- Objectionable organisms: *E. coli* and *Salmonella* in oral products, *P. aeruginosa* and *S. aureus* in topical and ophthalmic products.
- Preservatives act in the water phase and are lost to oil, plastics and surfactant micelles.
- Benzoic and sorbic acid need acid pH; parabens are mainly antifungal.
- Challenge test organisms: *S. aureus*, *P. aeruginosa*, *E. coli*, *C. albicans*, *A. brasiliensis*; counts are followed to 28 days.
- Cell culture: 37 °C, 5% CO2, pH 7.2–7.4, serum-containing medium, trypsin for subculture, DMSO for freezing.
- Primary → finite diploid cell line → continuous (transformed) line; HeLa, Vero and CHO are continuous.
