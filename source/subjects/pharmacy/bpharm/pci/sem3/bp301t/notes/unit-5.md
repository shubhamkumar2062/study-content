# Unit 5: Cycloalkanes

Cycloalkanes are saturated hydrocarbons whose carbons form a ring, general formula **CnH2n** (same as alkenes, so they are isomers of alkenes). Small rings (3 and 4 carbons) are strained and react like alkenes by **opening the ring**; rings of 5 or more behave like ordinary alkanes. This unit is about **why**: ring strain.

## 5.1 Nomenclature

1. Count ring carbons; name as the alkane with prefix **cyclo-** (cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclopentane, cyclohexane).
2. One substituent needs no number: methylcyclopentane.
3. Two or more substituents: number the ring to give the **lowest set of locants**; when there is a choice, the group first in **alphabetical order** gets C1.
4. Show geometry with **cis** (same side of the ring) or **trans** (opposite sides).
5. If the chain has more carbons than the ring, name the ring as a substituent (cyclopropyl, cyclohexyl).

| Structure (in words) | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- |
| Three-membered ring with one CH3 | Methylcyclopropane |
| Cyclobutane with CH3 on C1 and C2, same side | cis-1,2-Dimethylcyclobutane |
| Cyclohexane with C2H5 and CH3 on alternate carbons | 1-Ethyl-3-methylcyclohexane (ethyl first alphabetically) |
| Cyclohexane with Br and Cl on adjacent carbons, opposite sides | trans-1-Bromo-2-chlorocyclohexane |
| Cyclopropane carrying –COOH | Cyclopropanecarboxylic acid |
| Hexane chain with a cyclopropane on C3 | 3-Cyclopropylhexane |

## 5.2 Preparation of cyclopropane and cyclobutane

**1. Freund method (intramolecular Wurtz reaction)** — a 1,n-dihalide with zinc (or sodium) closes the ring.

```text
Br–CH2–CH2–CH2–Br  + Zn  —(heat, ethanol)→  cyclopropane + ZnBr2      (1,3-dibromopropane)
Br–CH2CH2CH2CH2–Br + Zn  —→                   cyclobutane  + ZnBr2      (1,4-dibromobutane)
```

**2. Malonic ester synthesis** — diethyl malonate is alkylated twice by a dihalide, then hydrolysed and decarboxylated.

```text
CH2(COOEt)2 + Br–CH2CH2–Br  —(2 NaOEt)→  diethyl cyclopropane-1,1-dicarboxylate
   —(KOH, then H+)→  cyclopropane-1,1-dicarboxylic acid  —(heat, –CO2)→  cyclopropanecarboxylic acid

Use 1,3-dibromopropane instead →  cyclobutanecarboxylic acid
```

**3. Simmons–Smith reaction** — adds a CH2 across a C=C to make a cyclopropane.

```text
CH2=CH2 + CH2I2  —(Zn-Cu couple, ether)→  cyclopropane + ZnI2
```

**4. Carbene addition** — diazomethane (CH2N2) + light gives :CH2, which adds to an alkene → cyclopropane.

**5. Cyclobutane by \[2+2\] photocycloaddition** — two alkenes join under UV light; or hydrogenate cyclobutene (H2/Ni).

## 5.3 Reactions of cyclopropane and cyclobutane

Cyclopropane opens its ring easily because opening releases strain. Cyclobutane needs harsher conditions. Both still undergo free-radical **substitution** with Cl2 in light.

| Reagent | Cyclopropane | Cyclobutane |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H2 / Ni | 80 °C → propane | 200 °C → n-butane |
| Br2 (CCl4) | Ring opens → 1,3-dibromopropane | No ring opening at room temp |
| HBr | Ring opens → 1-bromopropane | No reaction at room temp |
| conc. H2SO4 (then water) | Ring opens → 1-propanol | No reaction |
| Cl2, light | Substitution → chlorocyclopropane | Substitution → chlorocyclobutane |
| Cold dilute KMnO4 (Baeyer's test) | No reaction (unlike propene) | No reaction |

**Lab tip:** cyclopropane decolourises bromine but **not** cold KMnO4, while propene decolourises both. This tells the two isomers apart. Cyclopentane needs 300 °C with H2/Ni to open; cyclohexane does not open at all.

*Pharmacy links:* cyclopropane was once a fast general anaesthetic (stopped because it is explosive). Cyclopropyl rings appear in **ciprofloxacin** and other fluoroquinolones; cyclohexane chairs are the shape of every **steroid** ring.

## 5.4 Baeyer's strain theory (1885)

**Postulates**

1. All ring carbons lie in **one plane** (rings are flat).
2. The normal carbon bond angle is the tetrahedral **109°28'**.
3. If the ring forces a different angle, bonds are bent and the ring is **strained** (angle strain).
4. The **bigger the deviation**, the greater the strain, so the ring is less stable and more reactive.

**Formula for deviation (d) of each bond:**

```latex
d = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(109^\circ 28' - \alpha\right), \qquad \alpha = \frac{(n-2)\times 180^\circ}{n}
```

Here α = internal angle of a regular flat polygon with n carbons. The ½ shares the bending between the two bonds that form the angle.

| Ring | n | Flat-ring angle α | Deviation d | Baeyer's prediction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cyclopropane | 3 | 60° | +24°44' | Most strained |
| Cyclobutane | 4 | 90° | +9°44' | Strained |
| Cyclopentane | 5 | 108° | +0°44' | **Most stable** (wrong!) |
| Cyclohexane | 6 | 120° | −5°16' | Strained (wrong!) |
| Cycloheptane | 7 | 128°34' | −9°33' | More strained (wrong!) |
| Cyclooctane | 8 | 135° | −12°46' | Even more strained (wrong!) |

(+ = angle squeezed; − = angle widened.)

**Evidence that supports Baeyer (for small rings):** cyclopropane is more reactive than cyclobutane, which is more reactive than cyclopentane. **Heat of combustion per CH2** is highest for cyclopropane:

| Ring | Heat of combustion per CH2 (kcal/mol) | Total strain energy (kcal/mol) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cyclopropane | 166.6 | 27.6 |
| Cyclobutane | 164.0 | 26.4 |
| Cyclopentane | 158.7 | 6.5 |
| Cyclohexane | 157.4 | 0 (strain-free) |
| Cycloheptane | 158.3 | 6.3 |
| Cyclooctane | 158.6 | 9.6 |
| Open-chain alkane (reference) | 157.4 | 0 |

**Limitations of Baeyer's theory (very high probability)**

1. **Cyclohexane is the most stable**, with the same heat of combustion per CH2 as an open chain — not cyclopentane.
2. **Large rings (C7 to C30) are stable** and exist in nature (muscone C15 and civetone C17, musk perfumes). Baeyer predicted they could not exist.
3. The basic assumption is wrong: **rings of 6 or more carbons are not flat**; they pucker to keep 109°28'.
4. It ignores **torsional strain** (eclipsed hydrogens) and steric strain.
5. It cannot explain why cyclopropane, despite huge angle strain, is still fairly stable.

## 5.5 Coulson and Moffitt's modification — banana (bent) bonds

Baeyer assumed the bonds themselves bend to 60°. Coulson and Moffitt (1949) used orbital theory instead:

1. Carbon's hybrid orbitals cannot close to 60°; the smallest angle between them is about **104°**.
2. So the orbitals of neighbouring carbons do not point straight at each other; they overlap **outside the line joining the nuclei**.
3. The C–C bond is curved, like a **banana** — a **bent bond**.
4. Off-axis overlap is **less** than normal σ overlap, so the bonds are **weaker** and the ring opens easily. That explains cyclopropane's alkene-like reactions.
5. The real strain is less than Baeyer's 24°44' because the orbitals deviate only (109.5° − 104°)/2 ≈ 2°–3° each from the ideal; the rest is "absorbed" by the bending.
6. Ring bonds get more **p character**; C–H bonds get more **s character** (close to sp2), so cyclopropane C–H bonds are shorter and slightly acidic, and the ring behaves partly like a double bond.

```text
Draw: triangle of three C atoms. Between each pair, draw the bond as a curve
bowing OUTWARD from the straight side — that bulging curve is the "banana bond".
Mark: internuclear angle 60°, interorbital angle ~104°.
```

## 5.6 Sachse–Mohr theory of strainless rings

1. **Sachse (1890)** said rings of six or more carbons are **not planar**. They pucker so that every bond angle stays at **109°28'** — strainless rings.
2. For cyclohexane this gives two shapes: **chair** and **boat**.
3. Sachse predicted they would interconvert, so they could not be separated.
4. **Mohr (1918)** applied the idea to fused rings and predicted **two forms of decalin** (decahydronaphthalene): **cis** and **trans**, which cannot interconvert.
5. **W. Hückel (1925)** isolated both cis- and trans-decalin, confirming the theory.
6. The theory explains why cyclohexane and large rings are stable and why Baeyer's predictions failed for n ≥ 6.

## 5.7 Conformational analysis of cyclohexane

**Chair conformation (most stable)**

- All C–C–C angles ≈ 109.5° → **no angle strain**.
- All neighbouring C–H bonds are **staggered** → **no torsional strain**.
- About 99.9% of cyclohexane molecules are in the chair form at room temperature.

**Boat conformation**

- Angles still ≈ 109.5°, but two pairs of C–H bonds are **eclipsed** (torsional strain).
- The two "flagpole" hydrogens on C1 and C4 point at each other, only about 1.8 Å apart (steric strain).
- About **6.9 kcal/mol** less stable than the chair.

```text
Draw:
   CHAIR                          BOAT
      ____                          ↑ flagpole H  ↑ flagpole H
     /    \____                     C1          C4
     \____/    (one end up,         |\________/|
                other end down)     |          |    (both ends up)
                                     \________/
```

**Energy order (most stable first):**

| Conformation | Energy above chair |
| --- | --- |
| Chair | 0 kcal/mol |
| Twist-boat (skew boat) | \~5.5 kcal/mol |
| Boat | \~6.9 kcal/mol |
| Half-chair (transition state) | \~10.8 kcal/mol |

**Ring flipping:** at room temperature the ring flips chair ⇌ twist-boat ⇌ chair about 100,000 times a second, passing through the half-chair. Every flip turns all axial bonds into equatorial and vice versa.

**Axial and equatorial bonds**

- **6 axial** bonds point straight up or down, parallel to the ring's axis, alternating up/down on neighbouring carbons.
- **6 equatorial** bonds point outward, roughly along the ring's "equator".
- Each carbon has one of each.

**Monosubstituted cyclohexane (methylcyclohexane):** the **equatorial** form is preferred (\~95%). An axial CH3 bumps into the two axial H atoms three carbons away (**1,3-diaxial interactions**), costing \~1.7 kcal/mol. Bigger groups prefer equatorial more strongly; a **tert-butyl** group almost locks the ring with it equatorial.

**Disubstituted cyclohexanes (most stable arrangement)**

| Compound | Most stable form | Positions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| trans-1,2-Dimethyl | More stable isomer | e,e |
| cis-1,2-Dimethyl | Less stable | a,e |
| cis-1,3-Dimethyl | More stable isomer | e,e |
| trans-1,3-Dimethyl | Less stable | a,e |
| trans-1,4-Dimethyl | More stable isomer | e,e |
| cis-1,4-Dimethyl | Less stable | a,e |

**Other rings:** cyclobutane is slightly **puckered (butterfly)** to reduce eclipsing; cyclopentane adopts an **envelope** shape (one carbon out of plane).

**Types of strain to name in answers:** angle strain (Baeyer), torsional strain (eclipsed bonds, Pitzer), steric strain (atoms crowding, e.g. flagpole H, 1,3-diaxial), and transannular strain (medium rings C8–C11, atoms across the ring crowding).
