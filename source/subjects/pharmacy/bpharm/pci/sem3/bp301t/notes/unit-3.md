# Unit 3: Fats and oils

Fats and oils are **triglycerides**: one glycerol molecule joined by ester bonds to three fatty acids. Fats (ghee, lard, cocoa butter) are solid at room temperature because their fatty acids are mostly saturated; oils (groundnut, olive, castor) are liquid because their fatty acids are mostly unsaturated.

```text
Triglyceride (draw this):

   CH2–O–CO–R1
   |
   CH –O–CO–R2        R1, R2, R3 = long hydrocarbon chains of fatty acids
   |                   (same = simple glyceride; different = mixed glyceride)
   CH2–O–CO–R3
```

*Pharmacy examples:* castor oil (laxative), arachis/sesame oil (vehicle for oily injections), cod-liver oil (vitamins A and D), theobroma oil / cocoa butter (suppository base), wool fat (ointment base).

## 3.1 Fatty acids

Long-chain carboxylic acids, usually with an **even number of carbons** (made from 2-carbon units in nature) and an unbranched chain.

| Type | Name | Formula | Carbons : double bonds | Found in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Saturated | Butyric | C3H7COOH | C4:0 | Butter (gives its smell) |
| Saturated | Lauric | C11H23COOH | C12:0 | Coconut oil |
| Saturated | Palmitic | C15H31COOH | C16:0 | Palm oil, most fats |
| Saturated | Stearic | C17H35COOH | C18:0 | Animal fats, cocoa butter |
| Unsaturated | Oleic | C17H33COOH | C18:1 (Δ9) | Olive oil |
| Unsaturated (essential) | Linoleic | C17H31COOH | C18:2 (Δ9,12) | Sunflower, cottonseed |
| Unsaturated (essential) | Linolenic | C17H29COOH | C18:3 (Δ9,12,15) | Linseed oil |
| Unsaturated | Arachidonic | C19H31COOH | C20:4 | Animal tissue (prostaglandin source) |
| Hydroxy | Ricinoleic | 12-Hydroxyoleic acid | C18:1 + OH | Castor oil (\~90%) |

**Why unsaturated = liquid:** natural double bonds are **cis**, which put a kink in the chain. Kinked chains cannot pack tightly, so the melting point drops.

**Essential fatty acids** (linoleic, linolenic) cannot be made by the body and must come from food.

**Reactions of fatty acids:** (1) form salts (soaps) with alkali; (2) form esters with alcohols; (3) add H2 at double bonds (hydrogenation); (4) add halogens (I2, Br2) at double bonds — the basis of iodine value; (5) are oxidised at double bonds (rancidity).

## 3.2 Hydrolysis

Water splits the three ester bonds, giving glycerol and three fatty acids. It needs a catalyst: acid, superheated steam, or the enzyme **lipase** (as in digestion).

```text
Triglyceride + 3 H2O  —(H+ or lipase)→  Glycerol + 3 R–COOH (fatty acids)
```

## 3.3 Saponification

Hydrolysis with an **alkali** (NaOH or KOH). The fatty acids end up as their salts, which are **soaps**. It is irreversible.

```text
 CH2–OCOR                         CH2–OH
 |                                |
 CH –OCOR   + 3 NaOH  —heat→      CH –OH    + 3 R–COO− Na+
 |                                |              (soap)
 CH2–OCOR                         CH2–OH
                                (glycerol)
```

Sodium soaps are hard (bar soap); potassium soaps are soft (liquid soap, used in soft-soap and some liniments). Glycerol is the valuable by-product (humectant, solvent in syrups).

## 3.4 Hydrogenation (hardening of oils)

Hydrogen is added across C=C double bonds of an oil with **finely divided nickel** at about 150–200 °C and a few atmospheres.

```text
Oil (–CH=CH–)  + H2  —(Ni, 180 °C)→  Fat (–CH2–CH2–)
Example: triolein (liquid) + 3 H2 → tristearin (solid)
```

**Results:** liquid oil becomes semi-solid fat (vanaspati, margarine), melting point rises, iodine value falls, and the product resists rancidity. **Caution:** partial hydrogenation forms **trans fats**, which raise LDL cholesterol and heart-disease risk.

## 3.5 Rancidity

Rancidity is the **unpleasant smell and taste** a fat develops on storage.

| Type | Cause | What forms | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hydrolytic | Moisture + lipase enzymes (from microbes) | Free short-chain fatty acids | Butyric acid in stale butter |
| Oxidative (most common) | Air (O2) attacks double bonds; light, heat and metals (Cu, Fe) speed it | Peroxides → aldehydes and ketones (bad smell) | Old cooking oil, fish oils |
| Ketonic | Moulds act on saturated fats | Methyl ketones | Coconut oil, palm kernel oil |

**Mechanism of oxidative rancidity (free-radical chain, "autoxidation"):**

1. **Initiation** — light or a metal removes an H next to the double bond → lipid radical R•.
2. **Propagation** — R• + O2 → ROO• (peroxy radical); ROO• takes H from another lipid → ROOH (hydroperoxide) + new R•.
3. **Breakdown** — hydroperoxides split into smelly aldehydes and ketones.
4. **Termination** — two radicals combine; antioxidants stop the chain by donating H.

**Prevention:** store cool, dark and airtight (amber bottles, full containers, nitrogen flushing); keep dry; avoid copper/iron vessels; add **antioxidants** — BHA, BHT, propyl gallate, tocopherol (vitamin E), ascorbyl palmitate. *Pharmacy link:* this is why oily injections and vitamin capsules contain antioxidants and carry an expiry date.

## 3.6 Analytical constants

These numbers identify an oil, check its purity and catch adulteration. Each has a definition, a principle, a method and a meaning — learn all four.

**Formula symbols:** B = mL of titrant in the blank, S = mL in the sample, N = normality of titrant, W = weight of sample (g), 56.1 = molecular weight of KOH, 12.69 = 126.9 (equivalent weight of iodine) × 100 g basis / 1000 mL.

**(a) Acid value (AV)**

- **Definition:** mg of KOH needed to neutralise the **free fatty acids** in 1 g of fat or oil.
- **Method:** dissolve the weighed sample in neutralised ethanol–ether, add phenolphthalein, titrate with 0.1 N KOH to a pink end point.
- **Formula:** AV = (S × N × 56.1) / W
- **Meaning:** fresh oils have low AV; a high AV shows hydrolysis or rancidity. % free fatty acid (as oleic) ≈ AV / 2.

**(b) Saponification value (SV)**

- **Definition:** mg of KOH needed to **completely saponify** 1 g of fat or oil (free acids + esters).
- **Method:** reflux the sample with an excess of 0.5 N alcoholic KOH for 30–60 min. Back-titrate the leftover KOH with 0.5 N HCl (phenolphthalein). Run a blank without sample.
- **Formula:** SV = (B − S) × N × 56.1 / W
- **Meaning:** SV is **inversely proportional to the average chain length** (molecular weight). Short chains → high SV (coconut \~255, butter \~225); long chains → low SV (castor \~180). Mean molecular weight of the fat ≈ 168,300 / SV.

**(c) Ester value (EV)**

- **Definition:** mg of KOH needed to saponify the **esters** in 1 g of fat.
- **Formula:** EV = SV − AV. It tells how much true glyceride is present.

**(d) Iodine value (IV)**

- **Definition:** grams of **iodine absorbed by 100 g** of fat or oil.
- **Principle:** halogen adds across each C=C bond, so IV measures **unsaturation**.
- **Method (Wijs):** dissolve the sample in chloroform or CCl4; add Wijs reagent (iodine monochloride, ICl, in glacial acetic acid); keep 30 min in the dark. Add KI — unused ICl liberates I2. Titrate the I2 with 0.1 N sodium thiosulfate, starch indicator (blue → colourless). Run a blank. (Hanus method uses IBr.)
- **Formula:** IV = (B − S) × N × 12.69 / W
- **Meaning:** classifies oils — **non-drying** (IV < 100: olive, castor), **semi-drying** (100–130: cottonseed, sesame), **drying** (> 130: linseed). Drying oils harden in air and are used in paints and varnishes.

**(e) Acetyl value**

- **Definition:** mg of KOH needed to neutralise the acetic acid set free by hydrolysis of 1 g of **acetylated** fat.
- **Principle:** acetic anhydride acetylates free –OH groups, so the value measures **hydroxy fatty acids** (and free glycerol / partial glycerides).
- **Method:** boil the fat with acetic anhydride; wash out excess; find the saponification value of the acetylated fat.
- **Formula:** Acetyl value = (b − a) × 1335 / (1335 − a), where a = SV before and b = SV after acetylation.
- **Meaning:** castor oil has a very high acetyl value (\~145–150) because of ricinoleic acid; it identifies castor oil and detects its adulteration.

**(f) Reichert–Meissl (RM) value**

- **Definition:** mL of **0.1 N NaOH (or KOH)** needed to neutralise the **steam-volatile, water-soluble fatty acids** distilled from **5 g** of fat under fixed conditions.
- **Method:** saponify 5 g fat (glycerol–NaOH), acidify with H2SO4, steam-distil a fixed 110 mL, filter (insoluble acids stay on paper), titrate 100 mL of filtrate with 0.1 N NaOH.
- **Meaning:** measures **butyric and caproic acids**, found mainly in milk fat. Butter/ghee RM 17–35; most other fats < 1. Used to detect **adulteration of ghee** with vanaspati or animal fat.
- **Related:** **Polenske value** = the volatile but **water-insoluble** acids (caprylic, capric) left on the filter paper; high for coconut oil.

**Typical values (good for MCQs)**

| Oil / fat | Saponification value | Iodine value | RM value | Note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coconut oil | 250–264 | 7–11 | 6–8 | Short-chain, saturated |
| Butter / ghee | 220–233 | 26–38 | 17–35 | High RM |
| Olive oil | 187–196 | 79–88 | < 1 | Non-drying |
| Castor oil | 176–187 | 82–90 | < 1 | High acetyl value |
| Groundnut (arachis) oil | 188–196 | 85–99 | < 1 | Injection vehicle |
| Cottonseed oil | 190–198 | 100–115 | < 1 | Semi-drying |
| Linseed oil | 188–196 | 170–200 | < 1 | Drying |
