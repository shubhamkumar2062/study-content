# How to use this guide

Medicinal Chemistry II continues straight on from Medicinal Chemistry I, and the marks come from the same four places: **classification**, **structure–activity relationship (SAR)**, **mechanism of action with uses**, and the **synthesis of the starred drugs**. The difference is the territory: this paper is mostly cardiovascular, hormonal and anticancer chemistry. Work unit by unit — read the notes, write out the classification table from memory, draw the skeleton and number the SAR points against it, then write each starred synthesis as a numbered scheme without looking. Finish the unit with the question bank and the MCQs.

**How the units here match the syllabus.** The PCI syllabus has five units, and these notes keep one note file per syllabus unit, in the same order. Two things are worth knowing about the split. Unit 1 is the largest, because the syllabus puts antihistamines, anti-ulcer drugs and the whole of cancer chemotherapy into one 10-hour unit; it is written as three clearly separated blocks so you can revise them one at a time. Unit 4 has **no starred drug at all**, so nothing in it needs a synthesis — the marks there are entirely in steroid nomenclature, stereochemistry, SAR and uses.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; learn the full answer with the skeleton, the SAR points and the synthesis where there is one.
- **Medium** — asked every few years, or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for the objective section and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common patterns in pharmacy papers, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. **Draw the skeleton first, then number the SAR points against it.** The six that carry the most marks are the H1 antihistamine skeleton, the thiazide nucleus, the steroid nucleus with its numbering, the cardiac glycoside (steroid + lactone + sugar), the sulphonylurea bridge and the three-part local anaesthetic skeleton.
2. **Write syntheses as numbered schemes.** "Reagent → product", with the name of the reaction where it has one (Williamson, Fischer esterification, Michael addition, Strecker, chlorosulphonation, Grignard). Even without neat structures, a correct sequence carries most of the marks.
3. **Classify by mechanism or by site, not by adjectives.** Diuretics are classified by where they act in the nephron; antiarrhythmics by the Vaughan Williams class; anticancer drugs by chemical class. Examiners count the classes and the named examples.
4. **Attach one toxicity tag to each important drug.** Doxorubicin → heart, bleomycin → lung, vincristine → nerves, cisplatin → kidney, amiodarone → thyroid and lung, chlorpropamide → long hypoglycaemia, metformin → lactic acidosis, thioamides → agranulocytosis. These are one-mark gifts in Section A and finish off a Section C answer well.
5. **Two unit-4 traps.** β is up and α is down, and the difference between dexamethasone and betamethasone is only the 16α versus 16β methyl. Say it exactly; do not write "it has a methyl group somewhere".
6. **Do not confuse the look-alike SARs.** Antihistamines need two aryl rings and a tertiary amine; local anaesthetics need one aryl ring, an ester or amide chain and a tertiary amine; sulphonylureas need an aryl ring, the sulphonylurea bridge and an alkyl group. Writing one for another loses the whole question.
7. **Learn the starred drugs cold.** Nineteen drugs carry an asterisk in this syllabus and they are the most likely synthesis questions: diphenhydramine hydrochloride, triprolidine hydrochloride, promethazine hydrochloride, cimetidine, mechlorethamine, mercaptopurine, methotrexate, nitroglycerin, isosorbide dinitrate, acetazolamide, chlorothiazide, furosemide, methyldopate hydrochloride, disopyramide phosphate, warfarin, tolbutamide, benzocaine, procaine and dibucaine. Two of these — the organic nitrates — are industrial nitrate esters; for them, learn the **reaction type (esterification of a polyol with nitric acid), the bioactivation to nitric oxide and the SAR**, which is what a written paper can fairly ask. The notes deliberately stop there and give no plant procedure, and the same is true for the nitrogen mustard, where the examinable chemistry is the aziridinium mechanism.
8. **Link chemistry to the clinic in every answer.** Say *why* an ester local anaesthetic causes allergy, *why* an enteric coat is essential for omeprazole, *why* warfarin takes three days to work, *why* benzocaine cannot be injected. One such sentence per answer lifts the grade.
9. **This subject has no practical paper of its own in this semester's scheme**, so everything you learn here is examined in writing. Spend the time you would have spent on experiments on redrawing structures instead.
