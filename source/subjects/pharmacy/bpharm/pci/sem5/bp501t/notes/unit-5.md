# Unit 5: Antidiabetic agents and local anaesthetics

The last unit has two halves. The first is the **antidiabetic drugs**, from insulin itself to the five oral classes. The second is the **local anaesthetics**, where the SAR is explicitly named in the syllabus and is almost certain to appear in the paper. Starred drugs are **tolbutamide, benzocaine, procaine and dibucaine**.

## 5.1 Diabetes in one paragraph

Insulin, made by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets, lowers blood glucose by pushing glucose into muscle and fat (through the GLUT4 transporter), by storing it as glycogen, and by stopping the liver making new glucose. **Type 1** diabetes is loss of the beta cells and needs insulin. **Type 2** diabetes is resistance to insulin plus a failing beta cell, and is treated first with diet, exercise and oral drugs. The complications — retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy and large-vessel disease — are what treatment is really trying to prevent.

## 5.2 Insulin and its preparations

Insulin is a protein of **51 amino acids in two chains: chain A of 21 and chain B of 30, joined by two disulphide bridges**, with a third disulphide inside chain A. It is made as proinsulin and the connecting **C-peptide** is cut out before release; measuring C-peptide therefore shows how much insulin the patient's own pancreas is making. Because it is a protein it is **destroyed in the gut and must be injected** (or inhaled in special preparations).

Earlier preparations were extracted from pig and beef pancreas; almost all insulin used now is **human insulin made by recombinant technology**, or an **analogue** in which a few amino acids have been changed to alter how quickly it acts.

| Type | Examples | How the chemistry changes the action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rapid-acting analogue | Insulin lispro, aspart, glulisine | Amino acids swapped near the end of chain B so the molecules do not form hexamers; absorbed within minutes, taken with the meal |
| Short (regular) | Soluble human insulin | Clear solution; given before meals and the only form used intravenously in emergencies |
| Intermediate | Isophane (NPH) | Complexed with protamine, a basic protein, which slows release; cloudy suspension |
| Long-acting analogue | Insulin glargine, detemir | Glargine is soluble at acid pH and precipitates at tissue pH, releasing slowly; detemir carries a fatty acid chain that binds albumin |
| Premixed | 30:70 and similar | Fixed mixtures of short and intermediate forms |

Practical points: suspensions must be rolled, not shaken; unopened vials are kept refrigerated but never frozen; injection sites are rotated to prevent lipodystrophy. The main hazard is **hypoglycaemia** — sweating, tremor, confusion, coma — treated with oral glucose or intravenous dextrose, or glucagon when the patient cannot swallow.

## 5.3 Oral antidiabetic drugs

### Sulphonylureas

Tolbutamide*, chlorpropamide, glipizide and glimepiride all carry the **arylsulphonylurea** skeleton: Ar—SO2—NH—CO—NH—R.

**Mechanism.** They bind the SUR1 subunit of the **ATP-sensitive potassium channel** on the beta cell. The channel closes, the membrane depolarises, voltage-gated calcium channels open and stored **insulin is released**. They therefore work only while beta cells survive — useless in type 1 diabetes.

**SAR points:**

1. A **sulphonylurea bridge is essential**; replacing the urea by a thiourea reduces activity.
2. The **aryl ring must carry a para substituent**: methyl in tolbutamide, chlorine in chlorpropamide. First-generation drugs have small substituents; second-generation drugs (glipizide, glibenclamide, glimepiride) carry a large **arylcarboxamidoethyl** group that raises potency about a hundredfold, so they are given in much smaller amounts.
3. The **nitrogen substituent R** should be an alkyl or cycloalkyl group of roughly 3–6 carbons; too small or too large a group reduces activity. A cyclohexyl ring (glibenclamide, glimepiride) gives a long action.
4. Duration follows the metabolism: tolbutamide is oxidised quickly at its para methyl group and is short acting; chlorpropamide, with a chlorine that cannot be oxidised, lasts more than a day and is the most likely to cause prolonged hypoglycaemia.

**Adverse effects:** hypoglycaemia (worst with the long-acting ones and in the elderly), weight gain, nausea, rashes, and with chlorpropamide a disulfiram-like flush with alcohol and water retention.

**Synthesis of tolbutamide.** 1. **Toluene** is treated with chlorosulphonic acid to give **p-toluenesulphonyl chloride**, which is converted with **ammonia** to **p-toluenesulphonamide**. 2. The sulphonamide is treated with a base (sodium hydroxide or sodium methoxide) and then with **n-butyl isocyanate**; the sulphonamide nitrogen adds across the isocyanate to give the sulphonylurea. 3. Acidification and recrystallisation give **tolbutamide**. (An alternative route condenses p-toluenesulphonamide with n-butylamine through a carbamate or urethane intermediate.)

### Biguanides

**Metformin** is a small, very polar molecule: two linked guanidine units. It does **not** release insulin, so it does not by itself cause hypoglycaemia. It activates **AMP-activated protein kinase**, which reduces glucose production by the liver (less gluconeogenesis), improves insulin sensitivity in muscle and slightly reduces appetite. It is the **first-choice drug in type 2 diabetes**, especially with obesity, and does not cause weight gain.

Adverse effects: metallic taste, nausea and diarrhoea early in treatment; reduced vitamin B12 absorption on long use; and the rare but serious **lactic acidosis**, which is why it is avoided in renal impairment, severe liver disease, heart failure with poor perfusion and alcoholism, and is stopped before contrast radiography and major surgery.

### Thiazolidinediones

Pioglitazone and rosiglitazone contain the **2,4-thiazolidinedione ring**, whose acidic imide hydrogen is essential. They bind the nuclear receptor **PPAR-γ** in fat cells and change the transcription of genes that control insulin sensitivity; the effect therefore takes weeks to appear. They are called insulin sensitisers.

Adverse effects: fluid retention and oedema, weight gain, heart failure in susceptible patients, and bone fracture in women. (Troglitazone, an earlier member, was withdrawn for liver failure, so liver function is monitored.)

### Meglitinides

Repaglinide and nateglinide act on the **same potassium channel as the sulphonylureas but at a different site**, and they act and disappear quickly. Taken just before each meal they control the rise in glucose after eating, and are useful for irregular meal times. Repaglinide is a benzoic acid derivative, nateglinide a derivative of the amino acid D-phenylalanine.

### Alpha-glucosidase inhibitors

**Acarbose** and **voglibose** are sugar-like molecules that competitively inhibit the **α-glucosidase enzymes of the intestinal brush border**, so complex carbohydrates are broken down and absorbed more slowly and the peak after a meal is blunted. They act in the gut and are hardly absorbed.

Points to remember: they must be taken with the first mouthful of food; flatulence, abdominal cramps and diarrhoea are common because the unabsorbed carbohydrate reaches the colon; and if hypoglycaemia occurs in a patient also taking a sulphonylurea, it must be treated with **glucose, not sucrose**, since sucrose cannot be split while the enzyme is inhibited.

| Class | Main action | Causes hypoglycaemia alone? | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sulphonylureas | Release insulin | Yes | Gain |
| Biguanides | Less hepatic glucose output | No | Neutral or slight loss |
| Thiazolidinediones | Insulin sensitisers (PPAR-γ) | No | Gain |
| Meglitinides | Short insulin release at meals | Yes, briefly | Slight gain |
| Glucosidase inhibitors | Slower carbohydrate absorption | No | Neutral |

## 5.4 Local anaesthetics

A local anaesthetic reversibly blocks nerve conduction in a limited area without loss of consciousness.

**Mechanism.** The drug is a weak base. The **unionised form crosses the nerve membrane**, and inside the axon the **ionised (cationic) form binds the inner mouth of the voltage-gated sodium channel**, preferring the open and inactivated states. Sodium entry fails, the action potential cannot rise, and the impulse stops. Small, unmyelinated fibres are blocked first, so the order of loss is pain and temperature, then touch, then pressure, then motor power.

This explains two clinical facts: in **inflamed, acidic tissue** very little drug stays unionised, so the block is poor; and **adrenaline added to the injection** constricts vessels, keeps the drug where it was put, lengthens the block and reduces systemic toxicity (never in fingers, toes, nose, ear or penis).

### General structure and SAR

```text
   Aromatic (lipophilic) part ── Intermediate chain ── Amine (hydrophilic) part
        benzene / quinoline         ester —COO—            tertiary amine,
        ring, often with            or amide —NHCO—        mostly diethylamino
        an amino or alkoxy          2–3 atoms long         or piperidine
        substituent
```

SAR points, in the order worth writing:

1. **All three parts are needed.** Removing the aromatic ring destroys potency; removing the amine removes water solubility and the ability to form a usable salt.
2. **The aromatic ring supplies lipophilicity**, which governs potency and duration. An *para*-amino or *para*-alkoxy group (procaine, tetracaine, benoxinate) increases electron density on the carbonyl, strengthens binding and raises potency; adding alkyl chains (butyl in tetracaine, butoxy in dibucaine) raises potency and duration greatly but also toxicity.
3. **The linkage decides how the drug is destroyed.** **Esters** are hydrolysed quickly by plasma pseudocholinesterase, so they are short acting; their breakdown product **p-aminobenzoic acid causes the allergy** that ester anaesthetics are known for. **Amides** are metabolised slowly in the liver, last longer and rarely cause allergy. A patient allergic to procaine can safely be given lignocaine.
4. **Chain length** of 2–3 atoms between the carbonyl and the nitrogen is best; longer or shorter chains lose activity.
5. **The amine must be tertiary** (occasionally secondary) with a pKa near 8–9, so that enough unionised base exists at tissue pH to cross the membrane while enough cation exists inside to block the channel. A quaternary nitrogen gives a compound that cannot cross the membrane from outside.
6. **Branching near the nitrogen or the carbonyl** (mepivacaine, prilocaine, etidocaine) slows metabolism and lengthens action.
7. **Benzocaine is the exception**: it has no basic amine at all, so it cannot be made into a solution for injection and is used only on surfaces.

### Classification with examples

| Class | Drugs | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Benzoic acid derivatives | Cocaine, hexylcaine, meprylcaine, cyclomethycaine, piperocaine | Cocaine, from *Erythroxylum coca*, was the first local anaesthetic; it also blocks noradrenaline reuptake, so it constricts vessels — it is a controlled narcotic substance in India and is of historical and surface-anaesthetic interest only |
| Amino benzoic acid (PABA) esters | Benzocaine*, butamben, procaine*, butacaine, propoxycaine, tetracaine, benoxinate | Short acting, allergy-prone; benzocaine for surfaces, tetracaine for the eye, benoxinate for tonometry |
| Anilides (amides) | Lignocaine (lidocaine), mepivacaine, prilocaine, etidocaine | Most used clinically; lignocaine is also an antiarrhythmic; prilocaine can cause methaemoglobinaemia |
| Miscellaneous | Phenacaine, diperodon, dibucaine* | Dibucaine (cinchocaine) is a quinoline carboxamide, the most potent and most toxic of the group |

**Routes of use:** surface (gels, sprays, eye drops), infiltration, nerve block, spinal and epidural, and intravenous regional anaesthesia. **Systemic toxicity** appears when the drug reaches the blood in quantity: numbness of the tongue and a metallic taste, then light-headedness, tinnitus, twitching, convulsions, and finally depression of breathing and the heart. Always aspirate before injecting, use the smallest effective amount, and keep resuscitation facilities ready.

### Synthesis of benzocaine

1. **p-Nitrotoluene** is oxidised (potassium dichromate / sulphuric acid, or potassium permanganate) to **p-nitrobenzoic acid**.
2. The nitro group is reduced with **tin or iron and hydrochloric acid** (or by catalytic hydrogenation) to give **p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)**.
3. **Fischer esterification** with **ethanol and a little concentrated sulphuric acid** under reflux gives **ethyl p-aminobenzoate (benzocaine)**, which is neutralised, filtered and recrystallised from aqueous ethanol.

### Synthesis of procaine

1. **p-Nitrobenzoic acid** (from the oxidation of p-nitrotoluene, as above) is converted to its **acid chloride** with thionyl chloride.
2. The acid chloride is esterified with **2-diethylaminoethanol** in the presence of a base, giving 2-diethylaminoethyl p-nitrobenzoate.
3. The nitro group is **reduced with iron and hydrochloric acid** (or by catalytic hydrogenation) to the aromatic amine, giving **procaine**.
4. Dry hydrogen chloride in an inert solvent gives **procaine hydrochloride**, the water-soluble injectable form. (Combining procaine with penicillin G gives the long-acting procaine penicillin, and combining it with benzylpenicillin's chemistry is a standard example of salt formation to slow release.)

### Synthesis of dibucaine (cinchocaine)

1. **2-Hydroxycinchoninic acid** (2-hydroxyquinoline-4-carboxylic acid) is the starting material, obtained from isatin or anthranilic acid routes.
2. Treatment with **phosphorus oxychloride or phosphorus pentachloride** converts both the 2-hydroxyl and the carboxyl group, giving **2-chloroquinoline-4-carbonyl chloride**.
3. Reaction with **2-diethylaminoethylamine** forms the amide at position 4.
4. The chlorine at position 2 is displaced by heating with **sodium n-butoxide**, giving the 2-butoxy group and so **dibucaine**; the hydrochloride is made for solutions.

## Remember

1. Insulin = 51 amino acids, chains A (21) and B (30), two interchain disulphides; destroyed in the gut, so it is injected; C-peptide shows the patient's own output.
2. Sulphonylureas close the ATP-sensitive potassium channel and release stored insulin — useless without working beta cells; chlorpropamide is the longest acting and the most dangerous for hypoglycaemia.
3. Sulphonylurea SAR: sulphonylurea bridge essential, para substituent on the ring, 3–6 carbon group on the terminal nitrogen; second-generation drugs are about a hundred times more potent.
4. Metformin works through AMP-activated protein kinase, does not cause hypoglycaemia alone, and is avoided when lactic acidosis is a risk.
5. Glitazones act on PPAR-γ and take weeks; glucosidase inhibitors act in the gut and need **glucose**, not sucrose, to treat hypoglycaemia.
6. Local anaesthetic skeleton = aromatic + intermediate ester or amide chain + tertiary amine; the unionised base enters, the cation blocks the sodium channel from inside.
7. Esters are short acting, hydrolysed by plasma esterase and allergenic through PABA; amides are longer acting, hepatic and rarely allergenic.
8. Acidic, inflamed tissue blocks poorly; adrenaline prolongs the block but never in an end artery area.
9. Benzocaine has no basic nitrogen, so it is a surface anaesthetic only; dibucaine is the most potent and most toxic.
10. Syntheses to write without hesitation: tolbutamide (p-toluenesulphonamide + butyl isocyanate), benzocaine (oxidise, reduce, esterify), procaine (acid chloride + diethylaminoethanol, then reduce the nitro group), dibucaine (2-hydroxycinchoninic acid → POCl3 → amine → sodium butoxide).
