# Unit 5: Cosmetics, pharmaceutical aerosols and packaging materials science

This unit has three separate topics. Cosmetics covers six products the syllabus names; aerosols covers pressurised dosage forms; packaging covers the materials that hold and protect medicines. Each is a frequent source of short notes and essays.

## Part A: Cosmetics

A **cosmetic** is any article meant to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled, sprayed on or otherwise applied to the human body to cleanse, beautify, promote attractiveness or alter the appearance (in India, defined under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940, and now regulated also under the Cosmetics Rules, 2020).

## 5.1 Lipsticks

A lipstick is a moulded stick of colour in a fatty–waxy base, applied to the lips.

**Ideal properties:** smooth, even application without dragging; good colour and gloss; stays on (good "pay-off" and long wear); stable at room and tropical temperatures without sweating (oil droplets), blooming or cracking; non-toxic, non-irritant; pleasant taste and smell.

| Ingredient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Waxes | Structure, hardness, high melting point | Beeswax, carnauba wax (hard, raises melting point), candelilla wax, ozokerite, ceresin, paraffin wax |
| Oils | Spread, gloss, dissolve staining dyes | Castor oil (main solvent for bromo acid dyes), mineral oil, isopropyl myristate |
| Fats and emollients | Softness, smoothness | Lanolin and derivatives, cocoa butter, petrolatum |
| Colourants | Colour | Staining dyes (eosin, bromo acid dyes), lakes, pigments (iron oxides, titanium dioxide), pearlescent agents (mica) |
| Others | Taste, smell, stability | Perfume, flavour, antioxidants (BHA, BHT, tocopherol), preservatives |

**Manufacture:**

```text
Grind pigments in part of the oil (triple roller mill) → colour paste
Melt waxes and oils separately (about 80–85 °C) → mix with colour paste and dissolve staining dyes in castor oil
Add perfume and antioxidant at a lower temperature → de-aerate
Pour into split metal moulds (warmed) → cool quickly (refrigerated) → remove sticks
Flaming (passing briefly through a flame) for a glossy surface → fit into cases → label
```

**Evaluation:** melting point, breaking point (force or weight needed to break the stick), force of application, surface gloss and smoothness, sweating and blooming on storage (often at raised temperature), colour uniformity, pay-off, microbial limits and skin/lip safety.

## 5.2 Shampoos

A shampoo is a preparation that cleans the hair and scalp by removing sebum, dirt and dead cells without harming the hair.

**Ideal properties:** good lather; removes dirt and excess sebum but not all natural oil; leaves hair soft, shiny and easy to comb; non-irritant to scalp and eyes; pleasant smell; stable; easy to rinse.

| Ingredient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary surfactant (anionic) | Cleansing and foam | Sodium lauryl sulphate, sodium lauryl ether sulphate, ammonium lauryl sulphate |
| Secondary (co-) surfactant | Mildness, foam boosting | Cocamidopropyl betaine (amphoteric), cocamide DEA (non-ionic) |
| Conditioning agents | Softness, less static, easy combing | Silicones (dimethicone), cationic polymers (polyquaternium), lanolin derivatives, proteins |
| Thickener | Viscosity | Sodium chloride (with ether sulphates), cellulose gums, carbomer |
| Opacifier / pearlescent agent | Appearance | Glycol distearate |
| Sequestering agent | Stops lime soap scum in hard water | Disodium edetate |
| pH adjusters | Mild pH (about 5–7) | Citric acid |
| Preservatives, perfume, colour | Stability, appeal | Parabens, phenoxyethanol |
| Special additives | Anti-dandruff and others | Zinc pyrithione, ketoconazole (medicated), selenium sulphide, herbal extracts |

**Types:** clear liquid, cream, gel, powder (dry), conditioning (2-in-1), medicated (anti-dandruff), baby (mild, non-stinging amphoteric surfactants), herbal.

**Manufacture (liquid):** add surfactants to water with slow stirring (to avoid foam) → add conditioning agents, preservatives and colour → adjust pH → add perfume → thicken with salt → filter, de-aerate and fill.

**Evaluation:** appearance, pH, viscosity, foam height and stability (cylinder shake method or Ross–Miles method), cleansing action, surface tension, wetting time, solid content, dirt dispersion, conditioning (combability), eye and skin irritation, stability.

## 5.3 Cold cream and vanishing cream

### Cold cream

A **cold cream** is a water-in-oil emulsion cream that gives a cooling feel (water evaporates) and is used to cleanse and soften the skin. The classic formula is a **beeswax–borax** cream: borax neutralises the free fatty acids (cerotic acid) of beeswax to form sodium soap in situ, which acts as the emulsifier.

**Typical ingredients:** white beeswax, liquid paraffin (or a vegetable oil), borax, purified water, perfume and preservative.

**Method:** melt beeswax with liquid paraffin at about 70–75 °C (oil phase); dissolve borax in water at the same temperature (aqueous phase); add the aqueous phase slowly to the oil phase with stirring; continue stirring while cooling; add perfume at about 40 °C; fill.

### Vanishing cream

A **vanishing cream** is an oil-in-water emulsion based mainly on **stearic acid**, partly neutralised (saponified) by an alkali (potassium hydroxide, sodium hydroxide or triethanolamine) to form a stearate soap that emulsifies the remaining stearic acid. When rubbed on the skin it seems to disappear and leaves a thin, non-greasy, matt film; used as a foundation or day cream.

**Typical ingredients:** stearic acid (major part), potassium hydroxide or triethanolamine, glycerin (humectant), purified water, perfume, preservative; sometimes cetyl alcohol for body.

**Method:** melt stearic acid at about 75 °C; dissolve the alkali and glycerin in water and heat to the same temperature; add the aqueous phase to the melted stearic acid with constant stirring; continue stirring while cooling until a smooth white cream with a pearly sheen forms (the sheen comes from stearic acid crystals); add perfume near 40 °C.

| Point | Cold cream | Vanishing cream |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emulsion type | w/o (mainly) | o/w |
| Main ingredients | Beeswax, liquid paraffin, borax | Stearic acid, alkali, glycerin |
| Feel | Oily, cooling | Non-greasy, disappears |
| Use | Cleansing, night/massage cream | Foundation, day cream |

**Evaluation of creams:** appearance and texture, pH, viscosity, spreadability, emulsion type (dye and dilution tests), globule size, phase separation (centrifuge, freeze–thaw), washability, microbial limits, skin irritation.

## 5.4 Toothpastes

A **toothpaste (dentifrice)** is a paste used with a brush to clean the teeth and freshen the mouth, often carrying an active ingredient against caries or sensitivity.

| Ingredient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abrasive (polishing agent) | Removes plaque and stains | Dicalcium phosphate, calcium carbonate, hydrated silica, alumina |
| Humectant | Keeps paste moist, prevents drying in tube | Glycerin, sorbitol, propylene glycol |
| Binder / thickener | Body, prevents separation | Sodium CMC, carrageenan, xanthan gum |
| Foaming agent (detergent) | Foam, cleaning | Sodium lauryl sulphate |
| Sweetener | Taste | Sodium saccharin, sorbitol |
| Flavour | Freshness | Peppermint, spearmint, menthol, clove |
| Therapeutic agents | Anti-caries, anti-sensitivity, antibacterial | Sodium fluoride, sodium monofluorophosphate, potassium nitrate, strontium chloride, triclosan (now restricted in many places) |
| Preservative, colour, water | Stability, look | Methyl paraben, sodium benzoate |

**Manufacture:** disperse the binder in humectant → add water with sweetener and salts → mix in the abrasive in a vacuum mixer → add flavour and detergent last (with gentle mixing to avoid foam) → de-aerate under vacuum → fill tubes from the open end, then crimp the tail.

**Evaluation:** appearance, consistency and extrudability from the tube, abrasiveness (on a standard surface), pH, foaming, fluoride content, grittiness, moisture loss, stability, microbial limits, cleaning ability.

## 5.5 Hair dyes

Hair dyes change the colour of hair.

| Type | How it works | Lasts | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Temporary | Large dye molecules coat the hair surface; wash out with one shampoo | One wash | Coloured rinses, sprays |
| Semi-permanent | Small dye molecules enter the cuticle a little | Several washes | Nitro dyes, direct dyes |
| Permanent (oxidation) | Small colourless precursors enter the hair and are oxidised inside to large coloured molecules that stay trapped | Until hair grows out | p-Phenylenediamine (primary intermediate) + couplers (resorcinol) + hydrogen peroxide (developer) + ammonia (alkaliser) |
| Metallic (progressive) | Metal salts react with hair sulphur to form dark sulphides gradually | Long | Lead acetate, silver nitrate, bismuth citrate |
| Vegetable | Plant colouring | Long | Henna (lawsone), indigo, chamomile |

**Formulation of an oxidation dye:** two parts mixed just before use — (1) dye cream or lotion containing intermediates, couplers, ammonia, surfactants, thickeners and antioxidant (sodium sulphite), and (2) developer of hydrogen peroxide (stabilised). **Safety:** p-phenylenediamine can cause allergic dermatitis, so a **patch (skin sensitivity) test** 48 hours before use is advised on the label.

**Evaluation:** colour development and fastness to washing and light, uniformity of shade, stability of the two parts, pH, skin sensitisation testing, hair damage.

## 5.6 Sunscreens

Sunscreens protect the skin from ultraviolet radiation, which causes sunburn, tanning, premature ageing and skin cancer.

- **UVB (about 290–320 nm):** causes sunburn (erythema).
- **UVA (about 320–400 nm):** penetrates deeper; tanning, ageing, photosensitivity.

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chemical (organic) absorbers | Absorb UV and release it as heat | UVB: PABA and its esters (padimate O), cinnamates (octyl methoxycinnamate), salicylates (octyl salicylate), octocrylene; UVA: benzophenones (oxybenzone), avobenzone |
| Physical (inorganic) blockers | Reflect and scatter UV and visible light | Zinc oxide, titanium dioxide (micronised for less whiteness) |

**Sun Protection Factor (SPF):** SPF = minimal erythemal dose (MED) on protected skin ÷ MED on unprotected skin. It measures protection mainly against UVB. Broad-spectrum products also protect against UVA (expressed as PA ratings or UVA protection factor).

**Formulations:** lotions and creams (o/w or w/o), gels, sprays, sticks; water-resistant products use film-forming polymers.

**Evaluation:** SPF (in vivo on volunteers or in vitro by spectrophotometry on a substrate), UVA protection, water resistance, photostability, spreadability, pH, viscosity, stability and skin irritation.

## Part B: Pharmaceutical aerosols

## 5.7 Definition and components

A **pharmaceutical aerosol** is a pressurised dosage form in which the drug, dissolved or suspended, is expelled from a sealed container by a liquefied or compressed gas propellant when a valve is opened, as a fine mist, spray, foam or semisolid.

**Advantages:** dose can be taken without removing it from the container (no contamination, protected from air, moisture and light); quick action (inhalation); metered doses; easy, clean topical application without touching sore skin; lower drug dose for local lung action.

**Disadvantages:** costly; propellant toxicity and environmental concerns; flammability and explosion risk; need for patient coordination (inhalers); container may burst if heated.

**Components:** product concentrate (drug + solvents + other additives), propellant, container, valve, and actuator.

## 5.8 Propellants

The propellant supplies the pressure and often acts as the solvent or vehicle.

| Class | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) | Trichlorofluoromethane (CFC-11), dichlorodifluoromethane (CFC-12), dichlorotetrafluoroethane (CFC-114) | Non-flammable, ideal properties, but destroy stratospheric ozone; phased out under the Montreal Protocol |
| Hydrofluoroalkanes (HFAs/HFCs) | HFA-134a (tetrafluoroethane), HFA-227ea (heptafluoropropane) | Replaced CFCs in metered-dose inhalers; no ozone depletion but are greenhouse gases; need new formulations (poor solvent power) |
| Hydrocarbons | Propane, n-butane, isobutane | Cheap, environmentally safer for ozone, but flammable; used in topical and cosmetic aerosols |
| Compressed gases | Nitrogen, carbon dioxide, nitrous oxide | Pressure falls as product is used; nitrogen is inert and odourless; used for foods, ointment-type products |

**Naming of fluorinated propellants:** the number read as hundreds–tens–units, after adding 90, gives the number of carbon, hydrogen and fluorine atoms; remaining valences are chlorine. Example: 12 + 90 = 102 → 1 C, 0 H, 2 F, so 2 Cl → CCl2F2.

## 5.9 Containers and valves

**Containers:** must withstand internal pressure (often tested well above working pressure) and must not react with the product.

| Material | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Tin-plated steel | Cheap, strong; lined (epoxy, vinyl) to prevent corrosion |
| Aluminium | Light, corrosion-resistant, seamless (one-piece); most widely used for metered-dose inhalers; internally coated |
| Stainless steel | Very strong; small-volume products |
| Glass | Uncoated or plastic-coated; chemically inert, but breakable; for low pressures |
| Plastics | Low pressures; may be permeable |

**Valve assembly parts:** actuator (button with orifice), stem, gasket, spring, mounting cup (ferrule), housing, and dip tube.

```text
           Actuator (button + orifice)
                 │
               Stem ── Gasket (seals stem when closed)
                 │
     Mounting cup crimped onto container
                 │
           Housing (holds spring)
                 │
              Dip tube → reaches the liquid at the bottom
```

**Metering valves** deliver an exact volume each time (typically 25–100 µl in inhalers) from a metering chamber, essential for potent drugs. **Actuators** shape the spray: spray, foam, solid stream, or special inhalation and nasal actuators.

## 5.10 Types of aerosol systems

| System | Description | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two-phase (solution) system | Liquid phase (drug dissolved in propellant ± cosolvent) + vapour phase | Inhalation and topical sprays |
| Three-phase system | Two liquid layers (water and propellant) + vapour; e.g. water-based sprays, foams | Foams, aqueous sprays |
| Suspension (dispersion) system | Solid drug suspended in propellant; needs surfactant (oleic acid, sorbitan trioleate, lecithin) and fine particles (about 1–5 µm for lung delivery) | Metered-dose inhalers (salbutamol) |
| Foam systems | Emulsion of propellant with surfactants; aqueous stable foams, quick-breaking foams, non-aqueous foams | Topical, vaginal, rectal foams |
| Compressed gas systems | Gas above product pushes it out | Creams, ointments, food |
| Barrier systems (bag-in-can, piston) | Product separated from propellant by a bag or piston | Viscous products, sterile products |

## 5.11 Formulation and manufacture of aerosols

**Formulation:** drug, propellant(s) blended to the required vapour pressure, cosolvents (ethanol), surfactants (dispersing agents, valve lubricants), antioxidants, flavours (inhalers), and for suspensions micronised drug and moisture control.

**Filling methods:**

1. **Cold filling:** product concentrate and propellant chilled well below the propellant's boiling point (often to about −30 to −40 °C) and filled into the open container, which is then sealed with the valve. Not for aqueous products (water freezes) or for some valves.
2. **Pressure filling:** concentrate filled at room temperature, valve crimped on, then propellant forced in through the valve under pressure ("under-the-cap" filling injects propellant under the cup before crimping). Most widely used; suits most products and less propellant is lost.
3. **Compressed gas filling:** concentrate filled, valve crimped, gas introduced through the valve to the required pressure.

After filling: leak test in a hot water bath, weight check, valve fitting, labelling.

## 5.12 Evaluation, quality control and stability studies

**Flammability and combustibility:** flame projection (length of flame when sprayed at a candle) and flash point.

**Physicochemical:** vapour pressure (pressure gauge), density, moisture content (Karl Fischer), identification of propellants (gas chromatography), concentrate–propellant ratio.

**Performance:** aerosol valve discharge rate, spray pattern, net contents, dose uniformity (delivered dose through the life of the can), particle or droplet size (cascade impactor, laser diffraction), foam stability, leak test.

**Biological:** therapeutic activity, toxicity (inhalation and skin irritation), sterility where applicable.

**Quality control during manufacture:** testing of propellants (vapour pressure, density, purity), valves and actuators (delivery volume, dimensions, compatibility), containers (pressure resistance, lining integrity), weight checks on each can, and **hot water bath test** to detect leaks and weak containers.

**Stability studies:** storage at different temperatures and orientations (upright, inverted), checking appearance, pressure, leakage, valve delivery, particle size growth (suspensions), assay, corrosion of the can and valve, and dose uniformity over time.

## Part C: Packaging materials science

## 5.13 Materials used for packaging

**Packaging** is the art and science of enclosing and protecting a product for distribution, storage, sale and use. **Primary packaging** touches the product (bottle, blister, ampoule); **secondary** encloses primary packs (carton); **tertiary** is for bulk transport (shipper cartons, pallets).

**Functions:** containment, protection (moisture, light, air, microbes, breakage, tampering), presentation and identification, information (label), convenience, and patient compliance.

| Material | Advantages | Disadvantages | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Glass (Types I, II, III; amber for light protection) | Inert, transparent, impermeable, can be sterilised, good barrier | Heavy, fragile, may release alkali or flakes (delamination) | Injections, liquids, tablets |
| Plastics: polyethylene (LDPE, HDPE), polypropylene, PVC, PET, polystyrene, polycarbonate | Light, unbreakable, flexible designs, cheap | Permeable to gases and moisture; leaching of additives; sorption of drugs; static charge | Bottles, blisters (PVC, PVDC, Aclar), squeeze bottles, infusion bags, closures |
| Metals: tin, aluminium, tin-plated steel | Strong, opaque, impermeable | Corrosion, cost, may react | Collapsible tubes, aerosol cans, foil for blisters and strips, caps |
| Rubber | Resealable, elastic | Leaching, sorption, coring | Vial stoppers, droppers, plungers |
| Paper and board | Cheap, printable | Poor barrier unless laminated | Cartons, labels, leaflets, strip laminates |
| Laminates | Combine benefits (paper–foil–polyethylene) | Costlier | Strip packs, sachets |

**Common pack types:** blister packs (thermoformed PVC/PVDC + aluminium lidding foil; cold-form Alu–Alu for very moisture-sensitive drugs), strip packs (aluminium–polyethylene laminate), bottles with closures (screw caps, child-resistant caps, ROPP caps), sachets, tubes, ampoules and vials.

## 5.14 Factors influencing choice of containers

1. **Nature of the product:** solid, liquid, semisolid, sterile or not; dose form.
2. **Protection needed:** sensitivity to moisture, oxygen, light, heat, microbes.
3. **Compatibility:** no reaction, leaching, adsorption or absorption between pack and product.
4. **Route and use:** single or multi-dose, need for a dropper or measuring device, child safety, elderly-friendly opening.
5. **Sterilisation method** the container must survive.
6. **Mechanical strength** for transport and handling.
7. **Cost**, availability, machinability on packing lines.
8. **Appearance and information space** for labels.
9. **Legal and pharmacopoeial requirements.**
10. **Environmental impact** and disposal.

## 5.15 Legal and official requirements for containers

**Pharmacopoeial container definitions (IP and other pharmacopoeias):**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Well-closed container | Protects contents from extraneous solids and from loss under normal handling |
| Tightly closed (airtight) container | Protects from contamination by solids, liquids and vapours, and from loss, efflorescence, deliquescence or evaporation |
| Hermetically sealed container | Impervious to air and gases under normal conditions (ampoules) |
| Light-resistant container | Protects contents from light (amber glass, opaque materials, or an opaque outer pack) |
| Single-dose and multi-dose containers | Hold one dose, or several doses withdrawn on separate occasions |
| Tamper-evident container | Shows visibly if it has been opened |

**Legal requirements in India:** the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and Rules, 1945 require that containers do not react with or alter the drug; labelling rules state what must appear on the label (name, strength, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates, manufacturer's name and licence number, storage, warnings for scheduled drugs); Schedule M (Good Manufacturing Practices) covers packaging materials, their testing, storage and line clearance. Packaging materials must also meet the pharmacopoeial tests for that material (glass, plastics, rubber closures).

## 5.16 Stability aspects of packaging materials

Interactions between pack and product can cause loss of potency or safety:

| Interaction | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Permeation | Gas, moisture or volatile ingredients pass through the pack wall | Moisture entering plastic bottles; loss of volatile oils |
| Leaching (migration) | Components of the pack move into the product | Plasticisers (DEHP) from PVC bags; alkali from soda-lime glass raising pH; rubber additives into injections |
| Sorption (adsorption/absorption) | Product components taken up by the pack | Glyceryl trinitrate, diazepam and insulin onto PVC; preservatives into rubber stoppers, lowering their effective level |
| Chemical reaction | Pack reacts with product | Corrosion of metal tubes; lacquers dissolving |
| Modification | Pack changes physically | Plastics swelling, cracking, discolouring; glass flaking (delamination) |
| Light transmission | Light passes through and degrades drug | Use of amber glass or opaque packs |

Packaging is therefore part of formal stability studies: the product is tested in its final pack (and in alternative packs) under long-term and accelerated conditions.

## 5.17 Quality control tests of packaging materials

**Glass containers (IP):**

1. **Hydrolytic resistance** (tells the glass type): powdered glass test (crushed glass of fixed size autoclaved with water, extracted alkali titrated with dilute acid) for Types I and III; water attack (surface) test on whole containers for Type II.
2. **Arsenic test** for glass used for aqueous parenterals.
3. **Light transmission test** for coloured (light-resistant) glass, measured with a spectrophotometer in the range that most affects drugs.
4. **Thermal shock and internal bursting pressure** tests; **annealing** check with a polariscope (residual stress).

**Plastic containers:** leakage test, collapsibility (for bags), clarity, water vapour permeability, extractables (non-volatile residue, heavy metals, buffering capacity of water extracts), toxicity tests (systemic and intracutaneous injection tests, implantation, cytotoxicity) for containers of parenterals.

**Rubber closures:** penetrability (force for needle), fragmentation (coring), self-sealing capacity, extractables, sterilisation resistance, sterility of processed closures.

**Metal collapsible tubes:** lacquer integrity and curing, leakage test, metal particles (for eye ointment tubes).

**General tests for all materials:** identity, dimensions, appearance, strength (drop test, compression test, burst strength for cartons), seal integrity (leak testing of blister and strip packs by vacuum and dye), moisture permeability.

## Remember

- Lipstick = waxes (beeswax, carnauba) + oils (castor oil dissolves eosin) + lanolin + pigments + perfume; tests: melting point, breaking point, sweating.
- Shampoo: SLS / SLES + betaine + conditioners + salt thickener; tests: foam, viscosity, pH, irritation.
- Cold cream (w/o) = beeswax + liquid paraffin + borax; vanishing cream (o/w) = stearic acid + alkali + glycerin.
- Toothpaste: abrasive + humectant + binder + SLS + sweetener + flavour + fluoride.
- Permanent hair dye = p-phenylenediamine + coupler + H2O2 + ammonia; patch test first.
- UVB 290–320 nm, UVA 320–400 nm; SPF = MED protected ÷ MED unprotected; ZnO and TiO2 are physical blockers.
- Aerosol = concentrate + propellant + container + valve + actuator; CFCs → HFA-134a/227ea; fill by cold, pressure or compressed-gas methods; hot water bath leak test.
- Pack interactions: permeation, leaching, sorption, chemical reaction, modification.
- Glass Type I borosilicate; powdered glass test for I and III, water attack test for II.
