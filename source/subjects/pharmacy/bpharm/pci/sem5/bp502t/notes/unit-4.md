# Unit 4: Parenteral products and ophthalmic preparations

Parenteral and ophthalmic products go straight into the body or onto the delicate eye, bypassing the body's normal defences. They must therefore be **sterile**, and injections must also be **free from pyrogens and visible particles**. This unit explains how such products are formulated, made, filled and tested.

## Part A: Parenteral products

## 4.1 Definition, types, advantages and limitations

**Parenteral preparations** are sterile preparations intended for administration by injection, infusion or implantation into the body, avoiding the gastrointestinal tract (Greek *para enteron*, "beside the intestine").

**Types (by form)**

| Type | Description | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solutions (injections) | Drug dissolved in a vehicle | Calcium gluconate injection |
| Suspensions | Insoluble drug dispersed; not for IV | Procaine penicillin, some depot steroids |
| Emulsions | Oil-in-water for IV nutrition or drug delivery | Fat emulsion, propofol |
| Sterile powders for reconstitution | Dry powder dissolved before use | Ampicillin, ceftriaxone |
| Large-volume parenterals (infusions) | 100 ml or more, single-dose, usually IV | Dextrose, sodium chloride, Ringer lactate |
| Small-volume parenterals | Less than 100 ml | Ampoules, vials |
| Implants | Solid pellets placed under the skin | Hormone implants |

**Types (by route):** intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intradermal, and specialised routes (intra-arterial, intrathecal, intra-articular, intracardiac, epidural).

**Advantages:** rapid action (emergencies); 100% bioavailability by IV; useful when the patient cannot take oral medicine (unconscious, vomiting); for drugs destroyed or not absorbed orally (insulin, heparin); controlled and depot effects; correction of fluid and electrolyte balance; total parenteral nutrition.

**Limitations:** pain and fear; needs trained staff; once given, hard to reverse; risk of infection, thrombophlebitis, embolism and tissue damage; expensive to make (sterility, clean rooms, pyrogen control); strict quality standards.

## 4.2 Preformulation factors and essential requirements

**Preformulation factors:** solubility in water and at physiological pH; pKa and the risk of precipitation on dilution in blood; stability in solution (hydrolysis, oxidation) and to heat (decides the sterilisation method); light sensitivity; compatibility with containers, closures and infusion fluids; particle size (for suspensions); polymorphic form.

**Essential requirements of parenterals:**

1. Sterility — free from living micro-organisms.
2. Freedom from pyrogens (bacterial endotoxins).
3. Freedom from visible and limited sub-visible particles.
4. Stability — chemical, physical and microbiological.
5. Isotonicity with body fluids (as far as possible), and a pH close to 7.4 where stability allows.
6. Correct and uniform content and volume.
7. Compatibility with containers and closures.

## 4.3 Vehicles

| Vehicle | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Water for Injections (WFI) | Main vehicle; prepared by distillation (multiple-effect stills) or an equally effective validated process; must be pyrogen-free; stored hot (about 80 °C) and circulated to prevent growth |
| Sterile Water for Injections | WFI sterilised in single-dose containers for dissolving powders |
| Bacteriostatic water | Contains a preservative; not for neonates or large volumes |
| Water-miscible cosolvents | Ethanol, propylene glycol, polyethylene glycol, glycerin — for poorly water-soluble drugs (diazepam, phenytoin, digoxin) |
| Non-aqueous (oily) vehicles | Fixed oils such as sesame, arachis, cottonseed, ethyl oleate; for IM depot injections only (testosterone, progesterone esters); must be free of mineral oil, rancidity and moisture |

## 4.4 Additives (excipients) in parenterals

| Additive | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antimicrobial preservatives | Multi-dose containers; not used in large-volume infusions or intrathecal/intracardiac injections | Benzyl alcohol, phenol, cresol, chlorocresol, methyl and propyl paraben, benzalkonium chloride, thiomersal |
| Antioxidants | Prevent oxidation | Sodium metabisulphite, sodium bisulphite, ascorbic acid, BHA, BHT, tocopherols |
| Chelating agents | Bind metal ions that catalyse oxidation | Disodium edetate |
| Buffers | Hold pH for stability and comfort | Acetate, citrate, phosphate |
| Tonicity agents | Make isotonic | Sodium chloride, dextrose, glycerin |
| Solubilisers / surfactants | Increase solubility, wet particles | Polysorbate 80, lecithin, cyclodextrins |
| Suspending agents | Suspensions | Sodium CMC, methylcellulose |
| Bulking agents | Lyophilised cakes | Mannitol, lactose, glycine |
| Local anaesthetic | Reduce pain | Benzyl alcohol, lignocaine |
| Inert gases | Displace oxygen | Nitrogen, carbon dioxide |

## 4.5 Importance of isotonicity

A solution is **isotonic** when it has the same osmotic pressure as body fluids (blood, tears), so cells neither swell nor shrink. **0.9% w/v sodium chloride** is isotonic with blood; blood and tears freeze at about −0.52 °C.

- **Hypotonic** solutions make red cells swell and burst (haemolysis).
- **Hypertonic** solutions make cells shrink (crenation) and cause pain and tissue irritation.
- Isotonicity matters most for IV infusions, subcutaneous and intrathecal injections and eye preparations. Hypertonic IV solutions can be given slowly into large veins (e.g. total parenteral nutrition through central lines).

**Adjusting tonicity — freezing-point method:** W = (0.52 − a)/b, where W is g of adjusting substance per 100 ml, a is the freezing-point depression of the unadjusted solution and b is the depression caused by 1% w/v of the adjusting substance (0.576 °C for NaCl).

**Worked example:** a 1% w/v solution of a drug has a freezing-point depression of 0.10 °C. W = (0.52 − 0.10)/0.576 = 0.73 g NaCl per 100 ml.

Other methods: sodium chloride equivalent (E) method and the White–Vincent method (V = w × E × 111.1).

## 4.6 Production procedure

```text
Cleaning of containers & closures → washing, rinsing with WFI → depyrogenation of glass (dry heat)
                                             ↓
Compounding: weigh, dissolve in WFI, add additives, adjust pH and volume (in clean area)
                                             ↓
Filtration: clarifying filtration, then sterilising filtration (0.22 µm) where used
                                             ↓
Filling into ampoules/vials/bags in an aseptic area → sealing (ampoules) or stoppering and capping (vials)
                                             ↓
Terminal sterilisation (autoclave, e.g. 121 °C for 15 minutes) if the product can stand heat
                                             ↓
Quality control tests → inspection for particles and leaks → labelling and packing → release
```

**Sterilisation methods:**

| Method | Conditions (typical) | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moist heat (autoclave) | 121 °C for 15 minutes (or validated equivalents) | Aqueous solutions in sealed containers, rubber closures, garments |
| Dry heat (hot-air oven/tunnel) | 160 °C for 2 hours or equivalent; higher temperatures (about 250 °C) for depyrogenation | Glassware, metal items, oils, powders |
| Filtration | 0.22 µm membrane | Heat-sensitive solutions |
| Ionising radiation | Gamma rays, electron beam | Disposables, some powders |
| Gas (ethylene oxide) | Controlled humidity and temperature | Plastic devices, not solutions |

**Removal of pyrogens:** pyrogens (mainly bacterial endotoxins, lipopolysaccharides from Gram-negative bacteria) are heat-stable and pass through ordinary filters. They are prevented by using WFI, clean materials and good hygiene, and removed from glassware by dry heat at high temperature (about 250 °C) or chemical treatment.

## 4.7 Production facilities and controls

**Layout of a parenteral unit (areas of increasing cleanliness):**

```text
Clean-up area (washing) → Compounding area → Aseptic filling area (core, cleanest)
       ↑ material airlocks, pass boxes, personnel change rooms ↑
Quarantine & packaging areas outside
```

**Clean room grades (EU/WHO GMP, used in Schedule M):**

| Grade | Use | Equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Aseptic filling zone, open containers (laminar airflow) | ISO 5 (old class 100) |
| B | Background to Grade A | ISO 5 at rest, ISO 7 in operation |
| C | Less critical steps, e.g. preparation of solutions to be filtered | ISO 7 at rest |
| D | Handling components after washing | ISO 8 at rest |

**Environmental controls:** HEPA-filtered air (removes at least 99.97% of 0.3 µm particles); laminar (unidirectional) airflow over critical zones; positive pressure cascades from clean to less clean rooms; control of temperature and humidity; smooth, non-shedding, easily cleaned walls, floors and ceilings with coved corners; no sinks or drains in aseptic areas; monitoring of air particles, settle plates, contact plates and surfaces.

**Personnel:** trained, healthy staff; sterile gowns, hoods, masks, gloves and boots; gowning procedures; minimum number of people and movements in aseptic rooms.

## 4.8 Aseptic processing

**Aseptic processing** is filling a product that has been sterilised (by filtration or other methods) into sterile containers, and sealing them, in an environment that prevents microbial contamination. Used when the product cannot be terminally sterilised (proteins, many antibiotics, lyophilised products).

Key points: sterile components (containers, stoppers, equipment), sterile filtration of solutions, Grade A filling under laminar airflow with Grade B background, isolators and restricted access barrier systems, blow–fill–seal machines, and **media fill (process simulation)** runs with growth medium in place of product to prove the process keeps out contamination.

## 4.9 Formulation of injections, sterile powders, LVPs and lyophilised products

**Injections (solutions):** drug + WFI + buffer + tonicity agent ± antioxidant, chelating agent and preservative; pH chosen for stability and comfort; filled under nitrogen if oxidisable. Example: ascorbic acid injection — ascorbic acid neutralised partly with sodium bicarbonate, edetate disodium, filled under nitrogen.

**Sterile powders:** for drugs unstable in solution (many penicillins and cephalosporins). Made by (i) sterile crystallisation and aseptic powder filling into vials, (ii) spray drying, or (iii) freeze drying. Reconstituted with sterile water before use. Need good flow for accurate powder filling and low moisture.

**Large-volume parenterals (LVPs):** 100 ml or more, single-dose, given by IV infusion; must be sterile, pyrogen-free, free of particles and, as far as possible, isotonic; must **not contain antimicrobial preservatives**; terminally sterilised. Examples: dextrose 5%, sodium chloride 0.9%, Ringer's and Ringer lactate, amino acids, mannitol, fat emulsions, plasma expanders (dextran). Containers: glass bottles, PVC or non-PVC plastic bags, blow–fill–seal bottles.

**Lyophilised (freeze-dried) products:** water is removed from a frozen product by sublimation under vacuum, giving a dry cake that dissolves quickly.

```text
Freezing (below the eutectic or collapse temperature)
   → Primary drying (ice sublimes under vacuum; shelf heated gently)
   → Secondary drying (bound water removed by desorption at higher shelf temperature)
   → Stoppering under vacuum or nitrogen inside the chamber → capping
```

Uses: proteins, vaccines, some antibiotics, heat- and moisture-sensitive drugs. Formulation includes bulking agents (mannitol, glycine), cryoprotectants and lyoprotectants (sucrose, trehalose) and buffers. Advantages: long shelf life, quick reconstitution, no heat. Disadvantages: costly equipment, long cycle, risk of collapse or melt-back.

## 4.10 Containers and closures

**Glass** (IP types):

| Type | Composition | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Type I | Borosilicate glass; high hydrolytic resistance | Most parenterals, including alkaline and unbuffered products |
| Type II | Soda-lime glass with surface treatment (de-alkalised) | Acidic and neutral aqueous products, many infusion bottles |
| Type III | Soda-lime glass, untreated | Non-aqueous injections and dry powders (if suitable) |

**Plastics:** polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC and non-PVC bags for infusions; advantages of light weight and no breakage; concerns of leaching (plasticiser such as DEHP), permeation and sorption of drugs (e.g. glyceryl trinitrate, diazepam to PVC).

**Rubber closures:** natural, butyl, chlorobutyl, bromobutyl and silicone rubbers. Butyl-type rubbers have low gas and moisture permeability and are preferred. Problems: leaching of additives, sorption of preservatives, coring (pieces cut by the needle), fragmentation. Closures are washed, siliconised, sterilised and often coated (e.g. with fluoropolymer).

**Selection of containers and closures** depends on chemical compatibility, protection from light and moisture, single- or multi-dose use, sterilisation method, and convenience.

## 4.11 Filling and sealing of ampoules, vials and infusion fluids

- **Ampoules:** filled with a needle reaching below the neck to avoid wetting it; nitrogen flushing if required. **Sealing:** tip sealing (melting the tip — may leave a weak bead) or **pull sealing** (heating the neck while rotating and pulling the top off; gives a more reliable seal). Ampoule sealing machines combine filling and sealing.
- **Vials:** filled by volumetric piston or peristaltic pumps; rubber stopper inserted; aluminium seal crimped on (flip-off caps).
- **Infusion fluids:** bottles or bags filled by volumetric or gravity fillers in clean areas, stoppered and capped or heat-sealed; blow–fill–seal makes, fills and seals plastic containers in one step.
- **Overfill:** a small excess volume is added so that the labelled volume can be withdrawn (extractable volume).

## 4.12 Quality control tests of parenteral products

| Test | Outline |
| --- | --- |
| Sterility test (IP) | Membrane filtration (preferred) or direct inoculation into fluid thioglycollate medium (incubated at 30–35 °C, mainly for anaerobes and bacteria) and soybean–casein digest medium (20–25 °C, fungi and aerobes); incubate 14 days; no growth = passes |
| Bacterial endotoxin test (BET) | Limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) reacts with endotoxin: gel-clot, turbidimetric or chromogenic methods |
| Pyrogen test (rabbit test) | Product injected into the ear vein of healthy rabbits; rectal temperature recorded for 3 hours; rise compared with pharmacopoeial limits |
| Particulate matter | Visible: inspection against black and white backgrounds under good light. Sub-visible: light obscuration or microscopy. For LVPs the usual limits are not more than 25 particles per ml of 10 µm or larger and not more than 3 per ml of 25 µm or larger; for SVPs, not more than 6000 per container of 10 µm or larger and 600 of 25 µm or larger |
| Leaker test (ampoules) | Ampoules immersed in coloured dye (methylene blue) under vacuum; leaking ampoules take up dye. Vials are tested by other integrity tests |
| Others | Extractable volume, pH, assay, clarity, uniformity of content/weight (powders), osmolality, bacterial endotoxins limit, container closure integrity |

## Part B: Ophthalmic preparations

## 4.13 Introduction and formulation considerations

**Ophthalmic preparations** are sterile products applied to the eye surface or into the eye: eye drops (solutions and suspensions), eye ointments, eye lotions, gels, inserts and intraocular injections.

The eye has little defence: tears drain quickly through the nasolacrimal duct, so only a small part of a drop stays in contact. An infected eye (e.g. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* contamination) can lose sight quickly, so sterility is essential.

**Formulation considerations**

1. **Sterility and preservation:** sterile; multi-dose containers need a preservative — benzalkonium chloride (often around 0.01%), chlorobutanol, phenylmercuric nitrate or acetate, thiomersal, chlorhexidine; often with disodium edetate to improve activity against *Pseudomonas*.
2. **Tonicity:** close to tears (0.9% NaCl equivalent); the eye tolerates a fairly wide range without much discomfort.
3. **pH and buffering:** tears are near pH 7.4; a pH close to this is most comfortable, but some drugs need a lower pH for stability (pilocarpine, adrenaline); low buffer capacity lets tears bring the pH back quickly.
4. **Viscosity:** increasing viscosity (methylcellulose, HPMC, polyvinyl alcohol, carbomer) prolongs contact time.
5. **Clarity:** solutions free from particles (filtration).
6. **Stability:** antioxidants (sodium metabisulphite) for oxidisable drugs.
7. **Particle size:** suspensions and ointments must contain very fine particles to avoid irritation.
8. **Packaging:** sterile, tamper-evident containers.

## 4.14 Formulation of eye drops, eye ointments and eye lotions

| Preparation | Typical composition |
| --- | --- |
| Eye drops (solution) | Drug, buffer, tonicity agent, preservative, viscosity agent, antioxidant, purified water (sterile); e.g. chloramphenicol, timolol, pilocarpine eye drops |
| Eye drops (suspension) | Very fine drug, suspending agent, wetting agent, preservative; e.g. prednisolone acetate |
| Eye ointment | Drug in a sterile, non-irritant base, commonly yellow or white soft paraffin with liquid paraffin and wool fat (wool fat helps take up water); e.g. chloramphenicol, tetracycline eye ointments |
| Eye lotion | Large-volume sterile isotonic solution for washing the eye (first aid), e.g. sodium chloride eye lotion; usually without preservative, single-use |

## 4.15 Methods of preparation

- **Eye drops:** dissolve ingredients in water → filter to clarify → sterilise by (a) heating in the final sealed container (autoclave) if stable, or (b) sterile filtration (0.22 µm) and aseptic filling into sterile containers.
- **Eye ointments:** base melted, filtered hot and sterilised by dry heat (paraffins withstand dry heat); drug finely powdered and sterilised separately or dissolved; incorporated aseptically; filled into sterile collapsible tubes.
- **Eye lotions:** prepared like solutions and sterilised by autoclaving in their final containers.

## 4.16 Labelling and containers

**Containers:** glass or plastic dropper bottles; plastic squeeze bottles (LDPE) with dropper tips; single-dose units (blow–fill–seal); small collapsible metal or plastic tubes with narrow nozzles for ointments; tamper-evident seals. Containers protect from light and contamination and must not react with the product.

**Labelling:** name and strength of drug, preservative, "sterile", "not to be used more than 4 weeks after first opening" or the stated period, storage conditions, batch number, expiry date, "for external use only" where relevant, and directions. Eye lotions: "not for injection" and "use once and discard".

## 4.17 Evaluation of ophthalmic preparations

1. Sterility test.
2. Clarity and particle examination for solutions; particle size for suspensions and ointments.
3. pH and tonicity (osmolality).
4. Viscosity.
5. Drug content (assay) and preservative content.
6. Preservative efficacy (antimicrobial effectiveness) test.
7. For eye ointments: metal particles test (IP) and leakage test of tubes.
8. Container–closure integrity and stability studies; irritation tests on development products.

## Remember

- Parenteral essentials: sterile, pyrogen-free, particle-free, stable, isotonic where possible.
- WFI by distillation; stored hot and circulated. Oily vehicles only for IM.
- No preservatives in LVPs or intrathecal/intracardiac injections.
- Isotonic = 0.9% NaCl; freezing point −0.52 °C; W = (0.52 − a)/b with b = 0.576 for NaCl.
- Autoclave 121 °C 15 min; dry heat 160 °C 2 h; depyrogenation at about 250 °C; filtration 0.22 µm.
- Grade A = ISO 5 under laminar airflow; HEPA ≥ 99.97% at 0.3 µm.
- Lyophilisation: freezing → primary drying (sublimation) → secondary drying (desorption).
- Glass: Type I borosilicate, Type II treated soda-lime, Type III soda-lime.
- Sterility test: thioglycollate (30–35 °C) + soybean–casein digest (20–25 °C), 14 days; BET by LAL; leaker test with methylene blue.
- Eye preparations: sterile; benzalkonium chloride + edetate; viscosity agents prolong contact; eye ointment base = soft paraffin + liquid paraffin + wool fat.
