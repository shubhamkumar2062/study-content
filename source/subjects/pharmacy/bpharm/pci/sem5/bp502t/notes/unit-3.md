# Unit 3: Capsules and pellets

Capsules hold a drug inside a soluble shell made mainly of gelatin. They come in two kinds: **hard gelatin capsules**, filled with powders, granules or pellets, and **soft gelatin capsules**, filled mostly with liquids or pastes and sealed in one piece. This unit also covers **pellets**, small free-flowing spheres often filled into hard capsules.

## 3.1 Capsules: introduction

A **capsule** is a solid dosage form in which the drug, with or without excipients, is enclosed in a hard or soft soluble shell.

**Advantages:** tasteless and odourless to swallow; easy to fill in small or large batches; fewer excipients and less pressure than tablets, so good for drugs that compress poorly; quick release (shell dissolves in a few minutes); attractive colours for identification; can hold powders, pellets, tablets, semisolids or liquids; soft capsules give accurate liquid doses and better bioavailability for oily, poorly soluble drugs.

**Disadvantages:** not for very soluble salts that irritate the stomach (high local concentration, e.g. potassium chloride, bromides); not for highly efflorescent or deliquescent materials (they soften or dry out the shell); not suited to children who cannot swallow; gelatin is of animal origin (some patients refuse it); shells are sensitive to humidity and heat.

**Gelatin:** obtained by partial hydrolysis of collagen from animal skin, bones and connective tissue.

| Type | Process | Source | Isoelectric point (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Type A | Acid treatment | Mainly pork skin | pH 7–9 |
| Type B | Alkali (lime) treatment | Bones and cattle hides | pH 4.7–5 |

Mixtures of A and B are used to balance clarity, strength and plasticity. Gel strength is measured as **Bloom strength** (grams of force needed to depress a standard plunger into a set gel); capsule gelatin has a fairly high Bloom value. Viscosity controls the shell thickness.

**Non-gelatin shells:** hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and pullulan capsules are used for vegetarian products and moisture-sensitive drugs, because they hold less water.

## 3.2 Hard gelatin capsules

### a. Production of hard gelatin capsule shells

Shells are made in two pieces, a **body** and a slightly wider **cap**, by **dip moulding** on automatic machines.

```text
1 Dipping        stainless steel pins (moulds), lubricated, dipped in a warm gelatin solution
2 Spinning       pins rotated so the film spreads evenly
3 Drying         cool, controlled-humidity air sets and dries the film
4 Stripping      dried shells pulled off the pins
5 Trimming       cut to exact length
6 Joining        caps and bodies pre-locked together
7 Printing       identification ink (optional)
8 Sorting/packing in moisture-proof cartons
```

**Shell composition:** gelatin; colourants (dyes, iron oxides); opacifier (titanium dioxide); preservatives; sometimes a surfactant for wetting the pins; water. Hard shells contain about 13–16% moisture. Below this range they become brittle; above it they soften and stick.

**Locking designs:** traditional plain shells, and self-locking shells (Coni-Snap, Lok-Cap and similar) with grooves or dimples that hold the cap firmly and prevent separation on high-speed machines. Tapered body rims help the cap fit without splitting.

### b. Size of capsules

Sizes are numbered from 000 (largest) to 5 (smallest).

| Size | 000 | 00 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Approx. volume (ml) | 1.37 | 0.95 | 0.68 | 0.50 | 0.37 | 0.30 | 0.21 | 0.13 |

Size 000 is rarely used for humans because it is hard to swallow; sizes 0 to 4 are common. Larger sizes (e.g. 10, 11, 12) are for veterinary use.

**Choosing the size:** the fill weight is decided by the dose and the bulk (tapped) density of the powder. Fill volume needed = fill weight ÷ tapped density. Example: 300 mg of a blend of tapped density 0.6 g/ml needs 0.5 ml → size 1 capsule. If the volume is not filled, diluent is added; the smallest size that holds the dose is chosen.

### c. Filling of hard gelatin capsules

**Formulation of the fill:** drug + diluent (lactose, microcrystalline cellulose, starch) + glidant (colloidal silica) + lubricant (magnesium stearate) + disintegrant/wetting agent where needed (sodium lauryl sulphate helps hydrophobic drugs). The powder must flow well and, for dosator and tamping machines, form a loose plug.

**Steps:** rectification (orientation of empty capsules, body down) → separation of cap from body → filling of the body → joining (closing) the cap → ejection.

| Method | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Hand filling | Powder spread on a tile and capsules punched into it; for small extemporaneous lots |
| Hand-operated filling machine (e.g. Feton type) | Plate with 100 to 300 holes; bodies in holes, powder spread and levelled, caps replaced |
| Auger (screw) fill | Rotating auger pushes powder into bodies; older semi-automatic machines |
| Vibratory fill | Vibration settles powder into bodies |
| Dosator (piston-tamp, e.g. Zanasi type) | Hollow tube dips into powder bed, piston forms a plug, ejects it into the body |
| Dosing disc (tamping, e.g. Bosch/Höfliger type) | Powder tamped several times into holes of a disc to form plugs, then pushed into bodies |

Pellets, granules, tablets and semisolid or liquid fills (with band sealing) can also be filled into hard shells.

### d. Finishing

After filling, capsules are cleaned and polished to remove adhering powder: by tumbling with sodium chloride crystals, by cloth-lined pans, by brushes, or by automatic de-dusting and polishing machines. Sealing or banding (a strip of gelatin around the joint) prevents tampering and leakage of liquid fills.

### e. Special techniques of formulation

- **Enteric or modified-release capsules:** coating the filled capsule, filling enteric-coated pellets, or using acid-resistant shells.
- **Sustained release:** filling a mix of pellets with different coatings.
- **Liquid and semisolid fills:** thermosoftening or thixotropic fills filled hot and sealed by banding.
- **Combination fills:** tablet + powder, pellets + tablet, for incompatible drugs kept apart.
- **Taste and odour masking** of drugs such as fish oils, and protection of light-sensitive drugs by opaque shells.

### f. Manufacturing defects of hard capsules

Splitting of caps or bodies, telescoping (cap pushed down over the body too far), dented or crushed shells, double caps, uneven or short fill (weight variation), powder on the outside, broken or loose caps, holes or cracks, bubbles, colour or printing faults, brittleness (too dry) or softness and sticking (too humid).

### g. In-process and final product quality control tests

**In process:** fill weight checks at intervals, appearance, locking, moisture of shells and powder, flow of the blend.

**Finished product:**

1. **Appearance:** uniform size, colour, shape, no defects, clean printing.
2. **Uniformity of weight (IP):** weigh 20 intact capsules individually, then empty each and weigh the shells to get the content weight. Not more than two may differ from the average content weight by more than the percentage below, and none by more than twice that percentage.

| Average content weight | Allowed deviation |
| --- | --- |
| Less than 300 mg | 10% |
| 300 mg or more | 7.5% |

3. **Disintegration test:** IP apparatus as for tablets with discs; hard gelatin capsules usually must disintegrate within 30 minutes unless the monograph says otherwise; enteric capsules are tested first in acid, then in buffer.
4. **Dissolution test:** basket or paddle (with a sinker so capsules do not float).
5. **Uniformity of content** for low-dose capsules.
6. **Assay**, moisture content and microbial limits where required.

## 3.3 Soft gelatin capsules

**Soft gelatin capsules (softgels)** are one-piece, hermetically sealed shells made of gelatin with a plasticiser, filled usually with liquids, solutions, suspensions or pastes. They may be round, oval, oblong or tube-shaped.

**Uses:** oily vitamins (A, D, E), fish oils, cardiac glycosides and other potent drugs needing exact doses, poorly soluble drugs dissolved in oils or polyethylene glycols (better absorption), cyclosporin-type lipid systems, nifedipine, paracetamol liquid-fill, chewable and bath capsules, suppository and vaginal capsules.

### a. Nature of the shell

| Component | Role | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gelatin | Film former | Type A/B blends |
| Plasticiser | Makes the shell elastic | Glycerin, sorbitol, propylene glycol |
| Water | Solvent; much removed during drying | Purified water |
| Others | Appearance, protection | Colours, opacifier (titanium dioxide), preservatives, flavours |

The ratio of dry plasticiser to dry gelatin decides how soft or hard the shell is; ratios of roughly 0.4 to 1.0 cover shells from relatively hard to very soft. The finished shell keeps some water (about 6–10%).

### b. Nature of the capsule content

The fill must not dissolve, soften or harden the shell. Suitable: vegetable and mineral oils, medium-chain triglycerides, PEG 400 and 600, non-ionic surfactants, suspensions of solids in these. Unsuitable: water above about 5%, low-molecular-weight water-soluble solvents (ethanol, small alcohols) in large amounts, aldehydes (cross-link gelatin), emulsions (water migrates), strongly acidic (below about pH 2.5) or alkaline (above about pH 7.5) fills.

### c. Size of soft capsules

Soft capsules are described by shape and by the volume they hold, expressed in **minims** (1 minim ≈ 0.0616 ml), for example "10 oblong" or "5 round", rather than by the 000–5 numbers used for hard shells.

### d. Base adsorption and minim/gram factor

When a solid drug is suspended in a liquid base for filling, two factors help calculate the fill:

- **Base adsorption (BA):** grams of liquid base needed to wet and make 1 g of solid into a fillable, flowing paste. BA = weight of base ÷ weight of solid.
- **Minim/gram factor (M/g):** the volume in minims occupied by 1 g of solid plus the base needed to suspend it (its base adsorption). It is found by measuring the volume of the mixture. Fill volume per capsule = M/g × weight of solid per capsule.

**Worked example (illustrative figures):** a solid has BA = 0.7, so each gram of solid needs 0.7 g of base. If the mixture of 1 g solid + 0.7 g base occupies 25 minims, M/g = 25. A dose of 0.4 g of solid per capsule needs 0.4 × 25 = 10 minims of fill, so a capsule holding about 10 minims is chosen. Lower base adsorption gives a smaller capsule, so solids of fine, regular particles are preferred.

### e. Production of soft capsules

| Method | Principle |
| --- | --- |
| Plate process | Warm gelatin sheet laid over a die plate, fill poured in, second sheet placed on top, upper plate pressed to seal and cut; batch method, now largely historical |
| Rotary die process (Scherer) | Two gelatin ribbons pass between two rotating dies; fill injected between ribbons through a wedge just as the dies seal and cut the capsule; continuous, accurate, most widely used |
| Reciprocating die (Norton) | Ribbons pass between vertical dies that open and close; similar to rotary |
| Accogel process | Rotary process that can fill dry powders into soft shells |
| Seamless (bubble) method | Concentric nozzle drops gelatin and fill together into cold oil; makes seamless spheres |

After forming, capsules are washed to remove lubricant oil, tumble-dried and then tray-dried in low-humidity tunnels to the right moisture and hardness.

### f. In-process and final quality control tests (soft capsules)

In process: ribbon thickness, seal thickness and strength, fill weight, shell weight, shell moisture, hardness after drying. Final: appearance and leaks, uniformity of weight, uniformity of content, disintegration and dissolution, assay, moisture, microbial limits, burst strength / seal integrity, freedom from leaks after storage.

### g. Packing, storage and stability testing

- **Packing:** blister packs (PVC/PVDC with aluminium foil), strips, glass or HDPE bottles with desiccant where needed.
- **Storage:** cool, dry place away from heat and high humidity; high humidity makes shells soft and sticky, heat can melt or deform them, and very low humidity makes them brittle.
- **Stability testing:** shell–fill interaction (migration of fill components into the shell, cross-linking of gelatin by aldehydes that slows dissolution), physical changes (hardening, softening, leakage, colour), chemical assay over time under long-term and accelerated conditions, and dissolution changes. Cross-linking shows as a skin (pellicle) that slows dissolution; enzymes (pepsin or pancreatin) in the medium may be allowed to digest it in dissolution testing.

### h. Applications of soft capsules

Oral (liquids, oils, vitamins, potent drugs), chewable softgels, vaginal and rectal capsules, topical "twist-off" single-dose capsules, bath and cosmetic capsules, and better bioavailability for poorly water-soluble drugs presented in solution.

## 3.4 Pellets

**Pellets** are small, free-flowing, near-spherical agglomerates of drug and excipients, usually 0.5 to 1.5 mm in size, made for oral modified-release or multiparticulate systems. They are filled into hard capsules or compressed into tablets.

### a. Advantages of pellets

1. Spread evenly through the gut, so absorption is more uniform and less affected by stomach emptying.
2. Less risk of dose dumping than a single-unit sustained-release tablet.
3. Lower risk of local irritation.
4. Good flow and packing, uniform fill weights.
5. Pellets of different drugs or release rates can be mixed in one capsule.
6. Smooth surface suits even coating.

### b. Formulation requirements

| Component | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug | Active | — |
| Spheronisation aid / filler | Plasticity and water holding for extrusion and rounding | Microcrystalline cellulose (most important), lactose, mannitol |
| Binder | Strength | Povidone, HPMC |
| Granulating liquid | Wets mass | Water, water–alcohol |
| Others | Release control, disintegration, stability | Polymers, surfactants, buffers |
| Starter cores (layering) | Base to build on | Sugar spheres (non-pareils), MCC spheres |

Ideal pellets are spherical, of narrow size range, with a smooth surface, good strength and low friability, and good flow.

### c. Pelletisation processes

1. **Extrusion–spheronisation (most common):**

```text
Dry mixing → wet massing (granulating liquid) → extrusion through a screen (cylindrical extrudates)
   → spheronisation (extrudates broken and rolled into spheres on a rotating friction plate)
   → drying → sieving/size classification → coating (optional)
```

2. **Powder layering:** dry drug powder sprinkled onto starter cores wetted with binder solution in a coating pan or rotor.
3. **Solution or suspension layering:** drug solution or suspension sprayed onto inert cores in a fluidised bed (Wurster) or pan.
4. **Spray drying and spray congealing:** droplets dried by hot air (spray drying) or solidified by cooling of a melt (spray congealing) to give small spheres.
5. **Balling (spherical agglomeration):** powder wetted and tumbled in a drum or pan so particles roll into spheres.
6. **Cryopelletisation:** drops of liquid formulation frozen in liquid nitrogen, then freeze-dried.
7. **Melt pelletisation / hot-melt extrusion** with molten binders.
8. **Compression** into mini-tablets (a related approach).

### d. Equipment for manufacture of pellets

- **Extruders:** screw (axial or radial), sieve and basket, roll and ram extruders.
- **Spheroniser (marumeriser):** a bowl with a fast-rotating grooved friction plate at the bottom.
- **Fluidised bed with Wurster insert** or rotor (tangential spray) for layering and coating.
- **Coating pans** for powder layering.
- **Centrifugal (rotary) granulators** (e.g. CF granulator) that combine layering and rounding.
- **Spray dryers and spray congealers.**
- **Fluid-bed or tray dryers** and vibratory sieves for sizing.

**Evaluation of pellets:** size distribution (sieve analysis), shape (sphericity, aspect ratio by image analysis), surface (microscopy), density, flow, friability and crushing strength, drug content, dissolution and release profile.

## Remember

- Gelatin Type A (acid, pork skin, IEP 7–9); Type B (alkali, bones/hides, IEP 4.7–5). Bloom strength = gel strength.
- Hard shells: dip moulding (dip → spin → dry → strip → trim → join); 13–16% moisture.
- Capsule sizes 000 (1.37 ml) to 5 (0.13 ml); choose by fill weight ÷ tapped density.
- Filling machines: hand plate, auger, dosator (Zanasi), dosing disc (Bosch/Höfliger).
- IP weight variation of capsules (20): less than 300 mg → 10%; 300 mg or more → 7.5%.
- Soft capsules: gelatin + plasticiser (glycerin/sorbitol) + water; rotary die process; base adsorption = g base/g solid; minim/gram factor = minims per g of solid in its base.
- Unsuitable softgel fills: much water, aldehydes, emulsions, strongly acidic or alkaline fills.
- Pellets 0.5–1.5 mm; extrusion–spheronisation with microcrystalline cellulose; also layering, spray drying/congealing, cryopelletisation.
