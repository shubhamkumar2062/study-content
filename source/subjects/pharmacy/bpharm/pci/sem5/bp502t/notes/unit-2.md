# Unit 2: Tablets and liquid orals

Tablets are the most widely used dosage form in India and worldwide, and this is the heaviest unit of the subject. The first part covers how tablets are formulated, made, coated and tested; the second part covers the manufacture and evaluation of syrups, elixirs, suspensions and emulsions.

## Part A: Tablets

## 2.1 Introduction and ideal characteristics

A **tablet** is a solid unit dosage form containing one or more drugs, with or without excipients, made by compressing (or occasionally moulding) a powder or granules into a fixed shape.

**Advantages:** accurate dose in each unit; cheap to make in large numbers; easy to carry, swallow, pack and identify; better chemical stability than liquids; taste can be masked by coating; can be designed for delayed or sustained release.

**Disadvantages:** hard for children, elderly and unconscious patients to swallow; poorly compressible or poorly wettable drugs are difficult to formulate; slow onset compared with injections; some drugs irritate the gut.

**Ideal characteristics of a tablet**

1. Elegant appearance, free of cracks, chips, specks and contamination.
2. Enough strength to survive packing and transport, yet able to disintegrate and dissolve when swallowed.
3. Correct and uniform weight and drug content.
4. Releases the drug in a predictable, reproducible way (bioavailable).
5. Chemically and physically stable through its shelf life.
6. Acceptable to the patient in size, shape, taste and odour.

## 2.2 Classification of tablets

| Group | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Oral, swallowed | Compressed (plain), multiple compressed (layered, compression-coated), sugar-coated, film-coated, enteric-coated, chewable, controlled/sustained release |
| Used in the mouth | Buccal, sublingual (glyceryl trinitrate), lozenges and troches, dental cones, mouth-dissolving (orally disintegrating) |
| Taken after dissolving or dispersing | Effervescent, dispersible, soluble tablets |
| Other routes | Vaginal tablets, implants, hypodermic tablets (obsolete) |

## 2.3 Excipients used in tablets

| Class | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diluents (fillers) | Give bulk to make a tablet of practical size | Lactose, microcrystalline cellulose, dibasic calcium phosphate, starch, mannitol (chewable tablets), sucrose |
| Binders | Hold particles together in granules and tablets | Starch paste (about 5–10%), gelatin, acacia, polyvinylpyrrolidone (povidone), HPMC, methylcellulose, sucrose solution |
| Disintegrants | Break the tablet apart in fluid | Starch (about 5–10%), microcrystalline cellulose; superdisintegrants at low levels: croscarmellose sodium, sodium starch glycolate, crospovidone |
| Glidants | Improve flow of granules into the die | Colloidal silicon dioxide, talc |
| Lubricants | Reduce friction between tablet and die wall; help ejection | Magnesium stearate (commonly around 0.25–1%), stearic acid, talc, PEG, sodium stearyl fumarate |
| Antiadherents | Stop sticking to punch faces | Talc, colloidal silica, corn starch |
| Others | Appearance, taste, absorption, stability | Colours (lakes), flavours, sweeteners (saccharin, aspartame), wetting agents (sodium lauryl sulphate), adsorbents, buffers |

**How disintegrants work:** swelling (sodium starch glycolate), wicking or capillary action (microcrystalline cellulose, crospovidone), deformation recovery, and gas release (effervescent couple).

**Methods of adding disintegrant:** intragranular (inside the granules), extragranular (mixed with dry granules before compression) or both; adding part on each side gives fastest break-up into fine particles.

## 2.4 Granulation methods

**Why granulate?** To improve flow, improve compressibility, prevent segregation of ingredients, give uniform content, reduce dust, and control density.

### a. Wet granulation

```text
Weigh and sift drug + diluent (+ part of disintegrant)
   → dry mix
   → add binder solution → wet massing (dough-like mass)
   → wet screening (about 6–12 mesh)
   → drying (tray dryer or fluidised bed dryer)
   → dry screening (about 14–20 mesh)
   → add lubricant, glidant, extragranular disintegrant → final blend
   → compression
```

- **Equipment:** planetary mixer, sigma-blade mixer, high-shear mixer granulator (mixes, wets and granulates in one bowl), fluidised bed granulator (spray binder onto a fluidised powder; mixes, granulates and dries in one unit), oscillating granulator and multimill for sizing.
- **Advantages:** good flow and compressibility; uniform content of low-dose drugs; hydrophobic drugs become more wettable.
- **Disadvantages:** many steps, time, space and energy; not for moisture- or heat-sensitive drugs.
- **End point of wet massing:** the "ball test" (mass squeezed in the hand forms a ball that breaks cleanly), or power consumption of the mixer.

### b. Dry granulation

Used for moisture- or heat-sensitive drugs (aspirin, some vitamins) and when the drug compresses well.

- **Slugging:** large flat tablets ("slugs") are made on a heavy-duty press, then broken and sieved into granules.
- **Roller compaction:** powder is squeezed between two counter-rotating rollers into a sheet (ribbon), which is milled into granules. More controllable and faster than slugging.

### c. Direct compression

The drug is simply blended with directly compressible excipients and compressed. Needs excipients with good flow and compressibility: spray-dried lactose, microcrystalline cellulose, dibasic calcium phosphate dihydrate, directly compressible starch, mannitol.

- **Advantages:** fewest steps, cheapest, no heat or water, faster disintegration into primary particles.
- **Limits:** high-dose drugs with poor flow cannot be made this way; risk of segregation; special excipients cost more.

## 2.5 Compression: machines and tooling

**Steps in compression:** die filling → weight adjustment (excess removed) → compression between upper and lower punches → ejection by the lower punch → tablet pushed off the die table.

| Machine | Features | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single punch (eccentric) press | One die and one pair of punches; upper punch does the compressing; noisy, intermittent | Small, for research and small batches |
| Rotary (multi-station) press | Many dies on a rotating turret; both punches move; compression between rollers; feed frame fills dies | Very high, used in industry |
| Double rotary / high-speed press | Two compression points per turn; induced (force) feeders | Even higher |

**Tablet tooling:** the set of **upper punch, lower punch and die**. Punch parts: head, barrel, stem, tip (face). Tooling standards are named B and D type (D type is larger for bigger tablets); the IPT (Indian), TSM and EU standards describe dimensions. Punch face shapes: flat-faced, flat bevel-edged, shallow, standard and deep concave, and shaped (oval, capsule). Embossing and break lines are made on punch faces. Tooling must be polished, inspected for wear and stored oiled.

**Parts of a rotary press:** hopper, feed frame, die table (turret), upper and lower punches, cams (raise and lower punches), weight-adjustment cam, pre-compression and main compression rollers, ejection cam, take-off blade.

## 2.6 Processing problems in tablet manufacture

| Problem | What you see | Main causes | Remedies |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Capping | Top or bottom cap separates | Air trapped in granules, too many fines, too dry granules, worn or deep-concave tooling, too fast compression | Pre-compression, slower speed, more binder, right moisture, remove fines, new tooling |
| Lamination | Tablet splits into layers | Same as capping; also too much lubricant | As for capping |
| Picking | Material removed from tablet surface by punch face, esp. in embossing | Moist granules, too little lubricant, sharp embossing | Dry granules, more antiadherent, better embossing design, chromium-plated punches |
| Sticking | Granules stick to punch faces or die wall | Excess moisture, low-melting ingredients, little lubricant | Dry well, more lubricant, cool room |
| Chipping | Edges break off | Damaged punches, brittle granules | Polish/replace tooling, adjust binder |
| Cracking | Fine cracks on top surface | Fast expansion, deep-concave punches | Tapered dies, adjust formula |
| Mottling | Uneven colour | Dye migration during drying, colour differences of drug and excipient | Change solvent, use lakes, mill finer |
| Double impression | Embossing appears twice | Free-rotating lower punch that turns after first impression | Anti-turning device on punches |
| Binding | Tablet hard to eject, scored sides | Too little lubricant, moist granules, rough die | More lubricant, dry, polish dies |
| Weight variation | Uneven weights | Poor flow, wide granule size range, uneven die filling, segregation | Glidant, uniform granules, induced feeder |
| Hardness variation | Uneven strength | Weight variation, punch length differences | Correct fill, uniform tooling |

## 2.7 Tablet coating

**Reasons for coating:** mask bitter taste or smell; protect from moisture, light and air; protect the stomach from the drug or the drug from stomach acid (enteric coating); control release; improve appearance and identification; make swallowing easier.

### Types of coating

1. **Sugar coating** — traditional, thick, elegant, but slow and heavy.
2. **Film coating** — thin polymer film; fast and light; most common today.
3. **Enteric coating** — resists stomach acid and dissolves in the intestine.
4. **Compression (press) coating** — a coat compressed around a core in a special press.
5. **Controlled-release coating** — insoluble polymer membranes (ethylcellulose, methacrylate copolymers).

### Sugar coating steps

```text
Core tablets (de-dusted)
 1 Sealing     → water-proof layer (shellac, CAP, zein) to keep water out of the core
 2 Subcoating  → rounds edges and builds up the tablet (sugar syrup + gum + fillers such as talc, CaCO3)
 3 Smoothing   → thin syrup coats fill irregularities (grossing)
 4 Colouring   → syrup with soluble dye or insoluble pigment (lakes)
 5 Polishing   → waxes (beeswax, carnauba) in a canvas-lined pan
 6 Printing    → edible ink for identification
```

A sugar coat may double the tablet weight; the process takes hours to days and needs skilled operators.

### Film coating: formulation of coating composition

| Component | Role | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Film-forming polymer | Forms the film | HPMC, hydroxypropyl cellulose, methylcellulose, ethylcellulose (insoluble, for release control), povidone, polyvinyl alcohol, methacrylate copolymers |
| Plasticiser | Makes the film flexible, lowers glass transition | Polyethylene glycol, propylene glycol, glycerin, triethyl citrate, diethyl phthalate, castor oil |
| Solvent | Carries the polymer | Water (preferred now), ethanol, isopropanol, acetone, methylene chloride (organic solvents are being replaced) |
| Colourant / opacifier | Colour, light protection | Iron oxides, lakes, titanium dioxide |
| Others | Flavour, surfactant, antitacking agent | Talc, polysorbates |

**Enteric coating materials:** cellulose acetate phthalate (CAP), hydroxypropyl methylcellulose phthalate (HPMCP), polyvinyl acetate phthalate, methacrylic acid copolymers (Eudragit L and S types), shellac. They carry acidic groups that stay un-ionised in the stomach and ionise and dissolve at intestinal pH.

**Uses of enteric coating:** drugs destroyed by acid (erythromycin, pancreatin, omeprazole), drugs that irritate the stomach (aspirin, NSAIDs), drugs meant for local action in the intestine.

### Methods and equipment for coating

| Equipment | How it works | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Standard (conventional) coating pan | Inclined rotating pan, coating solution ladled or sprayed, hot air blown on surface | Simple; poor drying for film coating |
| Pan with immersion tube or immersion sword | Sprays and dries through a tube/sword in the tablet bed | Better drying |
| Perforated pans (e.g. Accela-Cota, Hi-Coater, Glatt type) | Side-vented or fully perforated drum; air passes through the bed | Efficient, automated; used for film coating |
| Fluidised bed (air-suspension, Wurster) coater | Tablets or pellets suspended in an air stream; spray from bottom through a partition column | Good for small particles and pellets |
| Compression coater | Press coats a core with dry granules | No solvent |
| Electrostatic and dip coating | Charge or dipping | Specialised |

Spray guns may be airless (high pressure) or air-spray (atomised by compressed air).

### Defects in coating

| Defect | Description | Cause and remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blistering | Film detaches as bubbles | Too high drying temperature traps vapour; dry more gently |
| Chipping | Film chips off at edges | Brittle film or pan too fast; more plasticiser, gentler pan |
| Cratering | Volcano-like craters exposing the core | Coating liquid penetrates core; control spray rate and drying |
| Picking | Pieces of film torn away | Tablets stick together while wet; reduce spray rate, raise drying |
| Pitting | Small pits on surface | Core temperature above melting point of an ingredient |
| Blooming | Film goes dull after storage | Low-molecular-weight plasticiser migrates; change plasticiser or level |
| Blushing | Whitish specks or haze | Polymer precipitates from too-hot drying; lower temperature |
| Colour variation | Uneven colour | Poor mixing, uneven spray, migration of soluble dye |
| Infilling | Break line or logo filled in | Foam or droplets in the logo; less foam, better atomisation |
| Orange peel (roughness) | Bumpy, orange-skin surface | Coating liquid too viscous or dries too fast before spreading |
| Cracking | Film cracks | Internal stress; use more plasticiser or higher-molecular-weight polymer |
| Twinning | Two tablets stuck together | Flat-faced tablets, slow drying; change shape, faster drying |

## 2.8 Quality control tests for tablets

### In-process quality control (IPQC)

Checked at intervals during compression: average weight and weight variation, thickness, hardness, friability, disintegration and appearance. During granulation: moisture content (loss on drying), granule size distribution, bulk density, flow and blend uniformity.

### Finished product tests

1. **General appearance:** size, shape, colour, odour, taste, surface texture, markings.
2. **Thickness and diameter:** measured with a vernier calliper or micrometer; must stay within a narrow range for packing and for uniform appearance.
3. **Uniformity of weight (IP):** weigh 20 tablets individually and find the average. Not more than two tablets may differ from the average by more than the percentage below, and none by more than twice that percentage.

| Average weight of tablet | Allowed deviation |
| --- | --- |
| 80 mg or less | 10% |
| More than 80 mg and less than 250 mg | 7.5% |
| 250 mg or more | 5% |

4. **Hardness (crushing strength):** force needed to break a tablet diametrically. Testers: Monsanto, Pfizer, Strong-Cobb, Erweka and modern motorised testers. Not an official test; a value around 4 kg is commonly quoted as the minimum for a satisfactory conventional tablet (chewable and orally disintegrating tablets are softer, sustained-release ones harder).
5. **Friability:** Roche friabilator turns at 25 rpm for 4 minutes (100 revolutions), dropping the tablets about 6 inches each turn. For tablets of unit weight 650 mg or less, take whole tablets weighing as near as possible to 6.5 g; for heavier tablets take 10 tablets. De-dust, weigh, rotate, de-dust and reweigh. % friability = 100 × (initial − final)/initial; a loss of not more than 1% is generally acceptable, with no broken tablets.
6. **Disintegration test (IP):** basket-rack assembly with six tubes and a 10-mesh screen, raised and lowered in water (or the stated medium) at 37 ± 2 °C. Usual IP times unless the monograph says otherwise:

| Tablet type | Time |
| --- | --- |
| Uncoated | 15 minutes |
| Film-coated | 30 minutes |
| Sugar-coated | 60 minutes |
| Dispersible | 3 minutes |
| Effervescent | 5 minutes |
| Enteric-coated | No disintegration in 0.1 M HCl for 2 hours; then disintegrates in phosphate buffer pH 6.8 within 60 minutes |

7. **Dissolution test:** measures the rate at which drug goes into solution. IP Apparatus 1 (basket) and Apparatus 2 (paddle); 900 ml medium at 37 ± 0.5 °C. Acceptance by stages: S1 six units, each not less than Q + 5%; S2 six more, average of 12 not less than Q and none less than Q − 15%; S3 twelve more, average of 24 not less than Q, not more than two below Q − 15% and none below Q − 25% (Q = amount stated in the monograph).
8. **Uniformity of content:** for low-dose tablets the drug is assayed in 10 single tablets. In the classic IP test each should lie within 85–115% of the average; if one lies outside this but within 75–125%, 20 more are tested. Newer editions also give the harmonised "uniformity of dosage units" test based on an acceptance value; follow the edition your college uses.
9. **Assay:** drug content of a powdered pooled sample, within the monograph limits.
10. Others where needed: moisture, microbial limits, wetting/coating tests, uniformity of dispersion (dispersible tablets).

## Part B: Liquid orals

## 2.9 Syrups and elixirs

**Syrup:** a concentrated, viscous aqueous solution of sucrose or another sugar, with or without flavours and medicaments. **Simple Syrup IP** is about 66.7% w/w sucrose in purified water; at this strength it resists microbial growth because so little free water is available (high osmotic pressure).

**Components:** sugar (sucrose; sorbitol or glycerin in sugar-free syrups), purified water, preservatives (methyl and propyl paraben, sodium benzoate), flavours, colours, the drug, sometimes alcohol, buffers and antioxidants.

**Manufacture:** (1) solution with heat — sugar dissolved in heated water (overheating causes inversion to glucose and fructose and caramelisation, darkening); (2) agitation without heat for heat-sensitive ingredients; (3) adding sucrose to a medicated liquid; (4) percolation (water passed slowly through a bed of sucrose). Syrup is filtered, made up to volume and filled hot or cold.

**Problems:** crystallisation of sugar (cap locking) if too concentrated; fermentation if too dilute; inversion and colour change if overheated.

**Elixir:** a clear, sweetened, hydroalcoholic solution for oral use. Alcohol helps dissolve drugs that are poorly soluble in water and acts as a preservative; glycerin, propylene glycol and sorbitol are also used. Examples: paracetamol and antihistamine elixirs. Prepared by simple solution; the alcoholic and aqueous parts are mixed with care because adding water can precipitate oils (filter with talc to clarify).

## 2.10 Suspensions

A **suspension** is a coarse dispersion of insoluble solid particles in a liquid (usually aqueous) vehicle. Used for insoluble drugs, to mask taste (insoluble salt), and for drugs unstable in solution.

**Ideal suspension:** settles slowly; sediment redisperses easily on gentle shaking; does not cake; pours easily; uniform dose per spoonful; pleasant taste.

**Stokes' law:** v = d²(ρs − ρl)g / 18η. Settling slows when particle size d is small, the density difference small and viscosity η high.

**Flocculated vs deflocculated**

| Property | Flocculated | Deflocculated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Particles | Loose aggregates (flocs) | Separate particles |
| Settling | Fast | Slow |
| Sediment | Loose, high volume, easy to redisperse | Compact; forms a hard cake |
| Supernatant | Clear quickly | Stays cloudy |
| Approach | Controlled flocculation with electrolytes, surfactants or polymers | Needs a structured vehicle to prevent caking |

**Formulation components:** wetting agents (polysorbates, glycerin, alcohol) for hydrophobic powders; suspending/viscosity agents (acacia, tragacanth, sodium alginate, methylcellulose, sodium CMC, xanthan gum, bentonite, Veegum); flocculating agents (electrolytes, surfactants); preservatives; buffers; sweeteners, flavours, colours.

**Manufacture:** mill drug to fine size → wet with wetting agent → disperse suspending agent in vehicle (hydrated) → mix drug slurry with vehicle → add other ingredients → make up volume → homogenise (colloid mill) → fill with continuous stirring so each bottle gets the same content. Many antibiotics are supplied as dry powder for reconstitution.

**Evaluation of suspensions:**

- **Sedimentation volume F = Vu/Vo** (ultimate sediment volume ÷ original suspension volume); F close to 1 is desirable.
- **Degree of flocculation β = F/F∞** (sediment volume of flocculated ÷ deflocculated suspension).
- Redispersibility (number of inversions to resuspend), rheology (viscosity with a Brookfield viscometer), particle size and zeta potential, pH, density, drug content uniformity, microbial limits.

## 2.11 Emulsions

An **emulsion** is a dispersion of one liquid in another immiscible liquid as droplets, stabilised by an emulsifying agent. Oil-in-water (o/w) for oral use (masks oily taste, improves absorption); water-in-oil (w/o) for many topical creams.

**Emulsifying agents:** natural (acacia, tragacanth, gelatin, lecithin, wool fat), surfactants chosen by HLB (sorbitan esters for w/o, polysorbates for o/w; HLB about 3–6 for w/o and 8–18 for o/w), finely divided solids (bentonite, aluminium hydroxide).

**Methods of preparation:** dry gum (continental) method — oil : water : gum = 4 : 2 : 1 for fixed oils, triturating oil with gum then adding water at once to form the primary emulsion; wet gum (English) method — gum mucilage first, oil added slowly; bottle method for volatile oils; in industry, mixers, homogenisers and colloid mills.

**Instability:** creaming (reversible), coalescence and cracking (irreversible), phase inversion, microbial growth, rancidity of oil.

**Tests for type:** dilution test, dye test (water-soluble dye colours the continuous phase of an o/w emulsion), conductivity (o/w conducts), fluorescence.

**Evaluation:** globule size distribution (microscopy), creaming rate, viscosity, phase separation after centrifuging or freeze–thaw cycles, pH, drug content, microbial limits.

## 2.12 Manufacturing considerations, filling and packaging of liquid orals

**Manufacturing considerations:** water quality (purified water), order of mixing, temperature, complete solution or uniform dispersion, avoidance of air entrapment, preservative efficacy, clarity (filtration for solutions), batch homogeneity, cleaning and microbial control of tanks and pipes.

**Equipment:** stainless steel jacketed tanks with stirrers, propeller and turbine mixers, colloid mills and homogenisers, filter presses and cartridge filters, transfer pumps.

**Filling methods:**

| Method | Principle | Suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gravity filling | Liquid flows from an overhead tank until a level is reached | Thin, free-flowing liquids |
| Vacuum filling | Container under vacuum draws liquid to a level | Thin liquids, glass bottles |
| Piston (volumetric) filling | Piston draws and pushes a fixed volume | Viscous syrups, suspensions, emulsions; accurate volume |
| Pressure / pump filling | Liquid pumped into container | High-speed lines |

**Packaging:** amber glass or plastic (PET, HDPE) bottles; closures such as screw caps with liners, roll-on pilfer-proof (ROPP) aluminium caps, child-resistant caps; measuring cups or droppers; labels and cartons.

**Evaluation of liquid orals (as in the pharmacopoeia):** appearance and clarity, pH, specific gravity or weight per ml, viscosity, drug content (assay), fill volume (extractable volume), alcohol content for elixirs, preservative content, sedimentation and redispersibility (suspensions), globule size (emulsions), microbial limits and freedom from specified organisms, and stability.

## Remember

- Three granulation routes: wet (most common), dry (slugging, roller compaction; for moisture-sensitive drugs), direct compression.
- Capping and lamination come from trapped air, fines and wrong moisture; picking and sticking from moisture and too little lubricant.
- Sugar coating steps: seal → subcoat → smooth → colour → polish → print. Film coat = polymer + plasticiser + solvent + colour.
- Enteric polymers: CAP, HPMCP, Eudragit L/S, shellac.
- IP weight variation (20 tablets): 10% (≤80 mg), 7.5% (80–250 mg), 5% (≥250 mg). Friability ≤1% at 100 revolutions. Disintegration: uncoated 15 min, film 30 min, sugar 60 min, dispersible 3 min, effervescent 5 min, enteric 2 h acid then buffer.
- Simple Syrup IP ≈ 66.7% w/w sucrose; elixir = sweet hydroalcoholic solution.
- Stokes' law v = d²(ρs − ρl)g/18η; F = Vu/Vo; flocculated suspensions do not cake.
- Dry gum ratio 4 : 2 : 1 (oil : water : gum) for fixed oils.
