# Unit 5: Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy is the use of chemical agents to treat infectious diseases and cancer. This unit covers antimicrobial drugs (antibacterials, antifungals, antivirals, anthelmintics, antiprotozoals) and antineoplastic agents. For each class you need the mechanism, classification, clinical uses, pharmacokinetics and adverse effects.

---

## 5.1 General principles of antimicrobial therapy

**Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)**: the lowest concentration that visibly inhibits growth. The lower the MIC, the more potent the antibiotic.

**Bactericidal vs bacteriostatic**:
- Bactericidal drugs kill bacteria (penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, quinolones, metronidazole).
- Bacteriostatic drugs inhibit growth; the host immune system finishes the job (tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, sulphonamides, macrolides, lincosamides). Bacteriostatic drugs are inadequate in immunocompromised patients.

**Mechanisms of antimicrobial action**:

| Mechanism | Drug examples |
| --- | --- |
| Cell wall synthesis inhibition | Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, vancomycin |
| Cell membrane disruption | Polymyxins, amphotericin B, azole antifungals |
| DNA gyrase / topoisomerase II inhibition | Fluoroquinolones |
| RNA polymerase inhibition | Rifampicin |
| Protein synthesis: 30S ribosome | Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines |
| Protein synthesis: 50S ribosome | Macrolides, chloramphenicol, clindamycin, linezolid |
| Folate synthesis inhibition | Sulphonamides (PABA block), trimethoprim (DHFR block) |
| DNA strand breakage | Metronidazole, nitrofurantoin |

**Antimicrobial resistance**: bacteria develop resistance by:
- Enzymatic inactivation (β-lactamase destroys penicillin ring).
- Target modification (mutated penicillin-binding protein 2a in MRSA).
- Efflux pumps (tetracycline, fluoroquinolone resistance).
- Reduced permeability (outer membrane porin changes).
- Bypass pathways (sulphonamide resistance: bacteria use exogenous folate).

## 5.2 β-Lactam antibiotics

### Penicillins

**Mechanism**: inhibit transpeptidase (penicillin-binding proteins, PBPs) → prevent cross-linking of peptidoglycan → cell wall weakens → cell lysis. Bactericidal.

**Classification**:

| Group | Examples | Spectrum / Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural | Benzylpenicillin (Pen G, IV/IM), Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Pen V, oral) | Streptococcus, syphilis, gas gangrene; Pen G is narrow-spectrum |
| Antistaphylococcal | Cloxacillin, flucloxacillin, dicloxacillin | β-Lactamase-producing Staphylococcus (MSSA) |
| Broad spectrum (aminopenicillins) | Ampicillin, amoxicillin | Gram-positive and many Gram-negative (H. influenzae, E. coli, Salmonella) |
| Broad + BLI combination | Amoxicillin-clavulanate (Co-amoxiclav), Ampicillin-sulbactam | Resistant organisms; ENT, RTI, UTI |
| Extended spectrum | Piperacillin (+ tazobactam) | Pseudomonas, hospital Gram-negatives |

**Adverse effects**: allergy (1–10%; anaphylaxis < 0.05%), diarrhoea (ampicillin); drug of choice if no allergy.

**Resistance**: β-lactamase hydrolyses the β-lactam ring. Overcome by: β-lactamase inhibitors (clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam) or resistant backbone (cloxacillin).

### Cephalosporins

Same mechanism as penicillins (PBP inhibition). More β-lactamase-stable than penicillins.

| Generation | Examples | Cover |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | Cephalexin (oral), cefazolin (IV) | Gram-positive (MSSA, streptococci); UTI, skin/soft tissue |
| 2nd | Cefuroxime, cefaclor | Gram-positive + H. influenzae, Moraxella; sinusitis, otitis media |
| 3rd | Cefotaxime, ceftriaxone (IV); cefixime (oral) | Extended Gram-negative; meningitis (crosses BBB), typhoid, gonorrhoea |
| 4th | Cefepime | Broad including Pseudomonas; hospital infections |
| 5th | Ceftaroline | MRSA activity; complicated skin infections |

**Cross-allergy with penicillin**: < 1–2%; safe to use cephalosporins in most penicillin-allergic patients (exception: anaphylaxis history — use with caution).

### Carbapenems and monobactams

| Drug | Spectrum / Use | ADR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Imipenem-cilastatin | Widest antibacterial spectrum; last resort for multi-drug resistant Gram-negative infections (NDM-1); inhibits seizure threshold | Seizures (especially with renal impairment) |
| Meropenem | Similar; better CNS safety | GI |
| Aztreonam (monobactam) | Only Gram-negative aerobic bacteria; safe for penicillin-allergic | — |

## 5.3 Aminoglycosides

**Examples**: gentamicin, amikacin, tobramycin, streptomycin, neomycin.

**Mechanism**: bind 30S ribosome → misreading of mRNA → faulty protein → bactericidal. Require oxygen for entry — inactive against strict anaerobes.

**Uses**: serious Gram-negative infections (sepsis, UTI, pneumonia); in combination with β-lactam for synergy; streptomycin for TB and plague.

**Pharmacokinetics**: not absorbed orally (given IV or IM); excreted unchanged in urine; nephrotoxicity risk proportional to trough levels.

**Adverse effects**: **ototoxicity** (irreversible cochlear or vestibular damage), **nephrotoxicity** (proximal tubule). Monitor drug levels (trough/peak); avoid concurrent furosemide. Once-daily dosing (high peak, low trough) reduces nephrotoxicity.

---

## 5.4 Tetracyclines

**Examples**: tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline.

**Mechanism**: bind 30S ribosome → block aminoacyl-tRNA from binding — bacteriostatic.

**Uses**: atypical pneumonia (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia*, *Legionella*); rickettsial infections; Lyme disease; acne; malaria (doxycycline prophylaxis); cholera.

**Adverse effects**: GI disturbance; photosensitivity; superinfection (oral candidiasis, *C. difficile*); tooth discolouration and bone growth inhibition (chelates Ca²⁺) — **contraindicated in children < 8 years and in pregnancy**.

**Interaction**: dairy products, antacids, iron chelate Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ → reduced absorption; take 1 hour before or 2 hours after.

---

## 5.5 Macrolides and clindamycin

**Macrolides** (erythromycin, azithromycin, clarithromycin): bind 50S ribosome → block translocation — bacteriostatic. Uses: atypical pneumonia, STIs, H. pylori (clarithromycin), MAC in HIV (azithromycin). ADR: GI motility increase (erythromycin — prokinetic), cholestasis; CYP3A4 inhibition (drug interactions).

**Clindamycin**: bind 50S; active against Gram-positive cocci and anaerobes; used for MRSA skin infections, dental infections, BV. ADR: *Clostridioides difficile* colitis (pseudomembranous colitis) — most serious ADR; avoid in patients at risk.

---

## 5.6 Fluoroquinolones

**Examples**: ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, ofloxacin.

**Mechanism**: inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II, Gram-negative) and topoisomerase IV (Gram-positive) → cannot uncoil DNA for replication → bactericidal.

**Uses**: UTI, RTI, GI infections (*Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *H. pylori* combination), TB (second-line, levofloxacin), gonorrhoea, anthrax. Moxifloxacin: community-acquired pneumonia.

**Adverse effects**: tendinitis/tendon rupture (especially Achilles, with steroids or in elderly); QT prolongation; CNS (seizures in susceptible patients); cartilage damage in growing animals → **avoid in children and pregnancy** (though widely used in adults). Interactions: antacids, sucralfate, iron reduce absorption (chelation).

---

## 5.7 Sulphonamides and trimethoprim

**Sulphonamides** (sulfamethoxazole): block PABA → cannot synthesise dihydrofolate. **Trimethoprim**: blocks DHFR → cannot reduce DHF to THF. **Cotrimoxazole** (sulphamethoxazole + trimethoprim, 5:1 ratio): sequential block → synergistic bactericidal action.

**Uses**: UTI, PCP prophylaxis (cotrimoxazole), *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia treatment, toxoplasmosis.

**ADR**: sulphonamides — hypersensitivity (Stevens-Johnson syndrome), haemolytic anaemia in G6PD deficiency, crystalluria (hydrate adequately), neonatal kernicterus (displaces bilirubin from albumin — avoid near term).

## 5.8 Antifungal drugs

| Drug | Class | Mechanism | Uses | Key ADR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amphotericin B | Polyene | Binds ergosterol → membrane pores → cell death | Systemic fungal infections (cryptococcosis, aspergillosis, disseminated candidiasis); intrathecal for fungal meningitis | Nephrotoxicity, infusion reactions (fever, rigors — liposomal form reduces this), hypokalaemia |
| Fluconazole | Triazole | Inhibits CYP51 (14α-lanosterol demethylase) → ergosterol synthesis blocked | Candidiasis (oral, vaginal, oesophageal), cryptococcal meningitis maintenance | CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 inhibition (warfarin, cyclosporin interactions); hepatotoxicity |
| Itraconazole | Triazole | Same as fluconazole | Aspergillosis, dermatophytosis, histoplasmosis | Hepatotoxicity; negative inotrope |
| Voriconazole | Triazole | Same | Invasive aspergillosis (1st line) | Visual disturbances, photosensitivity, hepatotoxicity |
| Griseofulvin | — | Binds tubulin → disrupts mitotic spindle → no replication | Dermatophyte (tinea) infections — oral for scalp, nail | Hepatotoxicity; induces CYP (reduces OCP efficacy) |
| Clotrimazole, miconazole | Imidazoles | Same as triazoles | Topical: vaginal candidiasis, tinea | Local irritation |
| Terbinafine | Allylamine | Inhibits squalene epoxidase → ergosterol synthesis blocked | Dermatophytosis; onychomycosis | Hepatotoxicity; taste disturbance |
| Caspofungin | Echinocandin | Inhibits β-1,3-glucan synthase → cell wall disruption | Aspergillosis (salvage), invasive candidiasis | Infusion reactions; hepatotoxicity |

## 5.9 Antiviral drugs

### Anti-herpes

| Drug | Use | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aciclovir | HSV, VZV | Phosphorylated by viral thymidine kinase → inhibits viral DNA polymerase (chain terminator) |
| Valaciclovir | Oral prodrug of aciclovir | Same; better bioavailability |
| Ganciclovir | CMV (retinitis, encephalitis) | Like aciclovir; phosphorylated by CMV kinase (UL97); myelosuppression |
| Foscarnet | CMV, aciclovir-resistant HSV | Direct viral DNA polymerase inhibitor; nephrotoxic |

### Anti-influenza

| Drug | Mechanism | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oseltamivir (Tamiflu) | Neuraminidase inhibitor → prevents viral release from cells | Influenza A and B; prophylaxis and treatment (within 48 h of symptom onset) |
| Zanamivir | Same | Inhaled |

### Antiretrovirals — see Pharmacology II Unit 6 (HIV nursing) for full ART; mechanism summary:

| Class | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| NRTIs (tenofovir, zidovudine) | Reverse transcriptase inhibition (chain terminator) |
| NNRTIs (efavirenz, nevirapine) | Non-competitive RT inhibition |
| PIs (ritonavir, lopinavir) | Protease inhibition |
| INSTIs (dolutegravir, raltegravir) | Integrase inhibition |

## 5.10 Anthelmintic drugs

| Drug | Target helminths | Mechanism | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mebendazole | Roundworm, hookworm, whipworm, pinworm (most intestinal nematodes) | Binds β-tubulin → disrupts microtubule polymerisation → impairs glucose uptake | Poor oral absorption; local effect on gut worms; safe in mass deworming |
| Albendazole | Broad spectrum (intestinal nematodes, tapeworms, hydatid, neurocysticercosis) | Same as mebendazole; better systemic absorption | Use with food; for systemic infections |
| Ivermectin | Filarial worms (*Wuchereria*, *Brugia*, *Onchocerca*), strongyloides | Opens glutamate-gated Cl⁻ channels → hyperpolarisation → paralysis | Microfilaricidal; Mazotti reaction (fever, rash from dead worms) |
| Diethylcarbamazine (DEC) | Filariasis | Immobilises microfilariae; exposes surface antigens | Mazotti reaction; treatment of choice for lymphatic filariasis |
| Praziquantel | Trematodes (schistosoma, liver flukes, lung flukes) and tapeworms (Taenia, *Echinococcus*) | Increases Ca²⁺ permeability → contraction then paralysis → worm dislodges | Drug of choice for schistosomiasis |
| Niclosamide | Tapeworms | Uncouples oxidative phosphorylation in worms | Only for intestinal tapeworms |

## 5.11 Antiprotozoal drugs

### Antimalarial drugs

**Life cycle review**: Sporozoites (mosquito) → liver (exoerythrocytic phase) → merozoites → red blood cells (erythrocytic phase) → gametocytes.

| Drug | Phase | Mechanism | Key notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chloroquine | Blood (erythrocytic) | Accumulates in parasite food vacuole; prevents haem polymerisation → toxic haem accumulates; schizontocidal | Resistance widespread; still first-line for *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*; retinal toxicity with long-term use |
| Artesunate / artemether | Blood | Free radical generation → damages parasite proteins and membranes; fastest-acting | ACT (artemisinin combination therapy) first-line for *P. falciparum*; IV artesunate for severe malaria |
| Primaquine | Liver (exoerythrocytic) + gametocytes | Mechanism unclear; radical cure (kills hypnozoites in *P. vivax* / *P. ovale*); gametocytocidal | Haemolysis in G6PD deficiency — test first; contraindicated in pregnancy |
| Mefloquine | Blood | Similar to chloroquine; schizontocidal | CNS side effects (vivid dreams, psychosis); prophylaxis |
| Quinine | Blood | Schizontocidal; IV for severe malaria | Cinchonism (tinnitus, deafness, nausea); QT prolongation |
| Doxycycline | Blood | Bacteriostatic on apicoplast | Prophylaxis; always used with another drug |
| Atovaquone-proguanil | Blood + liver | Atovaquone blocks mitochondrial electron transport; proguanil inhibits DHFR | Prophylaxis; Malarone; daily dosing |

### Antiamoebic drugs

| Drug | Use | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metronidazole | Invasive amoebiasis, giardiasis, trichomoniasis, BV, anaerobic bacterial infections | Activated by anaerobic organisms to toxic free radicals → DNA strand breaks; Disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol |
| Tinidazole | Same; longer half-life | Single-dose option for giardiasis/amoebiasis |
| Diloxanide furoate | Luminal amoebiasis (asymptomatic cysts); always follow metronidazole for intestinal amoebiasis | Acts only on bowel lumen |
| Chloroquine | Hepatic amoebiasis (amoebic liver abscess) | Used with metronidazole |

### Anti-leishmanial / anti-trypanosomal

| Drug | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium stibogluconate (SSG) | Visceral (kala-azar), cutaneous leishmaniasis; IV/IM |
| Miltefosine | Oral treatment of kala-azar; teratogenic — contraindicated in pregnancy |
| Amphotericin B (liposomal) | Visceral leishmaniasis; DOC in many guidelines |
| Pentamidine | Alternative kala-azar; *Pneumocystis* prophylaxis |
| Suramin | African sleeping sickness (*T. brucei rhodesiense*), early stage |
| Melarsoprol (arsenical) | CNS stage trypanosomiasis; highly toxic; encephalopathy risk |
| Benznidazole | Chagas disease (*T. cruzi*) |

## 5.12 Cancer chemotherapy (antineoplastic drugs)

**Principles**:
- Cancer cells are not in cycle all the time — only actively dividing cells are vulnerable to most cytotoxics.
- **Cell-cycle specific (CCS)** drugs kill only dividing cells (S-phase specific: antimetabolites, bleomycin; M-phase specific: vinca alkaloids, taxanes).
- **Cell-cycle non-specific (CCNS)** drugs kill resting and dividing cells (alkylating agents, cisplatin, anthracyclines).

### Classification

| Class | Examples | Mechanism | Key toxicities |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkylating agents | Cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, chlorambucil, melphalan, busulfan | Alkylate DNA guanine → cross-links → prevent replication (CCNS) | Myelosuppression; haemorrhagic cystitis (cyclophosphamide — give mesna + hydration); infertility; secondary leukaemia |
| Platinum compounds | Cisplatin, carboplatin, oxaliplatin | Form intrastrand and interstrand DNA cross-links (CCNS) | Cisplatin: nephrotoxicity (hydration essential), ototoxicity, nausea/vomiting (severe); carboplatin: thrombocytopenia (less nephrotoxic) |
| Antimetabolites | Methotrexate (folate antagonist), 5-fluorouracil (pyrimidine analogue), cytarabine (Ara-C, pyrimidine), mercaptopurine (purine) | Interfere with DNA synthesis (CCS, S-phase) | MTX: mucositis, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity; rescue with folinic acid (leucovorin). 5-FU: mucositis, hand-foot syndrome, cardiotoxicity |
| Vinca alkaloids | Vincristine, vinblastine, vinorelbine | Bind β-tubulin → prevent microtubule polymerisation → mitotic arrest (CCS, M-phase) | Vincristine: peripheral neuropathy (dose-limiting), constipation, SIADH. Vinblastine: myelosuppression |
| Taxanes | Paclitaxel, docetaxel | Stabilise polymerised microtubules → prevent depolymerisation → mitotic arrest (CCS, M-phase) | Peripheral neuropathy, neutropenia, hypersensitivity (premedicate with steroids + antihistamine) |
| Anthracyclines | Doxorubicin (adriamycin), epirubicin, daunorubicin | Intercalate DNA; inhibit topoisomerase II; free radical generation (CCNS) | Cardiomyopathy (cumulative, lifetime dose limit for doxorubicin 450–550 mg/m²); myelosuppression; vesicant |
| Topoisomerase inhibitors | Irinotecan, topotecan (Topo I); etoposide (Topo II) | Stabilise DNA-topoisomerase cleavage complex → permanent strand breaks | Diarrhoea (irinotecan, delayed — give loperamide); myelosuppression; secondary leukaemia (etoposide) |
| Targeted therapy (small molecule) | Imatinib (BCR-ABL), gefitinib (EGFR TKI), erlotinib, vemurafenib (BRAF), trastuzumab (anti-HER2 mAb) | Block specific kinase or surface receptor → inhibit tumour cell signalling | Imatinib: oedema, GI; targeted mAbs: infusion reactions, specific to target |
| Monoclonal antibodies | Rituximab (anti-CD20), cetuximab (anti-EGFR), bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) | Target surface antigens or soluble factors | Infusion reactions; rituximab: progressive multifocal leucoencephalopathy (PML) risk |
| Hormonal | Tamoxifen (SERM), aromatase inhibitors (letrozole, anastrozole), flutamide, bicalutamide (anti-androgen), leuprorelin (LHRH agonist) | Hormone-sensitive tumours (breast, prostate) — reduce or block hormone action | Tamoxifen: hot flushes, endometrial cancer risk, DVT. AIs: osteoporosis |
| Bleomycin | — | DNA strand breaks via free radical generation (CCS, G2/M-phase) | **Pulmonary fibrosis** (dose-limiting; bleomycin test dose first; check DLCO); skin reactions; **not myelosuppressive** |
| L-Asparaginase | — | Depletes asparagine → tumour cells (need exogenous asparagine) die | Hypersensitivity reactions; pancreatitis; coagulopathy |

### Combination chemotherapy rationale

Regimens use agents with:
1. Different mechanisms (additive/synergistic effects).
2. Non-overlapping toxicities (can give full doses of each).
3. Each drug active alone.
4. Different resistance mechanisms.

Examples: CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisolone) for lymphoma; BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) for testicular cancer; CMF (cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, 5-FU) for breast cancer.
