# Unit 4: Drugs for Respiratory, GI, Renal and Autacoids

This unit covers drugs for obstructive airway disease (asthma, COPD), upper respiratory tract symptoms, peptic ulcer disease and gastro-oesophageal reflux, GI motility and vomiting, bowel disorders, diuretics, and gout. Nurse practitioners frequently encounter and counsel patients on these medicines.

## 4.1 Bronchodilators

Bronchoconstriction in asthma involves airway smooth muscle spasm, mucosal oedema, and mucus hypersecretion. COPD involves a fixed obstruction with less reversibility.

### Beta-2 Agonists

**Short-acting (SABA):**

**Salbutamol (albuterol):**
- Selective β2 agonist → activates Gs → cAMP → protein kinase A → relaxes bronchial smooth muscle + inhibits mast cell mediator release
- Onset: 2–5 minutes (inhaled); duration 4–6 hours
- Uses: **Reliever** in acute asthma and COPD; hyperkalaemia; premature labour
- Adverse effects: Tremor, palpitations, hypokalaemia (at high doses), tachycardia
- Route: MDI, dry powder inhaler, nebuliser, IV (severe acute asthma)

**Terbutaline** — similar to salbutamol; also used as tocolytic.

**Long-acting (LABA):**

**Salmeterol, formoterol:**
- Duration 12 hours; used twice daily as **controller** (always with inhaled corticosteroid — never as monotherapy in asthma)
- Formoterol has fast onset, so it can also be used for quick relief (in SMART regimen with budesonide)

**Mechanism of LABA:** Same as SABA but lipophilic sidechain anchors drug in the lipid bilayer → prolonged receptor contact.

### Xanthines

**Aminophylline (theophylline ethylenediamine salt) and Theophylline:**
- Mechanism: Inhibit phosphodiesterase (enzyme that breaks down cAMP) → cAMP rises → bronchodilation; also antagonise adenosine receptors; have anti-inflammatory effects at low doses
- Uses: Asthma (oral theophylline as add-on), COPD (slow-release), **acute severe asthma** (IV aminophylline when inhaled therapy insufficient)
- Narrow therapeutic index: plasma level 10–20 mg/L (above 20 mg/L → toxicity)
- Adverse effects: Nausea, vomiting, insomnia, tremor, tachycardia; at toxic levels → seizures, arrhythmias
- Drug interactions: Levels raised by erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, cimetidine; levels lowered by rifampicin, phenytoin, smoking

**Nursing care:** Monitor plasma levels (draw 1–2 hours after modified-release dose for peak, or 4 hours); watch for nausea, agitation, palpitations; IV aminophylline must be given slowly (risk of arrhythmias with rapid infusion).

### Anticholinergic Bronchodilators

**Ipratropium bromide:**
- Quaternary ammonium compound; poorly absorbed; minimal systemic effects
- Mechanism: Blocks M3 muscarinic receptors in bronchial smooth muscle → bronchodilation; reduces secretions
- Uses: COPD (preferred reliever), acute asthma (adjunct to salbutamol — additive bronchodilation), rhinorrhoea
- Onset: 15–30 minutes; duration 4–6 hours
- Adverse effects: Dry mouth, bitter taste; urinary retention (caution in prostatic hypertrophy); precipitates narrow-angle glaucoma if sprayed into eye

**Tiotropium (long-acting, LAMA):**
- Once-daily inhalation; preferred controller anticholinergic in COPD

## 4.2 Anti-asthmatic Drugs

### Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS)

**Mechanism:** Enter cell → bind glucocorticoid receptor → complex translocates to nucleus → reduces transcription of pro-inflammatory genes (cytokines, adhesion molecules, COX-2) → reduces airway inflammation, oedema, mucus.

**Examples:** Beclomethasone dipropionate, budesonide, fluticasone, mometasone.

**Uses:** Regular **preventive (controller)** treatment for persistent asthma; not for acute attacks.

**Adverse effects (local — most common):** Oral candidiasis (thrush — rinse mouth and gargle after each dose), hoarse voice (laryngeal muscles weakened).

**Adverse effects (systemic — high doses):** Adrenal suppression, osteoporosis, cataract; use spacer device to reduce oropharyngeal deposition.

**Systemic corticosteroids (prednisolone, dexamethasone, methylprednisolone):**
- Short courses (3–5 days oral prednisolone) for exacerbations
- IV hydrocortisone/methylprednisolone for acute severe asthma
- Long-term oral: last resort; use lowest dose; monitor for Cushingoid effects, diabetes, osteoporosis

### Mast Cell Stabilisers

**Sodium cromoglicate (cromolyn sodium):**
- Mechanism: Prevents mast cell degranulation (blocks Cl- channels → prevents Ca2+ influx); also inhibits sensory nerve activation
- Not a bronchodilator; purely preventive — must be used **before** exposure to trigger
- Uses: Mild persistent asthma (prophylaxis), exercise-induced asthma, allergic rhinitis, allergic conjunctivitis
- Adverse effects: Minimal; throat irritation, coughing from inhaler
- **Now largely replaced by ICS**, but still used in children and in allergic rhinitis (nasal spray)

### Leukotriene Antagonists

**Montelukast, zafirlukast:**
- Block CysLT1 receptors for cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) → reduce bronchoconstriction, mucus, and airway oedema
- Oral; used as add-on therapy in asthma; useful when NSAID sensitivity, exercise-induced, or allergic rhinitis coexists
- Adverse effects: Generally well tolerated; rare neuropsychiatric effects (nightmares, depression, suicidal ideation — FDA label)

## 4.3 Antitussives and Expectorants

### Antitussives (Cough Suppressants)

**Codeine:**
- Opioid; raises the cough threshold centrally (acts on medullary cough centre)
- Risk of dependence; avoid in productive cough (suppress beneficial cough)
- Uses: Dry unproductive cough

**Dextromethorphan:**
- Non-opioid; NMDA receptor antagonist; suppresses cough centre
- No analgesic or addictive properties; widely available OTC
- Adverse effects: Nausea, dizziness; serotonin syndrome in high doses or with MAOIs

### Expectorants and Mucolytics

**Ammonium chloride:** Irritates gastric mucosa → reflex increased bronchial secretion → loosens mucus.

**Bromhexine and ambroxol:** Mucolytics — depolymerise mucopolysaccharides in mucus → less viscous secretions → easier expectoration.

**N-acetylcysteine:** Mucolytic for COPD and cystic fibrosis; also IV antidote in paracetamol overdose.

**Nursing care:** Encourage adequate fluids (best mucolytic), humidification, physiotherapy. Use expectorants in productive cough; use antitussives only in dry unproductive cough.

## 4.4 Antiulcer Drugs

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) involves mucosal break from acid-pepsin imbalance; *Helicobacter pylori* infection accounts for 90% of duodenal ulcers.

### Antacids

**Mechanism:** Directly neutralise gastric acid (HCl + base → salt + water). No systemic effect if non-absorbable.

| Antacid | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Constipating; binds phosphate (used in hyperphosphataemia in CKD) |
| Magnesium hydroxide | Laxative |
| Magnesium trisilicate | Long-acting |
| Calcium carbonate | Fast but rebound; acid-base disturbances (calcium alkali syndrome) |
| Combination (Mg+Al) | Counterbalance GI effects |

**Uses:** Symptomatic relief of heartburn and dyspepsia; not for healing of ulcers.

**Drug interactions:** Bind and reduce absorption of tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, iron — space by 2 hours.

### H2 Antagonists

**Ranitidine, famotidine, cimetidine** — competitive H2 receptor blockers on parietal cells → reduce histamine-stimulated acid secretion.

**Uses:** Peptic ulcer, GORD, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, stress ulcer prophylaxis in ICU.

**Adverse effects of cimetidine:** CYP enzyme inhibition (interactions with warfarin, phenytoin, theophylline), antiandrogenic (gynaecomastia), confusion in elderly.

(Ranitidine was withdrawn in 2020 due to NDMA contamination concerns — famotidine is preferred.)

### Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)

**Omeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, rabeprazole, esomeprazole:**

**Mechanism:** Prodrugs; concentrated in the parietal cell canaliculus → protonated by acid → converted to active sulphenamide → covalently binds cysteine on H+/K+-ATPase → **irreversible block** of the proton pump → sustained reduction of acid. Must be taken **30–60 minutes before first meal**.

**Uses:** Peptic ulcer (more effective than H2 blockers), GORD, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, *H. pylori* eradication (as part of triple therapy), prevention of NSAID-related ulcers, stress ulcer prophylaxis.

**Adverse effects:** Generally safe short-term; long-term use: hypomagnesaemia, risk of *Clostridium difficile* infection, increased fracture risk (reduced Ca2+ absorption), B12 deficiency, hyponatraemia, pneumonia risk.

### Sucralfate

- Aluminium salt of sucrose octasulphate
- In acidic stomach: forms a viscous paste that adheres to ulcer base → protective barrier against acid and pepsin
- Uses: Peptic ulcer, stress ulcer prophylaxis
- Adverse effects: Constipation; binds drugs (take other drugs 2 hours before sucralfate)

### H. pylori Eradication — Triple Therapy

Standard **7-14 day** triple therapy:
- PPI (omeprazole 20 mg) **twice daily**
- **Amoxicillin** 1 g twice daily
- **Clarithromycin** 500 mg twice daily

If penicillin allergy: substitute metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for amoxicillin.

Eradication confirmed by **urea breath test** or stool antigen test 4 weeks after completing therapy.

## 4.5 Prokinetics

**Metoclopramide:**
- Mechanism: Dopamine D2 antagonist in GI tract (and CTZ) + 5-HT4 agonist in GI → increases lower oesophageal sphincter tone, accelerates gastric emptying and small bowel transit
- Uses: GORD, gastroparesis, acute nausea and vomiting (especially post-op and chemotherapy-induced), hiccups
- Adverse effects: **Extrapyramidal symptoms** (acute dystonia — especially in children, young women; treat with benztropine or diphenhydramine), tardive dyskinesia with long-term use, **hyperprolactinaemia** (D2 block), diarrhoea

**Domperidone:**
- D2 antagonist; poorly crosses BBB (fewer CNS EPS than metoclopramide)
- Uses: Nausea, vomiting, gastroparesis; stimulates lactation (hyperprolactinaemia)
- Adverse effects: QT prolongation (rare); EPS rare

## 4.6 Antiemetics

Nausea and vomiting involve the **vomiting centre** (medullary) and **chemoreceptor trigger zone** (CTZ, area postrema, outside BBB). The CTZ has D2, 5-HT3, NK1 receptors. The vomiting centre has H1, M receptors.

| Drug | Class | Receptor | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ondansetron | 5-HT3 antagonist | 5-HT3 | Chemotherapy-induced, post-op |
| Granisetron, palonosetron | 5-HT3 antagonist | 5-HT3 | Chemotherapy-induced |
| Metoclopramide | D2 antagonist + 5-HT4 agonist | D2/5-HT4 | General, gastroparesis |
| Domperidone | D2 antagonist | D2 | General, gastroparesis |
| Promethazine | Antihistamine (H1) | H1 | Motion sickness, pregnancy |
| Cyclizine | Antihistamine (H1) | H1 | Motion sickness |
| Prochlorperazine | Phenothiazine (D2) | D2 | Vertigo, labyrinthitis |
| Dexamethasone | Steroid | Unclear | Chemotherapy adjunct |
| Aprepitant | NK1 antagonist | NK1 | Highly emetogenic chemotherapy |
| Hyoscine | Anticholinergic (M) | Muscarinic | Motion sickness (patch) |

**Ondansetron:** No sedation, no EPS; can prolong QT interval; use with caution in liver impairment.

## 4.7 Laxatives

| Class | Examples | Mechanism | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk-forming | Ispaghula husk, methylcellulose | Absorb water → increase faecal bulk → stimulate peristalsis | Constipation, IBS; must take with plenty of water |
| Osmotic | Lactulose, polyethylene glycol (macrogol), magnesium hydroxide | Draw water into colon osmotically | Constipation; lactulose also for hepatic encephalopathy |
| Stimulant | Senna, bisacodyl, sodium picosulphate | Stimulate myenteric plexus → increase peristalsis | Short-term constipation; bowel preparation; caution — cramping |
| Stool softeners | Docusate sodium | Detergent; softens stool | Prevents straining; use after MI, haemorrhoids |
| Lubricant | Liquid paraffin | Coats stool with oil | Rarely used; risk of anal leakage, lipoid pneumonia |

## 4.8 Antidiarrhoeals

**Loperamide:**
- Mechanism: Peripheral µ-opioid receptor agonist in gut → reduces peristalsis and increases anal sphincter tone; reduces fluid and electrolyte secretion; **does not cross BBB** → no CNS effects or dependence
- Uses: Acute and chronic non-infective diarrhoea; ileostomy control
- **Do not use in infective diarrhoea** with high fever or bloody stools — risk of prolonging infection and causing toxic megacolon

**Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS):**
- WHO ORS: sodium 75 mmol/L, glucose 75 mmol/L, potassium 20 mmol/L, chloride 65 mmol/L, citrate 10 mmol/L (osmolarity 245 mOsm/L)
- Glucose-facilitated Na+ absorption (SGLT1 co-transporter) maintains even during secretory diarrhoea
- First-line for acute diarrhoea in children and adults

## 4.9 Diuretics

(See also Unit 3 for antihypertensive use.)

### Thiazide Diuretics

**Mechanism:** Block Na+/Cl- co-transporter in the **distal convoluted tubule** → moderate diuresis with loss of Na+, Cl-, K+, Mg2+; **reduce urinary calcium** (useful in kidney stones, osteoporosis).

**Examples:** Hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone (longer-acting, preferred), indapamide.

**Uses:** Hypertension, oedema (mild-moderate), calcium-containing renal stones, **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus** (paradoxical antidiuretic effect — reduces free water excretion).

**Adverse effects:** Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperglycaemia (impaired insulin secretion and action), hyperuricaemia (gout), hypercalcaemia, dyslipidaemia.

### Loop Diuretics

**Mechanism:** Block Na+/K+/2Cl- co-transporter in the **thick ascending limb of loop of Henle** → powerful diuresis; also causes loss of Ca2+, Mg2+ (unlike thiazides).

**Frusemide (furosemide):**
- Uses: Acute pulmonary oedema (IV — rapid), chronic heart failure, oedema of renal/hepatic origin, hypercalcaemia, **effective in renal failure** (unlike thiazides)
- Adverse effects: Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, dehydration, hyperuricaemia, **ototoxicity** (dose-related, especially rapid IV or with aminoglycosides), metabolic alkalosis, hypomagnesaemia

**Ethacrynic acid:** Loop diuretic without sulphonamide group — used in sulphonamide allergy.

### Potassium-Sparing Diuretics

**Spironolactone:**
- **Mechanism:** Competitive aldosterone receptor antagonist in collecting duct → less Na+ absorption and less K+ excretion
- Uses: Heart failure (reduces mortality), ascites in liver cirrhosis, resistant hypertension, primary hyperaldosteronism
- Adverse effects: **Hyperkalaemia** (dangerous if combined with ACE inhibitor + K+ supplement), gynaecomastia (antiandrogenic), menstrual irregularities

**Amiloride, triamterene:**
- Block epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) directly in collecting duct — not aldosterone-dependent
- Used in combination with thiazides/loop diuretics to prevent K+ loss

**Nursing care with diuretics:**
- Weigh daily (1 kg ≈ 1 litre fluid); monitor electrolytes (K+, Na+) and creatinine
- Frusemide IV push: administer slowly (<4 mg/min) to avoid ototoxicity
- Spironolactone: check K+ frequently; warn about salt substitutes (KCl)
- Educate patient to take morning doses (loop diuretics cause diuresis within 1 hour) to avoid nocturia

## 4.10 Drugs for Gout

Gout is caused by uric acid crystal deposition in joints (especially first metatarsophalangeal joint) due to hyperuricaemia.

### Acute Gout Treatment

**NSAIDs (indomethacin, naproxen):** First-line for acute attack; reduce inflammation. Avoid in renal impairment or GI history.

**Colchicine:** Inhibits microtubule polymerisation → prevents neutrophil migration and phagocytosis of crystals; very effective for acute attack. Adverse effects: diarrhoea (dose-limiting), nausea; narrow therapeutic index.

**Corticosteroids:** Oral or intra-articular; used when NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated.

### Long-term Management (Urate-lowering Therapy)

**Allopurinol:**
- Mechanism: Competitive inhibitor of **xanthine oxidase** → less uric acid formed from xanthine and hypoxanthine → plasma urate falls
- Uses: Recurrent gout, uric acid nephrolithiasis, tumour lysis syndrome prophylaxis (high purine load from chemotherapy)
- **Do not start during acute attack** — mobilisation of uric acid may prolong attack; start 2–4 weeks after acute attack
- Adverse effects: Skin rash (stop if rash occurs — risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome); start with low dose and titrate; adjust in renal impairment
- Interaction: Markedly increases levels of **mercaptopurine** and azathioprine (inhibits their metabolism by xanthine oxidase) → reduce dose of these drugs by 50–75%

**Febuxostat:**
- Non-purine xanthine oxidase inhibitor; alternative to allopurinol; safer in mild-moderate renal impairment.

**Probenecid (Uricosuric):**
- Mechanism: Blocks URAT1 transporter in renal tubule → prevents urate reabsorption → increases urinary urate excretion
- Uses: Gout in patients who under-excrete urate; also prolongs penicillin levels (blocks tubular secretion)
- Requires adequate urine output and hydration; avoid with salicylates (block uricosuric effect); contraindicated in uric acid kidney stones

## Remember

1. Salbutamol: selective β2 agonist, bronchodilator; always check pulse; hypokalaemia with high doses.
2. Theophylline: narrow TI; levels affected by smoking, macrolides, ciprofloxacin. At toxic levels: seizures and arrhythmias.
3. Ipratropium: anticholinergic bronchodilator; dry mouth; avoid eye contact (glaucoma).
4. Inhaled corticosteroids: rinse mouth after use to prevent oral candidiasis.
5. PPI (omeprazole): irreversible proton pump block; take 30–60 min before meals; long-term may cause hypomagnesaemia.
6. H. pylori triple therapy: PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin × 7–14 days; confirm eradication with urea breath test.
7. Metoclopramide: D2 antagonist + 5-HT4 agonist; causes EPS (acute dystonia) — especially in young women and children.
8. Loperamide: peripheral opioid, no CNS effects; not in bloody/infective diarrhoea.
9. Loop diuretics lose Ca2+; thiazides retain Ca2+. Loop diuretics cause ototoxicity with aminoglycosides.
10. Allopurinol: inhibits xanthine oxidase; raises mercaptopurine levels; do not start in acute gout attack.
