# Unit 3: CVS Drugs

Cardiovascular drugs act on the heart, blood vessels, and blood. This unit covers cardiac glycosides, antihypertensive agents, antianginal drugs, antiarrhythmics, heart failure drugs, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, and hypolipidaemics. These are among the most prescribed drugs worldwide and require careful nursing monitoring.

## 3.1 Cardiac Glycosides

Digoxin and digitoxin are obtained from *Digitalis* species. **Digoxin** is the standard clinical glycoside.

**Chemical structure:** Three parts — a steroid nucleus (cis ring fusions), an α,β-unsaturated five-membered lactone at C17, and 2–4 digitose (deoxy) sugar molecules at C3. The sugars are non-essential for action but modify pharmacokinetics.

**Mechanism of action:**

```text
Digoxin binds Na+/K+-ATPase (extracellular) → inhibits the pump
  → Intracellular Na+ rises
  → Na+/Ca2+ exchanger extrudes less Ca2+
  → Intracellular Ca2+ rises
  → More Ca2+ stored in sarcoplasmic reticulum
  → Greater Ca2+ released with each action potential
  → Stronger, slower contraction (positive inotropy)
```

**Electrical effects:** Increased vagal tone → slows SA node rate → prolongs AV node conduction → **ventricular rate control** in atrial fibrillation. At toxic levels, automaticity rises → arrhythmias.

**Uses:**
- Chronic heart failure (especially with dilated heart and AF)
- Rate control in atrial fibrillation and flutter
- Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (second-line)

**Pharmacokinetics:**
- Oral bioavailability 60–80%; IV available
- Mostly renally excreted unchanged (adjust dose in renal impairment)
- Narrow therapeutic index: plasma level 0.5–2.0 ng/mL

**Digitalis toxicity:**

Precipitating factors: **hypokalaemia** (most important — K+ and digoxin compete for the same site on Na+/K+-ATPase), hypercalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, renal impairment, hypothyroidism, old age.

Drug interactions that raise digoxin levels: quinidine, verapamil, amiodarone (reduce digoxin dose by 50% when starting these).

Features of toxicity:
- **GI:** anorexia (earliest sign), nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea
- **CNS:** headache, fatigue, confusion, yellow-green visual haloes (xanthopsia), blurred vision
- **Cardiac:** virtually any arrhythmia; classic — ventricular ectopics, bigeminy, atrial tachycardia with block, various degrees of AV block

**Management of toxicity:**
1. Stop digoxin and potassium-wasting diuretic
2. Correct potassium (target 4.0–5.0 mmol/L)
3. Atropine for bradycardia
4. Lignocaine or phenytoin for ventricular arrhythmia (avoid quinidine and procainamide)
5. **Digoxin-specific antibody fragments (Digibind/DigiFab)** for severe poisoning

**Nursing care:**
- Take apical pulse for 1 minute before administration; hold if <60 bpm
- Monitor serum digoxin level, potassium, creatinine
- Educate patient: same time each day; report nausea, vision changes, palpitations
- If patient also on diuretic: ensure potassium supplementation

## 3.2 Antihypertensive Agents

Blood pressure = cardiac output × peripheral resistance. Hypertension is defined as BP consistently ≥140/90 mmHg (or ≥130/80 in high cardiovascular risk per newer guidelines). Treatment reduces risk of stroke, MI, heart failure, and renal failure.

### Stepped Approach to Antihypertensive Therapy (JNC/WHO Framework)

Step 1: Lifestyle modification (weight, sodium, exercise) + drug monotherapy
Step 2: Combine two agents (usually from different classes)
Step 3: Three agents; consider referral if refractory

### Diuretics as Antihypertensives

**Thiazides (hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone):**
- Initial volume depletion; sustained effect is from arteriolar vasodilation
- First-line in uncomplicated hypertension, especially in elderly and black patients
- Adverse effects: Hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperuricaemia, hyperglycaemia, hyperlipidaemia

**Loop diuretics (frusemide):**
- Used in hypertension with heart failure or renal impairment (when GFR <30 mL/min)
- More potent diuresis but shorter duration

**K-sparing (spironolactone, eplerenone):**
- Used in resistant hypertension (fourth agent), primary hyperaldosteronism, heart failure

### ACE Inhibitors

**Mechanism:** Inhibit angiotensin converting enzyme → less angiotensin II → vasodilation, reduced aldosterone → natriuresis + K+ retention; reduced breakdown of bradykinin → vasodilation, **dry cough**.

**Examples:** Enalapril, ramipril, lisinopril, captopril.

**Uses:** Hypertension, heart failure, post-MI (reduce remodelling), **diabetic nephropathy** (reduce proteinuria, slow CKD progression), microalbuminuria.

**Adverse effects:**
- **Dry cough** (bradykinin accumulation — class effect, affects 10–20%; switch to ARB)
- First-dose hypotension (especially with diuretic — start low)
- **Angioedema** (rare, life-threatening)
- **Hyperkalaemia** (caution with K-sparing diuretics or spironolactone)
- **Renal impairment** in bilateral renal artery stenosis (reduces glomerular filtration pressure)
- **Teratogenic** (fetal kidney damage) — contraindicated in pregnancy

### ARBs (Angiotensin Receptor Blockers)

**Examples:** Losartan, valsartan, candesartan, irbesartan.

**Mechanism:** Block AT1 receptor for angiotensin II; do not raise bradykinin → **no cough**.

**Uses:** Same as ACE inhibitors; preferred when ACE inhibitor cough is intolerable.

**Adverse effects:** Hyperkalaemia, first-dose hypotension, renal impairment; also contraindicated in pregnancy.

### Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

**Mechanism:** Block L-type voltage-gated Ca2+ channels → less Ca2+ entry into vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation; in cardiac muscle → reduced rate and conduction.

| Drug | Class | Main site | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Amlodipine | Dihydropyridine | Vascular > cardiac | Hypertension, stable angina |
| Nifedipine | Dihydropyridine | Vascular | Hypertension, Raynaud's; short-acting causes reflex tachycardia |
| Verapamil | Phenylalkylamine | Cardiac > vascular | Arrhythmias, rate control in AF, hypertension (avoid with β-blocker) |
| Diltiazem | Benzothiazepine | Both | Angina, rate control, hypertension |

**Adverse effects of dihydropyridines:** Flushing, headache, **ankle oedema** (arteriolar dilation without venous), reflex tachycardia (nifedipine).

**Adverse effects of verapamil:** Bradycardia, AV block, constipation, heart failure worsening.

### Beta Blockers (Antihypertensive Use)

Atenolol, metoprolol, bisoprolol — reduce cardiac output and renin release. Used in hypertension with angina, heart failure (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol extended-release), post-MI, arrhythmias, hyperthyroidism.

### Alpha Blockers

Prazosin, doxazosin — α1 blockade → arteriolar vasodilation. Second-line; used when other classes are contraindicated or as fourth agent. Also for benign prostatic hyperplasia (symptom relief).

### Central Agents

**Methyldopa:** Alpha-2 agonist in CNS → reduced sympathetic outflow. Preferred in **hypertension in pregnancy**. Side effects: sedation, positive Coombs test.

**Clonidine:** Alpha-2 agonist centrally. Rebound hypertension on sudden withdrawal.

## 3.3 Antianginal Drugs

Angina is myocardial ischaemia from an imbalance of oxygen supply and demand. Treatment reduces demand (rate, preload, contractility) or increases supply (coronary vasodilation).

### Organic Nitrates

**Mechanism:** Nitric oxide → activates guanylate cyclase → cGMP → smooth muscle relaxation. Primarily venodilate → reduce preload → reduce wall tension and O2 demand. Also dilate large coronary vessels and collaterals.

**Tolerance:** Continuous use depletes nitrate-activating enzymes and thiols → loss of effect within 24 hours. Prevent with a **nitrate-free interval of 8–12 hours** (usually overnight, e.g., remove patch at night or leave the morning dose as the last dose of the day).

| Drug | Route | Onset | Duration | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GTN (glyceryl trinitrate) | Sublingual tablet/spray | 1–3 min | 30 min | Acute angina attack |
| GTN | Transdermal patch | Slow | 24 h (but tolerance!) | Prophylaxis (remove 8–12 h/day) |
| Isosorbide dinitrate | Oral | 30–60 min | 4–6 h | Prophylaxis |
| Isosorbide mononitrate | Oral | 30–60 min | 8–10 h | Preferred oral nitrate (no first-pass) |

**Adverse effects:** Throbbing headache (cerebral vasodilation), flushing, postural hypotension, reflex tachycardia (counteracted by beta blocker). **Sildenafil/vardenafil/tadalafil** must not be combined — severe hypotension.

**Nursing care:** Sublingual GTN: sit or lie when taking; apply under tongue and let dissolve; if pain not relieved in 5 minutes, take second dose; if pain not relieved after third dose (15 minutes total), call emergency services. Store in dark glass bottle, replace every 8 weeks (volatile).

### Beta Blockers in Angina

Reduce heart rate, force, and pressure product → reduce O2 demand. First-line for stable angina. Cardioselective (atenolol, bisoprolol) preferred.

Do not stop abruptly — rebound angina and MI risk.

### Calcium Channel Blockers in Angina

Both rate-limiting (verapamil, diltiazem) and dihydropyridines (amlodipine) are used. Diltiazem and verapamil reduce rate and force → reduce demand. Amlodipine reduces afterload by arteriolar dilation.

Preferred for vasospastic (Prinzmetal's) angina: CCBs (nitrates also effective; beta blockers may worsen by allowing α-mediated spasm).

## 3.4 Antiarrhythmic Drugs

Arrhythmias arise from abnormal automaticity or re-entry. The Vaughan Williams classification:

| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I: Na+ channel blockers | Slow phase 0 | IA: quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide; IB: lignocaine, phenytoin; IC: flecainide |
| II: Beta blockers | Reduce sympathetic drive | Propranolol, atenolol, metoprolol, esmolol |
| III: K+ channel blockers | Prolong action potential | Amiodarone, sotalol |
| IV: CCBs (rate-limiting) | Slow AV conduction | Verapamil, diltiazem |

**Lignocaine (Class IB):**
- Given IV only (extensive first-pass metabolism)
- Use-dependent Na+ channel block; preferentially acts on ischaemic tissue
- Uses: Ventricular arrhythmias (especially post-MI), acute digitalis toxicity
- Adverse effects: CNS toxicity (drowsiness, seizures at high levels)

**Amiodarone (Class III):**
- Unique: has properties of all four classes (Na+, K+, Ca2+ block + β adrenergic block)
- Very long half-life (40–55 days) due to extreme lipophilicity; extensive tissue accumulation
- Uses: Life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias (VT, VF), AF rate and rhythm control
- Multi-organ toxicity: **thyroid** (hypo or hyperthyroidism — contains iodine), **pulmonary fibrosis**, corneal microdeposits, **photosensitivity** (slate-grey skin), hepatotoxicity, peripheral neuropathy
- Monitoring: TFT, LFT, CXR, pulmonary function every 6–12 months

**Digoxin as antiarrhythmic:**
- Rate control in AF and flutter (increases vagal tone → slows AV conduction)
- Not for ventricular arrhythmias

## 3.5 Heart Failure Drugs

Heart failure (HF) is impaired cardiac function unable to meet the body's demands. Compensation involves activation of RAAS, sympathetic nervous system, and ADH, all of which eventually worsen outcomes by causing remodelling.

**Goals of treatment:** Improve symptoms; reduce mortality; prevent hospitalisation.

**Key drugs:**
- **ACE inhibitors/ARBs:** Reduce afterload and preload; reduce RAAS-driven remodelling; central to all HF treatment (reduce mortality)
- **Beta blockers** (carvedilol, bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate): Counter excessive sympathetic activation; reduce heart rate; reduce remodelling; reduce mortality (must start at low doses and titrate)
- **Spironolactone/eplerenone:** Aldosterone antagonism; reduce fibrosis; reduce mortality in moderate-severe HF
- **Frusemide (loop diuretic):** Reduces oedema and symptoms (not shown to reduce mortality)
- **Digoxin:** Reduces symptoms and hospitalisation; no mortality benefit; use in AF-related HF

**ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan):** Newer class — ARB + neprilysin inhibitor → raises natriuretic peptides; superior to ACE inhibitor in HF with reduced EF.

## 3.6 Anticoagulants and Antiplatelets

### Heparin

**Mechanism:** Binds and activates **antithrombin III** → AT-III conformational change → AT-III rapidly inactivates thrombin (IIa), Xa, IXa, XIa, XIIa.

**Low Molecular Weight Heparins (LMWH):** Enoxaparin, dalteparin — mainly anti-Xa activity; more predictable, can be given SC OD or BD without monitoring in many patients.

**Uses:** DVT/PE treatment and prophylaxis, unstable angina/NSTEMI (UFH or LMWH), bridging anticoagulation when warfarin is stopped, haemodialysis.

**Monitoring:** UFH — aPTT (target 1.5–2.5× control); LMWH — anti-Xa level (not routinely needed except in extremes of weight, pregnancy, renal failure).

**Adverse effects:** Bleeding (treat with **protamine sulphate** — 1 mg per 100 units UFH), **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)** — immune-mediated platelet activation → paradoxical clotting; treat by stopping heparin, switching to a direct thrombin inhibitor.

**Nursing care:** SC injection technique (abdomen, rotate sites); never aspirate; apply gentle pressure; watch for bruising; check platelet count every 2–3 days.

### Warfarin

**Mechanism:** Inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase → vitamin K cycle blocked → factors II, VII, IX, X (and proteins C, S) not gamma-carboxylated → inactive prothrombin complex.

**Monitoring:** INR (International Normalised Ratio) — derived from prothrombin time. Target INR:
- 2.0–3.0 for AF, DVT/PE, mechanical prosthetic valves (some) 
- 2.5–3.5 for mechanical prosthetic aortic/mitral valves

**Adverse effects:** Bleeding (commonest); teratogenicity (crosses placenta — embryopathy); skin necrosis (rare — protein C deficiency); purple toe syndrome.

**Antidote:** Vitamin K1 (phytomenadione) — reverses in 6–24 hours; fresh frozen plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate for immediate reversal.

**Drug interactions:**
- Enhance warfarin (raise INR): cimetidine, metronidazole, fluconazole, amiodarone, aspirin, sulphonamides
- Reduce warfarin (lower INR): rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbitone, St John's Wort

**Nursing education:** Consistent vitamin K diet (leafy greens); regular INR monitoring; report unusual bleeding or bruising; avoid aspirin/NSAIDs; alert any new prescriber.

### Antiplatelets

**Aspirin:** Irreversibly acetylates COX-1 → reduces TXA2 (platelet aggregator). Used in MI, stroke, TIA, PCI, stable angina. Dose 75–300 mg/day.

**Clopidogrel:** Irreversibly blocks ADP (P2Y12) receptor on platelets. Used after coronary stenting (dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin), acute coronary syndromes, stroke.

**Ticagrelor, prasugrel:** Newer P2Y12 blockers; faster onset than clopidogrel; ticagrelor is reversible.

**GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors** (abciximab, tirofiban, eptifibatide): IV; block final common pathway of platelet aggregation; used in PCI.

## 3.7 Hypolipidaemic Agents

**Statins (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors):**

**Mechanism:** Competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase (rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis in liver) → less hepatic cholesterol → upregulation of LDL receptors → more LDL cleared from plasma.

**Examples:** Atorvastatin, rosuvastatin (most potent), simvastatin, pravastatin.

**Uses:** Primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular events; familial hypercholesterolaemia; diabetic dyslipidaemia.

**Adverse effects:**
- **Myopathy and myalgia** — dose-related; can progress to rhabdomyolysis (risk raised by gemfibrozil, cyclosporine, niacin, macrolide antibiotics, azole antifungals)
- Raised transaminases — usually mild; check LFT at baseline
- New-onset diabetes (slight increase in risk)

**Monitoring:** CK if patient reports muscle symptoms; LFT at baseline; fasting lipid profile after 4–12 weeks.

**Nursing care:** Take in the evening (cholesterol synthesis is highest at night — except atorvastatin/rosuvastatin which can be taken any time); report muscle pain immediately; avoid grapefruit juice (inhibits CYP3A4, raises simvastatin levels).

## Remember

1. Digoxin mechanism: inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase → Na+ rises → Na+/Ca2+ exchanger brings in more Ca2+ → positive inotropy. Yellow-green haloes and anorexia are early toxicity signs.
2. Hypokalaemia worsens digoxin toxicity — check K+ when patient is on digoxin + diuretic.
3. ACE inhibitors: dry cough is bradykinin-related. Contraindicated in pregnancy (renal agenesis), bilateral renal artery stenosis.
4. Beta blockers must not be stopped abruptly in angina or hypertension.
5. Nitrate tolerance: 8–12 hour free interval. Headache is the most common adverse effect. Never combine with PDE5 inhibitors.
6. Amiodarone: multi-organ toxicity (thyroid, lung, liver, skin, eyes). Check TFT every 6 months.
7. Heparin antidote: protamine sulphate. Warfarin antidote: vitamin K + FFP.
8. Warfarin: INR monitoring; major interactions include rifampicin (reduces) and metronidazole (increases). Avoid in pregnancy.
9. Statins: take at night; watch for myopathy; avoid with gemfibrozil.
