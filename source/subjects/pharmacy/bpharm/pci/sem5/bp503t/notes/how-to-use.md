# How to use this guide

Pharmacology II (BP503T) is the continuation of Pharmacology I. Where Pharmacology I focused on general pharmacological principles, autonomic drugs, CVS drugs, CNS drugs, and autacoids, Pharmacology II covers the drugs used in antimicrobial therapy (antibacterials, antifungals, antivirals, anthelmintics, antiprotozoals) and antineoplastic agents, alongside the ANS and autacoids that link both courses. It is a subject that requires systematic memorisation — there are many drug classes and each requires you to know mechanism, classification, uses, pharmacokinetics, and adverse effects.

## Exam pattern

BP503T is examined at the end of Semester 5 as a **theory paper** following the PCI B.Pharm end-semester exam format.

**Typical PCI B.Pharm end-semester theory paper (75 marks, 3 hours)**

| Section | Type | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 10 short-answer questions — attempt all | 10 × 2 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 4 questions | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short notes — attempt 5 of 8 questions | 5 × 7 = 35 |
| **Total** | | **75** |

Confirm the exact format with your institution, as universities may vary the section types and counts. Some papers use multiple-choice questions in Section A.

## Scoring tips for this subject

1. **Learn every drug class as a table.** For each class: mechanism of action → classification with examples → uses → pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion) → adverse effects → contraindications → interactions. Use the same format every time.

2. **Mechanism of action is 2–3 marks.** Even in a 7-mark short note, a clear one-sentence mechanism with a diagram (where applicable) earns 2–3 marks before you have written anything else.

3. **Link mechanism to toxicity.** Most serious drug toxicities are extensions of the mechanism: aminoglycoside ototoxicity is because cochlear hair cells concentrate the drug; cisplatin nephrotoxicity is because the drug crosses the GFR freely and concentrates in the tubules. Knowing why helps you remember and write convincingly.

4. **For chemotherapy**: focus on (a) classification by mechanism (CCS vs CCNS); (b) one example per class with its unique toxicity (cyclophosphamide → haemorrhagic cystitis; bleomycin → pulmonary fibrosis; vincristine → neuropathy; doxorubicin → cardiomyopathy); (c) the rationale for combination regimens.

5. **For antimicrobials**: master the β-lactam family first (penicillins and cephalosporins, their generations and spectra), then aminoglycosides (oto/nephrotoxic), fluoroquinolones (tendinitis, contraindicated in children), and tetracyclines (chelation, avoid in children/pregnancy). These are the most examined.

6. **For antiprotozoals**: always state which phase of the parasite life cycle the drug acts on; for malaria drugs, note chloroquine resistance and the role of ACTs.

7. **Adverse effects that examiners always test**: aminoglycoside ototoxicity, tetracycline tooth discolouration, fluoroquinolone tendon rupture, sulphonamide Stevens-Johnson syndrome, amphotericin nephrotoxicity, bleomycin pulmonary fibrosis, cisplatin nephrotoxicity, doxorubicin cardiomyopathy, vincristine neuropathy.

## Probability ratings

- **High** — asked in most years; commit the full answer to memory.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a broader question.
- **Low** — appears rarely; useful for MCQs and to round off a long essay.

These ratings are estimates based on PCI syllabus weightings and common exam patterns. They are a guide, not a guarantee.
