# Unit 1: General Pharmacology of ANS and Autacoids

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) controls involuntary functions — heart rate, blood pressure, smooth muscle tone in the gut and airways, glandular secretions, and pupil size. Drugs acting on the ANS are among the most prescribed in clinical practice. Autacoids are locally acting chemical mediators — histamine, serotonin, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes — that contribute to inflammation, allergy, and pain.

## 1.1 Neurohumoral Transmission

The ANS has two divisions: **sympathetic** (thoracolumbar, fight-or-flight) and **parasympathetic** (craniosacral, rest-and-digest). Both share the same basic wiring: a preganglionic neuron from the CNS synapses with a postganglionic neuron in a peripheral ganglion, and the postganglionic neuron innervates the effector organ.

**Neurotransmitters:**
- Preganglionic neurons of both divisions release **acetylcholine (ACh)** onto nicotinic receptors in the ganglion.
- Postganglionic parasympathetic neurons release **ACh** onto muscarinic receptors at the effector.
- Postganglionic sympathetic neurons release **noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** onto adrenergic receptors. The exception is sweat glands, where sympathetic postganglionic fibres release ACh.
- The adrenal medulla, which is a modified ganglion, releases mainly **adrenaline (epinephrine)** directly into the blood.

**Synthesis and storage of noradrenaline:**

```text
Tyrosine → (tyrosine hydroxylase) → DOPA → (DOPA decarboxylase) → Dopamine
Dopamine → (dopamine-β-hydroxylase, in vesicle) → Noradrenaline
Noradrenaline → (PNMT in adrenal medulla) → Adrenaline
```

Noradrenaline is stored in vesicles, released by exocytosis, acts at the synapse, then terminates action by: (1) **reuptake** into the nerve terminal (the main route), (2) metabolism by **MAO** (mitochondrial) and **COMT** (synaptic cleft).

**Adrenergic receptors:**

| Receptor | Location | Effect | Signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| α1 | Blood vessels (smooth muscle), prostate, iris dilator | Vasoconstriction, mydriasis, urinary retention | Gq → IP3/DAG, Ca2+ rises |
| α2 | Presynaptic nerve terminals, pancreatic β cells, CNS | Reduces noradrenaline release; reduces insulin | Gi → cAMP falls |
| β1 | Heart, juxtaglomerular cells | Increased rate, force, AV conduction; renin release | Gs → cAMP rises |
| β2 | Bronchi, uterus, blood vessels in skeletal muscle | Bronchodilation, uterine relaxation, vasodilation | Gs → cAMP rises |
| β3 | Adipose tissue, bladder | Lipolysis, bladder relaxation | Gs → cAMP rises |

**Muscarinic receptors:**

| Receptor | Location | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| M1 | Gastric parietal cells, CNS (cortex) | Acid secretion, cognitive |
| M2 | Heart (SA node, AV node) | Bradycardia, slows AV conduction |
| M3 | Smooth muscle, glands, eye (ciliary, sphincter) | Contraction, secretion, accommodation, miosis |

## 1.2 Classification of ANS Drugs

| Class | Action | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sympathomimetics (adrenergic agonists) | Mimic noradrenaline/adrenaline | Adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine, isoprenaline, salbutamol |
| Sympatholytics (adrenergic antagonists) | Block α or β receptors | Prazosin (α1), phentolamine (α1/α2), propranolol (β1+β2), atenolol (β1) |
| Parasympathomimetics (cholinergic agonists) | Mimic ACh at muscarinic receptors | Pilocarpine, neostigmine, physostigmine |
| Parasympatholytics (anticholinergics) | Block muscarinic receptors | Atropine, hyoscine, ipratropium |
| Ganglionic blockers | Block nicotinic receptors in ganglia | Hexamethonium (historical) |
| Neuromuscular blockers | Block nicotinic NMJ receptors | Tubocurarine, vecuronium (not ANS) |

## 1.3 Sympathomimetics

### Adrenaline (Epinephrine)

**Mechanism:** Acts on α1, α2, β1 and β2 receptors. At low doses β2 effects (vasodilation, bronchodilation) predominate; at high doses α1 effects (vasoconstriction) are added.

**Cardiovascular effects:** Increases heart rate (β1), increases force (β1), constricts skin and splanchnic vessels (α1) but dilates skeletal muscle vessels (β2); net effect is to raise systolic BP while diastolic may fall at low doses.

**Uses:**
- Anaphylaxis and angioedema (drug of choice, 0.5 mg IM)
- Cardiac arrest (IV)
- Adjunct to local anaesthetics — reduces bleeding and prolongs action
- Acute severe asthma
- Open-angle glaucoma (reduces aqueous production)

**Adverse effects:** Palpitations, anxiety, tremor, headache, hypertension, arrhythmias.

**Contraindications:** Hypertension, coronary artery disease, thyrotoxicosis, with non-selective β blockers (can precipitate hypertensive crisis).

### Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine)

**Mechanism:** Mainly α1 and β1; very weak β2. Powerful vasoconstrictor.

**Uses:** Acute hypotension (vasopressor in shock), cardiogenic shock; given as IV infusion only.

**Adverse effects:** Severe hypertension, reflex bradycardia, tissue necrosis if extravasation.

### Dopamine

**Mechanism:** At low doses (1–3 μg/kg/min) acts on D1 receptors in renal and mesenteric vessels → vasodilation, increased renal blood flow; at medium doses (3–10 μg/kg/min) activates β1 → increases cardiac output; at high doses (>10 μg/kg/min) activates α1 → vasoconstriction.

**Uses:** Cardiogenic shock, septic shock (maintains renal perfusion at low dose).

**Adverse effects:** Nausea, arrhythmias, vasoconstriction at high doses.

### Isoprenaline (Isoproterenol)

**Mechanism:** Non-selective β1 and β2 agonist; no α activity.

**Uses:** Complete heart block (before pacemaker insertion), severe bradycardia (rarely used now), status asthmaticus (now replaced by salbutamol).

**Adverse effects:** Tachycardia, palpitations, arrhythmias, hypotension (β2 vasodilation).

### Salbutamol (Albuterol)

**Mechanism:** Selective β2 agonist → bronchial smooth muscle relaxation.

**Uses:** Acute bronchospasm (asthma, COPD) — first-choice reliever; premature labour (stops uterine contractions); hyperkalaemia (drives K+ into cells via β2).

**Adverse effects:** Tremor, tachycardia, hypokalaemia (with high doses or nebulised), palpitations.

**Route:** Inhaler (MDI), nebuliser, IV for severe asthma.

### Indirect-acting sympathomimetics

Drugs such as **amphetamine** and **ephedrine** enter the nerve terminal and displace noradrenaline from vesicles (displacement mechanism) or inhibit its reuptake. Their effects are therefore blocked by prior depletion of catecholamine stores with reserpine.

**Ephedrine:** Combined α and β actions; raises BP, bronchodilator; used in spinal anaesthesia-induced hypotension; tachyphylaxis develops with repeated doses.

## 1.4 Adrenergic Antagonists

### Alpha Blockers

**Prazosin:**
- Selective α1 blocker
- Mechanism: Blocks post-synaptic α1 on blood vessels → vasodilation → fall in BP; presynaptic α2 is spared, so noradrenaline feedback is intact
- Uses: Hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia (relaxes prostate smooth muscle)
- Adverse effects: First-dose hypotension (take first dose at bedtime), dizziness, palpitations, nasal congestion
- **Important nursing point:** Warn patient about first-dose postural hypotension; have patient sit or lie when taking the first dose.

**Phentolamine:**
- Non-selective α1 and α2 blocker
- Uses: Diagnosis and management of phaeochromocytoma (pre-operative hypertensive crisis), hypertensive crisis from tyramine interaction with MAOIs, extravasation of vasopressors (local injection reverses ischaemia)
- Adverse effects: Severe hypotension, reflex tachycardia (α2 blocked → more noradrenaline → β1 stimulation)

### Beta Blockers

**General mechanism:** Competitive antagonism at β adrenoreceptors. Removes sympathetic drive from the heart (reduce rate, force, AV conduction velocity and renin release).

**Classification:**

| Drug | Selectivity | Special property |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Propranolol | Non-selective (β1 + β2) | Membrane-stabilising activity |
| Atenolol | Cardioselective (β1 >> β2) | Renal excretion, no CNS effects |
| Metoprolol | Cardioselective (β1) | Hepatic metabolism |
| Pindolol | Non-selective | Partial agonist (ISA) |
| Carvedilol | Non-selective + α1 block | Additional vasodilation |
| Labetalol | Non-selective + α1 block | Useful in hypertension in pregnancy |

**Propranolol:**
- Uses: Hypertension, angina, arrhythmias, thyrotoxicosis (symptom control), prophylaxis of migraine, essential tremor, portal hypertension
- Adverse effects: Bradycardia, cold extremities (β2 block in peripheral vessels), bronchoconstriction (β2 block — **contraindicated in asthma**), fatigue, impotence, nightmares (lipophilic, crosses blood-brain barrier), masking of hypoglycaemia signs
- Contraindications: Asthma, COPD, uncontrolled heart failure, heart block, bradycardia

**Atenolol:**
- Cardioselective β1 blocker; much safer in mild asthma than propranolol (though still caution)
- Uses: Hypertension (once daily), angina, post-myocardial infarction
- Excreted unchanged by kidney; dose reduced in renal impairment
- **Nursing care:** Monitor pulse before each dose; hold if <60 bpm and inform prescriber. Do not stop abruptly — taper over 2 weeks to prevent rebound angina/MI.

## 1.5 Parasympathomimetics (Cholinergic Agonists)

### Pilocarpine

**Mechanism:** Direct muscarinic agonist (M3 at the eye).

**Uses:**
- Open-angle glaucoma (topical eye drops — miosis and contraction of ciliary muscle improve trabecular drainage)
- Closed-angle glaucoma (acute — constricts iris and opens angle)
- Xerostomia (dry mouth) after radiotherapy — systemic

**Adverse effects (systemic):** Salivation, sweating, lacrimation, nausea, miosis; overdose causes cholinergic crisis (SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defaecation, GI upset, Emesis).

### Neostigmine

**Mechanism:** Reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (indirect-acting). Prevents ACh breakdown → accumulates at all cholinergic synapses (NMJ and muscarinic).

**Uses:**
- Reversal of non-depolarising neuromuscular block (given with atropine to prevent muscarinic effects)
- Myasthenia gravis (improves neuromuscular transmission)
- Post-operative urinary retention, paralytic ileus

**Adverse effects:** Bradycardia, hypotension, bronchospasm, excessive secretions, nausea, diarrhoea; treated with atropine.

**Physostigmine** is a tertiary amine (crosses blood-brain barrier); used in atropine poisoning (antidote) and Alzheimer's disease.

## 1.6 Anticholinergics (Antimuscarinic Drugs)

### Atropine

**Mechanism:** Competitive antagonist at all muscarinic receptors (M1, M2, M3).

**Pharmacological effects by dose:**
- 0.5 mg: slight bradycardia (paradoxical — blocks presynaptic M2 autoreceptors briefly)
- 1 mg: dry mouth, slight tachycardia
- 2 mg: tachycardia, mydriasis, dry skin
- 5 mg: urinary retention, delirium, hyperthermia

**Uses:**
- Preanesthetic medication (reduces secretions)
- Bradyarrhythmias and heart block
- Reversal of cholinesterase-inhibitor toxicity (organophosphate poisoning)
- Ophthalmology: mydriasis and cycloplegia
- Irritable bowel syndrome (antispasmodic)
- Biliary and renal colic (antispasmodic)
- Counteracting neostigmine muscarinic effects

**Adverse effects (mnemonic: Mad as a hatter, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, hot as a hare):** Confusion, blurred vision and photophobia, dry mouth and skin, flushing, hyperthermia. Also: tachycardia, urinary retention, constipation.

**Contraindications:** Closed-angle glaucoma, benign prostatic hyperplasia, paralytic ileus, myasthenia gravis, tachyarrhythmias.

### Hyoscine (Scopolamine)

**Mechanism:** Muscarinic antagonist, more selective for CNS than atropine; crosses blood-brain barrier readily.

**Uses:**
- Motion sickness (most effective of all antiemetics for this indication — transdermal patch)
- Pre-operative sedation and anti-emesis
- Excessive secretions in palliative care

**Adverse effects:** Sedation (more than atropine), dry mouth, blurred vision, confusion and amnesia; hallucinations in elderly.

## 1.7 Autacoids

### Histamine

Histamine is formed from histidine by L-histidine decarboxylase and is stored in mast cells, basophils and ECL cells of the stomach. Release is triggered by IgE-mediated reactions, drugs (morphine, tubocurarine), and direct injury.

**H1 antagonists (antihistamines):**

| Drug | Generation | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chlorphenamine (chlorpheniramine) | First | Sedating; also used as antiemetic, motion sickness |
| Promethazine | First (phenothiazine) | Strong sedation, antiemetic, anti-vertigo |
| Diphenhydramine | First (ethanolamine) | Sedation, motion sickness |
| Cetirizine | Second | Non-sedating, once daily |
| Levocetirizine | Second | Active (R) isomer of cetirizine |
| Fexofenadine | Second | Non-sedating, does not enter CNS |
| Loratadine | Second | Non-sedating |
| Desloratadine | Second | Active metabolite of loratadine |

**Uses of H1 antagonists:** Allergic rhinitis, urticaria, pruritus, anaphylaxis (adjunct), motion sickness, nausea and vomiting.

**Mechanism:** Competitive antagonism at H1 receptors; first generation also cross blood-brain barrier and block muscarinic receptors.

### Serotonin (5-HT)

Serotonin is synthesised from tryptophan by tryptophan hydroxylase, then aromatic amino acid decarboxylase. It is found in enterochromaffin cells (GI), platelets, and CNS neurons.

**Receptors:** 5-HT1 (Gi, decrease cAMP — triptans are agonists here), 5-HT2 (Gq, increase Ca2+), 5-HT3 (ion channel, rapid depolarisation — antiemetics block here), 5-HT4 (Gs — prokinetics work here).

**Drugs affecting serotonin:**
- **Triptans** (sumatriptan, zolmitriptan): 5-HT1B/D agonists; vasoconstriction in cranial vessels; used in acute migraine
- **Ondansetron, granisetron:** 5-HT3 antagonists; powerful antiemetics for chemotherapy, post-operative nausea
- **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, sertraline): block 5-HT reuptake; antidepressants
- **Metoclopramide:** 5-HT4 agonist (prokinetic) + D2 antagonist (antiemetic)

### Prostaglandins and NSAIDs

Arachidonic acid is released from membrane phospholipids by phospholipase A2, then converted by **cyclooxygenase (COX)** to prostaglandins, thromboxane A2, and prostacyclin.

**COX-1:** constitutive; protects gastric mucosa (PGE2 and PGI2 stimulate mucus and bicarbonate), promotes platelet aggregation (TXA2), maintains renal blood flow.

**COX-2:** inducible at sites of inflammation; produces prostaglandins that cause pain, fever, vasodilation and oedema.

**NSAIDs inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2** (non-selective) → anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic, but with gastric and renal adverse effects.

**COX-2 selective inhibitors** (celecoxib, etoricoxib): less GI toxicity but do not reduce platelet TXA2 → increased cardiovascular risk.

| NSAID | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Aspirin | Irreversible COX inhibitor; antiplatelet at low dose (75 mg) |
| Ibuprofen | Short-acting, relatively safe GI profile |
| Diclofenac | Topical and systemic; partial COX-2 preference |
| Indomethacin | Potent; used for gout, patent ductus arteriosus (closes PDA in neonates) |
| Paracetamol | Weak COX inhibition (mainly central); no antiplatelet, no anti-inflammatory |
| Celecoxib | Selective COX-2; spares platelets, GI-friendly |

**Adverse effects of NSAIDs:** Peptic ulcer and GI bleeding (COX-1 blockade removes gastric protection), renal impairment (reduce PGE2-mediated vasodilation), bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthma, fluid retention.

**Nursing care:** Take with food; watch for black stools (GI bleed); check renal function in elderly; avoid in last trimester of pregnancy (closes ductus arteriosus prematurely).

## Remember

1. The ANS has two divisions; both use ACh at ganglionic synapses, but the effector neurotransmitter is noradrenaline (sympathetic) or ACh (parasympathetic).
2. Adrenaline is the drug of choice in anaphylaxis; it stimulates α1 (vasoconstriction), β1 (heart), and β2 (bronchodilation).
3. Salbutamol is a selective β2 agonist — the first-choice bronchodilator; propranolol is contraindicated in asthma.
4. Beta blocker adverse effects: bradycardia, bronchospasm, cold limbs, impotence, masking of hypoglycaemia; never stop abruptly.
5. Atropine mnemonic: mad, blind, dry, red, hot. Contraindicated in glaucoma and prostatic hypertrophy.
6. Neostigmine reverses neuromuscular block and treats myasthenia gravis; always give atropine with it to block muscarinic side effects.
7. COX-1 is constitutive (gastro-protective, haemostatic); COX-2 is inducible (inflammatory). NSAIDs block both → GI bleeding risk.
8. First-generation antihistamines are sedating (enter brain) and anticholinergic; second-generation are non-sedating.
