# Unit 2: CNS Drugs

The central nervous system is the most complex site of drug action. CNS pharmacology covers general anaesthetics, sedatives, hypnotics, antiepileptics, antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics, opioid analgesics, and local anaesthetics. The common thread is neurotransmitter modulation: most drugs either enhance inhibitory (GABA, glycine) or reduce excitatory (glutamate) transmission, or they mimic, block or modify monoamine (noradrenaline, dopamine, serotonin) pathways.

## 2.1 Neurotransmitters in the CNS — Overview

| Transmitter | Pathway | Role | Drugs affecting it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GABA (γ-aminobutyric acid) | Widespread inhibitory interneurons | Main inhibitory transmitter | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, valproate, gabapentin |
| Glutamate | Corticospinal, limbic | Main excitatory transmitter | Ketamine (NMDA blocker), memantine |
| Dopamine | Nigrostriatal, mesolimbic, tuberoinfundibular, mesocortical | Movement, reward, prolactin, cognition | Antipsychotics (block D2), levodopa, cocaine, amphetamine |
| Noradrenaline | Locus coeruleus → cortex, limbic | Arousal, mood, autonomic | Tricyclic antidepressants, SNRIs, MAOIs |
| Serotonin (5-HT) | Raphe nuclei → limbic, cortex | Mood, sleep, appetite | SSRIs, TCAs, MAOIs, triptans |
| Acetylcholine | Basal forebrain → hippocampus | Memory, cognition | Anticholinesterases (Alzheimer's), antimuscarinics |
| Opioid peptides | Periaqueductal grey, limbic | Pain modulation, reward | Morphine, naloxone |

## 2.2 General Anaesthetics

General anaesthesia involves four components: **hypnosis** (unconsciousness), **analgesia**, **muscle relaxation**, and **loss of protective reflexes**. No single agent provides all four safely at clinical doses, so **balanced anaesthesia** combines:
- An IV induction agent (rapid onset)
- A volatile inhaled maintenance agent
- An opioid analgesic
- A muscle relaxant
- Nitrous oxide as an adjunct

**Stages of anaesthesia (Guedel's classification for ether — still taught):**
1. Stage I — Analgesia: conscious, decreased pain sensation
2. Stage II — Excitement: disinhibition, irregular breathing, risk of vomiting and laryngospasm — this stage is to be passed through quickly
3. Stage III — Surgical anaesthesia: regular breathing, absent reflexes — four planes depending on respiratory movement and eye reflexes
4. Stage IV — Medullary depression: respiratory and cardiovascular centres paralysed — FATAL

Modern IV induction agents make Stages I and II almost instantaneous.

### Pre-medication

Drugs given 30–60 minutes before induction to:
- Reduce anxiety (diazepam, midazolam)
- Reduce secretions (atropine, hyoscine — antisialagogue)
- Provide analgesia (opioids — morphine, pethidine)
- Reduce nausea (promethazine, metoclopramide)
- Raise gastric pH (ranitidine, sodium citrate — reduce aspiration risk)

### Intravenous Induction Agents

**Thiopentone sodium (thiopental):**
- Ultra-short-acting barbiturate
- Mechanism: enhances GABA action at GABAA receptor (increases chloride entry)
- Onset: 10–20 seconds (crosses BBB rapidly due to high lipophilicity)
- Duration: 5–10 minutes (redistribution from brain to muscle to fat)
- Uses: IV induction, status epilepticus (IV), cerebral protection in neurosurgery
- Adverse effects: Laryngospasm, bronchospasm (histamine release — caution in asthma), hypotension, respiratory depression
- **Contraindicated in porphyria**; extravasation causes tissue necrosis (highly alkaline solution)
- **Nursing care:** Have resuscitation equipment ready; monitor airway and respiration continuously.

**Propofol:**
- Alkylphenol; milky emulsion
- Mechanism: potentiates GABA at GABAA; possible glycine receptor action
- Onset: 15–45 seconds
- Duration: 5–10 minutes; rapidly cleared by hepatic conjugation
- Advantages over thiopentone: smooth and rapid recovery, anti-emetic properties, can be used for TIVA (total IV anaesthesia)
- Uses: Induction and maintenance of anaesthesia; sedation in ICU; procedural sedation
- Adverse effects: Pain on injection, hypotension, bradycardia, apnoea; propofol infusion syndrome at high doses (metabolic acidosis, cardiac failure — rare but fatal)

**Ketamine:**
- Mechanism: Non-competitive NMDA glutamate receptor blocker → **dissociative anaesthesia** (patient appears awake but is unresponsive)
- Maintains cardiovascular tone (useful in haemorrhagic shock)
- **Preserves airway reflexes and bronchodilates** (useful in asthma and difficult airway)
- Produces profound analgesia
- Uses: Trauma and emergency anaesthesia, paediatric anaesthesia, field surgery, procedural sedation, low-dose for analgesia
- Adverse effects: Emergence reactions — vivid dreams, hallucinations, delirium (reduced by giving diazepam)

### Volatile Inhaled Anaesthetics

**Halothane:**
- Halogenated hydrocarbon
- Mechanism: enhances GABA; inhibits NMDA receptors; affects ion channels
- Smooth induction, good muscle relaxation
- Adverse effects: **Cardiac sensitisation** to catecholamines → arrhythmias; **halothane hepatitis** (immune-mediated, rare, potentially fatal); malignant hyperthermia (triggered in susceptible patients — treat with dantrolene)
- Now largely replaced in high-income settings

**Isoflurane:**
- Preferred volatile agent in many centres
- Less cardiac sensitisation than halothane; does not cause hepatitis
- Causes coronary steal syndrome — caution in ischaemic heart disease (controversial)
- Adverse effects: Pungent smell, airway irritation, dose-dependent hypotension

**MAC (Minimum Alveolar Concentration):** The alveolar concentration of an inhaled agent at which 50% of patients do not move in response to a surgical stimulus. The lower the MAC, the more potent the agent.

| Agent | MAC (%) | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrous oxide | 104 | Weak anaesthetic alone; good analgesia; "laughing gas" |
| Halothane | 0.75 | Potent, sensitises heart |
| Isoflurane | 1.2 | Good choice, little organ toxicity |
| Sevoflurane | 2.0 | Smooth induction, popular in paediatrics |
| Desflurane | 6.0 | Rapid onset and offset; pungent |

**Balanced Anaesthesia:**

```text
Pre-medication (anxiolytic + antisialagogue + analgesic)
     ↓
IV Induction (propofol or thiopentone)
     ↓
Intubation facilitated by neuromuscular blocker (suxamethonium or vecuronium)
     ↓
Maintenance: volatile agent (isoflurane) + N2O + opioid infusion
     ↓
Reversal: neostigmine + atropine (reverses NM block) → recovery
```

**Nursing care in anaesthesia:**
- Confirm fasting (6 hours solid, 2 hours clear fluids) — prevents aspiration
- Check consent, allergies, pre-existing conditions
- Position carefully to prevent nerve injuries
- Continuous monitoring: SpO2, ECG, NIBP, ETCO2, temperature
- Keep patient warm (volatile agents impair thermoregulation)
- Post-op: check airway, respiration, consciousness before leaving recovery

## 2.3 Sedatives and Hypnotics

### Benzodiazepines

**Mechanism:** Positive allosteric modulators at GABAA receptors. Bind at the interface of α and γ2 subunits, increasing the **frequency** of Cl- channel opening in response to GABA (but not in GABA's absence). Do not replace GABA; they enhance its effect. This ceiling effect makes overdose with benzodiazepines alone rarely fatal.

**Effects:** Anxiolytic, sedative, hypnotic, anticonvulsant, muscle relaxant, amnesic (anterograde amnesia).

| Drug | Duration | t½ | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Diazepam | Long | 20–100 h (active metabolites) | Anxiety, alcohol withdrawal, status epilepticus, pre-op |
| Lorazepam | Intermediate | 10–20 h | Status epilepticus (IM/IV), pre-op |
| Midazolam | Short | 2–4 h | IV sedation, induction, pre-op (IM), procedural |
| Nitrazepam | Long | 15–38 h | Insomnia |
| Alprazolam | Short-intermediate | 12–15 h | Panic disorder, anxiety |

**Adverse effects:** Drowsiness, ataxia, impaired cognition, anterograde amnesia, respiratory depression (especially IV with opioids), tolerance and dependence with long-term use, withdrawal seizures.

**Antidote:** **Flumazenil** — competitive GABA antagonist at the benzodiazepine site; reverses sedation but has a short duration (may need repeated dosing); not for routine use as it can precipitate seizures in epileptic patients.

**Nursing care:** Do not drive or operate machinery; avoid alcohol; caution in elderly (fall risk); withdrawal must be gradual.

### Barbiturates

**Mechanism:** Enhance GABA at GABAA; at high concentrations also directly activate the channel (this is why overdose is fatal — no ceiling, unlike benzodiazepines). Also reduce glutamate excitation.

**Thiopentone** (see section 2.2). **Phenobarbitone** (phenobarbital) is discussed under antiepileptics.

## 2.4 Antiepileptics

Epilepsy is recurrent unprovoked seizures due to abnormal electrical discharge. Treatment targets: (1) block voltage-gated Na+ channels, (2) enhance GABA, or (3) reduce calcium or glutamate.

### Phenytoin

**Mechanism:** Blocks voltage-gated Na+ channels in a **use-dependent** (frequency-dependent) manner; binds preferentially to the inactivated state → stabilises inactive state → prolonged inactivation → reduces high-frequency firing without affecting resting neurons.

**Uses:** Generalised tonic-clonic seizures, partial (focal) seizures, status epilepticus (IV), trigeminal neuralgia.

**Pharmacokinetics:** Saturable (zero-order at therapeutic levels) → **non-linear kinetics**: small dose increases cause large plasma level rises. Plasma level monitoring is essential (target 10–20 μg/mL).

**Adverse effects:**
- Concentration-related: nystagmus (first sign), ataxia, diplopia, sedation, confusion, coma
- Idiosyncratic: skin rashes including Stevens-Johnson syndrome
- Chronic: **gingival hyperplasia** (cosmetically distressing; improved by dental hygiene), hirsutism, coarsening of facial features, **megaloblastic anaemia** (folate antagonism), peripheral neuropathy, osteomalacia (enzyme induction reduces vitamin D)
- Teratogenic (cleft palate, cardiac defects) — avoid in pregnancy; use **folic acid** supplements if unavoidable

**Drug interactions:** Major enzyme inducer of CYP2C9 and 3A4; reduces levels of warfarin, oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, cyclosporine. Levels raised by cimetidine, fluconazole.

**Nursing monitoring:** Level, CBC, liver function, dental hygiene, signs of toxicity.

### Sodium Valproate (Valproic Acid)

**Mechanism:** Multiple — enhances GABA (increases synthesis/decreases metabolism via GABA-T inhibition), blocks Na+ channels, blocks T-type Ca2+ channels in thalamus.

**Uses:** All seizure types including **generalised epilepsies** (absence, myoclonic, tonic-clonic), bipolar affective disorder (mood stabiliser), migraine prophylaxis.

**Adverse effects:**
- Common: weight gain, hair loss (temporary), nausea, tremor
- Serious: **hepatotoxicity** (rare, mostly in children under 2 on polytherapy; fatal hepatic failure), **pancreatitis**
- **Teratogenic — neural tube defects (spina bifida)** — highest teratogenic risk of common anticonvulsants; folic acid 5 mg/day if used in pregnancy; avoid in women of childbearing age if possible
- Hyperammonaemia — may cause confusion even with normal liver tests

### Carbamazepine

**Mechanism:** Primarily blocks voltage-gated Na+ channels (use-dependent, like phenytoin).

**Uses:** Partial (focal) and generalised tonic-clonic epilepsy, **trigeminal neuralgia** (drug of choice), bipolar disorder.

**Adverse effects:** Dizziness, diplopia, ataxia (early, often dose-related), **hyponatraemia** (SIADH), **bone marrow depression** (leukopenia, agranulocytosis, aplastic anaemia), Stevens-Johnson syndrome (linked to HLA-B*1502 allele in Han Chinese — genetic testing before starting).

**Drug interactions:** Potent enzyme inducer (CYP3A4, 2C9) — reduces its own levels over time (auto-induction) and reduces levels of oral contraceptives, warfarin, other antiepileptics.

**Monitoring:** CBC (before and during), sodium, plasma level.

### Phenobarbitone (Phenobarbital)

**Mechanism:** Enhances GABA at GABAA (increases duration of Cl- channel opening at higher doses); also inhibits AMPA glutamate receptors.

**Uses:** Tonic-clonic and partial seizures; neonatal seizures; status epilepticus; alcohol withdrawal.

**Adverse effects:** Sedation (limits use in adults), cognitive impairment in children, dependence, withdrawal seizures, osteomalacia (enzyme induction), paradoxical excitement in children.

**Long-acting** (t½ 50–150 h): once daily dosing. Enzyme inducer.

### Newer Antiepileptics

| Drug | Mechanism | Special feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lamotrigine | Na+ channel blocker, also inhibits glutamate release | Broad spectrum; preferred in women (less teratogenic) |
| Levetiracetam | Binds SV2A synaptic vesicle protein | Very few interactions, broad spectrum |
| Gabapentin | Binds α2δ subunit of Ca2+ channels | Also for neuropathic pain |
| Topiramate | Na+ block + GABA enhance + AMPA inhibit | Weight loss; risk of metabolic acidosis |

## 2.5 Antipsychotics

Antipsychotic drugs (neuroleptics) treat the positive symptoms of schizophrenia (hallucinations, delusions, thought disorder) primarily by blocking dopamine D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway. Negative symptoms (blunted affect, social withdrawal) are less responsive to typical antipsychotics.

**Dopamine hypothesis:** Excess dopaminergic activity in the mesolimbic pathway → positive symptoms; insufficient dopamine in the mesocortical pathway → negative symptoms.

### Typical (First-Generation) Antipsychotics

**Chlorpromazine:**
- Phenothiazine; prototype antipsychotic
- Mechanism: D2 blocker; also H1, α1, and muscarinic antagonism
- Uses: Schizophrenia, acute mania, nausea, hiccups, chlorpromazine augmented analgesia ("Lytic cocktail")
- Adverse effects: Sedation (H1), orthostatic hypotension (α1), dry mouth/constipation (muscarinic), **extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS)**, **tardive dyskinesia**, hyperprolactinaemia, **photosensitivity**, cholestatic jaundice, QT prolongation

**Haloperidol:**
- Butyrophenone; high-potency, less sedating
- Mechanism: Potent D2 blocker; much less H1 and muscarinic blockade → less sedation and autonomic effects, but **more EPS**
- Uses: Schizophrenia, acute psychosis, Tourette's syndrome, nausea/vomiting, delirium in ICU
- Adverse effects: Acute dystonia, parkinsonism, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia, **neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)**

**Extrapyramidal Side Effects (EPS) — Classification:**

| EPS | Onset | Features | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acute dystonia | Hours to days | Involuntary muscle contractions, torticollis, oculogyric crisis | Anticholinergic (benztropine) or antihistamine (diphenhydramine) IM |
| Parkinsonism | Days to weeks | Rigidity, bradykinesia, tremor | Reduce dose; benztropine; switch to atypical |
| Akathisia | Days to weeks | Severe restlessness, cannot sit still | Reduce dose; propranolol; benztropine |
| Tardive dyskinesia | Months to years | Repetitive involuntary orofacial movements | Difficult to treat; stop or reduce drug; switch to clozapine |

**Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS):** Rare but potentially fatal. Features: **hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, autonomic instability**. Lab: raised CK, leukocytosis. Treatment: stop antipsychotic, dantrolene, bromocriptine, intensive care.

**Hyperprolactinaemia:** D2 block in the tuberoinfundibular pathway → no dopamine inhibition → prolactin rises → gynaecomastia, galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea, impotence.

### Atypical (Second-Generation) Antipsychotics

| Drug | Mechanism | Advantages | Key risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clozapine | D4 > D2, 5-HT2A, α, H1, M | Treats refractory schizophrenia and positive/negative symptoms; no tardive dyskinesia | **Agranulocytosis** — weekly WBC monitoring required; metabolic syndrome; hypersalivation; seizures |
| Risperidone | D2 + 5-HT2A | Less EPS than typical; oral and LAI; also treats negative symptoms | Hyperprolactinaemia, weight gain, EPS at higher doses |
| Olanzapine | D2 + 5-HT2A + H1 + M | Good for positive and negative symptoms | Severe metabolic syndrome, diabetes, weight gain |
| Quetiapine | D2 + 5-HT2A | Useful in Parkinson's psychosis (low EPS), bipolar | Sedation, metabolic effects |

**Nursing care for antipsychotics:**
- Monitor weight, blood glucose, and lipids (metabolic syndrome)
- Assess for EPS on each visit; use a standardised scale (AIMS for tardive dyskinesia)
- Watch for NMS: temperature, rigidity, consciousness
- Ensure medication adherence; consider LAI (long-acting injectable) for poor compliance
- Clozapine: register patient, monitor WBC weekly for 18 weeks then fortnightly

## 2.6 Antidepressants

Depression involves reduced monoaminergic (noradrenaline, serotonin, dopamine) neurotransmission in key limbic and cortical circuits. The **monoamine hypothesis** states that increasing synaptic monoamines produces antidepressant effects.

### Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs)

**Mechanism:** Block the reuptake (NET and SERT) of noradrenaline and serotonin into the presynaptic nerve terminal. Also block H1, α1, and muscarinic receptors → side effects.

**Amitriptyline:**
- Uses: Depression, neuropathic pain (neuropathic dosing lower than antidepressant), migraine prophylaxis, bedwetting (nocturnal enuresis)
- Adverse effects: Sedation, dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, constipation (anticholinergic); orthostatic hypotension (α1 block); arrhythmias and cardiac toxicity in overdose (Na+ channel block → wide QRS, QT prolongation); **lowers seizure threshold**
- Toxic dose: **Fatal in overdose** — cardiac arrhythmias (treat with sodium bicarbonate), hypotension, seizures
- Drug interactions: MAOIs (serotonin syndrome), CNS depressants, adrenaline (exaggerated pressor response)

### SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors)

**Mechanism:** Selectively block SERT → serotonin accumulates in the synapse.

| Drug | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Fluoxetine | Long half-life (1–3 days + active metabolite norfluoxetine 5–16 days); inhibits CYP2D6; good in anorexia nervosa |
| Sertraline | Preferred in cardiac disease; fewer interactions |
| Paroxetine | Shortest t½; most anticholinergic; most discontinuation syndrome |
| Citalopram | Fewest interactions |
| Escitalopram | Active (S) isomer of citalopram |

**Uses:** Depression, generalised anxiety disorder, OCD, panic disorder, social phobia, PTSD, bulimia (fluoxetine), premenstrual dysphoric disorder.

**Adverse effects:** Nausea (most common early, usually resolves), insomnia, sexual dysfunction (most common persistent complaint), **activation/agitation in early treatment**, slight weight loss initially then gain long-term.

**Serious adverse effects:**
- **Serotonin syndrome:** serotonin excess from drug combinations (SSRI + MAOI, TCA, tramadol, triptans) → triad of **altered consciousness, autonomic instability (hyperthermia, diaphoresis), neuromuscular changes (clonus, hyperreflexia)**. Treat: stop offending drug, cyproheptadine, benzodiazepines, supportive care.
- Increased bleeding (5-HT needed for platelet aggregation) — caution with NSAIDs/anticoagulants
- Hyponatraemia (SIADH) — especially in elderly

**SSRI vs TCA:** SSRIs preferred as first-line because of safety in overdose, fewer adverse effects, and similar efficacy.

### MAOIs (Monoamine Oxidase Inhibitors)

**Mechanism:** Inhibit MAO-A and/or MAO-B → catecholamines and serotonin not metabolised → accumulate.

**Irreversible non-selective:** Phenelzine, tranylcypromine.

**Reversible MAOI-A (RIMA):** Moclobemide.

**Serious interaction — tyramine reaction:** Normally, dietary tyramine (in cheese, wine, pickled foods) is metabolised by intestinal and hepatic MAO-A. With MAOI, tyramine enters the circulation → displaces noradrenaline → **hypertensive crisis** ("cheese reaction"). **Low-tyramine diet** mandatory.

**Drug interactions:** Sympathomimetics (hypertensive crisis), serotonergic drugs (serotonin syndrome), pethidine (serotonin syndrome — **specific danger**), TCAs.

**Washout period:** At least 2 weeks between stopping MAOI and starting another serotonergic drug (5 weeks for fluoxetine due to its long half-life).

## 2.7 Opioid Analgesics

Opioid receptors (μ, κ, δ) are G-protein coupled (Gi): they reduce cAMP, hyperpolarise neurons, reduce Ca2+ entry → reduced neuronal excitability and transmitter release.

**μ (mu) receptor:** Main site of analgesia and euphoria, respiratory depression, constipation, miosis, reduced GI motility.

### Morphine

**Mechanism:** μ-opioid agonist. Acts in the spinal cord (dorsal horn), brain (periaqueductal grey, limbic system), and peripheral sensory nerves.

**Uses:** Severe pain (cancer, MI, post-operative, trauma), acute pulmonary oedema (reduces preload and anxiety), dyspnoea in palliative care.

**Adverse effects:**
- Respiratory depression — most dangerous; worse with head injury (↑ pCO2 → raises ICP)
- Constipation — does not develop tolerance; always prescribe laxative concurrently
- Nausea and vomiting (stimulates chemoreceptor trigger zone)
- Miosis (pinpoint pupils — diagnostic in overdose)
- Histamine release → pruritus, bronchospasm, hypotension
- Urinary retention
- Tolerance and physical dependence with prolonged use

**Contraindications:** Head injury (raises ICP), acute severe asthma, porphyria, concurrent MAOI.

**Antidote: Naloxone** (pure opioid antagonist at all receptor types). Short duration (30–60 min) — may need repeated dosing or infusion.

### Pethidine (Meperidine)

- Shorter duration than morphine; not preferred for chronic pain
- Toxic metabolite **norpethidine** accumulates → **seizures** (especially in renal impairment)
- **Serotonin syndrome** with MAOIs (contraindicated)
- Preferred in: biliary colic and pancreatitis (less spasm of sphincter of Oddi than morphine), shivering control post-anaesthesia

### Codeine

- Prodrug; converted to morphine by CYP2D6 (poor metabolisers get no effect; ultra-rapid metabolisers get toxicity)
- Milder analgesic; widely used in combination analgesics and cough suppressants
- Constipating — used in diarrhoea

### Tramadol

- Weak μ agonist + blocks noradrenaline and serotonin reuptake (dual mechanism)
- Uses: Moderate pain, neuropathic pain
- Adverse effects: Nausea, dizziness, seizures at high doses, **serotonin syndrome** with SSRIs/MAOIs
- Not fully reversed by naloxone

**Tolerance and dependence:** Regular opioid use → reduced response (tolerance); physical dependence shown by withdrawal syndrome on abrupt stopping (anxiety, sweating, lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, myalgia, diarrhoea, insomnia, piloerection).

## 2.8 Local Anaesthetics

Local anaesthetics reversibly block voltage-gated Na+ channels in sensory neurons, preventing propagation of action potentials.

**Mechanism:** Drugs are weak bases (unionised form crosses the axon membrane into the neuron; inside the lower pH ionises the drug; the cationic form binds the inner aspect of the Na+ channel and blocks it).

**Clinical pearl:** Faster in alkaline tissue; slower in acidic tissue (infection/pus) — this is why local anaesthetics work poorly in infected areas.

**Order of blockade:** Small unmyelinated fibres (pain, temperature) blocked first, then proprioception, then large myelinated fibres (motor) last.

### Lignocaine (Lidocaine)

- Amide local anaesthetic
- Metabolism: hepatic amidases; not plasma esterases
- Uses: Topical anaesthesia, infiltration, nerve blocks, spinal, epidural; IV for ventricular arrhythmias (Class IB)
- Duration: 1–2 hours (longer with adrenaline up to 3–4 hours)
- Adverse effects of systemic absorption: **CNS** — circumoral tingling, metallic taste, dizziness, seizures; **CVS** — bradycardia, hypotension, cardiac arrest; treat with IV lipid emulsion

### Bupivacaine

- Long-acting amide (4–8 hours)
- Uses: Epidural analgesia (labour and post-operative), spinal block, nerve blocks
- More potent than lignocaine; more cardiotoxic (binds Na+ channels more tightly — R-enantiomer)
- **Levobupivacaine (S-isomer)** less cardiotoxic; preferred in obstetrics
- Adverse effects: Cardiotoxicity more severe and difficult to reverse than lignocaine; IV lipid emulsion is antidote

### Types of Local Anaesthetic Blocks

| Technique | Site | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topical | Skin/mucosa surface | Minor procedures, eye drops |
| Infiltration | Into tissue | Minor surgery, wound closure |
| Field block | Around area | Same as infiltration with wider coverage |
| Nerve block | Around specific nerve trunk | Dental, upper limb, lower limb surgery |
| Epidural | Epidural space | Labour analgesia, post-op pain |
| Spinal (subarachnoid) | Subarachnoid space | Lower abdominal and limb surgery |

**Nursing care for local anaesthetics:**
- Monitor for signs of systemic toxicity (tinnitus, peri-oral numbness, agitation, seizures, bradycardia)
- Aspiration before injection to avoid intravascular injection
- Resuscitation equipment and IV lipid emulsion must be available for epidural/spinal
- In labour epidural: monitor fetal heart rate; assess motor block (density)

## Remember

1. Balanced anaesthesia: IV induction + volatile maintenance + opioid + NM blocker + N2O.
2. Propofol is preferred induction agent for day surgery (smooth, anti-emetic recovery). Ketamine preserves airway and cardiovascular tone — preferred in shock and difficult airway.
3. Benzodiazepines enhance GABA frequency; barbiturates enhance GABA duration AND at high doses directly activate (hence more dangerous in overdose).
4. Phenytoin toxicity signs in order: nystagmus → ataxia → diplopia → confusion → coma. Non-linear kinetics; small dose change = large level change.
5. Valproate is broad-spectrum. Main risks: teratogenicity (spina bifida), hepatotoxicity in young children.
6. EPS order: acute dystonia (days) → parkinsonism (weeks) → akathisia (weeks) → tardive dyskinesia (months–years).
7. Clozapine requires weekly WBC monitoring for agranulocytosis.
8. SSRI vs MAOI combination → serotonin syndrome (clonus, hyperthermia, agitation, autonomic instability).
9. Morphine adverse effects: respiratory depression, constipation, miosis, urinary retention, nausea. Antidote: naloxone.
10. Local anaesthetics: amides (lignocaine, bupivacaine) metabolised by liver; esters (procaine, cocaine) by plasma esterase.
