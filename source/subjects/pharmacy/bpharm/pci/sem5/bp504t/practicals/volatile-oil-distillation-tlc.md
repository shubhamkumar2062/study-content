# Distillation of volatile oils and detection of constituents by TLC

**Aim:** To obtain the volatile oil of a given drug (clove, fennel, coriander, cinnamon or mint) by hydrodistillation using a Clevenger apparatus, to find the percentage yield, and to detect its chief constituents by thin layer chromatography.

> Use the quantity of drug and the distillation time given in your lab manual. Check that the condenser water is running before heating, and never heat a closed apparatus.

## Principle

A **volatile (essential) oil** is a mixture of terpenoids and phenylpropanoids that evaporates at room temperature and is immiscible with water.

When a mixture of two immiscible liquids is heated, each exerts its own vapour pressure, and the mixture boils when the **sum** of the two vapour pressures equals atmospheric pressure. This happens below the boiling point of either liquid, so the oil distils over with the steam well below 100 °C even though many of its constituents boil above 200 °C. This is how heat-sensitive oils are distilled without decomposing.

The vapours condense, and in the **Clevenger** graduated side-arm the oil separates from the water by density, so the volume can be read directly. The apparatus has two patterns, one for oils lighter than water (clove leaf, mint, fennel, coriander) and one for oils heavier than water (clove bud oil and cinnamon bark oil are heavier).

TLC then separates the constituents of the oil, which are identified by their Rf values and by the colours given with **anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid** spray.

```text
Drug + water in flask ──heat──→ steam + oil vapour ──condenser──→ 
        graduated tube: oil layer over (or under) water → read volume
```

## Requirements

**Drug:** coarsely powdered or crushed clove, fennel, coriander, cinnamon bark or fresh mint leaves.

**Chemicals:** distilled water, anhydrous sodium sulphate, toluene and ethyl acetate (mobile phase, for example 93:7), anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid spray reagent, ferric chloride solution, standard eugenol/anethole if available.

**Apparatus:** Clevenger apparatus with a round-bottom flask (1 litre), heating mantle, anti-bumping granules, measuring cylinder, small sample vials, silica gel G plates, TLC chamber, capillaries, hot air oven, spray bottle.

## Procedure

**A. Distillation**

1. Weigh the drug (for example 25–50 g of crushed clove) and transfer it to the round-bottom flask.
2. Add enough distilled water to cover the drug well, plus a few anti-bumping granules.
3. Fit the Clevenger apparatus (choose the pattern suited to an oil lighter or heavier than water) and fill the graduated side arm with water as directed.
4. Start the condenser water, then heat the flask with a mantle. Keep the boiling gentle and steady.
5. Distil for the time given in your manual (commonly 3–4 hours), until no further increase in the volume of oil is seen in the graduated tube over about 30 minutes.
6. Stop heating, let the apparatus cool, read the **volume of oil** in the graduated tube, and run it into a vial.
7. Dry the oil with a pinch of anhydrous sodium sulphate, decant into a clean vial, label it and keep it closed in the dark.

**B. Examination of the oil**

8. Note its colour, odour and whether it is lighter or heavier than water.
9. Place a drop on filter paper; a volatile oil evaporates and leaves **no permanent translucent stain**, unlike a fixed oil.
10. Clove or cinnamon oil with ferric chloride gives a blue colour (phenolic eugenol).

**C. TLC**

11. Dissolve one drop of the oil in about 1 ml toluene.
12. Spot it on an activated silica gel G plate beside the standard, if available.
13. Develop in toluene : ethyl acetate (93:7) or the system given in your manual, in a saturated chamber.
14. Dry the plate, spray with anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid and heat at about 105 °C for 5 minutes.
15. Mark the coloured spots and calculate their Rf values.

| Oil | Main constituent to look for | Usual colour after spray |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clove | Eugenol | Brown to violet |
| Cinnamon bark | Cinnamaldehyde | Blue-violet to grey |
| Fennel | Anethole (also fenchone) | Violet to grey |
| Coriander | Linalool | Blue to violet |
| Mint | Menthol (also menthone) | Blue to violet |

**Worked example.** Clove taken = 40.0 g; oil collected = 6.2 ml.
% yield = 6.2 × 100 / 40 = **15.5% v/w**

Rf of each spot = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.

## Precautions

1. Start the condenser water before heating, and keep it flowing throughout.
2. Never heat a closed system; the apparatus must stay open to air at the top of the condenser.
3. Use anti-bumping granules and gentle, steady heating; violent boiling throws drug into the condenser.
4. Choose the correct Clevenger pattern for an oil lighter or heavier than water.
5. Read the volume only after the apparatus has cooled and the layers have separated cleanly.
6. Dry the oil over anhydrous sodium sulphate and store it in a well-filled, closed amber vial, since volatile oils oxidise and resinify in air and light.

## Viva questions

1. **Why does a volatile oil distil below 100 °C with water?** The two liquids are immiscible, so the mixture boils when the sum of their vapour pressures equals atmospheric pressure, which happens below the boiling point of either.
2. **Why is this better than simple distillation for essential oils?** The constituents would decompose at their own boiling points, which are much higher.
3. **How do you tell a volatile oil from a fixed oil?** A volatile oil evaporates completely from filter paper and leaves no permanent stain; it also has a characteristic odour and does not saponify in the ordinary way.
4. **What does the Clevenger apparatus do?** It condenses the distillate and collects the oil in a graduated side arm so its volume is read directly, with the water returning to the flask.
5. **Which clove oil is heavier than water?** Clove bud oil, because it is rich in eugenol; cinnamon bark oil is also heavier.
6. **Which spray reagent is general for volatile oils on TLC?** Anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid, followed by heating.
7. **Name three other extraction methods for volatile oils.** Expression (citrus peel), enfleurage or solvent extraction (delicate flowers) and supercritical carbon dioxide extraction.
8. **Why is the oil stored in a filled amber bottle?** To keep out light and air, which cause oxidation and resinification.
