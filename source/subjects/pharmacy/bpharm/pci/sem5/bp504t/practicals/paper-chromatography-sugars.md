# Separation of sugars by paper chromatography

**Aim:** To separate a mixture of sugars (glucose, fructose, lactose, sucrose) by ascending paper chromatography, to locate the spots with a suitable spray reagent and to calculate the Rf value of each sugar.

> Follow the solvent system and spray reagent given in your lab manual. Aniline and most locating sprays are harmful: use gloves and a fume cupboard, and let your teacher heat the sprayed sheet.

## Principle

Paper chromatography is a **partition** technique. Filter paper is cellulose, which holds a layer of water on its fibres; this bound water is the **stationary phase**. The developing solvent, an organic mixture saturated with water (for example n-butanol : acetic acid : water), is the **mobile phase**.

As the solvent rises by capillary action, each sugar distributes itself between the two liquid phases according to its partition coefficient. A sugar that is more soluble in the mobile phase travels farther; a more water-loving sugar is held back. Sugars are colourless, so the dried sheet is sprayed with a reagent that gives coloured spots: a reducing sugar reacts with **aniline hydrogen phthalate** or with ammoniacal silver nitrate.

**Rf = distance travelled by the solute ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front**

The Rf of a sugar is constant for a given paper, solvent and temperature, so an unknown is identified by running it beside standards.

```text
   solvent front  ── ── ── ── ── ──
                     o  fructose
                  o     glucose
               o        lactose
   baseline  ──x──x──x──x──  (spots applied, above solvent level)
   solvent   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
```

## Requirements

**Chemicals:** standard solutions (about 1% in water or 10% aqueous isopropanol) of glucose, fructose, lactose and sucrose; a test mixture; n-butanol, glacial acetic acid and water (solvent system, for example 4:1:5, upper layer); spray reagent (aniline hydrogen phthalate in water-saturated butanol, or ammoniacal silver nitrate).

**Apparatus:** Whatman No. 1 chromatography paper, glass chromatographic chamber or wide-mouth jar with a lid, capillary tubes or micropipettes, pencil and scale, stapler or thread, hot air oven, spray bottle, gloves.

## Procedure

1. Cut a strip of Whatman No. 1 paper to fit the chamber. **Handle it by the edges only**; finger marks leave grease spots.
2. Draw a faint **pencil** baseline about 2.5 cm from the lower edge. Mark four or five equally spaced points on it, at least 2 cm apart and 2 cm from the sides.
3. Prepare the mobile phase, shake it in a separating funnel and use the organic (upper) layer. Pour it into the chamber to a depth of about 1 cm and close the lid so the chamber becomes saturated.
4. Apply a small spot of each standard and of the test mixture with a capillary. Keep each spot small (2–3 mm); dry it with cold air and apply again if a stronger spot is needed.
5. Dry the spots completely.
6. Place the paper in the chamber so that the **baseline stays above the solvent level** and the paper does not touch the walls. Close the lid.
7. Allow the solvent to rise until the front is about 2–3 cm from the top (this often takes several hours; some manuals use an overnight run).
8. Remove the paper, mark the **solvent front at once** with a pencil, and dry it in an oven at about 100 °C.
9. Spray evenly with the locating reagent and heat the sheet at about 105 °C for a few minutes until the spots appear.
10. Outline each spot in pencil, mark its centre, and measure the distances.

**Worked example.** A spot travels 5.4 cm while the solvent front travels 12.0 cm.
Rf = 5.4 ÷ 12.0 = **0.45**

In a butanol–acetic acid–water system the order of movement is usually the monosaccharides first (fructose a little ahead of glucose), then the disaccharides (sucrose, then lactose), because the larger, more hydroxylated sugars stay in the water phase. Compare the Rf of each unknown spot with that of the standards run on the same sheet, never with a value from a book, since Rf depends on paper, solvent and temperature.

## Precautions

1. Handle the paper by its edges; use a pencil, never ink, for all markings.
2. Keep the spots small and well dried before development, or they will tail and overlap.
3. The baseline must stay above the solvent level, otherwise the spots dissolve into the solvent.
4. Saturate the chamber before the run and keep the lid closed throughout.
5. Mark the solvent front immediately after taking the paper out; the solvent evaporates quickly.
6. Spray in a fume cupboard with gloves, and heat only as directed.

## Viva questions

1. **What is the stationary phase in paper chromatography?** The water held on the cellulose fibres of the paper, not the paper itself.
2. **Which mechanism separates the sugars here?** Partition between the bound water and the organic mobile phase.
3. **What is Rf?** The ratio of the distance moved by the solute to the distance moved by the solvent front; it is always less than 1.
4. **Why is the chamber saturated first?** To stop the solvent evaporating from the paper, which would give uneven fronts and poor, irregular Rf values.
5. **Why is acetic acid added to the solvent?** It improves solubility and gives sharper spots by reducing tailing.
6. **Why are sugars not visible without spraying?** They are colourless and do not absorb UV light; the spray forms coloured products with them.
7. **Which sugar does not reduce the silver or aniline reagent readily?** Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, so it gives a weaker spot unless it is hydrolysed first.
8. **Name two types of paper chromatography by direction.** Ascending, descending, radial (circular) and two-dimensional.
