# Isolation and detection of atropine from belladonna

**Aim:** To study the general acid–base scheme by which tropane alkaloids are separated from belladonna leaf, and to detect atropine in the extract by chemical tests and thin layer chromatography.

> Belladonna and its alkaloids are poisons. This exercise is done only in the college laboratory under your teacher's supervision, with the quantities and solvent volumes given in your own lab manual. Wear gloves, never taste any fraction, wash your hands afterwards, and handle organic solvents in a fume cupboard away from flames. The purpose of the exercise is to understand and demonstrate the separation scheme and to identify the alkaloid, not to prepare a quantity of it.

## Principle

Belladonna leaf (*Atropa belladonna* L. or *Atropa acuminata* Royle, family **Solanaceae**) contains a small amount of **tropane alkaloids**, mainly **L-hyoscyamine** with a little **hyoscine**. **Atropine is DL-hyoscyamine**: it is formed when L-hyoscyamine racemises during extraction or on gentle warming with a trace of alkali. That is why the isolated product is usually described as hyoscyamine/atropine.

The separation depends on one simple fact, common to nearly all alkaloids:

| Form | Where it dissolves |
| --- | --- |
| Alkaloid **salt** (in acid) | Water |
| Alkaloid **free base** (in alkali) | Organic solvents such as chloroform or ether |

So moving the alkaloid between an acid aqueous phase and an alkaline organic phase separates it from the fats, chlorophyll, sugars and tannins of the leaf, each time leaving some impurity behind.

```text
Powdered leaf
   │  moisten with an alkali (ammonia or sodium carbonate solution) → free bases
   ↓
Extract with an organic solvent (chloroform or ether)
   │  shake with dilute acid → alkaloid salts pass into the water layer
   ↓
Acid aqueous layer (fats and pigments left in the solvent)
   │  make alkaline with ammonia → free bases again
   ↓
Re-extract with chloroform → evaporate → crude hyoscyamine (with hyoscine)
   │  gentle warming with a trace of alkali
   ↓
Atropine (racemic), identified by its tests
```

## Requirements

**Drug and reagents (quantities as in your lab manual):** powdered belladonna leaf, dilute ammonia solution or sodium carbonate solution, chloroform, dilute sulphuric acid, anhydrous sodium sulphate, fuming nitric acid, acetone, alcoholic potassium hydroxide, Mayer's and Dragendorff's reagents, silica gel G plates and a solvent system from your manual, atropine standard.

**Apparatus:** mortar and pestle, conical flasks, separating funnel, filter papers and funnel, water bath, evaporating dish, TLC chamber and capillaries, UV lamp or spray bottle.

## Procedure (outline)

1. Moisten the powdered leaf with the alkali solution and mix well, so that the alkaloid salts present in the drug are converted to free bases.
2. Extract the moist powder with the organic solvent by the method and for the time stated in your manual, and filter.
3. Shake the filtered solvent extract in a separating funnel with dilute sulphuric acid. The alkaloids move into the acid water layer; pigments and fats stay in the solvent.
4. Separate the acid layer and make it alkaline with ammonia (check with pH paper).
5. Extract the alkaline layer with fresh chloroform. Dry the chloroform layer over anhydrous sodium sulphate.
6. Evaporate the chloroform on a water bath in a fume cupboard. The residue is crude hyoscyamine with hyoscine; warming it gently with a trace of alkali, as directed in your manual, racemises it to atropine.
7. Use a small part of the residue for the identification tests and TLC below. Hand over the rest to your teacher for safe disposal.

## Identification tests

| Test | Procedure | Observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vitali–Morin test** | Treat a little residue with fuming nitric acid and evaporate to dryness on a water bath; cool, add acetone and then a drop of alcoholic KOH | **Violet colour** — confirms a tropane ester (atropine, hyoscine) |
| Mayer's reagent | Dissolve a little residue in dilute acid and add the reagent | Cream precipitate (alkaloid) |
| Dragendorff's reagent | As above | Orange-red precipitate (alkaloid) |
| Optical rotation | Polarimeter, if available | Atropine is **racemic and optically inactive**; L-hyoscyamine is laevorotatory |
| TLC | Spot the residue and the atropine standard on silica gel G; develop in the system given in your manual; spray with Dragendorff's reagent | Orange spot with the same Rf as the standard |

## Result

The alkaloid fraction separated from belladonna leaf gave a positive Vitali–Morin test and a TLC spot with the same Rf as standard atropine, showing that the drug contains tropane alkaloids of the hyoscyamine/atropine type.

## Precautions

1. Treat every fraction as poisonous; do not pipette by mouth and do not taste anything.
2. Keep to the quantities in your lab manual and work only under supervision.
3. Make the aqueous layer properly alkaline before the second extraction, or the alkaloid stays in the water.
4. Vent the separating funnel often and shake gently to avoid emulsions.
5. Fuming nitric acid is corrosive and gives off fumes: use the fume cupboard and add it drop by drop.
6. Evaporate chloroform only on a water bath, never over a flame.
7. Give all residues and solvents to your teacher for proper disposal.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is the powder moistened with ammonia first?** To free the alkaloid bases from their salts so they dissolve in the organic solvent.
2. **How is atropine related to hyoscyamine?** Atropine is the racemic (DL) form; the plant contains mainly L-hyoscyamine, which racemises during extraction.
3. **Which test confirms a tropane alkaloid?** The Vitali–Morin test, a violet colour given by tropane esters.
4. **Why is the extract shaken with dilute acid?** The alkaloids pass into the acid water as salts, leaving fats and pigments in the solvent, which purifies the fraction.
5. **How can atropine and hyoscyamine be told apart?** By optical rotation: atropine is optically inactive, hyoscyamine is laevorotatory.
6. **Name two other plants used commercially for tropane alkaloids.** *Duboisia* species and *Hyoscyamus muticus*.
7. **What are the medical uses of atropine sulphate?** As an antimuscarinic: preanaesthetic medication, sinus bradycardia, antidote in organophosphate poisoning, and as a mydriatic and cycloplegic in eye drops.
