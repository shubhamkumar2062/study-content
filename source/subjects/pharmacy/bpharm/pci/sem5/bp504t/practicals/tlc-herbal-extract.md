# Thin layer chromatography of a herbal extract

**Aim:** To prepare a thin layer chromatogram of the given herbal extract on silica gel G, to locate the separated constituents and to calculate their Rf values.

> Use the solvent system and spray reagent given in your lab manual for the drug supplied. Sulphuric acid sprays are corrosive: spray in a fume cupboard with gloves and goggles.

## Principle

Thin layer chromatography (TLC) separates compounds on a thin layer of adsorbent, usually **silica gel G** (G = with calcium sulphate as binder), spread on a glass plate. The silica surface is polar and acts as the **stationary phase**; the developing solvent is the **mobile phase**.

Separation is mainly by **adsorption**. A polar compound is held tightly by the silica and moves little; a less polar compound is held weakly, spends more time in the mobile phase and travels farther. Raising the polarity of the solvent moves everything farther up the plate.

TLC is the standard identity test for herbal drugs: the pattern of spots, their colours and their Rf values make a **fingerprint** that is compared with a standard or with a reference extract.

```text
          solvent front  ── ── ── ── ──
                           o   (least polar, high Rf)
                       o
                   o       (most polar, low Rf)
          baseline ──x────x──   sample | standard
          solvent  ~~~~~~~~~~
```

## Requirements

**Chemicals:** herbal extract (for example an alcoholic extract of senna, turmeric, clove or liquorice), the mobile phase named in your manual (such as toluene : ethyl acetate, or chloroform : methanol), standard marker solution if available, locating reagents — anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid, 10% ethanolic sulphuric acid, Dragendorff's reagent or alcoholic KOH, iodine crystals.

**Apparatus:** silica gel G, glass plates (20 × 5 cm or 20 × 20 cm), TLC spreader or a clean glass rod, oven, TLC chamber with lid, capillary tubes, pencil and scale, UV cabinet (254 and 365 nm), spray bottle.

## Procedure

**A. Preparation of the plate** (or use a ready-made pre-coated plate)

1. Make a slurry of silica gel G with distilled water in the ratio given in your manual (commonly about 1 part gel to 2 parts water) and mix quickly to a smooth paste free of lumps.
2. Spread it on clean, dry plates to a uniform thickness of about 0.25 mm using a spreader.
3. Air-dry the plates, then **activate** them in an oven at about 110 °C for 30 minutes. Cool and store in a desiccator.

**B. Running the chromatogram**

4. Draw a faint pencil baseline about 1.5–2 cm from the lower edge and mark the points of application.
5. Apply small spots of the extract (and the standard beside it) with a capillary, keeping each spot 2–3 mm wide. Dry between applications.
6. Pour the mobile phase into the chamber to a depth of about 1 cm, line it with filter paper and close the lid to saturate it.
7. Place the plate so the baseline is **above** the solvent level, close the lid and let the solvent rise until the front is about 1–2 cm from the top.
8. Remove the plate, mark the solvent front at once and dry it.

**C. Detection**

9. Examine first under **UV 254 nm** (dark spots on a fluorescent plate) and **UV 365 nm** (fluorescent spots). Outline the spots in pencil.
10. Then use a chemical method: iodine vapour in a closed jar (brown spots, non-destructive), or spray with the reagent for the class of compounds concerned and heat as directed.

| Class of constituent | Usual spray | Colour of spots |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Terpenoids, volatile oils | Anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid, heat 105 °C | Blue, violet, pink, grey |
| Alkaloids | Dragendorff's reagent | Orange to reddish-brown |
| Anthraquinone glycosides | Alcoholic KOH (or ammonia vapour) | Pink to red |
| Flavonoids | Aluminium chloride, view in UV 365 nm | Yellow fluorescence |
| Steroids, triterpenoids | Liebermann–Burchard reagent, heat | Green, blue or pink |
| General (non-specific) | Iodine vapour, or 10% ethanolic H2SO4 with heating | Brown; charred dark spots |

Rf = distance travelled by the spot ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front

**Worked example.** Spot moves 6.0 cm, solvent front 8.0 cm → Rf = 6.0 ÷ 8.0 = **0.75**

Some books also report the **Rx** value, the Rf of the spot divided by the Rf of a reference substance run on the same plate.

## Precautions

1. Clean the plates well; grease spoils the layer and the run.
2. Spread an even layer and activate the plates before use, since adsorbed moisture lowers the activity of silica.
3. Keep spots small, and far enough apart that they do not merge.
4. The baseline must remain above the solvent level.
5. Saturate the chamber and keep the lid closed during development.
6. Mark the solvent front immediately after removing the plate.
7. Spray only in a fume cupboard; heat charring sprays uniformly on a hot plate.

## Viva questions

1. **What does the "G" in silica gel G mean?** It contains calcium sulphate (gypsum) as a binder.
2. **Which mechanism works in TLC on silica?** Mainly adsorption, though partition also plays a part on some layers.
3. **Why are the plates activated at 110 °C?** To drive off adsorbed moisture, which would otherwise deactivate the silica and lower reproducibility.
4. **What is a TLC fingerprint?** The whole pattern of spots, colours and Rf values of an extract, used to identify a herbal drug and to check batch-to-batch consistency.
5. **Give two advantages of TLC over paper chromatography.** Faster runs with sharper spots, and corrosive sprays and heating can be used because the layer is inorganic.
6. **What is HPTLC?** High performance TLC — fine, uniform pre-coated plates with automatic application and a densitometric scanner, which allows quantitative work.
7. **How can spots be seen without any reagent?** Under UV light, or with iodine vapour, which is non-destructive so the compound can be recovered.
8. **Why must the chamber be saturated?** To prevent solvent evaporating from the plate surface, which distorts the front and changes Rf values.
