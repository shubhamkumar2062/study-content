# How to use this guide

Pharmacognosy and Phytochemistry II builds on Pharmacognosy I. Unit 1 is theory (how plants make their chemicals), Unit 2 is a long list of drug monographs, Units 3 and 4 are about getting single compounds out of plants in the laboratory and in the factory, and Unit 5 is the toolbox of extraction and analytical techniques. Marks come from correct **biological sources with families**, correct **chemical constituents**, neat **classifications** and clean **flow diagrams** of pathways and processes.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Unit 2 is the biggest (14 of the 45 teaching hours) and carries eight groups of drugs — spread it over three days and write each monograph out from memory. Units 3 and 4 overlap: Unit 3 is laboratory isolation and identification, Unit 4 is industrial production, estimation and use of the same kind of compounds. Study them one after the other and keep a single table for the compounds that appear in both (artemisinin, atropine, caffeine, podophyllotoxin).

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

The question bank has long essay (10-mark), short answer (5-mark) and very short (2-mark) questions. Very short questions help with Section A and with quick revision.

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with tables, examples and a diagram where one fits.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common patterns in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Every monograph has the same skeleton: **synonym → biological source with family → geographical source → chemical constituents → chemical tests → uses → commercial applications → adulterants.** Write each heading even if you can manage only one line under it.
2. Write the botanical name in full the first time, genus with a capital letter and species in small letters (underline it in handwriting), followed by the family: *Catharanthus roseus*, Apocynaceae. A wrong family loses an easy mark.
3. Name the **active constituent** with its chemical class, not just the plant: "artemisinin, a sesquiterpene lactone with an endoperoxide bridge", "sennosides A and B, dianthrone glycosides".
4. For pathway questions draw the **flow chart first**, then explain it in short numbered lines. A clear chart of the shikimic acid or mevalonate pathway earns most of the marks on its own.
5. For isolation questions use a **numbered flow of steps** with the reagent and purpose at each step, and finish with identification and assay.
6. Learn the paired tests so you never mix them up: Borntrager (anthraquinones) and modified Borntrager (C-glycoside aloin); Keller–Killiani (2-deoxy sugar); Vitali–Morin (tropane esters); murexide (purine alkaloids); thalleioquin (quinine); Shinoda (flavonoids); matchstick (catechins); copper acetate (colophony); umbelliferone fluorescence (asafoetida).
7. In technique questions always give **principle → instrumentation or procedure → detection → applications**, with one real pharmacognostic example.
8. Do not confuse similar pairs: hydrolysable versus condensed tannins; cardenolides versus bufadienolides; *Digitalis purpurea* versus *D. lanata*; Sumatra versus Siam benzoin; Curacao versus Cape aloes; Tinnevelly versus Alexandrian senna; vincristine (neuropathy) versus vinblastine (bone-marrow suppression); normal versus reverse phase HPLC.
9. For practical examinations, keep the chemical tests of asafoetida, benzoin, colophony, aloes and myrrh at your fingertips: they are the usual "identify the given drug" question.
