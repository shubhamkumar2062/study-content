# Unit 5: Basics of phytochemistry — extraction and modern analytical techniques

This unit covers the tools used in the earlier units: how a plant constituent is taken out of the drug (**extraction**), and how it is separated, purified and identified by **chromatography**, **spectroscopy** and **electrophoresis**.

## 5.1 Extraction — principles

**Extraction** is the separation of the useful constituents of a drug from the inert material using a selective solvent. The solvent dissolves the wanted compounds; the undissolved residue is the **marc**.

**Steps that decide the yield**

1. **Size reduction** — a smaller particle gives a larger surface, so extraction is faster. Too fine a powder packs down and clogs the percolator.
2. **Wetting and penetration** — the solvent enters the cells.
3. **Dissolution** — the constituents dissolve.
4. **Diffusion** — the dissolved material moves out into the bulk solvent, driven by the concentration difference. Stirring and fresh solvent keep this difference high.

**Choice of solvent (menstruum)**

| Solvent | Extracts | Remarks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Sugars, gums, glycosides, tannins, proteins, salts | Cheap and safe, but spoils quickly and extracts much inert matter |
| Alcohol (ethanol) and hydro-alcohol | Most constituents: alkaloids, glycosides, flavonoids, resins | The most generally useful; self-preserving above about 20% |
| Methanol | Similar to ethanol, more polar | Toxic; residues limited |
| Acetone, ethyl acetate | Resins, aglycones, moderately polar compounds | Good for defatting after hexane |
| Chloroform, dichloromethane | Alkaloid bases, aglycones, terpenoids | Useful in partitioning |
| Ether, hexane, petroleum ether | Fats, waxes, volatile oils, carotenoids | Used for defatting; highly flammable |
| Glycerine | Tannins | Used in some preparations |

A good solvent should be selective, cheap, non-toxic, non-flammable if possible, easy to recover, and should not react with the constituents.

**Polarity order (least to most):** petroleum ether < hexane < toluene < chloroform < ether < ethyl acetate < acetone < ethanol < methanol < water. Successive extraction with solvents of increasing polarity separates the constituents into groups.

## 5.2 Classical methods of extraction

| Method | How it is done | Suitable for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Maceration** | The drug is soaked in the whole solvent in a closed vessel for several days with occasional shaking; the liquid is strained and the marc pressed | Unorganised drugs and those with easily soluble constituents; thermolabile compounds |
| **Percolation** | Moistened drug is packed in a percolator; solvent passes slowly through it; fresh solvent keeps the concentration gradient | Most tinctures and extracts; more efficient than maceration |
| **Digestion** | Maceration with gentle heat (about 40–60 °C) | Hard drugs whose constituents are heat-stable |
| **Infusion** | Fresh solution made by macerating with cold or boiling water for a short time | Light, quickly soluble constituents |
| **Decoction** | Drug boiled with water for a set time | Water-soluble, heat-stable constituents (Ayurvedic kwatha) |
| **Soxhlet (continuous hot) extraction** | Solvent is distilled, condensed over the drug and siphoned back; the drug is always met by fresh solvent | Laboratory scale; thermostable compounds; uses little solvent |
| **Counter-current extraction** | Drug and solvent move in opposite directions through stages | Industrial scale; high efficiency, low solvent use |
| **Steam / hydro-distillation** | Volatile constituents distil over with steam and are separated in a receiver | Volatile oils (Clevenger apparatus in the laboratory) |
| **Expression** | Mechanical pressing | Citrus peel oils, fixed oils |

## 5.3 Modern methods of extraction

| Method | Principle | Advantages | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Supercritical fluid extraction (SFE)** | A fluid above its critical temperature and pressure (CO2: about 31 °C, 74 bar) behaves between a gas and a liquid — it diffuses like a gas and dissolves like a liquid. Pressure and temperature, and a co-solvent (modifier) such as ethanol, control what is extracted | No solvent residue (CO2 evaporates), low temperature protects heat-sensitive compounds, non-flammable, non-toxic, selective, no oxidation | High cost of equipment; CO2 is non-polar, so polar compounds need a modifier |
| **Microwave-assisted extraction (MAE)** | Microwaves heat polar molecules and water inside the plant cells directly; the cells burst and release contents | Very fast (minutes), less solvent, good yields | Needs a solvent or sample that absorbs microwaves; not for very heat-sensitive compounds |
| **Ultrasound-assisted extraction (sonication)** | Ultrasonic waves create and collapse tiny bubbles (cavitation), which breaks cell walls | Simple, cheap, fast, works at low temperature | Local heat and free radicals may degrade some compounds; scale-up is harder |
| **Accelerated / pressurised solvent extraction (ASE)** | Solvent held above its boiling point under pressure, so it stays liquid at high temperature | Fast, automated, little solvent | Expensive instrument; not for thermolabile compounds |
| **Enzyme-assisted extraction** | Cellulase, pectinase or hemicellulase break the cell wall before solvent extraction | Mild, improves yield, environment-friendly | Enzyme cost; needs controlled pH and temperature |
| **Headspace and solid-phase microextraction** | Volatiles are trapped on a coated fibre and desorbed into a GC | Solvent-free, tiny sample | Volatiles only |

**Phytonics (hydrofluorocarbon solvent) process** uses a non-ozone-depleting fluorocarbon solvent at near-ambient temperature to extract delicate aromatic and heat-sensitive materials.

## 5.4 Chromatography

**Principle.** Chromatography separates the components of a mixture between a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase** that moves through it. A component that interacts more strongly with the stationary phase moves more slowly.

**Mechanisms:** adsorption, partition, ion exchange, size exclusion and affinity.

```text
Rf = distance travelled by the solute ÷ distance travelled by the solvent front

Rf is always less than 1. It is constant for a compound only when the adsorbent,
solvent system, temperature, chamber saturation and plate thickness are the same.
```

### 5.4.1 Paper chromatography

- **Stationary phase:** water held on the cellulose fibres of the paper (so this is **partition** chromatography). **Mobile phase:** an organic solvent, usually saturated with water (for example n-butanol : acetic acid : water, 4:1:5).
- **Procedure:** spot the sample about 2 cm from one edge, dry, place in a saturated chamber with the solvent below the spot line, develop, mark the solvent front, dry and spray the locating reagent.
- **Development:** ascending, descending, radial (circular) and two-dimensional.
- **Detection:** UV light, iodine vapour, or sprays — ninhydrin (amino acids), aniline hydrogen phthalate (sugars), Dragendorff's (alkaloids).
- **Uses:** sugars, amino acids and other polar, water-soluble substances; a classic practical exercise (separation of sugars).
- **Reverse-phase paper chromatography:** the paper is made non-polar (impregnated with paraffin or silicone) and the mobile phase is polar.

### 5.4.2 Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

- **Stationary phase:** a thin layer (about 0.25 mm) of **silica gel G** (G = contains calcium sulphate as binder), alumina, kieselguhr or cellulose on a glass, aluminium or plastic plate. Mostly **adsorption** chromatography.
- **Procedure:** activate the plate (about 110 °C for 30 minutes if needed); spot the sample and a reference standard; develop in a saturated chamber; dry; detect.
- **Detection:** UV 254 nm (fluorescence quenching on a plate containing a fluorescent indicator, F254) and 365 nm; iodine vapour; sprays — anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid (terpenoids), Dragendorff's (alkaloids), natural products reagent (flavonoids), ninhydrin (amino acids), vanillin–sulphuric acid.
- **Advantages:** fast, cheap, needs little sample, many samples at once, corrosive sprays can be used, preparative work possible.
- **Uses:** identity tests in pharmacopoeial monographs, following a column separation, checking purity, fingerprinting herbal extracts, detecting adulteration.
- **HPTLC (high-performance TLC):** pre-coated plates with very fine, uniform particles, sample applied by an automatic applicator as bands, development in a controlled chamber, and **densitometric scanning** to measure each spot. It gives quantitative results and is widely used for **fingerprinting and standardising herbal products**.

### 5.4.3 Column chromatography

- A glass column is packed with the adsorbent (silica gel, alumina) as a slurry; the sample is applied at the top; the mobile phase runs through and fractions are collected.
- **Elution:** isocratic (one solvent) or **gradient** (polarity increased step by step). Flash chromatography uses gentle pressure to speed the run.
- **Uses:** preparative separation of larger amounts, as in the isolation of artemisinin, podophyllotoxin and reserpine.

### 5.4.4 Gas chromatography (GC)

- **Mobile phase:** an inert carrier gas (nitrogen, helium). **Stationary phase:** a liquid film on a solid support (packed column) or coated on the wall of a capillary column.
- The sample must be **volatile and thermally stable**; non-volatile compounds are made volatile by **derivatisation** (silylation, methylation).
- **Parts:** carrier gas supply → injector → oven with column (isothermal or temperature-programmed) → detector → recorder.
- **Detectors:** flame ionisation (FID, general for organics), thermal conductivity (TCD), electron capture (ECD, for halogens and pesticides), nitrogen–phosphorus, and **mass spectrometer (GC–MS)**.
- **Uses in pharmacognosy:** composition of **volatile oils** (menthol, citral, eugenol, anethole), residual solvents, pesticide residues.

### 5.4.5 High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

- A liquid mobile phase is pumped at high pressure through a column packed with very small particles.
- **Normal phase:** polar stationary phase (silica), non-polar mobile phase. **Reverse phase (the common one):** non-polar stationary phase (C18 or C8 bonded silica) with a polar mobile phase (water or buffer + methanol or acetonitrile). In reverse phase, **polar compounds come out first**.
- **Elution:** isocratic or gradient.
- **Parts:** solvent reservoirs and degasser → pump → injector (loop or autosampler) → column (often in an oven) → detector → data system.
- **Detectors:** UV–visible and **photodiode array (PDA)**, fluorescence, refractive index, **evaporative light scattering (ELSD)** for compounds without a chromophore (artemisinin, saponins), electrochemical, and **mass spectrometer (LC–MS/MS)**.
- **Uses:** assay of sennosides, digoxin, paclitaxel, podophyllotoxin, caffeine, curcuminoids; fingerprinting of herbal extracts; stability and impurity testing.
- Related: **UPLC** (sub-2 µm particles, faster and sharper) and **preparative HPLC** for isolating pure compounds.

**Terms used in column methods**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Retention time (tR) | Time from injection to the peak maximum |
| Resolution | How completely two neighbouring peaks are separated |
| Theoretical plates (N) | Measure of column efficiency; more plates means sharper peaks |
| Tailing (asymmetry) factor | Shape of the peak; should be close to 1 |

### 5.4.6 Other chromatographic methods

- **Ion-exchange chromatography:** charged resins hold ions of opposite charge; used for alkaloid salts, amino acids and water softening.
- **Size-exclusion (gel filtration) chromatography:** porous gel beads (Sephadex); large molecules come out first; used for proteins, polysaccharides and tannins.
- **Affinity chromatography:** a specific ligand bound to the support binds only its partner molecule; used for enzymes and antibodies.

## 5.5 Spectroscopy

Spectroscopy studies how a compound interacts with electromagnetic radiation. Each technique answers a different question about the structure.

| Technique | Radiation | What it tells | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **UV–visible** | 200–800 nm; electron transitions | Conjugation and chromophores; λmax | Identity, and quantity through Beer–Lambert law |
| **IR** | 4000–400 cm−1; bond vibrations | Functional groups; the **fingerprint region** (1500–600 cm−1) identifies the compound | Identity testing in pharmacopoeias |
| **NMR (1H and 13C)** | Radio waves in a magnetic field | Number, type and neighbours of hydrogen and carbon atoms | Full structure elucidation |
| **Mass spectrometry** | Ionisation, then separation by mass/charge | Molecular weight and fragments; molecular formula with high-resolution MS | Structure, and trace analysis with GC–MS or LC–MS |

### 5.5.1 UV–visible spectroscopy

- **Beer–Lambert law:** A = ε c l, where A is absorbance, ε the molar absorptivity, c the concentration and l the path length (usually 1 cm). Absorbance is proportional to concentration, so UV is used for assays.
- **Chromophores** (C=C, C=O, aromatic rings) absorb; **auxochromes** (–OH, –NH2, –OCH3) shift the absorption.
- **Shifts:** bathochromic (red, to longer wavelength), hypsochromic (blue), hyperchromic (greater absorbance), hypochromic (smaller).
- **In phytochemistry:** flavonoids give two characteristic bands, and **shift reagents** (sodium methoxide, aluminium chloride, sodium acetate, boric acid) move them in ways that show where the free hydroxyl groups are. Caffeine is assayed at about 273 nm, curcuminoids at about 425 nm.

### 5.5.2 Infrared spectroscopy

- Absorption occurs when a bond vibration changes the dipole moment.
- Useful group frequencies: O–H and N–H 3200–3600 cm−1 (broad for OH), C–H near 2900, C≡N near 2250, C=O 1650–1800 (ester and lactone higher than ketone and amide), C=C about 1600–1650, C–O 1000–1300 cm−1.
- Samples are run as KBr discs, nujol mulls, thin films, or directly by **ATR**.
- Two compounds with superimposable IR spectra (fingerprint region) are the same substance — the basis of pharmacopoeial identity tests. **NIR** is used for quick, non-destructive screening of raw materials.

### 5.5.3 NMR spectroscopy

- Nuclei with spin (1H, 13C) line up in a strong magnetic field and absorb radio waves at their resonance frequency.
- **1H NMR gives:** chemical shift δ (what kind of proton; TMS = 0 ppm), **integration** (how many protons), **multiplicity** (how many neighbours; n+1 rule) and **coupling constant J** (geometry, for example cis or trans).
- **13C NMR** shows how many different carbons there are and their type; DEPT distinguishes CH3, CH2 and CH.
- **2D NMR** (COSY, HSQC, HMBC, NOESY) links the atoms together and settles the full structure and stereochemistry of a new natural product.

### 5.5.4 Mass spectrometry

- The sample is ionised, the ions are separated by their mass-to-charge ratio and detected.
- **Ionisation:** electron impact (EI, gives many fragments, good library matching), chemical ionisation (softer), and for non-volatile natural products **ESI** and **MALDI**.
- The **molecular ion peak (M+)** gives the molecular weight; fragment peaks and isotope peaks (for example the M+2 peak of chlorine or bromine) help work out the structure.
- **Hyphenated techniques** — GC–MS, LC–MS, LC–MS/MS, LC–NMR — combine separation with identification and are the standard tools for **dereplication** (finding out quickly whether a compound in an extract is already known), for trace analysis, and for pesticide and heavy-metal screening in herbal drugs.

## 5.6 Electrophoresis

**Principle.** Charged particles move in an electric field towards the electrode of opposite charge. The speed depends on the charge, the size and shape of the molecule, the field strength, the pH and the ionic strength of the buffer, and the supporting medium.

```text
Anode (+)  ←── anions move          cations move ──→  Cathode (−)

Mobility = charge ÷ (size × friction);  pH decides the charge, so buffer choice is critical.
```

**Types**

| Type | Support | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paper / cellulose acetate** | Strip wetted with buffer | Serum proteins, amino acids, small molecules |
| **Gel electrophoresis — agarose** | Agarose gel | Nucleic acids (DNA fingerprinting of medicinal plants) |
| **Gel electrophoresis — polyacrylamide (PAGE)** | Polyacrylamide | Proteins and enzymes; **SDS-PAGE** separates by molecular weight alone |
| **Isoelectric focusing** | pH gradient gel | Proteins separate at their isoelectric point (pI) |
| **Two-dimensional** | IEF then SDS-PAGE | Complex protein mixtures (proteomics) |
| **Capillary electrophoresis (CE)** | Narrow fused-silica capillary with high voltage | Very high resolution, tiny sample; alkaloids, flavonoids, organic acids; variants MEKC and CZE |

**Uses in pharmacognosy and phytochemistry**

- **DNA barcoding and fingerprinting** (RAPD, AFLP, ISSR markers run on agarose gels) to confirm the identity of a medicinal plant and to detect substitution — important where powdered drugs look alike.
- Isoenzyme and seed-protein patterns to tell varieties apart.
- Purity and identity of proteins and enzymes used as drugs (papain, bromelain, streptokinase).
- Capillary electrophoresis for separating plant acids, alkaloids and flavonoids, as an alternative to HPLC.

## 5.7 Putting it together: standardisation of a herbal drug

```text
Authentic plant material
   ↓  macroscopy, microscopy, DNA barcoding
Identity confirmed
   ↓  ash values, extractive values, moisture, foreign matter, heavy metals, pesticides, microbes
Quality parameters within limits
   ↓  extraction (classical or modern)
Extract
   ↓  TLC / HPTLC fingerprint; HPLC or GC assay of marker compounds
Standardised extract
   ↓  formulation and stability study
Finished herbal product
```

A **marker compound** is a constituent used for quality control. It may be an **active marker** (the compound responsible for the effect, such as sennoside B in senna) or an **analytical marker** (a compound that is simply easy to measure and characteristic of the plant).

## Remember

- Extraction depends on size reduction, wetting, dissolution and diffusion; the solvent decides what is pulled out.
- Maceration, percolation, digestion, infusion, decoction, Soxhlet, counter-current, distillation and expression are the classical methods.
- Modern methods: supercritical CO2 (no residue, low temperature), microwave-assisted, ultrasound-assisted, pressurised solvent and enzyme-assisted extraction.
- Rf = distance of solute ÷ distance of solvent front, and is reproducible only under identical conditions.
- Paper chromatography is partition on water held by cellulose; TLC is mainly adsorption on silica gel G; HPTLC adds a densitometer for quantity.
- GC needs volatile, stable samples; HPLC in reverse phase elutes polar compounds first; ELSD is used when a compound has no chromophore.
- UV gives conjugation and quantity (A = ε c l); IR gives functional groups and the fingerprint region; NMR gives the structure; MS gives the molecular weight.
- Electrophoresis separates by charge and size; agarose for DNA, PAGE for proteins, capillary electrophoresis for small molecules; DNA fingerprinting confirms plant identity.
- Standardisation combines identity, purity, a chromatographic fingerprint and an assay of marker compounds.
