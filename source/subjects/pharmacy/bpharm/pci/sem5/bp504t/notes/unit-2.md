# Unit 2: Secondary metabolites — chemical classes and drug monographs

This is the largest unit (14 hours in the syllabus). For each class you must know a short **general introduction** (definition, composition, chemistry and chemical classes), and for each drug its **biological source with family**, **chemical constituents**, **therapeutic uses** and **commercial applications**. The drugs are grouped exactly as in the syllabus.

**How to write any monograph in the exam**

```text
Synonym → Biological source (part, botanical name, family) → Geographical source
→ (Cultivation/collection, if known) → Description (in brief)
→ Chemical constituents (with class) → Chemical tests → Uses → Commercial applications
→ Adulterants / substitutes (if any)
```

## 2.1 Alkaloids

### 2.1.1 General introduction

**Alkaloids** are basic, nitrogen-containing compounds of plant origin, usually with the nitrogen in a heterocyclic ring, usually physiologically active even in small doses, and usually derived from amino acids. Most are bitter, crystalline, colourless solids (nicotine and coniine are liquids; berberine is yellow). The free bases dissolve in organic solvents such as chloroform and ether; their salts dissolve in water. This difference is the basis of their extraction.

**Classification (chemical, by ring system)**

| Class | Example (source) |
| --- | --- |
| Pyridine–piperidine | Lobeline, nicotine, arecoline |
| Tropane | Atropine, hyoscine (belladonna), cocaine |
| Quinoline | Quinine, quinidine (cinchona) |
| Isoquinoline | Morphine, codeine, papaverine (opium), berberine, emetine |
| Indole | Reserpine (rauwolfia), vinblastine (vinca), ergometrine, strychnine |
| Imidazole | Pilocarpine |
| Purine | Caffeine (tea, coffee), theobromine |
| Steroidal | Solasodine, conessine |
| Amino-alkaloids (protoalkaloids) | Ephedrine, colchicine, mescaline |
| Diterpene | Aconitine, taxine |

**General tests:** Mayer's (potassium mercuric iodide → cream precipitate), Dragendorff's (potassium bismuth iodide → orange-red), Wagner's (iodine in KI → reddish-brown), Hager's (picric acid → yellow), tannic acid (buff precipitate).

### 2.1.2 Vinca (Catharanthus)

- **Synonyms:** Madagascar periwinkle, sadabahar, *Vinca rosea*.
- **Biological source:** Dried whole plant (or leaves) of *Catharanthus roseus* (L.) G. Don, family **Apocynaceae**.
- **Geographical source:** Native to Madagascar; grown throughout India, also in the USA, Israel and South Africa.
- **Chemical constituents:** more than a hundred **monoterpenoid indole alkaloids**.
  - **Dimeric (bisindole) alkaloids:** **vinblastine** (vincaleukoblastine) and **vincristine** (leurocristine), present in very small amounts.
  - **Monomers:** **vindoline** and **catharanthine** (the two halves of vinblastine), **ajmalicine** (raubasine), serpentine, lochnerine, tetrahydroalstonine.
- **Uses:**
  - Vinblastine: Hodgkin's disease and other lymphomas, testicular cancer, Kaposi's sarcoma, choriocarcinoma.
  - Vincristine: acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (especially in children), lymphomas, Wilms' tumour.
  - Both bind tubulin and stop formation of the mitotic spindle, arresting cells in metaphase. Vincristine mainly causes nerve damage (neuropathy); vinblastine mainly suppresses the bone marrow.
  - Ajmalicine: circulatory disorders and hypertension.
- **Commercial applications:** source of anticancer alkaloids; vindoline and catharanthine for semi-synthesis of vinblastine and of vinorelbine and vindesine; ornamental plant.

### 2.1.3 Rauwolfia

- **Synonyms:** Sarpagandha, Indian snakeroot.
- **Biological source:** Dried roots of *Rauwolfia serpentina* Benth. ex Kurz, family **Apocynaceae**.
- **Geographical source:** India (sub-Himalayan tracts, Bihar, Assam, Western Ghats), Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Thailand. Over-collection made wild stocks scarce, so it is now cultivated.
- **Chemical constituents:** about 0.7–2% total alkaloids of the **indole** type (amounts vary with source).
  - **Ester alkaloids** (weakly basic, tertiary): **reserpine**, rescinnamine, deserpidine — the hypotensive and sedative ones.
  - **Indoline / indolenine alkaloids:** **ajmaline**, ajmalicine, yohimbine-type.
  - **Anhydronium (quaternary) bases:** serpentine, serpentinine.
  - Also starch, resin and oleoresin.
- **Uses:**
  - Reserpine: hypertension (mild) and formerly as a tranquilliser in psychiatry. It depletes noradrenaline, dopamine and serotonin from nerve endings.
  - Ajmaline: an antiarrhythmic.
  - In Ayurveda: insomnia, hypertension, insanity.
- **Commercial applications:** production of reserpine and of ajmaline; standardised extracts.
- **Adulterants:** roots of *R. tetraphylla*, *R. vomitoria* (African), *R. densiflora*.

### 2.1.4 Belladonna

- **Synonyms:** Deadly nightshade.
- **Biological source:** Dried leaves and other aerial parts of *Atropa belladonna* L. (European belladonna) or *Atropa acuminata* Royle ex Lindley (Indian belladonna), family **Solanaceae**. The roots are also used as belladonna root.
- **Geographical source:** Europe; *A. acuminata* in the western Himalayas (Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh).
- **Chemical constituents:** **tropane alkaloids** (the leaf contains less than 1%).
  - **L-Hyoscyamine** is the main alkaloid. During extraction it racemises to **atropine** (DL-hyoscyamine).
  - **Hyoscine** (scopolamine) occurs in smaller amounts.
  - Minor: apoatropine, belladonnine.
  - **Scopoletin** (a fluorescent coumarin), flavonoids, calcium oxalate (sandy crystals).
- **Chemical test:** **Vitali–Morin test** — residue + fuming nitric acid, evaporated, then acetone and alcoholic KOH → violet colour (tropane esters).
- **Uses (anticholinergic, antimuscarinic):**
  - antispasmodic for intestinal and biliary colic; reduces gastric and bronchial secretions;
  - preanaesthetic medication;
  - atropine as a mydriatic and cycloplegic in eye examination;
  - atropine for bradycardia and as an antidote to organophosphate poisoning;
  - hyoscine for motion sickness.
- **Commercial applications:** belladonna tincture, extract and plasters; source of atropine. Related plants used commercially for tropane alkaloids include *Datura*, *Hyoscyamus* and *Duboisia*.
- **Adulterants:** leaves of *Phytolacca americana* and *Ailanthus*.

### 2.1.5 Opium

- **Synonyms:** Afim, raw opium.
- **Biological source:** Dried latex obtained by incising the unripe capsules of *Papaver somniferum* L., family **Papaveraceae**.
- **Geographical source:** India is a major licit producer (licensed cultivation in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh; processed at government factories such as Ghazipur and Neemuch). Also Turkey, Australia (as poppy straw).
- **Collection:** Capsules are incised a few days after the petals fall, when they turn from green to yellowish-green. Shallow vertical cuts are made in the afternoon with a multi-bladed knife (nushtur), and the coagulated latex is scraped off the next morning.
- **Chemical constituents:** about 25 alkaloids, mostly combined with **meconic acid**.
  - **Phenanthrene group:** **morphine** (the main alkaloid, about 10%), **codeine** and **thebaine**.
  - **Benzylisoquinoline group:** **papaverine**, **noscapine** (narcotine) and narceine.
  - Also meconic acid, sugars, resin, wax and mucilage.
- **Chemical test:** an aqueous extract with ferric chloride gives a deep red colour (meconic acid) that is **not** removed by dilute HCl.
- **Uses:**
  - Morphine: severe pain (myocardial infarction, cancer, after surgery).
  - Codeine: cough suppressant and mild analgesic.
  - Papaverine: smooth-muscle relaxant (vasodilator).
  - Noscapine: antitussive.
  - Thebaine: raw material for semi-synthetic opioids and antagonists (for example oxycodone, buprenorphine, naloxone).
  - Opium preparations were used for diarrhoea.
- **Commercial applications:** opium and poppy straw are the raw material for alkaloid factories. Poppy seeds (khus-khus) contain almost no alkaloid and are used in food; poppy seed oil is also used.
- **Law:** opium, poppy straw and their alkaloids are controlled under the **Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (NDPS) Act, 1985**. Cultivation needs a licence from the Central Bureau of Narcotics.

## 2.2 Phenylpropanoids and flavonoids

### 2.2.1 General introduction

**Phenylpropanoids** are compounds with a C6–C3 skeleton (benzene ring + three-carbon side chain) formed from phenylalanine through cinnamic acid (shikimic acid pathway).

| Group | Skeleton | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hydroxycinnamic acids | C6–C3 | Caffeic, ferulic, p-coumaric acids |
| Phenylpropenes | C6–C3 | Eugenol, anethole, myristicin |
| Coumarins | C6–C3 lactone | Umbelliferone, psoralen, scopoletin |
| **Lignans** | (C6–C3)2, linked through the middle carbons of the side chains | Podophyllotoxin, sesamin, phyllanthin |
| Neolignans | (C6–C3)2, linked otherwise | Magnolol |
| Flavonolignans | Flavonoid + phenylpropane | Silybin (milk thistle) |
| Lignin | Polymer of cinnamyl alcohols | Wood |

**Flavonoids** have a C6–C3–C6 skeleton (two benzene rings A and B joined by a three-carbon unit that usually forms a pyran ring C). Ring B and the C3 unit come from phenylalanine; ring A comes from three acetate (malonate) units.

| Class | Feature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flavones | C2=C3 double bond, 4-keto | Apigenin, luteolin |
| Flavonols | Flavone + 3-OH | Quercetin, kaempferol, rutin (glycoside) |
| Flavanones | C2–C3 saturated | Naringenin, hesperidin, liquiritigenin |
| Isoflavones | Ring B at C3 | Genistein, daidzein (soya) |
| Chalcones | Open C ring | Isoliquiritigenin (liquorice) |
| Flavan-3-ols (catechins) | No 4-keto | Catechin, epigallocatechin gallate (tea) |
| Anthocyanidins | Flavylium cation (coloured) | Cyanidin, delphinidin |

**Tests:** Shinoda (Mg turnings + conc. HCl → pink to red: flavones, flavonols, flavanones); alkali (NaOH → yellow); lead acetate (yellow precipitate); ferric chloride (green or blue-black with free phenolic OH).

**Uses of flavonoids:** antioxidants, "vitamin P" activity (reduce capillary fragility and permeability), anti-inflammatory, venotonic, hepatoprotective (silymarin), phyto-oestrogenic (isoflavones).

### 2.2.2 Lignans

Lignans are covered as a class, with podophyllum as the main drug.

| Lignan | Source (family) | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Podophyllotoxin** | Rhizomes and roots of *Podophyllum hexandrum* Royle (Indian podophyllum) and *P. peltatum* L. (American podophyllum, May apple), **Berberidaceae** | Topical treatment of genital warts; starting material for the anticancer drugs **etoposide** and **teniposide** |
| Silybin (flavonolignan, part of silymarin) | Fruits of *Silybum marianum*, Asteraceae | Liver protection |
| Phyllanthin, hypophyllanthin | *Phyllanthus amarus*, Phyllanthaceae (formerly Euphorbiaceae) | Hepatoprotective |
| Sesamin, sesamolin | Seeds of *Sesamum indicum*, Pedaliaceae | Antioxidant; sesamin synergises pyrethrum insecticides |
| Nordihydroguaiaretic acid | *Larrea* species, Zygophyllaceae | Antioxidant |

**Podophyllum in more detail**

- **Constituents:** podophyllum resin (podophyllin), which contains podophyllotoxin, α- and β-peltatins (more in the American drug), 4'-demethylpodophyllotoxin and their glycosides, and kaempferol and quercetin. Indian podophyllum gives more resin, and its resin is richer in podophyllotoxin.
- **Actions:** podophyllotoxin binds tubulin and stops cell division. Etoposide instead inhibits topoisomerase II.
- **Uses:** etoposide is used in small-cell lung cancer and testicular cancer. The resin is a drastic purgative (now obsolete) and a topical treatment for warts.
- **Conservation:** *P. hexandrum* is threatened by over-collection and is listed in CITES Appendix II.

### 2.2.3 Tea

- **Biological source:** Prepared leaves and leaf buds of *Camellia sinensis* (L.) Kuntze (syn. *Thea sinensis*), family **Theaceae**.
- **Geographical source:** India (Assam, West Bengal–Darjeeling, Nilgiris, Kerala), China, Sri Lanka, Kenya.
- **Types:**
  - **green tea** — leaves steamed or pan-fired at once to destroy the oxidising enzymes, then rolled and dried;
  - **black tea** — leaves withered, rolled, "fermented" (actually enzymic oxidation by polyphenol oxidase) and dried;
  - **oolong** — partly oxidised.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Purine alkaloids:** **caffeine** (the main one), with small amounts of theobromine and theophylline.
  - **Polyphenols:** catechins (flavan-3-ols) such as **epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG)**, epicatechin, epicatechin gallate and epigallocatechin. In black tea, oxidation turns them into the red-brown **theaflavins** and **thearubigins**.
  - Flavonol glycosides (quercetin, kaempferol), tannins.
  - The amino acid **L-theanine**, which gives the savoury taste.
  - Volatile oil (aroma), vitamins and fluoride.
- **Uses:** mild CNS stimulant, diuretic, astringent. Green tea catechins are antioxidants and are studied for heart disease and cancer prevention. Theophylline (made synthetically today) is a bronchodilator.
- **Commercial applications:** beverage (instant tea, iced tea); **tea dust and tea waste are a source of caffeine**; green tea extracts in nutraceuticals and cosmetics; catechins as natural antioxidants.
- **Test:** caffeine gives the **murexide test** (purple with ammonia).

### 2.2.4 Ruta

- **Synonyms:** Rue, garden rue, sudab.
- **Biological source:** Dried leaves and flowering tops of *Ruta graveolens* L., family **Rutaceae**.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Flavonoid glycoside rutin** — quercetin-3-rutinoside. Rutinose = rhamnose + glucose. The name rutin comes from this plant.
  - **Furanocoumarins:** psoralen, bergapten, xanthotoxin (methoxsalen).
  - **Furoquinoline and quinoline alkaloids:** skimmianine, dictamnine, graveoline.
  - **Volatile oil** with methyl nonyl ketone (2-undecanone).
- **Uses:** rutin is used for capillary fragility, varicose veins, haemorrhoids and venous insufficiency, often with vitamin C. Rue has been used traditionally as an antispasmodic and emmenagogue. Large doses are toxic and may cause abortion, and the furanocoumarins make the skin sensitive to sunlight (phytophotodermatitis).
- **Commercial applications:** rutin is produced commercially mainly from buckwheat (*Fagopyrum esculentum*, Polygonaceae) and from the flower buds of *Sophora japonica* (Fabaceae), as well as from rue. Psoralens are used with UV-A light (PUVA) in vitiligo and psoriasis. Rue is also used as a flavour and insect repellent.

## 2.3 Steroids, cardiac glycosides and triterpenoids

### 2.3.1 General introduction

- **Steroids** have the cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene nucleus (four fused rings A–D). They are formed from squalene through cycloartenol (in plants).
- **Steroidal saponins** (for example dioscin) have a spirostan side chain. They foam in water and haemolyse red cells. Their aglycones (sapogenins such as **diosgenin**, hecogenin) are the raw material for steroid drugs.
- **Cardiac glycosides** are steroids with a lactone ring at C-17 and sugars at C-3; they strengthen heart contraction.
  - **Cardenolides** (C23) have a five-membered unsaturated lactone (butenolide) — digitalis.
  - **Bufadienolides** (C24) have a six-membered doubly unsaturated lactone — squill.
  - Typical features: 3β-OH carrying the sugar, 14β-OH, cis A/B and C/D ring junctions. The special 2,6-dideoxy sugars (digitoxose) give the Keller–Killiani test.
- **Triterpenoids** (C30) such as glycyrrhetinic acid, oleanolic acid, ursolic acid and the triterpenoid saponins (glycyrrhizin) also come from squalene.

**Tests:**
- **Liebermann–Burchard** (acetic anhydride + conc. H2SO4 → green-blue for steroids, pink-red-violet for triterpenoids).
- **Salkowski** (chloroform + conc. H2SO4 → red chloroform layer).
- For cardiac glycosides: **Keller–Killiani**, **Legal**, **Baljet**, **Kedde** and **Raymond** tests.
- **Foam test** and **haemolysis** for saponins.

### 2.3.2 Liquorice

- **Synonyms:** Glycyrrhiza, mulethi, yashtimadhu.
- **Biological source:** Dried, peeled or unpeeled roots and stolons of *Glycyrrhiza glabra* L., family **Leguminosae** (Fabaceae, subfamily Papilionaceae).
- **Geographical source:** Spain, Italy, Russia, Iran, Iraq, China; grown in Punjab and sub-Himalayan India.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Glycyrrhizin** (glycyrrhizic acid), a **triterpenoid saponin** present as potassium and calcium salts. It is about 50 times sweeter than sucrose. On hydrolysis it gives the aglycone **18β-glycyrrhetinic acid (glycyrrhetic acid)** and two molecules of glucuronic acid.
  - **Flavonoids:** liquiritin, isoliquiritin and their aglycones liquiritigenin (a flavanone) and isoliquiritigenin (a chalcone, which gives the yellow colour). Also isoflavonoids such as glabridin.
  - Sugars, starch, asparagine and coumarins.
- **Chemical test:** a section or powder treated with 80% sulphuric acid turns deep yellow.
- **Uses:**
  - demulcent and expectorant in cough mixtures;
  - sweetening and flavouring agent that masks bitter tastes;
  - treatment of peptic ulcer — **carbenoxolone** is a derivative of glycyrrhetinic acid;
  - anti-inflammatory.
  - Large or long-term doses cause sodium and water retention, low potassium and high blood pressure ("pseudoaldosteronism"), because glycyrrhetinic acid blocks the enzyme that inactivates cortisol.
- **Commercial applications:** confectionery, tobacco flavouring, beverages and herbal teas; deglycyrrhizinated liquorice for ulcers; ammonium glycyrrhizinate as a sweetener and foaming agent; cosmetics (glabridin as a skin-lightening agent).

### 2.3.3 Dioscorea

- **Synonyms:** Yam, kanta alu.
- **Biological source:** Dried tubers (rhizomes) of various *Dioscorea* species, family **Dioscoreaceae** — *Dioscorea deltoidea* Wall. (Indian, Himalayan), *D. floribunda* M. Martens & Galeotti and *D. composita* Hemsl. (Mexican, cultivated in India), *D. prazeri* and others.
- **Geographical source:** Mexico, Central America, India (Himalayas, North-East; cultivated in Karnataka and Assam), China.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Steroidal saponins**, chiefly **dioscin**, which on acid hydrolysis gives the sapogenin **diosgenin** (a spirostan, 25R) with yamogenin (its 25S isomer).
  - Starch, phytosterols and tannins.
- **Uses:**
  - **Diosgenin** is the most important starting material for **semi-synthesis of steroid hormones**. By the **Marker degradation** it gives **16-dehydropregnenolone acetate (16-DPA)**, which is converted to progesterone, corticosteroids (cortisone, hydrocortisone, prednisolone, dexamethasone), androgens, oestrogens and oral contraceptives.
  - Some species are eaten as yams after cooking (the bitter steroidal species are not food).
- **Commercial applications:** the steroid industry. Other sources of steroid starting materials are fenugreek seeds (diosgenin), *Costus speciosus*, *Solanum* species (solasodine), sisal waste (hecogenin, Agave) and soya sterols (stigmasterol, sitosterol).

### 2.3.4 Digitalis

- **Synonyms:** Foxglove leaf.
- **Biological source:** Dried leaves of *Digitalis purpurea* L. (purple foxglove), family **Scrophulariaceae** (now placed in Plantaginaceae). The leaves are dried quickly at about 60 °C soon after collection to stop enzymic breakdown. *Digitalis lanata* Ehrh. (Grecian or woolly foxglove) leaves are the source of digoxin.
- **Geographical source:** Europe, England, USA; cultivated in Kashmir and the Nilgiris in India.
- **Chemical constituents:** **cardenolide (cardiac) glycosides**.
  - ***D. purpurea*:** the primary glycosides **purpurea glycoside A** and **B**. On storage or enzymic action (digipurpidase) they lose glucose to give **digitoxin** and **gitoxin**. Also gitaloxin. The aglycones are digitoxigenin and gitoxigenin.
  - ***D. lanata*:** **lanatosides A, B, C** (with D and E). These are acetylated and carry glucose. **Lanatoside C** gives **digoxin** (aglycone digoxigenin) after removal of the acetyl group and the glucose.
  - The sugar chain is three molecules of digitoxose plus glucose.
  - Also steroidal saponins (digitonin, mainly in seeds), anthraquinones, flavonoids and the enzyme digipurpidase.
- **Chemical tests:** Keller–Killiani test (glacial acetic acid + a trace of FeCl3, then conc. H2SO4 run down the side → reddish-brown ring at the junction and bluish-green upper layer: 2-deoxy sugar); Legal test (pink to red); Baljet test (orange).
- **Uses:**
  - **Digoxin** — congestive heart failure and control of ventricular rate in **atrial fibrillation**. It inhibits Na+/K+-ATPase, raising calcium in heart muscle (positive inotropic effect), and slows AV conduction.
  - **Digitoxin** — longer acting.
  - These drugs have a **narrow therapeutic index**, so blood levels are monitored.
- **Commercial applications:** production of digoxin, digitoxin, lanatoside C and deslanoside; foxglove is also an ornamental plant.
- **Adulterants:** leaves of *Verbascum thapsus* (mullein), *Symphytum*, *Primula*.

## 2.4 Volatile oils

### 2.4.1 General introduction

**Volatile (essential) oils** are odorous, volatile products of plants that evaporate at room temperature. They are usually mixtures of **terpenoids** (mainly monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes) and **phenylpropanoids**. They occur in special secretory structures: glandular hairs (Lamiaceae), oil cells (Lauraceae), oil glands (Myrtaceae, Rutaceae) and vittae or oil ducts (Apiaceae).

| Chemical class | Example (drug) |
| --- | --- |
| Hydrocarbons (terpenes) | Limonene, pinene |
| Alcohols | Menthol (mentha), linalool (coriander), geraniol |
| Aldehydes | Citral (lemongrass), cinnamaldehyde (cinnamon) |
| Ketones | Menthone, carvone, fenchone, camphor |
| Phenols | Eugenol (clove), thymol (ajowan) |
| Phenolic ethers | Anethole (fennel), safrole, myristicin |
| Esters | Menthyl acetate, linalyl acetate |
| Oxides | 1,8-Cineole (eucalyptus) |

**Extraction:** steam or water distillation (most), expression (citrus peels), solvent extraction and enfleurage (delicate flowers such as jasmine and rose), supercritical CO2 extraction.

**Tests:**
- a spot on filter paper evaporates without a lasting grease stain (unlike a fixed oil);
- Sudan III stains oil globules red;
- the oil gives a characteristic odour;
- TLC with anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid spray.

### 2.4.2 Mentha (peppermint and mint)

- **Biological source:** Fresh or dried flowering tops (leaves) of *Mentha piperita* L. (peppermint) and of *Mentha arvensis* L. var. *piperascens* (Japanese or corn mint), family **Lamiaceae** (Labiatae). Spearmint is *M. spicata*. The oil is obtained by steam distillation.
- **Geographical source:** India (Uttar Pradesh — Barabanki, Rampur, Moradabad; Punjab) is the world's largest producer of mint oil and menthol. Also the USA, China and Brazil.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Peppermint oil:** **menthol** (the main constituent, free and as esters), menthone, menthyl acetate, menthofuran, 1,8-cineole, limonene and pulegone.
  - **Japanese mint oil:** very rich in menthol, which crystallises when the oil is cooled. It is the main commercial source of natural menthol.
  - **Spearmint:** carvone.
- **Uses:**
  - carminative and antispasmodic (enteric-coated peppermint oil capsules for irritable bowel syndrome);
  - flavour and freshener;
  - menthol as a counter-irritant and cooling agent in balms and rubs — it acts on cold receptors (TRPM8);
  - nasal decongestant inhalations, throat lozenges, antiseptic.
- **Commercial applications:** toothpastes, mouthwashes, chewing gum, confectionery, pan masala, cigarettes, cough drops and cosmetics. Dementholised oil is used as a cheaper flavour.

### 2.4.3 Clove

- **Synonyms:** Lavang, caryophyllum.
- **Biological source:** Dried flower buds of *Syzygium aromaticum* (L.) Merr. & L. M. Perry (syn. *Eugenia caryophyllus*), family **Myrtaceae**.
- **Geographical source:** Indonesia, Zanzibar and Pemba (Tanzania), Madagascar; in India, Kerala and Tamil Nadu.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Volatile oil** (about 15–20%). Its main constituent is the phenol **eugenol**, with acetyl eugenol, β-caryophyllene and small amounts of methyl amyl ketone (a contributor to the odour).
  - **Tannins** (gallotannic acid).
  - Oleanolic acid, crategolic acid, sterols and fixed oil.
- **Chemical tests:**
  - an alcoholic extract or a drop of oil with ferric chloride gives a **blue** colour (eugenol);
  - with strong KOH solution the oil forms needle-shaped crystals of potassium eugenate.
- **Uses:**
  - dental analgesic and antiseptic (clove oil for toothache);
  - **zinc oxide–eugenol** dental cement;
  - carminative, stomachic, flavouring agent and spice;
  - eugenol is a starting material for **vanillin** (through isoeugenol).
- **Commercial applications:** spice, perfumery, toothpaste, kretek cigarettes.
- **Adulterants:** clove stalks (contain stone cells and calcium oxalate prisms), mother cloves (ripe fruits, with starch), blown cloves (expanded flowers) and exhausted cloves (oil removed; they float horizontally on water).

### 2.4.4 Cinnamon

- **Synonyms:** Dalchini, Ceylon cinnamon.
- **Biological source:** Dried inner bark of the shoots of coppiced trees of *Cinnamomum zeylanicum* Blume (syn. *C. verum*), family **Lauraceae**. The outer bark (cork and cortex) is scraped off, so the drug consists of pericycle and phloem. **Cassia bark** (Chinese cinnamon) is *Cinnamomum cassia* Blume (syn. *C. aromaticum*), also Lauraceae.
- **Geographical source:** Sri Lanka, South India (Kerala), Seychelles; cassia from China and Vietnam.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Volatile oil**, mainly **cinnamaldehyde (cinnamic aldehyde)**, with **eugenol**, cinnamyl acetate, linalool and benzaldehyde. Ceylon cinnamon bark oil has a noticeable amount of eugenol. Cinnamon **leaf** oil is mostly eugenol.
  - Tannins, mucilage, calcium oxalate (fine needles), starch.
  - Cassia oil has a higher share of cinnamaldehyde, very little eugenol, and contains **coumarin**.
- **Chemical test:** the oil (or an extract) gives a yellow-orange precipitate with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (aldehyde).
- **Uses:** carminative, stomachic, aromatic, mild astringent, antiseptic and flavouring agent. Cinnamon is studied for helping blood glucose control.
- **Commercial applications:** spice, bakery, beverages, perfumes and soaps; cinnamon oil in mouthwashes and toothpastes. Cassia is the common "cinnamon" in trade, but its coumarin limits large-scale use.

### 2.4.5 Fennel

- **Synonyms:** Saunf, foeniculum.
- **Biological source:** Dried ripe fruits (cremocarps) of *Foeniculum vulgare* Mill., family **Umbelliferae** (Apiaceae). Sweet fennel is var. *dulce*; bitter fennel is var. *vulgare*.
- **Geographical source:** India (Gujarat, Rajasthan), Egypt, Romania, Mediterranean countries.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Volatile oil** (about 2–6%), with **trans-anethole** as the main constituent (sweet), **fenchone** (bitter, camphoraceous; more in bitter fennel), estragole (methyl chavicol), limonene and α-pinene.
  - Fixed oil and proteins.
- **Uses:**
  - carminative, mild expectorant and flavouring agent;
  - in gripe water for infant colic;
  - mouth freshener after meals;
  - used traditionally to improve milk flow.
- **Commercial applications:** spice, liqueurs, confectionery, toothpaste and soap; anethole as a flavour.
- **Adulterants:** exhausted fennel (oil removed), fruits of other umbellifers.

### 2.4.6 Coriander

- **Synonyms:** Dhania.
- **Biological source:** Dried ripe fruits of *Coriandrum sativum* L., family **Umbelliferae** (Apiaceae).
- **Geographical source:** India (Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat — India is a leading producer), Morocco, Russia, Bulgaria.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Volatile oil** (about 0.3–1%), mainly **(+)-linalool (coriandrol)**, with α-pinene, γ-terpinene, camphor, geraniol and geranyl acetate.
  - Fixed oil (rich in petroselinic acid), proteins and tannin.
  - The unripe fruit and the fresh leaves have an unpleasant odour from aliphatic aldehydes (decanal), which mostly disappears on ripening.
- **Uses:** carminative, stomachic, flavouring agent and spice; used to mask the taste of senna and rhubarb preparations.
- **Commercial applications:** curry powder and masala, sausages, gin and liqueurs, perfumery; linalool from the oil.

## 2.5 Tannins

### 2.5.1 General introduction

**Tannins** are complex polyphenols that **precipitate proteins** (they convert hide into leather), giving an astringent taste.

| Type | Hydrolysable tannins | Condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Esters of gallic acid (gallotannins) or ellagic acid (ellagitannins) with glucose | Polymers of flavan-3-ols (catechin, epicatechin) linked C–C |
| Acid treatment | Hydrolysed to gallic/ellagic acid + sugar | Polymerise to red insoluble **phlobaphenes** (tanner's reds) |
| Ferric chloride | **Blue-black** | **Green-black** |
| Heat (dry) | Give pyrogallol | Give catechol (pyrocatechin) |
| Bromine water | No precipitate | Yellow precipitate |
| Examples | Nutgall, myrobalan, rhubarb, clove | **Catechu**, **kino (Pterocarpus)**, cinnamon, tea |

**Pseudotannins** (gallic acid, catechins, chlorogenic acid) are smaller and do not tan hide.

**Tests:**
- **gelatin test** — 1% gelatin + 10% NaCl → white precipitate;
- **goldbeater's skin test** — the skin turns brown or black after treatment with tannin and then FeSO4;
- **phenazone test**;
- **matchstick (vanillin–HCl) test** for catechins.

### 2.5.2 Catechu

- **Black catechu (cutch, kattha):** Dried aqueous extract of the heartwood of *Acacia catechu* Willd. (syn. *Senegalia catechu*), family **Leguminosae** (Mimosoideae).
  - **Preparation:** chips of heartwood are boiled with water; the decoction is concentrated and poured into moulds or allowed to cool and harden.
- **Pale catechu (gambier):** Dried aqueous extract of the leaves and young shoots of *Uncaria gambier* Roxb., family **Rubiaceae**. It is made in Malaysia and Indonesia as cubes.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **catechins** (catechin, epicatechin, called acacatechin in black catechu);
  - **catechutannic acid** (a condensed tannin);
  - quercetin, catechu red (a phlobaphene) and gum.
  - Pale catechu also contains a fluorescent substance (gambier fluorescein).
- **Chemical tests:**
  - **matchstick test:** a matchstick dipped in the aqueous extract, dried, moistened with conc. HCl and warmed near a flame turns **pink or purple** — catechins react with the phloroglucinol formed from the lignin of the wood;
  - **gambier fluorescein test** (pale catechu): alcoholic NaOH extract shaken with light petroleum → green fluorescence in the petroleum layer.
- **Uses:**
  - astringent in diarrhoea and in throat lozenges and gargles;
  - black catechu is eaten with betel leaf (paan);
  - tanning and dyeing (khaki shades of cotton and fishing nets).

### 2.5.3 Pterocarpus

The syllabus name covers two Indian *Pterocarpus* drugs of family **Leguminosae** (Fabaceae).

**(a) Indian kino and heartwood of *Pterocarpus marsupium* Roxb.** (vijaysar, Malabar kino tree)
- **Biological source:**
  - **kino** — the dried juice (gum-kino) that exudes from incisions made in the stem bark, drying to dark red, brittle, shining pieces;
  - **heartwood** — used in Ayurveda.
- **Constituents:**
  - **kino** — kinotannic acid (a condensed, catechin-type tannin), kino red (phlobaphene), catechin and pyrocatechol;
  - **heartwood** — (−)-epicatechin, the stilbene **pterostilbene**, marsupin, pterosupin, liquiritigenin, isoliquiritigenin and other flavonoids.
- **Uses:**
  - kino as an astringent in diarrhoea and in gargles;
  - heartwood as an **antidiabetic** in Ayurveda — water stored in tumblers carved from vijaysar wood is drunk. Epicatechin and the flavonoids have shown blood-glucose-lowering activity in animal studies.

**(b) Red sandalwood (red sanders), *Pterocarpus santalinus* L. f.**
- **Biological source:** Heartwood.
- **Constituents:** red pigments **santalin A and B**, savinin, pterocarpin and other isoflavonoids.
- **Uses:** colouring agent for foods and drugs, astringent and cooling paste for skin in traditional medicine, dye.
- It grows wild mainly in the Andhra Pradesh hills. It is protected, and export of its wood is restricted.

## 2.6 Resins

### 2.6.1 General introduction

**Resins** are amorphous, brittle solids or semisolids formed by plants. They are mixtures of resin acids, resin alcohols (resinols, resinotannols), resin esters and inert resenes. They are insoluble in water but soluble in alcohol, ether and chloroform, and they burn with a smoky flame.

| Type | Composition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resins proper | Mainly resin | Colophony, podophyllum resin |
| Oleoresins | Resin + volatile oil | Turpentine, ginger oleoresin, capsicum |
| Gum resins | Resin + gum | — (rare) |
| Oleo-gum-resins | Resin + volatile oil + gum | **Asafoetida, myrrh, guggul** |
| Balsams | Resin with **cinnamic and/or benzoic acid** or their esters | **Benzoin**, tolu, Peru balsam, storax |
| Glycoresins | Resin + glycoside | Jalap |

### 2.6.2 Benzoin

- **Biological source:** Balsamic resin obtained from incisions made in the stems of *Styrax benzoin* Dryander and *S. paralleloneurum* Perkins (**Sumatra benzoin**) or of *Styrax tonkinensis* (Pierre) Craib (**Siam benzoin**), family **Styracaceae**. Benzoin is a pathological product: it forms only after the tree is wounded.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Sumatra benzoin:**
    - free balsamic acids — **cinnamic acid** and benzoic acid;
    - esters — coniferyl cinnamate, cinnamyl cinnamate (styracin), phenylpropyl cinnamate;
    - sumaresinolic acid and vanillin.
  - **Siam benzoin:** mainly coniferyl benzoate, free benzoic acid and vanillin, with **little or no cinnamic acid**.
- **Chemical tests:**
  - heated in a dry test tube it gives a white **sublimate of benzoic acid**;
  - **Sumatra benzoin** warmed with KMnO4 solution smells of **benzaldehyde** (bitter almond) because cinnamic acid is oxidised; Siam benzoin does not.
- **Uses:**
  - **Compound benzoin tincture (Friar's balsam)** as a steam inhalation for colds and bronchitis, and as an antiseptic protective on wounds and cracked skin;
  - mild expectorant;
  - preservative for fats (benzoinated lard);
  - incense and perfumery (Siam benzoin);
  - a source of benzoic acid.

### 2.6.3 Guggul

- **Synonyms:** Indian bdellium, gum guggul.
- **Biological source:** Oleo-gum-resin obtained by incising the bark of *Commiphora wightii* (Arn.) Bhandari (syn. *C. mukul*), family **Burseraceae**.
- **Geographical source:** Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka; Pakistan. The shrub is threatened by heavy tapping.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Guggulsterones E and Z** (pregnane steroids), the hypolipidaemic principles;
  - guggulsterols;
  - diterpenoids (cembrenes);
  - myrrhanol;
  - volatile oil (myrcene);
  - gum (galactose, arabinose).
- **Uses:**
  - hypolipidaemic — the standardised ethyl acetate extract "**gugulipid**" lowers cholesterol and triglycerides;
  - anti-inflammatory and antiarthritic in Ayurveda (yogaraj guggulu, kaishore guggulu);
  - obesity, acne;
  - also used as an incense and fixative.

### 2.6.4 Ginger

- **Synonyms:** Adrak (fresh), sonth (dry).
- **Biological source:** Scraped or unscraped dried rhizomes of *Zingiber officinale* Roscoe, family **Zingiberaceae**.
- **Geographical source:** India (Kerala — Cochin ginger; North-East), Jamaica, Nigeria, China.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Oleoresin** (pungent principles):
    - **gingerols** — [6]-gingerol is the main one;
    - **shogaols** — formed from gingerols by loss of water on drying and storage; more pungent;
    - zingerone — formed by heating.
  - **Volatile oil** (about 1–3%) — **zingiberene**, ar-curcumene, β-bisabolene, β-sesquiphellandrene and citral. These give the aroma; the oil is not pungent.
  - Starch (about half of the drug), resin and mucilage.
- **Uses:**
  - carminative, stomachic and appetiser;
  - **antiemetic** in motion sickness and pregnancy nausea;
  - anti-inflammatory;
  - counter-irritant;
  - flavour and spice.
- **Commercial applications:** ginger oleoresin (solvent extraction), ginger oil, ginger beer and ale, candied ginger, confectionery.

### 2.6.5 Asafoetida

- **Synonyms:** Hing, devil's dung.
- **Biological source:** Oleo-gum-resin obtained by incising the living rhizomes and roots of *Ferula assa-foetida* L., *Ferula foetida* Regel and other *Ferula* species, family **Umbelliferae** (Apiaceae).
- **Geographical source:** Iran and Afghanistan (imported to India); cultivation has recently begun in the Lahaul valley of Himachal Pradesh.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **resin** — asaresinotannol, largely as ferulic acid esters; free **ferulic acid**; **umbelliferone** in combined form (not free in the genuine drug);
  - **gum** — glucose, galactose, arabinose;
  - **volatile oil** — organic **sulphur compounds** such as (R)-2-butyl propenyl disulphide, which give the strong garlic-like odour.
- **Chemical tests:**
  - a fresh fracture touched with conc. H2SO4 turns red to reddish-brown;
  - with 50% HNO3 it turns green;
  - **umbelliferone test** — boil with HCl, filter, add ammonia → **blue fluorescence**;
  - triturated with water it forms a milky emulsion.
- **Uses:**
  - carminative, antispasmodic, expectorant;
  - used in flatulence, colic and whooping cough (historical);
  - **condiment** (hing) in Indian cooking.
- **Commercial applications:** compounded hing (mixed with gum arabic and wheat or rice flour) for the food trade.
- **Adulterants:** colophony, gum arabic, galbanum, stones, sand and red clay.

### 2.6.6 Myrrh

- **Synonyms:** Gum myrrh, bola.
- **Biological source:** Oleo-gum-resin obtained from the stem of *Commiphora myrrha* (Nees) Engl. (syn. *C. molmol*) and other *Commiphora* species, family **Burseraceae**.
- **Geographical source:** Somalia, Ethiopia, Yemen, Arabia.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **gum** — the largest part, with an oxidase enzyme;
  - **resin** — commiferin, heerabomyrrholic acids, myrrhols;
  - **volatile oil** — **furanosesquiterpenes** such as furanoeudesma-1,3-diene and curzerene, which give the odour, with cuminaldehyde and eugenol.
- **Chemical test:** an ether extract, evaporated and exposed to bromine vapour (or treated with nitric acid), gives a **violet** colour from the furanosesquiterpenes.
- **Uses:**
  - astringent and antiseptic in mouthwashes and gargles for ulcers, gingivitis and sore throat;
  - carminative;
  - in tincture of myrrh;
  - incense, perfumery and embalming in history.

### 2.6.7 Colophony

- **Synonyms:** Rosin, resina pini.
- **Biological source:** The residue left after distilling off the volatile oil (turpentine) from the oleoresin of various **Pinus** species — *Pinus palustris* Mill., *P. elliottii* Engelm. and, in India, *P. roxburghii* Sarg. (chir pine) — family **Pinaceae**.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - mostly **resin acids** — the abietic acid group, including abietic, levopimaric, neoabietic, palustric and dehydroabietic acids, with pimaric acids;
  - resene;
  - a trace of volatile oil.
- **Chemical tests:**
  - a solution in acetic anhydride with a drop of conc. H2SO4 gives a **purple-violet** colour that quickly fades (Storch–Morawski type test);
  - a solution in light petroleum shaken with dilute **copper acetate** gives an **emerald-green** colour in the petroleum layer (copper abietate).
- **Uses:** in plasters (zinc oxide adhesive plaster), ointments and adhesives; varnishes, paints, printing inks, paper sizing, soaps and sealing wax; for violin bows; in soldering flux. It is a known **contact allergen**.

## 2.7 Glycosides

### 2.7.1 General introduction

**Glycosides** are compounds in which a sugar (glycone) is joined through a glycosidic bond to a non-sugar part (**aglycone or genin**). They are hydrolysed by acids or by enzymes present in the same plant.

| Basis | Classes |
| --- | --- |
| Type of linkage | O-glycosides (senna sennosides), C-glycosides (aloin — resistant to acid), S-glycosides (sinigrin), N-glycosides (nucleosides) |
| Chemical nature of aglycone | Anthraquinone (senna, aloe), cardiac (digitalis), saponin (liquorice, dioscorea), cyanogenetic (bitter almond), isothiocyanate (mustard), flavonoid (rutin), coumarin, aldehyde (vanilla), phenol (bearberry), bitter/iridoid (gentian) |

**Anthraquinone glycosides** (from the acetate–malonate pathway) act as **stimulant laxatives**. They pass unabsorbed to the colon, where bacteria release the active anthrones.

**Tests:**
- **Borntrager's test** — the drug is boiled with dilute acid, extracted with benzene or chloroform, and the organic layer shaken with ammonia → **pink-red** ammoniacal layer;
- **modified Borntrager's test** — boiled with FeCl3 + HCl to oxidise anthrones and split C-glycosides.

### 2.7.2 Senna

- **Biological source:** Dried leaflets of ***Cassia angustifolia*** Vahl (**Tinnevelly or Indian senna**; now *Senna alexandrina* Mill.) or of ***Cassia acutifolia*** Delile (**Alexandrian senna**), family **Leguminosae** (Caesalpinioideae). Senna pods (fruits) are also official.
- **Geographical source:** Tinnevelly senna is cultivated in Tamil Nadu (Tirunelveli, Madurai) and Gujarat. Alexandrian senna comes from Egypt and Sudan.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Dianthrone O-glycosides:**
    - **sennosides A and B** — glucosides of sennidins A and B, stereoisomers of a rhein dianthrone;
    - **sennosides C and D** — heterodianthrones of rhein and aloe-emodin.
  - Free and combined anthraquinones (rhein, aloe-emodin).
  - Flavonols (kaempferol, isorhamnetin), mucilage, calcium oxalate, resin.
- **Chemical test:** Borntrager's test (after hydrolysis) → pink-red ammonia layer.
- **Microscopy:**
  - isobilateral leaf with **paracytic (rubiaceous) stomata** on both surfaces;
  - **unicellular, thick-walled, warty trichomes**;
  - a **crystal sheath** of calcium oxalate prisms around the fibres of the midrib;
  - cluster crystals in the mesophyll.
- **Uses:**
  - **stimulant laxative** for constipation;
  - bowel cleansing before X-ray and colonoscopy.
  - Colonic bacteria convert sennosides to **rhein anthrone**, which increases peristalsis and fluid secretion.
  - Often given with carminatives (ginger, coriander) to reduce griping.
- **Commercial applications:** **calcium sennosides** tablets, senna granules and syrups; India is a leading exporter of senna leaves and pods.
- **Adulterants:** *Cassia auriculata* (Avaram), *C. obovata* (dog senna), *Solenostemma argel* (argel leaves) and *Coriaria myrtifolia* leaves.

### 2.7.3 Aloes

- **Biological source:** Dried juice of the leaves of various *Aloe* species, family **Liliaceae** (now Asphodelaceae / Xanthorrhoeaceae):
  - *Aloe barbadensis* Miller (syn. *A. vera*) — **Curacao aloes**;
  - *Aloe ferox* Miller and its hybrids — **Cape aloes**;
  - *Aloe perryi* Baker — **Socotrine aloes**.
- **Preparation:** cut leaves are stood so that the yellow juice from the pericyclic cells drains out. The juice is concentrated by boiling (or sun drying) and poured into containers to set.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Aloin (barbaloin)** — a **C-glycoside** of aloe-emodin anthrone (10-glucosyl);
  - isobarbaloin (in Curacao aloes);
  - aloinosides A and B, free aloe-emodin, the chromone **aloesin**, and resin.
- **Chemical tests:**
  - **modified Borntrager's test** (C-glycoside needs FeCl3 + HCl);
  - **Schouteten's test** (borax → green fluorescence);
  - **bromine test** (pale yellow precipitate of tetrabromaloin);
  - **nitric acid test** (colours differ for the commercial varieties);
  - **cupraloin test** for isobarbaloin (wine-red with Curacao aloes).
- **Uses:**
  - **purgative** — powerful, may cause griping; seldom used now;
  - **aloe gel** (from the inner leaf parenchyma, a different product with no anthraquinones) for burns, wounds, sunburn and cosmetics.
- **Commercial applications:** aloe gel in cosmetics, shampoos, juices and "aloe vera" health products; bitter flavour in some beverages.

### 2.7.4 Bitter almond

- **Biological source:** Dried ripe seeds of *Prunus amygdalus* Batsch var. *amara* (syn. *Prunus dulcis* var. *amara*), family **Rosaceae**. Sweet almond is var. *dulcis*.
- **Geographical source:** Mediterranean countries, Iran, California; Kashmir in India.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **amygdalin** — a **cyanogenetic glycoside**, mandelonitrile gentiobioside; sweet almond has very little;
  - **emulsin** — an enzyme mixture of amygdalase, prunase and hydroxynitrile lyase;
  - **fixed oil** (almond oil), mainly olein;
  - proteins.
- **Hydrolysis** (when the crushed seed is moistened with water):

```text
Amygdalin  ──amygdalase──→  Prunasin + Glucose
Prunasin   ──prunase────→  Mandelonitrile + Glucose
Mandelonitrile ──hydroxynitrile lyase──→  Benzaldehyde + HCN
```

- **Chemical test:** crushed moist seeds kept in a closed tube with a strip of **sodium picrate paper** turn it **brick-red** (HCN; Guignard's test).
- **Uses:**
  - **almond oil** (expressed from bitter or sweet almonds) — emollient, demulcent, nutritive, vehicle for oily injections, ear drops, cosmetics;
  - **volatile oil of bitter almond** (benzaldehyde, after HCN is removed — "free from prussic acid") — flavouring agent.
  - Amygdalin ("laetrile") has been promoted as an anticancer agent, but there is no proof that it works, and it can cause cyanide poisoning.
- **Toxicity:** eating many bitter almonds can be fatal because of HCN.

## 2.8 Iridoids, other terpenoids and naphthoquinones

### 2.8.1 General introduction

- **Iridoids** are monoterpenes (C10) with a cyclopentane ring fused to a six-membered oxygen ring (cyclopenta[c]pyran), usually present as glucosides. **Secoiridoids** have the cyclopentane ring opened (gentiopicrin, swertiamarin, secologanin). Many are very **bitter** and are used as bitter tonics (gentian, chirata, picrorhiza, valerian's valepotriates).
- **Other terpenoids** in this group are artemisinin (sesquiterpene lactone), taxol (diterpene) and carotenoids (tetraterpenes).
- **Naphthoquinones** are coloured compounds with a naphthalene-1,4-dione nucleus.

| Naphthoquinone | Source (family) | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lawsone** | Leaves of henna, *Lawsonia inermis*, Lythraceae | Hair and skin dye |
| **Plumbagin** | Roots of chitrak, *Plumbago zeylanica*, Plumbaginaceae | Digestive in Ayurveda; antimicrobial; vesicant |
| **Juglone** | Walnut, *Juglans regia*, Juglandaceae | Dye, antifungal |
| **Shikonin** | Roots of *Lithospermum erythrorhizon*, Boraginaceae | Red dye, wound healing; first secondary metabolite produced commercially by plant cell culture |
| Vitamin K (phylloquinone) | Green leaves | Blood clotting |

### 2.8.2 Gentian

- **Biological source:** Dried, fermented rhizomes and roots of *Gentiana lutea* L. (yellow gentian), family **Gentianaceae**.
- **Geographical source:** Mountains of central and southern Europe (Alps, Pyrenees). In India, chirata (*Swertia chirata*) and kutki (*Picrorhiza kurroa*) are used as similar bitters.
- **Preparation:** the collected roots are piled in heaps and dried slowly. This "fermentation" darkens the colour, develops the odour and reduces the bitterness a little.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **secoiridoid bitter glycosides:**
    - **gentiopicrin (gentiopicroside)** — the main bitter;
    - swertiamarin and sweroside;
    - **amarogentin** — present in small amounts but the most bitter natural compound known;
  - **xanthones** (gentisin, isogentisin) — give the yellow colour;
  - the alkaloid gentianine (an artefact);
  - sugars (gentianose, gentiobiose, sucrose) and pectin. **Starch is absent.**
- **Uses:** **bitter tonic** and stomachic to stimulate appetite and gastric secretion (reflex action through bitter taste receptors); in compound gentian infusion and tincture.
- **Commercial applications:** bitter liqueurs and aperitifs.

### 2.8.3 Artemisia

- **Biological source:** Dried leaves and flowering tops of *Artemisia annua* L. (sweet wormwood, qinghao), family **Asteraceae** (Compositae).
- **Geographical source:** Native to China; cultivated in China, Vietnam, East Africa, and in India (developed varieties from CSIR-CIMAP, Lucknow).
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **artemisinin (qinghaosu)** — a **sesquiterpene lactone** with a rare **endoperoxide bridge** (1,2,4-trioxane ring), essential for activity;
  - its precursors artemisinic acid and dihydroartemisinic acid;
  - arteannuin B;
  - volatile oil — artemisia ketone, camphor, 1,8-cineole;
  - flavonoids.
- **Uses:**
  - artemisinin and its semi-synthetic derivatives — **artesunate** (water-soluble), **artemether** and arteether (oil-soluble), dihydroartemisinin;
  - fast-acting **antimalarials** against *Plasmodium falciparum*, including chloroquine-resistant and cerebral malaria;
  - always given as **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)** to prevent resistance — for example artemether + lumefantrine, or artesunate + sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (used in India's national programme).
  - The endoperoxide reacts with iron (haem) in the parasite to produce damaging free radicals.
  - Tu Youyou received the Nobel Prize in 2015 for this discovery.
- **Other species:** *A. maritima* (santonin, an old anthelmintic), *A. absinthium* (wormwood, thujone).

### 2.8.4 Taxus

- **Biological source:** Bark and leaves (needles) of *Taxus brevifolia* Nutt. (Pacific yew), *Taxus baccata* L. (European yew) and *Taxus wallichiana* Zucc. (Himalayan yew, talispatra), family **Taxaceae**.
- **Chemical constituents:**
  - **Taxanes (diterpenoids):**
    - **paclitaxel (taxol)** — a taxane ring with a four-membered **oxetane** ring and a side chain (N-benzoyl-3-phenylisoserine) at C-13, which is needed for activity;
    - **10-deacetylbaccatin III (10-DAB)** — abundant in the needles;
    - baccatin III, cephalomannine.
  - **Taxine alkaloids** (very toxic).
  - Lignans and flavonoids.
- **Uses:**
  - **paclitaxel** for ovarian, breast and non-small-cell lung cancer, and AIDS-related Kaposi's sarcoma;
  - **docetaxel** (semi-synthetic from 10-DAB) for breast and prostate cancer.
  - Taxanes **stabilise microtubules** (promote their assembly and prevent breakdown), so cell division stops.
- **Commercial applications:** paclitaxel was first obtained from Pacific yew bark, where its content is very low, and stripping the bark kills the tree. It is now made by **semi-synthesis from 10-DAB** taken from the renewable needles, and by **plant cell fermentation**. The Himalayan yew is protected because of over-harvesting.

### 2.8.5 Carotenoids

- **Chemistry:** **tetraterpenoids (C40)** made of eight isoprene units, formed from two GGPP molecules through phytoene. A long chain of conjugated double bonds gives the yellow, orange or red colour.
  - **Carotenes** are hydrocarbons: α- and β-carotene, lycopene.
  - **Xanthophylls** are oxygenated: lutein, zeaxanthin, capsanthin, astaxanthin.
  - **Apocarotenoids** are degraded carotenoids: bixin, crocetin.

| Carotenoid | Source (family) |
| --- | --- |
| β-Carotene | Carrot (*Daucus carota*, Apiaceae), alga *Dunaliella salina*, palm oil |
| Lycopene | Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum*, Solanaceae), watermelon |
| Lutein | Marigold flowers (*Tagetes erecta*, Asteraceae) |
| Capsanthin, capsorubin | Paprika (*Capsicum annuum*, Solanaceae) |
| Crocin (crocetin glycoside) | Saffron stigmas (*Crocus sativus*, Iridaceae) |
| Bixin | Annatto seeds (*Bixa orellana*, Bixaceae) |
| Astaxanthin | Alga *Haematococcus pluvialis*; shrimp, salmon |

- **Test:** with conc. H2SO4, or with antimony trichloride in chloroform (Carr–Price reagent), carotenoids give a **blue** colour.
- **Uses:**
  - **provitamin A** — one β-carotene gives two molecules of retinal;
  - antioxidants — lutein and zeaxanthin protect the macula of the eye; lycopene;
  - **natural food colours** (annatto in butter and cheese, paprika, saffron);
  - astaxanthin in fish feed;
  - photoprotection in skin products.

## Remember

- Alkaloids: vinca (vinblastine, vincristine — anticancer, Apocynaceae), rauwolfia (reserpine — antihypertensive, Apocynaceae), belladonna (hyoscyamine/atropine — Solanaceae), opium (morphine, codeine, papaverine — Papaveraceae; NDPS Act).
- Lignans: podophyllotoxin (Berberidaceae) → etoposide. Tea (Theaceae): caffeine + catechins. Ruta (Rutaceae): rutin, furanocoumarins.
- Liquorice (Leguminosae): glycyrrhizin → glycyrrhetinic acid. Dioscorea (Dioscoreaceae): diosgenin → steroid hormones. Digitalis (Scrophulariaceae): digitoxin; *D. lanata* → digoxin.
- Volatile oils: mentha — menthol (Lamiaceae); clove — eugenol (Myrtaceae); cinnamon — cinnamaldehyde (Lauraceae); fennel — anethole; coriander — linalool (both Umbelliferae).
- Tannins: catechu (*Acacia catechu*, Leguminosae; gambier, Rubiaceae) — matchstick test; Pterocarpus — kino, vijaysar (antidiabetic), red sandalwood (santalins).
- Resins: benzoin (Styracaceae, balsam), guggul and myrrh (Burseraceae, oleo-gum-resins), ginger (Zingiberaceae, gingerols), asafoetida (Umbelliferae, sulphur compounds, umbelliferone test), colophony (Pinaceae, abietic acid, copper acetate test).
- Glycosides: senna (sennosides, Leguminosae), aloes (aloin, C-glycoside, Liliaceae), bitter almond (amygdalin → benzaldehyde + HCN, Rosaceae).
- Gentian (Gentianaceae): gentiopicrin, amarogentin — bitter tonic. Artemisia (Asteraceae): artemisinin — endoperoxide, ACT. Taxus (Taxaceae): paclitaxel — stabilises microtubules. Carotenoids: C40, provitamin A, colours.
