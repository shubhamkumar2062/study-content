# Unit 5: Pricing and emerging concepts in marketing

Price is the only element of the marketing mix that brings in money; all the others cost money. Medicines are special because they are essential goods: a price too high can stop a patient from completing treatment, so in India the government controls the prices of essential medicines. The second part of this unit looks at newer ways of thinking about marketing — vertical and horizontal marketing systems, rural marketing, consumerism, industrial marketing and global marketing.

## 5.1 Meaning and importance of price

**Price** is the amount of money charged for a product or service, or the sum of values that customers exchange for the benefits of having or using it.

**Price terms used in Indian pharma trade**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| MRP (maximum retail price) | Highest price at which the pack may be sold to the consumer; printed on the label, inclusive of taxes |
| PTR (price to retailer) | Price at which the stockist sells to the chemist |
| PTS (price to stockist) | Price at which the company sells to the stockist |
| Trade margin | Difference between buying and selling prices at each level (retailer margin, wholesaler margin) |
| Ceiling price | Maximum price fixed by the government for a scheduled formulation |

**Importance of price**

- Directly decides revenue and profit; small price changes have a large effect on profit.
- Affects demand, market share and access for patients.
- Communicates quality and positioning (premium vs economy brand).
- A competitive weapon, especially among branded generics of the same molecule.
- Influences trade support, because margins decide how willing chemists and stockists are to stock a brand.
- In pharma it is also a public-health issue, since many medicines are bought out of pocket.

## 5.2 Pricing objectives

1. **Survival** — cover costs in times of heavy competition.
2. **Profit maximisation** — highest current profit.
3. **Target return on investment** — a planned return on capital invested.
4. **Market share leadership** — low or competitive price to win share.
5. **Product quality leadership** — premium price for a high-quality image.
6. **Meeting or preventing competition** — match competitors, discourage new entrants.
7. **Social objectives** — affordable prices for essential medicines, access programmes.
8. **Stabilising prices** — avoid price wars.

## 5.3 Determinants of price (factors affecting pricing decisions)

**Internal factors**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Marketing objectives | Share, profit or image goals set the price level |
| Costs | Fixed and variable costs set the floor below which price cannot go for long |
| Marketing mix strategy | Price must fit the product's positioning, channel margins and promotion budget |
| Product life cycle stage | Launch, growth, maturity and decline need different prices |
| Organisation | Who decides price (top management, product managers) |

**External factors**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Nature of market and demand | Price elasticity; chronic therapy and life-saving drugs are often less price sensitive, OTC more |
| Competition | Prices of competing brands of the same molecule and of therapeutic alternatives |
| Government regulation | Price control (DPCO), taxes (GST), trade margin rules |
| Economic conditions | Inflation, raw material (API) costs, currency changes for imported APIs |
| Channel members | Expected trade margins |
| Customers' ability to pay | Out-of-pocket spending, insurance, government schemes |
| Ethical and social considerations | Fair pricing of essential medicines |

**Price elasticity of demand** = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price. If the value is greater than 1 (in absolute terms), demand is elastic; if less than 1, inelastic.

## 5.4 Pricing methods

### Cost-based methods

- **Cost-plus (mark-up) pricing:** price = unit cost + a fixed percentage mark-up.
  - Example: unit cost ₹40, mark-up 25% → price = 40 + 10 = ₹50.
- **Break-even analysis and target-profit pricing:** break-even volume = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost per unit).
  - Example: fixed cost ₹1,00,000, price ₹50, variable cost ₹30 → break-even = 1,00,000 ÷ 20 = 5,000 units.
- **Target return pricing:** price set to give a planned return on investment at expected volume.

### Demand-based (value-based) methods

- **Perceived-value pricing:** price based on the value customers see, not on cost (for a product with a clear clinical advantage).
- **Demand differential pricing:** different prices for different segments, pack sizes or markets where allowed.

### Competition-based methods

- **Going-rate pricing:** price close to competitors' average — very common among branded generics.
- **Sealed-bid (tender) pricing:** used for hospital and government tenders; price set by expected bids of rivals.

## 5.5 Pricing strategies

| Strategy | Meaning | Pharma use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Market skimming | High launch price, lowered later | Innovative or patented products with few competitors |
| Market penetration | Low launch price to gain share quickly | New branded generic entering a crowded molecule market |
| Product line pricing | Price steps between items of a line | Different strengths of the same brand |
| Bundle pricing | Combined product price | Kits, combination packs |
| Psychological pricing | Prices that look lower | Mostly OTC products |
| Promotional pricing | Temporary schemes and offers | Trade schemes, OTC offers (within rules) |
| Geographical pricing | Different prices by region | Export markets |
| Differential (tiered) pricing | Lower prices for lower-income markets | Access programmes for essential medicines |
| Prestige pricing | High price signalling quality | Premium nutraceuticals and cosmetics |

## 5.6 Issues in price management in the pharmaceutical industry

- **Price control** — ceiling prices for scheduled medicines limit freedom and margins; companies may stop making low-margin products, risking shortages.
- **Many brands, same molecule** — wide price differences between brands of the same drug cause public concern.
- **Trade margins** — high margins on some generics and pressure from the trade for more margin.
- **Rising input costs** — API prices, imports, inflation and currency changes while selling prices are capped.
- **Affordability and access** — high out-of-pocket spending; pressure for lower prices from government schemes and Jan Aushadhi stores.
- **Patented and new drugs** — balancing the cost of research with affordability.
- **Tender pricing** — very low prices in institutional supply.
- **Ethical issues** — avoiding overcharging, misleading MRPs, and price-based inducements.
- **Compliance** — correct MRP labelling, annual revisions as allowed, reporting to the regulator.

## 5.7 Overview of the Drug (Prices Control) Order (DPCO)

The **Drugs (Prices Control) Order** is issued by the Government of India under **section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955** to regulate the prices of medicines.

**History:** earlier orders were issued in 1970, 1979, 1987 and 1995. These older orders fixed prices mainly on the basis of the **cost of manufacture** plus an allowance for post-manufacturing expenses. The current order is **DPCO 2013**, based on the **National Pharmaceutical Pricing Policy, 2012**.

**Main features of DPCO 2013**

1. **Scope based on essentiality:** the medicines listed in the **National List of Essential Medicines (NLEM)** form its **First Schedule** (scheduled formulations). The NLEM is revised from time to time (editions of 2011, 2015 and 2022), and the schedule follows the revised list.
2. **Market-based pricing (not cost-based):** the **ceiling price** of a scheduled formulation is worked out as the **simple average of the price to retailer (PTR)** of all brands and generic versions of that formulation that have a **market share of 1% or more**, and then a **notional retailer margin of 16%** is added.
3. **Ceiling price** is notified in the Gazette of India; all manufacturers must sell at or below it (plus applicable taxes).
4. **Annual revision:** ceiling prices may be revised on **1 April** each year in line with the **wholesale price index (WPI)** of the previous year; prices go down if the WPI falls.
5. **Non-scheduled formulations:** manufacturers may not raise the MRP by more than **10% in a year**.
6. **New drugs:** the retail price of a "new drug" (for example a new combination or new strength of a scheduled drug) is fixed by the government before it can be marketed.
7. **Special powers:** in extraordinary circumstances, in public interest, the government can fix or cap the price of any drug.
8. **Exemptions:** certain new drugs developed through research in India and patented here are exempt for a limited period (five years) under the order, subject to its conditions.
9. **Overcharging:** amounts charged above the notified price are recovered with interest.
10. **Display:** retailers must display the price list supplied by the manufacturer.

## 5.8 National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority (NPPA)

The **National Pharmaceutical Pricing Authority** was set up by a Government of India resolution dated **29 August 1997**. It is an independent body of experts, attached to the **Department of Pharmaceuticals, Ministry of Chemicals and Fertilizers**.

**Functions of NPPA**

1. Fix and revise the ceiling prices of scheduled formulations and the retail prices of new drugs under the DPCO.
2. Enforce and implement the provisions of the DPCO.
3. Monitor the prices of decontrolled (non-scheduled) drugs and act on unreasonable increases.
4. Recover amounts overcharged by manufacturers from consumers, with interest.
5. Monitor the availability of medicines, identify shortages and take remedial action.
6. Collect and keep data on production, imports, exports and market share of companies.
7. Deal with legal matters arising from its decisions.
8. Advise the government on changes and revisions in drug pricing policy.

**DPCO vs NPPA in one line:** the DPCO is the legal order containing the price rules; the NPPA is the authority that applies and enforces them.

## 5.9 Emerging concepts in marketing

### Vertical marketing system (VMS)

In a **conventional channel**, the producer, wholesaler and retailer are independent businesses, each trying to maximise its own profit, often with conflict. A **vertical marketing system** makes them act as one unified system.

| Type | How unity is achieved | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Corporate VMS | One firm owns successive stages | A company owning its own distribution arm or pharmacy outlets |
| Contractual VMS | Independent firms join through contracts | Franchise pharmacies, distributor agreements |
| Administered VMS | Leadership by the size and power of one member | A large company coordinating stockists through its policies |

**Benefits:** less conflict, better control, economies of scale, coordinated planning.

### Horizontal marketing system

Two or more companies **at the same level** join together to follow a new marketing opportunity, combining their capital, production, distribution or marketing strengths.

Examples: **co-marketing** (two companies sell the same molecule under their own brands), **co-promotion** (two companies promote one brand together), joint ventures for distribution or export, in-licensing deals.

### Rural marketing

**Rural marketing** is the planning and carrying out of the marketing mix for rural customers.

**Features and challenges of rural healthcare markets:** scattered population, poor roads and transport, fewer qualified doctors and chemists, lower and seasonal incomes, low awareness, language diversity, and difficulty in cold-chain supply.

**Strategies**

- Low-unit packs and affordable prices.
- Wider distribution through sub-stockists and rural retail points.
- Promotion in local languages; health camps; use of local influencers and health workers.
- Dedicated rural field divisions.
- Use of mobile phones and digital payments for ordering and information.
- Products suited to rural disease patterns.

### Consumerism

**Consumerism** is an organised movement of citizens and government to strengthen the rights and power of buyers in relation to sellers.

**Consumer rights** (commonly listed): right to safety, right to be informed, right to choose, right to be heard, right to seek redressal, right to consumer education.

**In India:** the **Consumer Protection Act, 2019** provides for consumer commissions at district, state and national levels and the Central Consumer Protection Authority, and covers unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements.

**Effect on pharma marketing:** need for truthful labels and claims, complaint handling, recall systems, fair pricing, product liability awareness, patient information leaflets.

### Industrial marketing

**Industrial (business-to-business) marketing** is the marketing of goods and services to organisations that use them to make other products or to run their operations.

**Pharma examples:** selling APIs and excipients to formulation companies, contract manufacturing and testing services, selling equipment and packaging materials, supplying hospitals and government institutions.

**Features:** few large buyers, derived demand, technical specifications, group buying decisions, long negotiation, long-term relationships, personal selling as the main tool (see industrial buying behaviour in Unit 1).

### Global marketing

**Global (international) marketing** is marketing a company's products in many countries with a coordinated strategy.

**Modes of entry**

| Mode | Risk and control |
| --- | --- |
| Exporting (direct or through agents) | Low risk, low control |
| Licensing and contract manufacturing | Low investment, shared returns |
| Joint venture | Shared ownership, shared risk |
| Direct investment (own subsidiary or plant) | High investment, full control |

**Key issues in pharma:** registration and approval by each country's regulatory authority, GMP standards and inspections, patent laws, pricing and reimbursement systems, cultural and language differences, documentation and dossiers, and the choice between a **standardised** and an **adapted** marketing mix.

**India's role:** India is a major supplier of generic medicines and vaccines to many countries, which makes global marketing skills important for Indian pharmacists.

## Remember

- Price terms: MRP, PTR, PTS, trade margin, ceiling price.
- Pricing methods: cost-plus, break-even, perceived value, going rate, sealed bid.
- Skimming = high launch price; penetration = low launch price.
- DPCO is issued under section 3 of the Essential Commodities Act, 1955; current order DPCO 2013, based on the National Pharmaceutical Pricing Policy 2012 and the NLEM.
- Ceiling price = simple average PTR of brands with ≥1% market share + 16% retailer margin; revised each 1 April by WPI.
- Non-scheduled drugs: MRP increase limited to 10% a year.
- NPPA: set up on 29 August 1997, under the Department of Pharmaceuticals; fixes prices, enforces DPCO, recovers overcharging, monitors availability.
- VMS: corporate, contractual, administered. Horizontal system: co-marketing, co-promotion.
- Consumer Protection Act, 2019 protects buyers.
