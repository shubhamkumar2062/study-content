# Unit 4: Marketing channels and the professional sales representative

A doctor can prescribe a brand only if the patient can actually find it at the nearest chemist. This unit covers two parts of the marketing mix that work in the field: **place** (the channels and physical distribution that move medicines from factory to patient) and the **professional sales representative**, who carries the company's message to doctors and the trade.

## 4.1 Marketing channels — meaning and functions

A **marketing (distribution) channel** is a set of interdependent organisations involved in making a product available for use or consumption by the final user. The organisations in between are **intermediaries** or **middlemen**.

**Why use intermediaries?** A company cannot reach lakhs of chemists directly. Intermediaries reduce the number of transactions, hold stock close to the market, give credit, and break bulk into small quantities.

```text
Without intermediary: 3 makers x 6 chemists = 18 contacts
With one stockist:    3 + 6 = 9 contacts
```

**Functions of channel members**

| Function | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Information | Collecting and passing market information (stock levels, demand, competitor schemes) |
| Promotion | Displays and recommendation at the point of sale |
| Contact | Finding and serving buyers |
| Matching / breaking bulk | Dividing large lots into smaller quantities |
| Negotiation | Agreeing on price and terms |
| Physical distribution | Transport and storage |
| Financing | Giving credit to the next level |
| Risk taking | Bearing the risk of expiry, damage and slow sales |

## 4.2 Channel members in the Indian pharmaceutical industry

```text
MANUFACTURER (factory / central warehouse)
        |
CARRYING & FORWARDING (C&F) AGENT  -- one per state or region; stores and
        |                              dispatches on the company's behalf
STOCKIST / DISTRIBUTOR (wholesaler)  -- buys, holds stock, gives credit
        |
RETAIL CHEMIST / HOSPITAL PHARMACY
        |
PATIENT (on doctor's prescription, or OTC purchase)
```

| Member | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Manufacturer | Makes and markets the product; sets trade terms |
| C&F agent | Works on commission; receives goods, stores them under proper conditions, invoices and dispatches to stockists; does not usually own the goods |
| Stockist / distributor | Licensed wholesaler; buys from the company, holds stock and supplies retailers and institutions; extends credit |
| Sub-stockist / sub-distributor | Serves small towns and rural chemists |
| Retail chemist | Licensed retailer; dispenses prescriptions and sells OTC products |
| Institutional channel | Hospitals, government medical stores, tenders and procurement agencies |
| Online pharmacy and chains | Newer channels selling through apps and organised retail stores |

All drug wholesalers and retailers must hold the appropriate sale licence under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act and Rules, and must keep proper records, especially for scheduled drugs.

## 4.3 Designing a channel

**Steps in channel design**

1. **Analyse customer needs** — how much and how quickly patients and chemists need the product, how widely it must be available, need for credit and service.
2. **Set channel objectives** — target level of service and coverage at the lowest cost.
3. **Identify major alternatives** — types of intermediaries, number of intermediaries, and terms and responsibilities of each member.
4. **Evaluate the alternatives** — on economic, control and adaptive criteria.

**Channel levels**

| Level | Path | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zero-level (direct) | Manufacturer → consumer | Company supplying a hospital directly, or own online sales where allowed |
| One-level | Manufacturer → retailer → consumer | Supply to large pharmacy chains |
| Two-level | Manufacturer → wholesaler → retailer → consumer | Usual route through stockists |
| Three-level | Manufacturer → C&F → stockist → retailer → consumer | Common Indian pharma route |

**Number of intermediaries (intensity)**

- **Intensive distribution** — as many outlets as possible; suits common prescription brands and OTC products.
- **Selective distribution** — a few chosen outlets in each area; suits speciality products needing care.
- **Exclusive distribution** — one outlet or distributor per area; suits very costly or cold-chain products needing special handling.

## 4.4 Selecting the appropriate channel

**Factors to consider**

| Group | Factors |
| --- | --- |
| Product | Prescription or OTC; perishability and shelf life; cold-chain need; unit value; bulk |
| Market | Number and spread of customers; urban or rural; retail or institutional buyers; order size |
| Company | Financial strength, size of field force, desire for control, product range |
| Intermediaries | Availability, reputation, financial capacity, storage facilities, licences, coverage |
| Environment | Legal requirements, economic conditions, competitors' channels, technology (e-pharmacy) |

**Criteria for choosing a stockist:** valid drug licence, sound finance and credit record, storage facilities (including cold storage where needed), coverage of chemists in the area, delivery capacity, reputation, and willingness to follow company policy.

## 4.5 Conflict in channels

**Channel conflict** arises when one channel member's actions prevent another member, or the channel, from achieving its goals.

**Types**

- **Vertical conflict** — between different levels (company vs stockist over margins, credit, targets).
- **Horizontal conflict** — between members at the same level (two stockists in the same area cutting prices or selling outside their territory).
- **Multichannel conflict** — between different channels serving the same market (online pharmacy discounts vs neighbourhood chemists).

**Causes**

- Disagreement over margins, discounts and schemes.
- Goal incompatibility (company wants volume, stockist wants profit and low inventory).
- Unclear roles and territories.
- Delayed payments and credit problems.
- Pushing excess stock to meet targets (loading), causing expiry returns.
- Different perceptions of the market.

**Managing conflict**

- Clear written agreements on territories, margins, credit and returns.
- Regular communication and joint planning.
- Fair and consistent policies for all members.
- Prompt handling of expired and damaged goods.
- Mediation or arbitration in serious disputes.
- Avoid stock loading; base supply on real demand.

## 4.6 Physical distribution management

**Physical distribution** (market logistics) means planning, carrying out and controlling the physical flow of goods from the point of origin to the point of use to meet customer needs at a profit.

### Strategic importance

- Medicines must be available when and where needed — a stock-out means a lost prescription and possibly a doctor switching brands permanently.
- Distribution cost is a large part of total marketing cost; efficiency improves profit.
- Many products need controlled storage (cold chain for vaccines, insulin and biologics) to keep their quality.
- Good service to stockists and chemists is a competitive advantage.
- Expiry and batch control (first-expiry-first-out) reduce losses and protect patients.

### Tasks in physical distribution management

| Task | What is done |
| --- | --- |
| Order processing | Receiving orders from stockists, checking credit, invoicing, dispatching quickly and accurately |
| Warehousing | Number and location of depots (C&F points), storage conditions, temperature and humidity control |
| Inventory management | How much stock to hold; reorder level and quantity; avoiding stock-outs and over-stocking; expiry management |
| Transportation | Choice of mode (road, rail, air, courier), cold-chain vehicles and boxes, cost versus speed |
| Materials handling | Proper packing, loading and unloading to avoid damage |
| Returns and recalls | Handling expired, damaged and recalled batches with traceable records |

**Total cost concept:** reduce the total of transport + warehousing + inventory + order-processing costs while keeping the required customer service level; cutting one cost often raises another.

## 4.7 The professional sales representative (PSR)

The **professional sales representative** — usually called the **medical representative (MR)** — is the company's field employee who promotes its products to doctors, pharmacists and hospitals, and services the trade in a given territory (headquarters and outstation areas).

## 4.8 Duties of a PSR

1. **Detailing** to doctors in the territory as per the call plan.
2. **Distributing samples, literature and reminder items** within the marketing code and with records.
3. **Calling on chemists** to check stock, take orders, place new products and solve problems.
4. **Visiting stockists** to monitor stock, orders, expiry returns and payments.
5. **Hospital work** — meeting heads of departments, pharmacy staff and purchase committees; helping with product listing.
6. **Collecting market information** — competitor activity, new doctors, prescription trends, complaints.
7. **Organising activities** — CMEs, camps, launch meetings, exhibition stalls.
8. **Reporting** — daily call reports, expense statements, monthly sales and planning reports.
9. **Passing on adverse event reports** and product complaints to the company.
10. **Achieving sales targets** while following ethics and the law.

## 4.9 Purpose of detailing

**Detailing** is the presentation of a product's scientific information to a doctor in a planned, systematic way during a personal call.

**Purposes**

- Introduce new products and new information (indications, dosage forms, evidence).
- Explain the product's benefits over alternatives with supporting data.
- Clarify doubts and handle objections.
- Remind the doctor of established brands.
- Obtain a commitment to prescribe (closing).
- Gather feedback on product performance and side effects.

**Structure of a detail:** opening (attention) → presentation of features, advantages and benefits (FAB) using the visual aid → handling objections → summary and request for prescription → giving samples or literature → recording the call.

## 4.10 Selection and training of PSRs

### Selection

**Qualities sought:** a science background (B.Pharm, B.Sc., D.Pharm), good communication, product knowledge ability, honesty, self-motivation, persistence, good appearance and manners, willingness to travel, and local language skills.

**Selection process:** job analysis and description → sources (campus, advertisements, referrals, consultants) → application screening → written test → group discussion → interviews (personal and technical) → reference and background check → medical check → appointment and induction.

### Training

**Content**

- Company: history, policies, products, organisation.
- Product and medical knowledge: anatomy, physiology, disease, pharmacology, competitor products.
- Selling skills: call planning, detailing, objection handling, closing.
- Market and territory knowledge: doctors, chemists, stockists, routes.
- Ethics, the marketing code, pharmacovigilance and legal requirements.
- Reporting systems and digital tools.

**Methods:** classroom induction, role play, field training with the area manager ("joint work"), e-learning, product tests, refresher courses and conferences.

## 4.11 Supervising PSRs

The **area manager (first-line manager)** supervises a team of representatives. Supervision includes:

- Field visits and **joint work** with each representative, observing calls and giving feedback.
- Reviewing call reports, coverage of the doctor list and frequency of visits.
- Monitoring sales against targets and stock at stockists.
- Coaching, solving problems, and maintaining discipline.
- Communication through meetings and review sessions.

## 4.12 Norms for customer calls

Companies set **call norms** so that effort is spread to the most valuable doctors.

| Norm | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Doctor list | Classified list of doctors in the territory |
| Doctor classification | Doctors graded by prescription potential (for example A, B, C categories or core and non-core) |
| Call frequency | High-potential doctors visited more often per month than low-potential ones |
| Daily call average | Expected number of doctor calls, chemist calls and stockist visits per working day |
| Coverage | Percentage of listed doctors met in the month |
| Tour plan | Monthly plan of headquarters and outstation working days |

Exact numbers differ from company to company; what matters is that norms are set by doctor potential and are realistic.

## 4.13 Motivating PSRs

**Financial motivators:** salary increments, incentives on reaching targets, bonuses, allowances.

**Non-financial motivators:** recognition and awards, promotion opportunities, training and development, fair and transparent targets, good leadership, job security, good working conditions, participation in decisions.

**Motivation theories applied:** Maslow's hierarchy of needs, Herzberg's two-factor theory (hygiene factors such as salary prevent dissatisfaction; motivators such as achievement and recognition create satisfaction), and expectancy theory (effort → performance → reward must be clearly linked).

## 4.14 Evaluating PSRs

**Quantitative measures**

- Sales achieved against target (value and units), growth over last year.
- Number of calls per day, doctor coverage and call frequency.
- Number of new prescribers won; performance of new products.
- Expense-to-sales ratio.
- Collection and stock position at stockists.

**Qualitative measures**

- Product knowledge and detailing skill (observed in joint work).
- Relationship with doctors, chemists and stockists.
- Quality and timeliness of reports.
- Attitude, discipline, ethics and teamwork.

**Methods:** comparing representatives with each other, comparing current with past performance, and appraisal forms with ratings.

## 4.15 Compensation of PSRs

| Plan | Features | Merits | Demerits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Straight salary | Fixed pay | Security; easy to control; encourages non-selling duties | Little incentive for extra effort |
| Straight commission | Pay only as a percentage of sales | Strong incentive; cost varies with sales | Insecurity; neglect of non-selling duties |
| Combination (salary + incentive) | Fixed salary plus incentive or bonus | Security plus motivation; most common in pharma | More complex to administer |

Other parts of compensation: daily allowance, travel allowance, mobile and internet allowance, medical and insurance benefits, provident fund and gratuity.

## 4.16 Future prospects of the PSR

- **Career path:** medical representative → senior representative → area manager → regional manager → zonal manager → sales or marketing head; switching to product management, training or key account management is common.
- **Changing role:** from frequent product reminders to scientific, value-based discussions; more **key account management** for hospitals; **digital detailing** (e-detailing, remote calls, digital literature) alongside visits.
- **Skills needed for the future:** stronger medical and scientific knowledge, data and digital skills, ethics and compliance awareness, and customer-focused service.
- **Challenges:** restrictions on promotional practices, more informed doctors, price control, high targets and competition.

## Remember

- Usual Indian channel: manufacturer → C&F agent → stockist → retailer → patient.
- Channel design: analyse needs → set objectives → identify alternatives → evaluate.
- Intensive, selective and exclusive distribution.
- Conflict types: vertical, horizontal, multichannel.
- Physical distribution tasks: order processing, warehousing, inventory, transport, handling, returns.
- PSR duties: detail, sample, chemist and stockist work, information, reports, adverse event reporting.
- Detailing structure: opening → FAB presentation → objections → closing → record.
- Compensation: salary, commission, combination (most common).
