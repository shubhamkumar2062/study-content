# Unit 2: Product decisions

The product is the heart of the marketing mix. Price, promotion and distribution only make sense once the company knows what it is selling, to whom, and how it differs from competitors. In pharmacy the "product" is not just the molecule: it includes the dosage form, strength, pack, brand name, label, quality image, and the information and service that come with it.

## 2.1 What is a product?

A **product** is anything that can be offered to a market for attention, acquisition, use or consumption that might satisfy a want or need. It includes physical goods, services, ideas and information.

**Three levels of a product**

```text
   +-------------------------------------------+
   | AUGMENTED PRODUCT                          |
   |  patient leaflets, medical information,    |
   |  doctor support, availability, complaint   |
   |  handling, patient-support programmes      |
   |   +-----------------------------------+    |
   |   | ACTUAL PRODUCT                     |    |
   |   |  brand name, dosage form, strength,|    |
   |   |  pack, label, quality, taste       |    |
   |   |   +---------------------------+    |    |
   |   |   | CORE BENEFIT               |    |    |
   |   |   |  relief, cure, prevention  |    |    |
   |   |   +---------------------------+    |    |
   |   +-----------------------------------+    |
   +-------------------------------------------+
```

## 2.2 Classification of products

**General classification**

| Basis | Classes |
| --- | --- |
| Durability | Durable goods, non-durable goods, services |
| Consumer goods | Convenience (staple, impulse, emergency), shopping, speciality, unsought |
| Industrial goods | Materials and parts, capital items, supplies and services |

**Pharmaceutical classification**

| Basis | Classes and examples |
| --- | --- |
| Legal / sale status | Prescription (ethical) drugs, including scheduled drugs (Schedule H, H1, X); OTC (non-prescription) products |
| Patent status | Patented new drugs; off-patent branded generics; unbranded generics |
| Therapeutic class | Anti-infectives, cardiovascular, anti-diabetic, analgesics, respiratory, gastrointestinal, vitamins and supplements |
| Dosage form | Tablets, capsules, liquids, injections, topicals, inhalers |
| Nature of molecule | Small molecules, biologics and biosimilars, vaccines |
| Buyer | Retail (consumer through chemist) vs institutional (hospital, government) |
| Lifecycle of therapy | Acute-therapy products vs chronic-therapy products (repeat use) |

**Why classification matters:** a prescription drug is promoted to doctors through medical representatives, while an OTC product can be advertised to consumers; chronic-therapy products need focus on patient adherence and continuity; institutional products are sold by tender.

## 2.3 Product line and product mix decisions

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product item | A specific version (strength, form, pack) | Brand X 500 mg tablet, strip of 10 |
| Product line | A group of closely related products | All of the company's anti-diabetic brands |
| Product mix (portfolio) | All lines and items a company sells | Cardiac + diabetic + gastro + anti-infective lines |

**Dimensions of the product mix**

- **Width** — number of product lines.
- **Length** — total number of items in all lines.
- **Depth** — number of versions of each product (strengths, forms, pack sizes).
- **Consistency** — how closely the lines are related in use, production or distribution.

**Product line decisions**

1. **Line length** — too short: lost opportunities; too long: higher cost and confusion.
2. **Line stretching** — downward (cheaper versions), upward (premium versions), two-way.
3. **Line filling** — adding items within the present range (a new strength, a paediatric syrup, a sustained-release form).
4. **Line modernisation** — updating packs and forms (from tablet to orally disintegrating tablet).
5. **Line featuring** — promoting a few items to attract attention to the whole line.
6. **Line pruning** — dropping slow-moving, unprofitable or outdated products.

**Product mix strategies:** expand the mix (new lines, new therapy divisions), contract it (drop weak lines), alter existing products, or **line extension** (new combination, new dosage form of a successful brand).

## 2.4 Product life cycle (PLC)

The **product life cycle** describes the sales and profit of a product over its life in the market.

```text
 Sales / profit
   ^
   |                    _______ sales
   |                 _-'        '-_
   |              _-'              '-_
   |           _-'   ....profit....   '-_
   |        _-'   ..'            '..     '
   |   ___-'   ..'                  '..
   |__'_____..'__________________________'...__> Time
     Intro-   Growth     Maturity      Decline
     duction
```

| Stage | Sales | Profit | Competition | Marketing focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Introduction | Low, slow growth | Negative or low (high launch cost) | Few | Create awareness among doctors; detailing, samples, CME, launch meetings |
| Growth | Rapid rise | Rising | Increasing | Build brand preference; widen distribution; new strengths |
| Maturity | Peak, slowing | Highest, then levelling | Many (branded generics) | Defend share; reminder promotion, line extensions, new indications, pack changes |
| Decline | Falling | Falling | Fewer | Reduce cost, harvest, or withdraw; shift to low-cost segments |

**Strategies to extend the life cycle:** new indication or new patient group, new dosage form or delivery system, fixed-dose combinations where rational, new pack sizes, entry into new geographic or rural markets, and switching a suitable prescription drug to OTC status where the law allows.

**Pharma features:** patent expiry causes a sharp fall in price and share of the innovator as generics enter; in branded-generic markets many brands of the same molecule are in maturity at the same time.

## 2.5 Product portfolio analysis

**Portfolio analysis** evaluates all products (or strategic business units) to decide where to invest, hold or withdraw.

### BCG growth–share matrix

```text
                 Relative market share
                 HIGH             LOW
             +--------------+--------------+
 Market  H   |   STAR       |  QUESTION    |
 growth  I   |  invest to   |  MARK        |
 rate    G   |  grow        |  invest or   |
         H   |              |  drop        |
             +--------------+--------------+
         L   |  CASH COW    |   DOG        |
         O   |  milk; funds |  harvest or  |
         W   |  others      |  divest      |
             +--------------+--------------+
```

- **Star** — high growth, high share; needs investment, becomes a cash cow later.
- **Cash cow** — low growth, high share; earns more cash than it needs (a well-established mature brand).
- **Question mark (problem child)** — high growth, low share; needs cash, may become a star or fail.
- **Dog** — low growth, low share; candidate for pruning.

### GE–McKinsey matrix

A 3 × 3 grid of **industry attractiveness** (high, medium, low) against **business strength** (strong, average, weak). Green cells = invest/grow, yellow = selective, red = harvest/divest. It uses many factors, not just growth and share.

### Ansoff product–market grid (growth strategies)

| | Existing products | New products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing markets | Market penetration | Product development |
| New markets | Market development | Diversification |

## 2.6 Product positioning

**Positioning** is arranging for a product to occupy a clear, distinctive and desirable place in the target customer's mind relative to competitors.

**Bases of positioning**

| Basis | Example message |
| --- | --- |
| Attribute or feature | Once-daily dosing, sugar-free, fast dissolving |
| Benefit | Better compliance, faster relief |
| Use or application | For travellers' diarrhoea, for children |
| User | For the elderly, for pregnancy |
| Against a competitor | Fewer side effects than older agents |
| Quality / price | Premium quality, or affordable for long-term therapy |
| Product class | A modern alternative to an older class |

**Steps:** identify competitive advantages → choose the right ones (important, distinctive, superior, communicable, affordable, profitable) → communicate them consistently. A **perceptual map** plots brands on two attributes (for example price and efficacy) to find gaps.

**Unique selling proposition (USP)** — the single strongest reason a doctor should prescribe this brand. Claims must be supported by evidence and approved product information.

## 2.7 New product decisions

**Sources of new products in pharma:** in-house research, in-licensing, acquisitions, contract or co-marketing arrangements, new formulations and combinations of known molecules, and launches of generics after patent expiry.

**Types of new products:** new-to-the-world products, new product lines, additions to existing lines, improvements, repositioned products, lower-cost versions.

**New product development process**

```text
1 Idea generation       -> doctors, R&D, competitors, market research
2 Idea screening        -> drop ideas that do not fit or cannot be made
3 Concept development and testing
4 Marketing strategy    -> target, positioning, price, launch budget
5 Business analysis     -> sales, cost and profit estimates
6 Product development   -> formulation, stability, regulatory approval
7 Test marketing        -> limited areas or limited divisions
8 Commercialisation     -> launch: when, where, to whom, how
```

**Why new products fail:** wrong market estimate, poor positioning, no real advantage over existing brands, poor timing, high price, weak promotion, regulatory delay, quality problems.

**Adoption process by doctors:** awareness → interest → evaluation → trial → adoption. Adopter categories: innovators, early adopters (often key opinion leaders), early majority, late majority, laggards.

## 2.8 Branding decisions

A **brand** is a name, term, sign, symbol, design or a combination that identifies a seller's product and differentiates it from competitors. The **trademark** is the legally protected brand.

**Benefits of branding:** identifies the product, builds loyalty and repeat prescription, supports a premium, legal protection, helps new product introduction under a known family name.

**Qualities of a good pharmaceutical brand name**

- Short, easy to say, spell and remember.
- Distinctive; not confusing with other brands (look-alike and sound-alike names cause dispensing errors).
- Suggests a benefit or therapy area without making false claims.
- Legally registrable as a trademark.
- Acceptable in different languages and regions.

**Branding strategies**

| Strategy | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Individual brand names | Each product has its own brand |
| Family (umbrella) brand | One name across many products |
| Company name + individual name | Company name gives trust, brand gives identity |
| Brand extension | A known brand used for a new form or combination |
| Generic (unbranded) | Sold under the non-proprietary name |
| Co-marketing | Two companies sell the same molecule under different brands |

**Brand equity** is the added value a brand name gives a product (awareness, perceived quality, loyalty, associations).

## 2.9 Packaging decisions

**Packaging** is designing and making the container or wrapper of a product.

**Levels:** primary (in contact with the product: blister, bottle, ampoule), secondary (carton holding the primary pack), tertiary or shipping pack (corrugated box for transport).

**Functions of pharmaceutical packaging**

1. **Protection** — from light, moisture, oxygen, contamination and breakage, so the product stays stable until expiry.
2. **Identification and information** — through the label.
3. **Convenience** — unit-dose strips, calendar packs, measuring cups, droppers.
4. **Safety** — tamper-evident and child-resistant closures.
5. **Promotion** — distinctive colours and design help recognition at the chemist.
6. **Compliance** — pack size matched to course length.

**Packaging decisions:** material (glass, plastic, aluminium), form (strip, blister, bottle), pack size, design and colour, cost, compatibility and stability data, and environmental concerns.

## 2.10 Labelling decisions

The **label** carries information printed on or attached to the pack. In India labelling of drugs must follow the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945.

**Information usually required on a drug label:** name of the drug (proprietary and non-proprietary name), content of active ingredients per unit, net content, batch number, manufacturing licence number, date of manufacture and date of expiry, name and address of the manufacturer, and the maximum retail price. Scheduled drugs carry the prescribed warnings (for example the Rx symbol and the Schedule H or H1 caution).

**Marketing role of labels:** identifies and grades the product, describes it, promotes through design, and protects the company legally by giving proper directions and warnings.

## 2.11 Product management in the pharmaceutical industry

In most companies a **product manager** (or brand manager) is responsible for a group of brands, like a "mini general manager" for those products.

**Duties of a product manager**

- Studying the market, competitors and prescription trends for the brands.
- Preparing the annual **brand plan**: objectives, target doctors, positioning, promotional strategy, budget, sales forecast.
- Designing promotional inputs: visual aids, literature, samples, gifts within the code, digital material.
- Training medical representatives on product knowledge and selling messages.
- Coordinating with sales, production, medical, regulatory, finance and distribution.
- Planning new product launches and line extensions.
- Monitoring sales, market share and promotional effectiveness; correcting the plan.
- Handling product complaints and feedback from the field.

**Organisation:** product managers usually report to a group product manager, who reports to the marketing head. Strength: focused attention on each brand. Weakness: responsibility without full authority over the sales force.

## Remember

- Product levels: core benefit → actual product → augmented product.
- Mix dimensions: width, length, depth, consistency.
- PLC: introduction, growth, maturity, decline; extend it with new indications, forms, packs and markets.
- BCG: star, cash cow, question mark, dog.
- Ansoff: penetration, market development, product development, diversification.
- Positioning gives a clear place in the customer's mind; claims must be backed by evidence.
- New product process: idea → screening → concept → strategy → business analysis → development → test market → launch.
- A good brand name is short, distinctive and not confusing with other drug names.
- Packaging protects, informs, assists use and promotes; labels must follow the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules.
- The product manager plans and controls the brands.
