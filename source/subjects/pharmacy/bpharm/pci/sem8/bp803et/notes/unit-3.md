# Unit 3: Promotion

Promotion is the communication part of marketing: telling the right people about a product, persuading them that it meets their need, and reminding them to keep using it. In pharmacy, promotion is shaped by a special fact: for prescription medicines the target is the doctor (and pharmacist), not the patient, and the message must be scientific, balanced and within the law.

## 3.1 Meaning and objectives of promotion

**Promotion** is the coordination of all seller-initiated efforts to set up channels of information and persuasion in order to sell goods or services or promote an idea.

**Objectives of pharmaceutical promotion**

1. **Inform** — create awareness of a new product, its indications, dose forms and evidence.
2. **Persuade** — build preference for the brand over competitors.
3. **Remind** — keep the brand in the doctor's mind (reminder detailing, reminder items).
4. **Educate** — continuing medical education (CME) on diseases and rational use.
5. **Build image** — present the company as reliable and quality-focused.
6. **Support the trade** — help chemists and stockists stock and sell the product.

**AIDA model** of how promotion works: **Attention → Interest → Desire → Action** (prescription or purchase).

## 3.2 Methods (tools) of promotion — the promotional mix

| Tool | Description | Main use in pharma |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Personal selling | Face-to-face presentation by a sales representative | Detailing prescription drugs to doctors — the main tool |
| Advertising | Paid, non-personal communication by an identified sponsor | Journal advertisements to professionals; mass media only for OTC products |
| Sales promotion | Short-term incentives to encourage trial or purchase | Physician samples, trade schemes for chemists, consumer offers on OTC packs |
| Public relations | Building good relations with publics and a favourable image | Health camps, disease awareness, press releases, sponsorship of CME |
| Direct marketing | Direct communication with targeted individuals | Direct mail, e-mail, messages to doctors |
| Digital / online | Websites, social media, search, apps, e-detailing | OTC consumer reach; scientific content for professionals |

**Push versus pull strategy**

- **Push** — promotion is directed at channel members and prescribers, who "push" the product to the next level (detailing, trade schemes).
- **Pull** — promotion is directed at final consumers, who "pull" the product through the channel by asking for it (mainly OTC advertising).
- Prescription drugs rely mostly on push through doctors; OTC products use more pull.

## 3.3 Determinants of the promotional mix

The right mix depends on:

| Determinant | Effect on the mix |
| --- | --- |
| Type of product | Prescription drugs: personal selling, journals, samples; OTC: advertising, retail display |
| Type of market / target | Few, technical buyers (doctors, hospitals) → personal selling; mass consumers → advertising |
| Stage of product life cycle | Introduction: heavy detailing, samples, launch meets; growth: build preference; maturity: reminders, trade schemes; decline: minimum spend |
| Buyer readiness stage | Awareness and knowledge → advertising, PR; conviction and purchase → personal selling |
| Push or pull strategy | Push → field force and trade; pull → consumer advertising |
| Available budget | Small budgets favour focused personal selling and direct mail |
| Competition | Match or out-do competitors' share of voice |
| Legal and ethical rules | Restrict who and how a product may be promoted |

## 3.4 Promotional budget

The **promotional budget** is the money set aside for all promotional activities for a period.

**Methods of setting the budget**

1. **Affordable method** — spend what the company thinks it can afford; simple but ignores market needs.
2. **Percentage-of-sales method** — a fixed percentage of current or expected sales; easy, but treats sales as the cause of promotion rather than its result.
3. **Competitive-parity method** — match competitors' spending; avoids promotion wars but assumes competitors are right.
4. **Objective-and-task method** — define objectives, list the tasks needed (number of calls, samples, meetings, advertisements), cost them, and add up; the most logical method.

**Main items in a pharma promotional budget:** field force costs, samples, literature and visual aids, journal advertising, conferences and CME support, digital promotion, reminder items, market research.

## 3.5 Personal selling (overview)

Personal selling is the most important promotional tool for prescription drugs. The **medical representative** (professional sales representative, PSR) visits doctors, explains the product with scientific evidence (**detailing**), answers questions, gives samples and literature, and also visits chemists and stockists. Its strengths are two-way communication, flexibility and relationship building; its weakness is high cost per contact. Details of the PSR are in Unit 4.

**Steps in personal selling:** prospecting and pre-call planning → approach → presentation (detailing) → handling objections → closing (asking for prescriptions) → follow-up.

## 3.6 Advertising

**Advertising** is any paid form of non-personal presentation of ideas, goods or services by an identified sponsor.

**Types**

- **Product advertising** — informative (new product), persuasive (build preference), reminder (mature product).
- **Institutional (corporate) advertising** — builds the company's image.

**Media:** medical journals, newspapers and magazines, television and radio (OTC only), outdoor, internet and social media, point-of-sale material at chemists.

**Legal limits in India (overview)**

- The **Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954** prohibits advertisements claiming to cure the diseases and conditions listed in its schedule, and misleading claims about drugs.
- Under the **Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945**, drugs may not be advertised to the public as preventing or curing the diseases and ailments listed in Schedule J.
- Prescription medicines are promoted to registered medical practitioners and pharmacists, not advertised to the general public.
- The **Uniform Code for Pharmaceutical Marketing Practices (UCPMP)** issued by the Department of Pharmaceuticals (first in 2014, revised in 2024) requires claims to be accurate, balanced, up to date and supported by evidence, and restricts gifts, travel and hospitality to healthcare professionals.

**Developing an advertising programme (5 Ms):** Mission (objectives), Money (budget), Message (what to say), Media (where), Measurement (evaluation of recall and sales effect).

## 3.7 Direct mail

**Direct mail** is promotional material posted (or sent electronically) directly to selected doctors, pharmacists or consumers.

- **Content:** product leaflets, reprints of published studies, newsletters, reminder cards, invitations to CME.
- **Advantages:** reaches doctors the field force cannot visit often (remote areas, busy specialists), selective, personal, low cost per contact, easy to test.
- **Limitations:** often discarded unread, needs a correct and updated mailing list, no two-way discussion.
- Today much direct mail is replaced by e-mail newsletters and messaging, which must respect consent and privacy.

## 3.8 Journals (journal advertising)

Medical and pharmacy journals carry advertisements and sponsored inserts aimed at professionals.

- **Advantages:** reaches a targeted professional audience; credibility of the journal; the advertisement stays in the issue for repeated viewing; supports detailing.
- **Limitations:** cluttered with many advertisements; cannot be personalised; effect is hard to measure.
- **Good practice:** include the generic name, approved indications, and a reference to full prescribing information; claims must be supported by evidence.

## 3.9 Sampling

**Physician samples** are small quantities of a product given free to doctors so that they can become familiar with it and try it in their patients.

**Purposes:** encourage trial of a new product, remind about an existing brand, help start therapy, support patients who cannot afford a full course (at the doctor's discretion).

**Good sampling practice**

- Give samples only to doctors qualified to prescribe the product.
- Mark samples "Physician's sample — not to be sold".
- Give a reasonable quantity; keep records of samples given.
- Store samples properly; follow the marketing code's limits.
- Never sell samples or give them as an inducement.

**Limitations:** costly, can be misused or sold, and may encourage unneeded prescribing if overused.

## 3.10 Retailing (retail promotion)

The retail chemist is the final link to the patient. **Retail promotion** aims to make sure the product is stocked, visible and recommended.

**Methods:** window and counter displays, shelf space, posters, dispensers, point-of-purchase material for OTC products, trade schemes (bonus quantities, discounts within regulated margins), chemist meetings, product information for pharmacists, and prompt supply and credit.

**Role of the pharmacist:** gives advice on OTC products, ensures correct dispensing, may suggest a brand where substitution is permitted, and is a source of market feedback.

## 3.11 Medical exhibitions

Companies set up **stalls at medical conferences, CMEs and exhibitions** to meet many doctors in one place.

**Advantages:** contact with many specialists at low cost per contact; launch of new products; demonstration of devices; collection of doctors' feedback and names; builds company image.

**Planning an exhibition stall:** choose the right conference (speciality matches the products), design an attractive and informative stall, send trained representatives and medical advisers, prepare literature and visual material, keep a register of visitors, and follow up after the event.

## 3.12 Public relations

**Public relations (PR)** builds goodwill and a favourable image with the company's various publics: doctors, patients, government, media, employees, investors and the local community.

**Tools:** press releases and media relations, health camps and screening camps, disease-awareness campaigns, sponsorship of scientific events within the code, corporate social responsibility (CSR) activities, publications and newsletters, crisis management (product recall communication).

**Value:** high credibility (news is believed more than advertisements), low cost, protects reputation in a crisis, supports all other promotion.

## 3.13 Online promotional techniques for OTC products

OTC (non-prescription) products can be promoted to consumers, and online methods are now central.

| Technique | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Company and brand websites | Product information, usage directions, FAQs |
| Search engine optimisation and search advertising | Appear when consumers search a symptom or product |
| Social media marketing | Brand pages, short videos, health tips, responding to queries |
| Influencer marketing | Endorsement by health or lifestyle creators (with disclosure) |
| E-mail and messaging marketing | Newsletters and offers to subscribed users |
| Display and video advertising | Banners and video ads on websites and apps |
| E-commerce and online pharmacy listings | Product pages, ratings, offers on online platforms |
| Mobile apps | Reminders, health trackers linked to brands |
| Content marketing | Articles and videos on health topics that build trust |

**Precautions:** claims must be truthful and permitted for that product; no promotion of prescription drugs to consumers; no claims for Schedule J diseases or those under the Objectionable Advertisements Act; respect privacy and data-protection rules; monitor online comments for adverse event reports.

**Advantages:** wide reach, precise targeting, low cost, measurable results (clicks, views, conversions), two-way interaction.

## 3.14 Ethics in pharmaceutical promotion

- Information must be accurate, balanced, fair, objective and supported by evidence.
- Do not hide side effects or contraindications.
- No misleading comparisons with competitors.
- No inducements (cash, costly gifts, travel) to doctors to prescribe.
- Samples only for evaluation, never for sale.
- The WHO's *Ethical Criteria for Medicinal Drug Promotion* (1988) also guide these standards.

## Remember

- Promotion informs, persuades and reminds; AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action.
- Promotional mix: personal selling, advertising, sales promotion, PR, direct marketing, digital.
- Prescription drugs → push through doctors; OTC → pull through consumers.
- Budget methods: affordable, percentage of sales, competitive parity, objective-and-task (best).
- Samples are marked "not to be sold" and given only to prescribers.
- Exhibitions reach many doctors at once; PR builds credibility.
- OTC online tools: websites, search, social media, e-mail, apps, e-commerce listings.
- Law and code: Drugs and Magic Remedies Act 1954, Schedule J of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, UCPMP.
