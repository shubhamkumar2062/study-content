# Unit 1: Marketing and the pharmaceutical market

A medicine can be well made and still fail if nobody knows about it, the wrong people are told about it, or it does not reach the shop. Marketing is the set of activities that connects a company's products with the people who need them. In the pharmaceutical industry this is unusual, because the person who decides (the doctor), the person who pays (the patient, an insurer or the government) and the person who consumes (the patient) are often different. This unit covers the basic ideas of marketing and then looks at the pharmaceutical market and its customers.

## 1.1 Definition and general concepts of marketing

**Marketing** is the process of finding out what customers need and want, developing products that satisfy those needs, and delivering them at a price and place that suits the customer, while earning a profit for the organisation.

- The American Marketing Association describes marketing as the activity and processes for creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society.
- Philip Kotler describes it as a social and managerial process by which individuals and groups obtain what they need and want through creating and exchanging products and value with others.

**Core concepts**

| Concept | Meaning | Pharmacy example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Need | A basic state of felt deprivation | Relief from pain or fever |
| Want | The form a need takes, shaped by culture and person | A particular paracetamol brand |
| Demand | A want backed by ability and willingness to pay | Patients who can buy the prescribed brand |
| Product / offering | Anything offered to satisfy a need | Tablet, syrup, diagnostic kit, service |
| Value | Benefits received compared with cost | Effective, well-tolerated, affordable drug |
| Exchange | Getting something by offering something in return | Medicine for money at the chemist |
| Market | All actual and potential buyers of a product | All diabetic patients and their doctors |

**The marketing mix (4 Ps)** — the controllable tools a company uses:

| P | Decisions | Unit in this subject |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product | Range, quality, brand, pack, label | Unit 2 |
| Price | List price, margins, discounts, regulation | Unit 5 |
| Place | Channels, stockists, physical distribution | Unit 4 |
| Promotion | Detailing, sampling, journals, exhibitions, PR | Unit 3 |

For services, three more Ps are often added: **people, process and physical evidence**.

**Marketing management philosophies (orientations)**

1. **Production concept** — customers prefer products that are widely available and cheap; focus on efficient production.
2. **Product concept** — customers prefer the best quality; focus on improving the product, risk of "marketing myopia" (loving the product, forgetting the need).
3. **Selling concept** — customers will not buy enough unless pushed; focus on aggressive selling.
4. **Marketing concept** — find the needs of target customers and satisfy them better than competitors.
5. **Societal marketing concept** — satisfy customers while also protecting the long-term welfare of society. This is the most suitable for medicines, since public health, rational drug use and ethics matter.

## 1.2 Scope of marketing

Marketing covers much more than selling:

- Market research and forecasting of demand
- Product planning and development of new products
- Branding, packaging and labelling
- Pricing decisions within legal limits
- Selecting channels and managing distribution
- Promotion: personal selling, advertising, sampling, public relations
- Sales management: recruiting, training and controlling the field force
- After-sales service and customer relations (product complaints, pharmacovigilance reports passed to the right department)
- Marketing control: comparing results with targets

In a pharmaceutical company the marketing department usually has **product management** (product managers and brand managers), **sales** (field force), **market research**, **medical services** and **distribution/logistics** working together.

## 1.3 Marketing versus selling

| Point | Selling | Marketing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starting point | Factory, existing product | Market, customer needs |
| Focus | Seller's need to convert product to cash | Buyer's needs and satisfaction |
| Time view | Short term, volume today | Long term, relationship and loyalty |
| Means | Selling and promotion push | Integrated marketing mix |
| End | Profit through sales volume | Profit through customer satisfaction |
| Scope | Narrow — one part of marketing | Wide — includes selling |

**Remember the one-liner:** selling tries to get customers to want what the company has; marketing tries to make what customers want.

## 1.4 Marketing environment

The marketing environment is all the forces outside marketing management that affect its ability to build and keep good relations with target customers.

```text
            MACRO-ENVIRONMENT (cannot control)
   Demographic  Economic  Political-legal  Technological
   Social-cultural  Natural/ecological
        +--------------------------------------+
        |       MICRO-ENVIRONMENT               |
        |  Suppliers   Intermediaries           |
        |  Competitors  Customers  Publics      |
        |     +--------------------------+      |
        |     |  COMPANY (internal):     |      |
        |     |  marketing mix, R&D,     |      |
        |     |  production, finance     |      |
        |     +--------------------------+      |
        +--------------------------------------+
```

**Micro-environment (close to the company)**

- **Company itself** — top management, R&D, production, quality, finance.
- **Suppliers** — of active ingredients (APIs), excipients, packing material.
- **Marketing intermediaries** — C&F (carrying and forwarding) agents, stockists (wholesalers), retail chemists, hospital pharmacies, transporters, advertising agencies.
- **Customers** — doctors, patients, hospitals, institutions, government tenders.
- **Competitors** — companies with similar molecules or therapeutic alternatives.
- **Publics** — media, government, financial bodies, local community, citizen groups.

**Macro-environment (wider society)**

| Force | Pharmaceutical examples |
| --- | --- |
| Demographic | Growing elderly population, rising lifestyle diseases, urbanisation |
| Economic | Income levels, out-of-pocket spending, health insurance coverage |
| Political–legal | Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940 and Rules, price control (DPCO), patent law, advertising law, ethics codes |
| Technological | New delivery systems, biologics, e-pharmacy, digital detailing |
| Social–cultural | Health awareness, self-medication, preference for traditional systems |
| Natural | Environmental rules on manufacturing waste, climate-related disease patterns |

**SWOT analysis** links the environment to the company: internal **strengths and weaknesses**, external **opportunities and threats**.

## 1.5 Industry and competitive analysis

**Porter's five forces** explain how attractive (profitable) an industry is:

```text
                 Threat of new entrants
                          |
  Bargaining power  -> RIVALRY AMONG  <- Bargaining power
  of suppliers         EXISTING FIRMS      of buyers
                          |
                 Threat of substitutes
```

| Force | In pharma |
| --- | --- |
| Rivalry | Many companies sell branded generics of the same molecule, so rivalry is intense |
| New entrants | Barriers: GMP investment, licences, field force, brand loyalty of doctors; lower for third-party (contract) manufacturing |
| Substitutes | Other molecules in the same class, traditional medicines, non-drug therapy |
| Buyer power | Hospitals, government tenders and large chains bargain strongly; individual patients have little power |
| Supplier power | High when an API comes from few sources (import dependence) |

**Competitor analysis** asks: who are the competitors, what are their strategies, objectives, strengths and weaknesses, and how will they react? Competitors can be leaders, challengers, followers or nichers.

## 1.6 Consumer buying behaviour

**Consumer buying behaviour** is how individuals and households buy goods for personal use. For medicines this mainly applies to **OTC products** and to the patient's choice of doctor and chemist.

**Factors that influence buying behaviour**

| Group | Factors |
| --- | --- |
| Cultural | Culture, subculture, social class |
| Social | Family, reference groups, roles and status |
| Personal | Age, life-cycle stage, occupation, income, lifestyle, personality |
| Psychological | Motivation, perception, learning, beliefs and attitudes |

**Buyer decision process (five stages)**

```text
Need recognition -> Information search -> Evaluation of alternatives
        -> Purchase decision -> Post-purchase behaviour
```

Example: a person with acidity (need) asks the chemist and family (search), compares an antacid gel and tablets (evaluation), buys one (decision), and buys it again if it worked (post-purchase satisfaction or dissonance).

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs** (physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualisation) explains motivation; health products serve safety needs first.

## 1.7 Industrial (organisational) buying behaviour

Organisations such as hospitals, government medical stores and companies buying raw materials buy differently from individuals.

| Feature | Industrial buying |
| --- | --- |
| Buyers | Fewer but larger |
| Decision | Group decision (buying centre), formal procedure |
| Demand | Derived from the demand for final products or services |
| Process | Specifications, tenders, quotations, negotiation, contracts |
| Relationship | Long-term, close buyer–seller relations |

**Buying centre roles:** users (doctors, nurses), influencers (pharmacy and therapeutics committee), buyers (purchase officer), deciders (medical superintendent or committee), gatekeepers (who control information).

**Buying situations:** straight rebuy, modified rebuy, new task.

**Stages:** problem recognition → general need description → product specification → supplier search → proposal (tender) → supplier selection → order routine → performance review.

## 1.8 The pharmaceutical market

### Quantitative and qualitative aspects

- **Quantitative aspects** describe how much: value and volume of sales, number of companies, number of brands, growth rate, share of each therapy area, number of doctors and chemists. These are measured by market research audits of retail sales and prescriptions. Exact figures change every year, so always quote them from a current, named source.
- **Qualitative aspects** describe what the market is like: who prescribes, why they choose a brand, attitudes of patients, influence of chemists, quality image of companies, ethical standards.

### Size and composition

The Indian market is mainly a **branded generics** market: many companies sell the same molecule under their own brand names. It can be divided by:

| Basis | Divisions |
| --- | --- |
| Prescription status | Prescription (ethical) drugs vs OTC products |
| Patent status | Patented, off-patent branded generics, unbranded (trade) generics |
| Therapy area | Anti-infectives, cardiac, gastrointestinal, anti-diabetic, pain, respiratory, vitamins and others |
| Channel | Retail (chemist) market vs institutional (hospital, government) market |
| Company origin | Indian companies vs multinational companies |
| Geography | Domestic vs export; urban vs rural |

India is also a major exporter of generic medicines, and the domestic market is highly fragmented with intense competition.

### Demographic and socio-psychological description of the consumer

- **Demographic description:** age, sex, income, education, occupation, location (urban/rural), family size, disease pattern. Example: a cardiac product targets older urban adults.
- **Socio-psychological characteristics:** attitudes to health, belief in a doctor's advice, fear of side effects, trust in brands, tendency to self-medicate, cultural beliefs about medicines, lifestyle.

## 1.9 Market segmentation and targeting

**Market segmentation** is dividing a market into distinct groups of buyers with similar needs or characteristics who may need separate products or marketing mixes.

**Bases of segmentation**

| Basis | Variables | Pharma example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Geographic | Region, state, urban/rural, climate | Rural-focused division with low-cost packs |
| Demographic | Age, sex, income | Paediatric syrups, products for women |
| Psychographic | Lifestyle, personality, values | Health-conscious buyers of supplements |
| Behavioural | Usage rate, loyalty, benefits sought | Chronic vs acute users |
| Prescriber-based | Speciality, type of practice, prescribing volume | Cardiologists, general practitioners, gynaecologists |

**Requirements of good segments:** measurable, substantial (large enough), accessible, differentiable, actionable.

**Targeting** is evaluating each segment's attractiveness and selecting one or more to enter. Strategies:

1. **Undifferentiated** — one offer for the whole market.
2. **Differentiated** — different offers for several segments (separate divisions for cardiology, gynaecology, etc.).
3. **Concentrated (niche)** — one segment served deeply (only oncology or only dermatology).
4. **Micromarketing** — local or individual targeting.

**Positioning** then fixes the product's place in the customer's mind against competitors (see Unit 2). The sequence is **segmentation → targeting → positioning (STP)**.

## 1.10 Consumer profile

A **consumer profile** is a description of the typical user of a product: age, sex, disease, severity, income, purchasing power, where they live, who influences them, and how they buy (prescription or self-selection). For ethical drugs the profile has two parts — the **prescriber profile** (speciality, patient load, prescribing habits) and the **patient profile**.

## 1.11 Motivation and prescribing habits of the physician

A doctor's choice of brand depends on:

| Factor | How it works |
| --- | --- |
| Efficacy and safety | Clinical evidence, own experience with patients |
| Company image | Trust in quality and consistency of the manufacturer |
| Information sources | Medical representatives, journals, conferences (CME), colleagues, key opinion leaders |
| Patient factors | Affordability, dosing convenience, taste for children |
| Availability | Brand stocked at nearby chemists |
| Habit and loyalty | Familiar brands are repeated |
| Service | Regular, informed visits by representatives; scientific literature |

Doctors are motivated by better patient outcomes, professional reputation, up-to-date knowledge and convenience. Ethical promotion appeals to these, not to personal gifts (gifts and hospitality to doctors are restricted by the doctors' ethics regulations and the pharmaceutical marketing code).

## 1.12 Patient's choice of physician and retail pharmacist

**Choice of physician:** reputation and recommendation by family or friends, qualification and speciality, distance and availability, fees, behaviour and time given to the patient, hospital attachment.

**Choice of retail pharmacist (chemist):** nearness to home or clinic, availability of all prescribed items, price and discounts, trust and advice given, home delivery, opening hours, credit facility for regular customers. The pharmacist strongly influences OTC purchases and, where substitution happens, generic choice.

## 1.13 Analysing the market and the role of market research

**Market analysis** studies market size, growth, segments, competitors, channels and customer behaviour before decisions are made.

**Market research** is the systematic design, collection, analysis and reporting of data relevant to a specific marketing situation.

**Process**

```text
1 Define the problem and objectives
2 Develop the research plan (sources, methods, sample)
3 Collect data (secondary first, then primary)
4 Analyse and interpret
5 Report findings and recommend action
```

**Data sources**

- **Secondary data** — company sales records, published audits of retail sales and prescriptions, journals, government reports.
- **Primary data** — surveys of doctors and chemists, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing.

**Role in the pharmaceutical industry**

1. Estimating market size and demand for a new product.
2. Studying prescribing behaviour and brand share.
3. Testing a new brand name, pack, or promotional message.
4. Evaluating the effect of a promotional campaign and field force.
5. Studying competitors' products and prices.
6. Finding unmet needs for new product ideas.
7. Sales forecasting and territory planning.

## Remember

- Marketing starts with customer needs; selling starts with the product.
- The 4 Ps: product, price, place, promotion.
- Societal marketing concept suits medicines best.
- Micro-environment: company, suppliers, intermediaries, customers, competitors, publics; macro: demographic, economic, political–legal, technological, social–cultural, natural.
- Porter's five forces: rivalry, entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power.
- Buyer decision: need → search → evaluation → purchase → post-purchase.
- Industrial buying uses a buying centre and tenders.
- STP: segmentation → targeting → positioning.
- The doctor is the key decision-maker for prescription drugs; the chemist influences OTC and generic choice.
- Never quote a market-size figure without its source and year.
