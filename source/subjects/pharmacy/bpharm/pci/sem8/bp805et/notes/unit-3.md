# Unit 3: Vaccine safety, pharmacovigilance methods and communication

Vaccines are given to healthy people, often children, in huge numbers, so even a rare problem — or a rumour about one — can damage a whole immunisation programme. This unit first covers vaccine pharmacovigilance. It then describes the methods used to collect safety data (passive, stimulated and active surveillance, observational studies and targeted investigations), how signals are found in that data, and finally how safety information is communicated, including during a crisis.

## 3.1 Vaccine pharmacovigilance

**Definition.** Vaccine pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and communication of **adverse events following immunisation (AEFI)** and other vaccine- or immunisation-related problems, and their prevention.

**Why vaccines need special attention**

- Given to **healthy** people, often infants and pregnant women, so tolerance of risk is low.
- Given to **very large populations** in campaigns; rare events appear in absolute numbers.
- Many events occur by **coincidence** around the age of vaccination (for example, febrile seizures, sudden infant death).
- Biological products: lot-to-lot variation, cold-chain problems, contamination.
- Public confidence is fragile; a safety scare can cause outbreaks of vaccine-preventable disease.

## 3.2 Adverse events following immunisation (AEFI)

**AEFI** — any untoward medical occurrence which follows immunisation and which does not necessarily have a causal relationship with the use of the vaccine. It may be an unfavourable sign, abnormal laboratory finding, symptom or disease.

**Cause-specific classification (WHO/CIOMS)**

| Type | Cause | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vaccine product-related reaction | Inherent property of the vaccine | Fever, local swelling; vaccine-associated paralytic polio after oral polio vaccine; anaphylaxis |
| Vaccine quality defect-related reaction | Manufacturing defect (including the administration device) | Incompletely inactivated vaccine causing infection |
| Immunisation error-related reaction | Inappropriate handling, prescribing or administration — preventable | Abscess from non-sterile injection; toxic shock from a contaminated reconstituted vial; wrong diluent; injection given in the wrong site |
| Immunisation anxiety-related reaction | Fear or pain of injection, not the vaccine | Fainting (vasovagal syncope), hyperventilation, mass psychogenic reactions in schools |
| Coincidental event | Caused by something other than the vaccine or process | An infant with a pre-existing infection who develops fever |

**Seriousness and severity** follow the same logic as for drugs: serious = death, life-threatening, hospitalisation, disability, congenital anomaly; severe = intensity.

**Common minor reactions:** pain, redness and swelling at the site, fever, irritability, malaise. **Rare serious reactions:** anaphylaxis, seizures, thrombocytopenia, Guillain–Barré syndrome (reported with some vaccines), intussusception (with some rotavirus vaccines).

**Case definitions.** The **Brighton Collaboration** publishes standard case definitions for AEFIs (for example, anaphylaxis, fever, GBS) with levels of diagnostic certainty, so that reports from different places are comparable.

**AEFI surveillance in India**

- Run under the Universal Immunisation Programme by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, with **district, state and national AEFI committees**.
- Serious AEFIs (deaths, hospitalisations, clusters) are reported promptly and **investigated** by the district team: case details, vaccine and lot, storage, handling, other vaccinees from the same vial.
- Causality is assessed with the **WHO AEFI causality algorithm**, which places a case as **consistent with causal association**, **indeterminate**, **inconsistent** (coincidental) or **unclassifiable**.
- Minor AEFIs are recorded and reported in routine reports; PvPI also accepts vaccine ADR reports.

**Management at the site:** observe vaccinees for about 30 minutes after vaccination; keep an anaphylaxis kit (adrenaline) ready; train staff in basic life support.

## 3.3 Vaccination failure

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Vaccine failure** | Occurrence of the specific vaccine-preventable disease in a person who was appropriately and fully vaccinated |
| **Primary failure** | Person never develops protective immunity after vaccination (no seroconversion) |
| **Secondary failure** | Immunity develops at first but wanes over time |
| **Failure due to vaccination (programme) problems** | Vaccine not given correctly: cold-chain break, expired vaccine, wrong dose schedule, wrong route |
| **Failure due to vaccinee** | Immunodeficiency, immunosuppressive drugs, interfering maternal antibodies, concurrent infection |
| **Failure due to vaccine** | Strain not covered by the vaccine, inherent lower efficacy, poor-quality lot |

Confirmed failure needs laboratory proof of disease (and ideally of the pathogen strain); suspected failure is based on clinical diagnosis. Failure reports are part of vaccine pharmacovigilance because they may reveal quality or cold-chain problems.

## 3.5 Passive surveillance

**Spontaneous reporting** — health professionals (and patients) voluntarily report suspected ADRs to a national centre or the MAH on a standard form. Examples: PvPI form, UK Yellow Card, US MedWatch.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Cheap and simple; covers all drugs and all patients | **Under-reporting** (often most ADRs are never reported) |
| Works for the whole life of a drug | Reporting bias: new drugs, serious and media-publicised reactions over-reported |
| Best method to find **rare, new, unexpected** reactions — the main source of signals | No denominator — cannot calculate incidence |
| Generates hypotheses quickly | Poor quality or incomplete reports; duplicates |
| Includes special groups excluded from trials | Cannot easily detect delayed or common-background events |

**Case series** — a collection of reports of the same reaction with the same drug. A well-documented case series (for example, the thalidomide reports) can establish a signal, especially for rare events with almost no background rate. It cannot give rates or prove causation alone.

## 3.6 Stimulated reporting

Methods that **encourage and make easier** the reporting of particular events or drugs for a period:

- reporting campaigns, posters, CMEs and online reporting for new drugs;
- **black triangle**-type schemes that ask for all reactions to new medicines to be reported;
- **early post-marketing phase vigilance** (used in Japan) — intensive reporting during the first six months after launch;
- focused programmes in public health campaigns (for example, new antimalarials or antiretrovirals).

Stimulated reports are still spontaneous reports and must be interpreted carefully because the stimulation itself changes reporting rates.

## 3.7 Active surveillance

Active surveillance tries to find **all** events in a defined population through an organised process, giving more complete data and often a denominator.

**a) Sentinel sites** — selected hospitals or clinics where staff actively review records, interview patients and capture all events in patients on a drug. Good for high-risk groups and drugs used in hospital.

**b) Drug event monitoring (prescription event monitoring, PEM)**

1. Patients are identified from prescriptions (in the UK, through the Drug Safety Research Unit).
2. Questionnaires are sent to the prescribing doctors after a set time asking about **any event**, not just suspected ADRs.
3. Event rates are calculated for the cohort (often around 10,000 patients) and compared over time or with other drugs.

The **cohort event monitoring** used by WHO in public health programmes (for example, antimalarials and antiretrovirals) works on the same principle.

**c) Registries** — lists of patients with the same exposure (**drug registry**, for example a pregnancy exposure registry) or the same disease/outcome (**disease registry**, for example a registry of aplastic anaemia or birth defects). Used to compare outcomes in exposed and unexposed people.

**d) Automated database / record linkage** — electronic health records and insurance claims databases searched for exposure and outcome.

## 3.8 Comparative observational studies (pharmacoepidemiology)

**Pharmacoepidemiology** is the study of the use and effects of drugs in large numbers of people. It applies epidemiological methods to clinical pharmacology.

**a) Cross-sectional study** — data on exposure and outcome collected at one point in time (a survey or snapshot).

- Measures **prevalence** of drug use or ADRs; quick and cheap.
- Cannot establish whether exposure came before the outcome, so it is weak for causation.
- Example: a survey of prevalence of ADRs among patients admitted to a hospital on one day.

**b) Case-control study** — start from the **outcome**.

```text
           Exposed to drug?    Yes   No
Cases (with ADR/disease)         a     b
Controls (without)               c     d

Odds ratio (OR) = (a × d) / (b × c)
```

- Compares past drug exposure in **cases** with that in **controls**.
- Efficient for **rare outcomes** and long latency; fewer patients, cheaper.
- Weaknesses: recall bias, selection bias, cannot give incidence directly.
- Classic examples: diethylstilbestrol in pregnancy and vaginal clear-cell adenocarcinoma in daughters; aspirin and Reye's syndrome in children.

**c) Cohort study** — start from **exposure** and follow people forward.

```text
                     Developed ADR?    Yes    No
Exposed to drug                          a      b
Not exposed                              c      d

Relative risk (RR) = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)]
Attributable (excess) risk = a/(a+b) − c/(c+d)
```

- Prospective (follow from now) or retrospective (historical records).
- Gives **incidence**, relative risk, and can study several outcomes of one exposure.
- Weaknesses: large and long for rare events, costly, loss to follow-up, confounding.
- Example: oral contraceptives and venous thromboembolism.

**Comparison**

| Feature | Cross-sectional | Case-control | Cohort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Starting point | Population at one time | Outcome | Exposure |
| Direction | Snapshot | Backward | Forward (or historical) |
| Measure | Prevalence | Odds ratio | Incidence, relative risk |
| Best for | Burden of drug use or ADRs | Rare outcomes | Rare exposures, several outcomes |
| Main bias | Temporality unclear | Recall and selection | Loss to follow-up |

**Confounding** — a third factor linked to both exposure and outcome (for example, "confounding by indication": sicker patients get a particular drug). Controlled by matching, restriction, stratification and statistical adjustment.

## 3.9 Targeted clinical investigations

When a signal is found, specific studies may be designed to test it:

- **pharmacokinetic studies** in groups at risk (elderly, renal or hepatic impairment);
- **drug interaction studies**;
- **pharmacogenetic studies** to find genetic risk factors;
- laboratory or mechanistic studies;
- **large simple trials** — randomised studies with minimal data collection in many patients, to compare risks of rare outcomes between treatments.

## 3.10 Signal detection

**Signal (WHO)** — reported information on a possible causal relationship between an adverse event and a drug, the relationship being unknown or incompletely documented previously. Usually more than one report is needed.

**Qualitative detection** — clinical review of individual reports and case series: unusual or serious events, positive rechallenge, designated medical events (for example, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, agranulocytosis, liver failure).

**Quantitative detection (disproportionality analysis)** — compares how often an event is reported with a drug against how often it is reported with all other drugs in the database.

```text
                     Event of interest    All other events
Drug of interest            a                  b
All other drugs             c                  d

Proportional reporting ratio  PRR = [a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)]
Reporting odds ratio          ROR = (a × d) / (b × c)
```

- A common screening rule: **PRR ≥ 2, chi-square ≥ 4 and at least 3 cases**.
- **Information component (IC)** — Bayesian measure used by WHO–UMC (Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network); a signal is flagged when the lower limit of its credibility interval is above zero.
- **Empirical Bayes geometric mean (EBGM)** — used by the US FDA (multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker).

Disproportionality shows a **statistical association in reporting**, not proof of risk; every statistical signal needs clinical review.

**Signal management process**

1. Signal detection
2. Signal validation (is there enough evidence to look further?)
3. Analysis and prioritisation (seriousness, public health impact)
4. Signal assessment (all data: trials, literature, mechanism, studies)
5. Recommendation for action (label change, restriction, study, withdrawal)
6. Exchange of information with regulators and partners

## 3.11 Communication in pharmacovigilance

**Effective communication** means giving the right safety information to the right people at the right time in a form they can understand and act on.

**Principles** (reflected in the WHO-supported **Erice Declaration, 1997** on communicating drug safety information):

- Information should be honest, evidence-based, transparent and timely.
- Uncertainty should be explained, not hidden.
- Benefits must be presented alongside risks.
- Patients and the public have a right to safety information.
- Education in understanding and using safety information is needed for professionals and the public.

**Tools:** label changes (contraindications, warnings, boxed warnings), Dear Healthcare Professional letters (DHPC), drug safety alerts and newsletters (for example, PvPI alerts), patient information leaflets, medication guides, websites and press releases, educational material under risk-minimisation plans.

## 3.12 Communication in a drug safety crisis

A **drug safety crisis** is a sudden situation (serious new ADR, contamination, media report, deaths) that threatens patients and public confidence and needs urgent decisions.

**Crisis management steps**

1. **Preparation** — a crisis management plan, a named team (safety, medical, regulatory, legal, quality, communications), a single trained spokesperson, contact lists.
2. **Rapid assessment** — collect and verify facts, assess the scale and risk.
3. **Decision** — protective action (batch recall, suspension, restriction, label change) in agreement with the regulator.
4. **Communication** — fast, consistent, honest messages to each audience.
5. **Follow-up** — monitor the situation, correct misinformation, review lessons learned.

**Communicating with different audiences**

| Audience | How and what |
| --- | --- |
| Regulatory agencies | Immediate notification, expedited reports, joint decisions on action; honest disclosure of all data |
| Business partners | Safety data exchange agreements; prompt sharing of cases and decisions with licensors, distributors, CROs |
| Healthcare facilities and professionals | DHPC letters, alerts, revised guidance, advice on managing patients already exposed |
| Media and public | Press releases and briefings through one spokesperson; plain language; explain risk in context with benefits; avoid speculation |
| Patients | What to do: do not stop essential drugs without advice; where to report problems |

## Remember

- AEFI does not need a causal link; 5 cause types: vaccine product, quality defect, immunisation error, immunisation anxiety, coincidental.
- WHO AEFI causality: consistent, indeterminate, inconsistent, unclassifiable. Brighton Collaboration gives case definitions.
- Vaccine failure: primary (never immune) vs secondary (waning).
- Passive = spontaneous reports (cheap, best for rare new ADRs, under-reporting, no denominator).
- Active = sentinel sites, PEM/cohort event monitoring, registries.
- Case-control → odds ratio (rare outcomes); cohort → relative risk and incidence; cross-sectional → prevalence.
- PRR = [a/(a+b)]/[c/(c+d)]; ROR = ad/bc; WHO–UMC uses IC; FDA uses EBGM.
- Crisis: one spokesperson, fast, honest, audience-specific messages; Erice Declaration 1997.
