# Unit 1: Introduction to pharmacovigilance and adverse drug reactions

No medicine is completely safe. Before approval a new drug is usually tested in a few thousand people for a limited time, so rare, delayed and population-specific harms are often found only after it is sold to millions. Pharmacovigilance is the science and the system that keeps watching medicines throughout their life. This unit covers what pharmacovigilance is, how it developed, the WHO and Indian programmes, and the basic language of adverse drug reactions: definitions, classification, detection, causality, severity, seriousness, preventability and management.

## 1.1 Definition and aims

**Pharmacovigilance (PV)** — WHO defines it as the science and activities relating to the **detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem**.

The word comes from *pharmakon* (Greek, drug) and *vigilare* (Latin, to keep watch).

**Scope.** Besides classical adverse reactions, PV today also covers:

- medication errors and misuse, abuse and overdose
- lack of efficacy and therapeutic failure (for example, from substandard or falsified products)
- drug interactions with other drugs, food and herbal products
- herbal, traditional and complementary medicines, blood products, biologicals, vaccines and medical devices
- off-label use and use in special groups (children, elderly, pregnancy)

**Aims of pharmacovigilance**

1. Improve patient care and safety in relation to the use of medicines.
2. Improve public health and safety.
3. Detect problems early: new ADRs, rare ADRs, changes in frequency, risk factors.
4. Assess the benefit–risk balance of medicines throughout their life.
5. Encourage safe, rational and cost-effective use of medicines.
6. Promote education and clinical training, and communicate safety information to health professionals and the public.

## 1.2 Why safety monitoring after approval is needed

Pre-marketing clinical trials have built-in limits, often summarised as the **"five toos"**:

| Limitation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Too few | A few thousand patients cannot reliably show reactions occurring in 1 in 10,000 or rarer |
| Too short | Delayed effects (cancer, organ damage, effects on offspring) need years |
| Too simple | Patients with many diseases or many drugs are usually excluded |
| Too median-aged | Children, the elderly and pregnant women are under-represented |
| Too narrow | Strict indications; real-world off-label use is not studied |

**Rule of three.** To be about 95% sure of seeing at least one case of a reaction that occurs in 1 in *n* patients, about **3n** patients must be exposed. A reaction of frequency 1 in 10,000 therefore needs around 30,000 exposed patients — more than most development programmes include.

So the final picture of a drug's safety is built only by **post-marketing surveillance**.

## 1.3 History and development of pharmacovigilance

| Period | Event | Effect on drug safety |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1848 | Death of Hannah Greener, a girl given chloroform anaesthesia (UK) | Concern about anaesthetic deaths led to early efforts to collect case reports |
| 1937 | Sulfanilamide elixir made with diethylene glycol killed over 100 people in the USA | Led to the US Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act, 1938, requiring proof of safety before marketing |
| 1957–1961 | **Thalidomide**, a sedative used in pregnancy, caused phocomelia (limb defects) in thousands of babies | Withdrawn 1961 after reports by McBride (Australia) and Lenz (Germany); the turning point for modern pharmacovigilance |
| 1962 | Kefauver–Harris amendments (USA) | Proof of efficacy as well as safety; stricter control of trials |
| 1963–1964 | UK Committee on Safety of Drugs; **Yellow Card** scheme for spontaneous reporting | Model for national reporting systems |
| 1963 | World Health Assembly resolution calling for rapid exchange of information on ADRs | Basis of the WHO programme |
| 1968 | **WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring** started as a pilot with 10 countries | International pooling of reports |
| 1970s | Practolol caused the oculomucocutaneous syndrome, missed by spontaneous reports | Stimulated active methods such as prescription event monitoring |
| 1978 | Technical work moved to Uppsala, Sweden — **Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC)** | WHO Collaborating Centre for International Drug Monitoring |
| 1990 | ICH formed (Europe, USA, Japan) | Harmonised E2 guidelines on safety reporting |
| 2004 | Rofecoxib withdrawn worldwide (heart attack and stroke risk) | Debate on signal detection and risk management |

## 1.4 WHO International Drug Monitoring Programme

**Structure**

```text
Health professionals / patients
        │  suspected ADR reports
        ▼
National pharmacovigilance centre (e.g. PvPI, India)
        │  reports in ICSR (E2B) format, e.g. through VigiFlow
        ▼
WHO – Uppsala Monitoring Centre, Sweden
        │  stores in VigiBase, screens for signals
        ▼
Signals and information shared with member countries and WHO
```

**Key points**

- Started in **1968**; managed technically by the **WHO–UMC, Uppsala** since **1978**.
- Membership has grown to well over 170 countries and territories (full and associate members).
- **VigiBase** — the WHO global database of individual case safety reports; the largest of its kind.
- **VigiFlow** — web-based tool used by national centres (including India) to manage and send reports.
- **VigiLyze / VigiAccess** — tools for searching VigiBase; VigiAccess is open to the public.
- UMC also maintains **WHODrug** (drug dictionary) and developed the **WHO–UMC causality system** and Bayesian signal detection (information component).

**Functions of the programme:** collecting reports from member countries, identifying signals, sharing information, developing tools and terminologies, training, and supporting new national centres.

## 1.5 Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)

**Background**

- India joined the WHO programme in the late 1990s (1998 in most sources).
- A National Pharmacovigilance Programme (NPP) was launched by CDSCO in **November 2004** with zonal and regional centres; it ended after its funding period.
- **PvPI** was approved by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in **July 2010**, with **AIIMS, New Delhi** as the first National Coordination Centre (NCC).
- In **April 2011** the NCC moved to the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad**.
- In **2017** IPC–PvPI became a **WHO Collaborating Centre** for pharmacovigilance in public health programmes and regulatory services.

**Mission:** to safeguard the health of the Indian population by ensuring that the benefits of medicines outweigh their risks.

**Organisation**

```text
CDSCO (regulator, New Delhi)  ◄── recommendations ── NCC-PvPI, IPC Ghaziabad
                                                          ▲
                                                          │ reports (VigiFlow)
                                   ADR Monitoring Centres (AMCs) — mostly in
                                   medical colleges and hospitals
                                                          ▲
                                   Doctors, pharmacists, nurses, patients, MAHs
```

- **ADR Monitoring Centres (AMCs)** collect, check and enter reports; there are now several hundred across India.
- **Reporting tools:** the *Suspected Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Form* (for health professionals), a *Medicines Side Effect Reporting Form* for consumers (available in Indian languages), the **ADR PvPI mobile app**, e-mail, and the toll-free **helpline 1800-180-3024**.
- Related programmes: **Materiovigilance Programme of India (MvPI)** for medical devices, and AEFI surveillance for vaccines under the immunisation programme.
- The NCC analyses data and recommends regulatory action (label changes, restrictions, safety alerts) to **CDSCO**.

**Who can report?** Any health professional (doctor, dentist, pharmacist, nurse) and, since consumer forms were introduced, patients themselves. Reporting a suspicion is enough; proof of causation is not needed.

## 1.6 Basic terminologies

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Adverse event (AE)** | Any untoward medical occurrence in a patient given a medicine, which does **not necessarily** have a causal relationship with the treatment |
| **Adverse drug reaction (ADR)** | WHO: a response to a drug that is noxious and unintended and occurs at doses normally used in man for prophylaxis, diagnosis or therapy of disease or for modification of physiological function |
| **Adverse drug event (ADE)** | Injury resulting from use of a drug, including harm from errors |
| **Side effect** | Any unintended effect at normal doses related to the drug's pharmacological properties; may even be beneficial |
| **Unexpected ADR** | A reaction whose nature, severity or outcome is not consistent with the reference safety information (label, investigator's brochure) |
| **Serious AE / ADR** | One that results in death, is life-threatening, needs or prolongs hospitalisation, causes persistent or significant disability, causes a congenital anomaly, or is otherwise medically important |
| **Signal** | Reported information on a possible causal relationship between an adverse event and a drug, previously unknown or incompletely documented |
| **Medication error** | A preventable failure in prescribing, dispensing, administering or monitoring a drug |
| **Toxicity** | Harm from an overdose or accumulation above the normal range |

**Regulatory terminologies:** marketing authorisation holder (MAH), reference safety information, company core safety information, ICSR, PSUR/PBRER, DSUR, SUSAR (suspected unexpected serious adverse reaction), risk management plan (RMP), expedited report, qualified person for pharmacovigilance (QPPV), black box warning, Dear Healthcare Professional letter. These are explained in Units 4 and 5.

**Note:** every ADR is an adverse event, but not every adverse event is an ADR. An AE becomes a suspected ADR when there is at least a reasonable possibility of a causal relationship.

## 1.7 Classification of ADRs

**A. Rawlins–Thompson classification, extended (A to F)**

| Type | Name | Features | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Augmented | Exaggerated normal pharmacological effect; dose-related, predictable, common, low mortality | Bleeding with anticoagulants, hypoglycaemia with insulin, bradycardia with beta-blockers |
| B | Bizarre | Not dose-related, unpredictable, uncommon, often immunological or genetic, high mortality | Anaphylaxis with penicillin, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, malignant hyperthermia |
| C | Chronic | Related to dose and duration of use | Adrenal suppression with long-term corticosteroids, analgesic nephropathy |
| D | Delayed | Appears long after exposure | Carcinogenesis, teratogenesis (thalidomide), tardive dyskinesia |
| E | End of use | Withdrawal effects on stopping | Rebound hypertension after clonidine, opioid withdrawal, seizures after stopping benzodiazepines |
| F | Failure | Unexpected failure of therapy, often from interactions | Oral contraceptive failure with enzyme inducers |

**B. Other classifications**

- **By mechanism:** pharmacological (type A), immunological (types I–IV hypersensitivity), idiosyncratic/genetic, drug interactions.
- **DoTS classification:** Dose relatedness (toxic, collateral, hypersusceptibility), Time course, Susceptibility (genetic, age, sex, disease, physiological change).
- **By frequency (CIOMS):** very common ≥1/10; common ≥1/100 to <1/10; uncommon ≥1/1,000 to <1/100; rare ≥1/10,000 to <1/1,000; very rare <1/10,000.

## 1.8 Detection and reporting

**Methods of detection** (details in Unit 3): spontaneous reporting, intensive hospital monitoring, chart review, prescription event monitoring, cohort and case-control studies, registries, data mining of large databases.

**Clinical clues to an ADR:** a new symptom after starting a drug; abnormal laboratory value; sudden stop of a drug; use of "antidote" drugs (naloxone, vitamin K, antihistamines, flumazenil) as triggers; prolonged hospital stay.

**What a good report contains** (minimum four items):

1. An identifiable patient (initials, age or sex).
2. An identifiable reporter.
3. A suspect drug.
4. A suspected reaction.

Useful additional details: dates of start and stop, dose and route, indication, concomitant drugs, dechallenge and rechallenge results, outcome, relevant history and tests.

**Barriers to reporting (Inman's "seven deadly sins"):** complacency (serious ADRs are well known), fear of litigation, guilt, ambition to publish personally, ignorance of how to report, diffidence (reporting only certain cases), lethargy. Under-reporting is the main weakness of spontaneous systems.

## 1.9 Causality assessment

Causality assessment asks: how likely is it that this drug caused this event?

**WHO–UMC system**

| Category | Main criteria |
| --- | --- |
| Certain | Plausible time relationship; cannot be explained by disease or other drugs; response to withdrawal plausible; event definitive pharmacologically or phenomenologically; rechallenge satisfactory if needed |
| Probable / likely | Reasonable time relationship; unlikely to be due to disease or other drugs; clinically reasonable response on withdrawal; rechallenge not required |
| Possible | Reasonable time relationship; could also be explained by disease or other drugs; withdrawal information lacking or unclear |
| Unlikely | Time relationship makes a link improbable; disease or other drugs give a plausible explanation |
| Conditional / unclassified | More data needed for proper assessment, or data under examination |
| Unassessable / unclassifiable | Report cannot be judged because information is insufficient or contradictory |

**Naranjo algorithm (ADR probability scale)** — 10 questions answered yes / no / do not know, each with a score:

1. Previous conclusive reports on this reaction?
2. Did the event appear after the suspected drug was given?
3. Did it improve when the drug was stopped or an antagonist given (dechallenge)?
4. Did it reappear when the drug was given again (rechallenge)?
5. Are there alternative causes?
6. Did it reappear with a placebo?
7. Was the drug detected in blood in toxic concentrations?
8. Was it more severe with a higher dose or less severe with a lower dose?
9. Did the patient have a similar reaction to the same or similar drugs before?
10. Was the event confirmed by objective evidence?

Total score: **≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful.**

Other methods: Karch–Lasagna algorithm, Kramer algorithm, Bayesian methods, and RUCAM for drug-induced liver injury.

**Dechallenge and rechallenge.** Positive dechallenge = reaction subsides when the drug is stopped. Positive rechallenge = reaction returns when the drug is restarted. Rechallenge is strong evidence but is usually unethical for serious reactions.

## 1.10 Severity and seriousness assessment

**Severity** describes intensity; **seriousness** describes outcome and is the regulatory criterion that decides reporting timelines. A severe headache is not serious; a mild-looking reaction leading to hospital admission is serious.

**Modified Hartwig and Siegel severity scale**

| Level | Description | Category |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | ADR needs no change in treatment | Mild |
| 2 | Drug held, stopped or changed; no antidote, no longer stay | Mild |
| 3 | Treatment changed and/or antidote or other treatment needed; no longer stay | Moderate |
| 4 | Admission or longer stay because of the ADR (or level 3 with longer stay) | Moderate |
| 5 | Intensive care needed | Severe |
| 6 | Permanent harm to the patient | Severe |
| 7 | Death, directly or indirectly | Severe |

**Seriousness criteria (ICH E2A):** death; life-threatening; in-patient hospitalisation or prolongation of existing hospitalisation; persistent or significant disability/incapacity; congenital anomaly/birth defect; other medically important event (for example, intensive treatment for allergic bronchospasm at home).

## 1.11 Predictability and preventability

**Predictability** — whether the ADR could have been expected from the drug's pharmacology, known label information or patient risk factors. Type A reactions are usually predictable; type B are usually not.

**Preventability — modified Schumock and Thornton criteria.** An ADR is **preventable** if any answer is "yes":

1. Was the drug inappropriate for the patient's condition?
2. Were the dose, route or frequency inappropriate for age, weight or disease?
3. Was required therapeutic drug monitoring or another test not done?
4. Was there a history of allergy or previous reaction to the drug?
5. Was a known drug interaction involved?
6. Was a toxic serum concentration documented?
7. Was poor compliance involved?

ADRs are then classed as **definitely preventable, probably preventable or not preventable**.

## 1.12 Management of ADRs

1. **Recognise** the reaction; take a full history and examine.
2. **Stop or reduce** the suspected drug (dechallenge) where possible; consider whether the benefit needs the drug to continue.
3. **Treat** symptoms or give an antidote: adrenaline for anaphylaxis, naloxone for opioid excess, vitamin K for warfarin bleeding, antihistamines for rashes.
4. **Substitute** a safer drug from a different class.
5. **Document** in the case sheet, and flag allergy in the record.
6. **Report** to the AMC / PvPI.
7. **Educate** the patient: give an allergy card, explain what to avoid in future.
8. **Prevent recurrence:** review similar drugs (cross-reactivity), adjust doses in renal or hepatic impairment, monitor high-risk drugs.

**Role of the pharmacist in PV:** detecting reactions during counselling and dispensing, completing reports, causality assessment in AMCs, educating staff and patients, and maintaining drug safety bulletins.

## Remember

- PV = detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem (WHO).
- Thalidomide (1961) → modern PV; WHO programme **1968**; WHO–UMC Uppsala **1978**; VigiBase is its database.
- PvPI: July **2010** (AIIMS NCC) → IPC Ghaziabad **April 2011**; WHO-CC **2017**; helpline **1800-180-3024**.
- AE need not be causal; ADR is noxious, unintended, at normal doses.
- Types A–F: Augmented, Bizarre, Chronic, Delayed, End of use, Failure.
- Naranjo: ≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful. WHO–UMC: certain, probable, possible, unlikely, conditional, unassessable.
- Severity (Hartwig) ≠ seriousness (ICH E2A outcome criteria).
- Preventability: modified Schumock and Thornton.
