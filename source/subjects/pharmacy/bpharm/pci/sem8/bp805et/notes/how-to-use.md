# How to use this guide

Pharmacovigilance is **one of the semester-8 electives** of the PCI B.Pharm course. It has no practical; the whole subject is the 75-mark theory paper. The subject links closely with Pharmacy Practice (BP703T) in semester 7, which introduced ADRs and the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India — the facts used here (PvPI started in 2010, IPC Ghaziabad as the National Coordination Centre, helpline 1800-180-3024, Naranjo scores) are the same. Study one unit a day: read the notes, draw each diagram once from memory (WHO programme flow, PvPI organisation, MedDRA hierarchy, case-control and cohort 2 × 2 tables, drug development life cycle), then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus papers.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Signal detection and the basic measures of pharmacoepidemiology (odds ratio, relative risk, PRR) are taught inside Unit 3, next to the study designs they come from. Writing case narratives, listed among the syllabus objectives, is covered with ICSRs in Unit 4.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with a table or diagram and examples.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start every answer with the official definition (WHO definitions of PV, ADR, signal, AEFI; E2A definitions of serious and unexpected). Examiners look for these words.
2. Use tables: ADR types A–F with examples, WHO–UMC categories, study designs compared, ICH E2 guidelines, CIOMS working groups. A clear table in a 5-mark answer is quicker than paragraphs.
3. Draw flow diagrams: reporter → AMC → NCC → UMC; the MedDRA ladder; the case processing workflow; the drug life cycle from pre-clinical to PMS.
4. Learn the numbers exactly and do not guess others: WHO programme 1968 (10 countries), UMC 1978, PvPI July 2010, NCC to IPC April 2011, helpline 1800-180-3024; Naranjo ≥9 / 5–8 / 1–4 / ≤0; expedited 7 + 8 and 15 days; PSUR 6-monthly × 2 years then annual × 2 years; MedDRA 27 SOCs; ICH 1990.
5. In comparison questions (AE vs ADR, severity vs seriousness, active vs passive surveillance, case-control vs cohort, PSUR vs PBRER), write at least six points in two columns.
6. For pharmacogenomics, give the gene, the drug and the reaction for each example — three items per row, five or six rows.
7. Write formulas for odds ratio, relative risk and PRR with the 2 × 2 table labelled a, b, c, d; a small worked figure earns full marks.
