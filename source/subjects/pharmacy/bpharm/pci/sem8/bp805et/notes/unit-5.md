# Unit 5: Pharmacogenomics of ADRs, special populations, CIOMS and CDSCO

The same medicine at the same dose can be harmless in one person and dangerous in another. Genes, age, pregnancy and breastfeeding change how a drug is handled and how the body responds. This unit explains the genetic basis of many ADRs (with a focus on pharmacokinetic genes), how drug safety is judged in children, pregnant and breastfeeding women and the elderly, the international CIOMS standards for ADR reporting, and the Indian regulatory framework — CDSCO, the Drugs and Cosmetics Act and Schedule Y — compared with global requirements.

## 5.1 Pharmacogenomics of adverse drug reactions

**Pharmacogenetics** — study of how inherited differences in single genes change drug response. **Pharmacogenomics** — the genome-wide study of these differences. Both explain many type B ("idiosyncratic") reactions and some type A reactions that occur at normal doses.

**Genetic polymorphism** — a variant of a gene present in more than 1% of a population. Depending on their genes, people are grouped as **poor, intermediate, normal (extensive) or ultrarapid metabolisers**.

```text
Poor metaboliser        →  drug accumulates  → type A toxicity at normal doses
                           (prodrug: no active form → therapeutic failure)
Ultrarapid metaboliser  →  drug cleared fast → failure
                           (prodrug: too much active form → toxicity)
```

## 5.2 Genetics-related ADRs affecting pharmacokinetics

| Gene / enzyme | Effect of variant | Drugs | ADR / consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **CYP2D6** | Poor and ultrarapid metabolisers | Codeine and tramadol (prodrugs activated by CYP2D6) | Ultrarapid: excess morphine → respiratory depression (deaths reported in children and breastfed infants); poor: no analgesia |
| CYP2D6 | Poor metabolisers | Some tricyclic antidepressants, metoprolol | Higher levels → anticholinergic, cardiac effects; bradycardia |
| **CYP2C9** (with VKORC1, a pharmacodynamic gene) | Reduced activity | Warfarin, phenytoin | Over-anticoagulation and bleeding; phenytoin toxicity |
| **CYP2C19** | Poor metabolisers | Clopidogrel (prodrug) | Less active metabolite → reduced antiplatelet effect, stent thrombosis |
| CYP2C19 | Poor metabolisers | Proton pump inhibitors | Higher exposure (greater acid suppression) |
| **N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2)** | **Slow acetylators** | Isoniazid | Peripheral neuropathy, hepatotoxicity |
| NAT2 slow acetylators | | Hydralazine, procainamide | Drug-induced lupus |
| **TPMT** (thiopurine methyltransferase), also NUDT15 | Low activity | Azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine | Severe bone-marrow suppression |
| **DPYD** (dihydropyrimidine dehydrogenase) | Deficiency | 5-fluorouracil, capecitabine | Severe mucositis, neutropenia, diarrhoea, can be fatal |
| **UGT1A1*28** (Gilbert-type variant) | Reduced glucuronidation | Irinotecan | Neutropenia and diarrhoea |
| **Plasma (pseudo)cholinesterase** (BCHE) | Atypical enzyme | Succinylcholine (suxamethonium), mivacurium | Prolonged apnoea after anaesthesia |
| **G6PD** deficiency (red-cell enzyme) | Low NADPH in red cells | Primaquine, dapsone, sulphonamides, nitrofurantoin, rasburicase | Acute haemolytic anaemia |

**Pharmacodynamic and immune examples (HLA)**

| HLA allele | Drug | Reaction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HLA-B\*57:01 | Abacavir | Hypersensitivity syndrome (screening before use is standard) |
| HLA-B\*15:02 | Carbamazepine (and phenytoin) | Stevens–Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis, mainly in South-East and South Asian populations |
| HLA-B\*58:01 | Allopurinol | SJS/TEN, DRESS |
| — | Halothane, succinylcholine (RYR1 variants) | Malignant hyperthermia |

**Applications in pharmacovigilance**

- Explains clusters of ADRs in certain ethnic groups (CYP2C19 poor metabolisers and HLA-B\*15:02 are more common in Asian populations).
- Pre-treatment **genetic testing** (HLA-B\*57:01, TPMT, DPYD) prevents serious reactions.
- **Genotype-guided dosing** (warfarin with CYP2C9/VKORC1).
- Labels now include pharmacogenomic information (boxed warnings, testing recommendations).
- Collection of DNA samples in case-control studies of serious ADRs (SJS/TEN, liver injury).

## 5.3 Drug safety evaluation in paediatrics

**Why children are special**

- Children are **under-represented in trials**; much paediatric prescribing is **off-label** or unlicensed.
- Age-related changes in pharmacokinetics:

| PK step | Neonates and infants compared with adults |
| --- | --- |
| Absorption | Higher gastric pH, irregular emptying; thinner skin with greater topical absorption |
| Distribution | More body water, less fat; lower plasma protein binding; more permeable blood–brain barrier |
| Metabolism | Immature enzymes, especially glucuronidation, at birth; faster than adults in older children |
| Excretion | Lower glomerular filtration and tubular secretion in neonates |

- Weight-based and body-surface dosing; risk of calculation errors (tenfold errors), lack of child-friendly dosage forms.
- Effects on **growth and development** may appear years later.

**Classic paediatric ADRs**

| Drug | ADR |
| --- | --- |
| Chloramphenicol in neonates | Grey baby syndrome (immature glucuronidation) |
| Sulphonamides, ceftriaxone in neonates | Kernicterus from displacement of bilirubin |
| Tetracyclines | Discoloration of teeth, effect on bone growth |
| Fluoroquinolones | Concern over cartilage damage (seen in young animals) |
| Aspirin | Reye's syndrome in children with viral illness |
| Benzyl alcohol preservative | Gasping syndrome in neonates |
| Codeine | Respiratory depression in ultrarapid metabolisers |

**PV measures:** paediatric investigation plans and age-appropriate trials, paediatric formulations, active surveillance in neonatal and paediatric units, paediatric-specific reporting, causality assessment with age-specific background rates, and careful counselling of parents.

## 5.4 Drug safety in pregnancy

**Why it is difficult:** pregnant women are excluded from almost all trials, so human data come mainly from post-marketing reports, registries and observational studies. Two lives are exposed; effects may be delayed (as with diethylstilbestrol).

**Physiological changes:** increased plasma volume and body water, reduced albumin, increased glomerular filtration, altered hepatic enzyme activity — so drug levels may change.

**Timing of exposure**

```text
Weeks after conception:  0–2            3–8                 9 to term
                         "all or none"  ORGANOGENESIS       growth and functional
                         (loss or no    (major structural   development (growth
                         effect)        malformations)      restriction, organ toxicity)
```

**Known teratogens and fetotoxic drugs (examples)**

| Drug | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Thalidomide | Phocomelia (limb defects) |
| Isotretinoin and other retinoids | Severe craniofacial, cardiac and CNS defects |
| Sodium valproate | Neural tube defects; developmental problems |
| Warfarin | Fetal warfarin syndrome (nasal hypoplasia, bone stippling); bleeding |
| Phenytoin | Fetal hydantoin syndrome |
| ACE inhibitors, ARBs (2nd and 3rd trimester) | Fetal renal damage, oligohydramnios |
| Tetracyclines | Teeth and bone effects |
| Diethylstilbestrol | Vaginal clear-cell adenocarcinoma in daughters |
| Methotrexate | Abortion, malformations |

**Risk classification.** The older US FDA letter categories (A, B, C, D, X) were replaced in 2015 by the **Pregnancy and Lactation Labelling Rule**, which uses narrative sections (risk summary, clinical considerations, data) instead of letters. Many Indian textbooks still describe the letter categories, so know both.

**PV methods in pregnancy:** pregnancy exposure registries, follow-up of every exposed pregnancy reported (collect outcome at birth and later), birth-defect registries, case-control studies of malformations, teratology information services, and linkage of mother and child records. Exposure during pregnancy is reported even without an adverse outcome.

## 5.5 Drug safety in lactation

**Transfer into milk depends on:**

- **lipid solubility** (high → more transfer);
- **molecular weight** (small molecules pass easily);
- **protein binding** (highly bound drugs pass less);
- **ionisation** — milk is slightly more acidic than plasma, so **weak bases** are trapped in milk;
- maternal plasma level, half-life, timing of feeds;
- oral bioavailability in the infant and infant's age (premature babies at greater risk).

**Measures:** milk-to-plasma (M/P) ratio; **relative infant dose** (infant dose via milk as a percentage of the mother's weight-adjusted dose — values under about 10% are generally considered compatible).

**Drugs needing caution or avoidance (examples):** anticancer drugs, radioactive agents, lithium, amiodarone, ergot alkaloids (suppress lactation), and codeine (infant respiratory depression when the mother is an ultrarapid metaboliser).

**PV in lactation:** report infant effects (sedation, poor feeding, rash), use resources such as LactMed, prefer drugs with established safety, time doses just after feeds.

## 5.6 Drug safety in geriatrics

**Why the elderly are at higher risk**

- **Polypharmacy** — many diseases, many prescribers, more interactions.
- **Pharmacokinetic changes:** reduced renal function (most important), reduced liver blood flow and mass, more body fat and less body water (fat-soluble drugs last longer, water-soluble drugs reach higher levels), lower albumin.
- **Pharmacodynamic changes:** increased sensitivity to CNS-acting drugs, blunted baroreceptor reflexes (postural hypotension), impaired homeostasis.
- Cognitive impairment, poor vision, swallowing problems → adherence errors.
- **Prescribing cascade** — an ADR mistaken for a new disease and treated with another drug.
- Under-represented in trials, especially the very old and frail.

**Common geriatric ADRs**

| Drug group | ADR |
| --- | --- |
| Benzodiazepines, sedatives, antipsychotics | Falls, fractures, confusion |
| Anticholinergics (first-generation antihistamines, tricyclics) | Confusion, urinary retention, constipation |
| NSAIDs | GI bleeding, kidney injury, fluid retention |
| Diuretics, SSRIs | Hyponatraemia |
| Digoxin | Toxicity (renal decline) |
| Antihypertensives | Postural hypotension, falls |
| Anticoagulants | Bleeding |

**Tools:** **Beers criteria** (American Geriatrics Society list of potentially inappropriate medications for older adults), **STOPP/START** criteria (screening for inappropriate prescriptions and omitted beneficial ones), medication review by pharmacists, renal dose adjustment, deprescribing.

## 5.7 CIOMS

The **Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS)** is an international non-governmental organisation set up in **1949 by WHO and UNESCO**, based in Geneva. Its working groups bring together regulators, industry and academics to propose harmonised approaches; many CIOMS proposals later became ICH guidelines.

**Important CIOMS working groups**

| Group | Topic and outcome |
| --- | --- |
| CIOMS I | International reporting of individual ADRs — the **CIOMS I form**; basis of ICH E2A |
| CIOMS II | Periodic safety update summaries — basis of the PSUR (ICH E2C) |
| CIOMS III | Core clinical safety information (company core data sheet) |
| CIOMS IV | Benefit–risk balance for marketed drugs |
| CIOMS V | Current challenges: consumer reports, literature, solicited reports, lack of efficacy |
| CIOMS VI | Management of safety information from clinical trials |
| CIOMS VII | Development safety update report (basis of ICH E2F) |
| CIOMS VIII | Practical aspects of signal detection |
| CIOMS IX | Risk minimisation |
| CIOMS X | Meta-analysis in safety evaluation |

CIOMS also publishes **international ethical guidelines for health-related research** and the frequency terms (very common to very rare) used in labelling.

**CIOMS I form** — the standard paper form for reporting a single suspected ADR from manufacturers to regulators. Sections:

1. **Reaction information** — patient initials, country, date of birth/age, sex, reaction onset date, description of the reaction including relevant tests, seriousness tick-boxes (patient died, involved or prolonged hospitalisation, persistent disability or incapacity, life-threatening).
2. **Suspect drug information** — drug name, daily dose, route, indication, therapy dates and duration; did the reaction abate after stopping? did it reappear after reintroduction?
3. **Concomitant drugs and history** — other drugs with dates, relevant history (diagnoses, allergies, pregnancy).
4. **Manufacturer information** — name and address, manufacturer control number, date received, report source (study, literature, health professional), report type (initial or follow-up).

## 5.8 CDSCO (India) and pharmacovigilance

The **Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO)**, under the Directorate General of Health Services, Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, is India's national drug regulator, headed by the **Drugs Controller General of India (DCGI)**.

**PV-related functions**

- Approval of new drugs and clinical trials; conditions for post-marketing studies.
- Receiving SAE reports from trials, PSURs and serious ADR reports from MAHs.
- Acting on PvPI recommendations: label changes, safety alerts, restrictions, suspension or prohibition of drugs and irrational fixed-dose combinations.
- Inspections and enforcement under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act.
- Working with IPC (PvPI), the Materiovigilance programme and state drug controllers.
- With IPC it has issued pharmacovigilance guidance for marketing authorisation holders (first version 2018), requiring companies to have a PV system, a person responsible for PV, and timely reporting of ADRs to the national programme.

## 5.9 Drugs and Cosmetics Act and Schedule Y

- **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940** and **Rules, 1945** — the main law on import, manufacture, sale and distribution of drugs in India.
- **Schedule Y** of the Rules (major revision in **2005**) set requirements for permission to import or manufacture new drugs and to conduct clinical trials. PV-related points:
  - **PSURs** for a new drug: every **six months for the first two years** after approval, then **annually for the next two years**.
  - Reporting of **serious adverse events** in clinical trials by the investigator to the sponsor, ethics committee and licensing authority within short fixed timelines, followed by an analysed report.
  - Formats for SAE reporting and for clinical study reports; requirements for informed consent and ethics committees.
- In **2019** the clinical trial and new drug provisions of Schedule Y were replaced by the **New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019**, which keep the six-monthly then annual PSUR requirement and set fixed timelines: the investigator reports an SAE **within 24 hours**, and an analysed report follows **within 14 days**. Questions set on the PCI syllabus still use the term Schedule Y, so describe both.

## 5.10 Differences between Indian and global pharmacovigilance requirements

| Aspect | India | Global (ICH regions, EU, USA) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| National system | PvPI under IPC with AMCs mostly in medical colleges; CDSCO as regulator | Long-established systems: EU EudraVigilance, US FAERS, UK Yellow Card |
| Legal basis | D&C Act and Rules, NDCT Rules 2019, PvPI guidance for MAHs | Detailed laws and guidance (EU Good Pharmacovigilance Practices modules, US federal regulations) |
| Responsible person | Person responsible for PV in the company, as per guidance | EU: Qualified Person for PV (QPPV) resident in the EU, PV System Master File |
| Periodic reports | PSUR 6-monthly × 2 years, then annual × 2 years | PBRER with EU reference dates; frequency set by risk |
| Risk management | Less formal RMP requirements | RMP mandatory in EU; REMS in USA |
| Signal management | Mainly at NCC level | Formal signal management by MAHs and regulators |
| Reporting culture | Under-reporting; low awareness among doctors and pharmacists; growing consumer reporting | Higher reporting rates, mature consumer reporting |
| Special issues | Many brands and FDCs, self-medication, OTC misuse, traditional medicines (AYUSH has a separate PV scheme), language diversity, substandard drugs | Biosimilar tracking, large electronic health record networks for active surveillance |

**Common ground:** ICH definitions (E2A), MedDRA coding, E2B electronic reporting through VigiFlow, WHO programme membership.

## Remember

- Polymorphism → poor / intermediate / normal / ultrarapid metabolisers.
- CYP2D6 codeine, CYP2C9 warfarin, CYP2C19 clopidogrel, NAT2 isoniazid (slow acetylators), TPMT azathioprine, DPYD 5-FU, UGT1A1 irinotecan, pseudocholinesterase succinylcholine, G6PD primaquine; HLA-B\*15:02 carbamazepine, HLA-B\*57:01 abacavir, HLA-B\*58:01 allopurinol.
- Children: grey baby, kernicterus, teeth staining, Reye's syndrome.
- Pregnancy: weeks 3–8 organogenesis; thalidomide, isotretinoin, valproate, warfarin; FDA letters replaced by PLLR in 2015.
- Lactation: weak bases trapped in milk; relative infant dose under about 10%.
- Elderly: polypharmacy, renal decline, falls; Beers and STOPP/START.
- CIOMS (1949, WHO + UNESCO); CIOMS I form; working groups I–X.
- CDSCO/DCGI; D&C Act 1940, Rules 1945; Schedule Y 2005 → NDCT Rules 2019; PSUR 6-monthly × 2 years then annual × 2 years.
