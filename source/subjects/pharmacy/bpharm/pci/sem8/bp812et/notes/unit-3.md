# Unit 3: Free radicals, and dietary fibre as a functional food ingredient

The first half of this unit explains what free radicals are, how cells make them, and how they damage the four big classes of biomolecules — lipids, proteins, carbohydrates and nucleic acids. This is the base for Unit 4 (free radicals in disease, and antioxidants). The second half covers dietary fibre and complex carbohydrates, the most widely used functional food ingredients.

## 3.1 Free radicals — definitions

A **free radical** is **any atom or molecule that has one or more unpaired electrons** in its outer orbital and can exist on its own. The unpaired electron is shown by a dot (•). Because electrons are stable only in pairs, a radical is **highly reactive**: it takes an electron from (oxidises) or gives an electron to (reduces) a nearby molecule, or adds to it. The molecule that loses an electron often becomes a radical itself, so radicals start **chain reactions**.

Radicals form in three ways:

1. **Homolytic cleavage** of a covalent bond — each fragment keeps one electron (A:B → A• + B•); caused by heat, UV light, ionising radiation.
2. **Loss of one electron** from a normal molecule (oxidation).
3. **Gain of one electron** by a normal molecule (reduction) — for example O2 + e⁻ → O2•⁻.

### Reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS)

"Reactive oxygen species" is a wider term: it includes oxygen radicals **and** reactive non-radical oxygen compounds.

| Species | Symbol | Radical? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Superoxide anion | O2•⁻ | Yes | First product of one-electron reduction of oxygen; made by mitochondria and NADPH oxidase |
| Hydroxyl radical | •OH | Yes | **Most reactive** and most damaging; reacts with almost anything at the site where it forms |
| Hydroperoxyl radical | HO2• | Yes | Protonated superoxide; more lipid-soluble |
| Peroxyl radical | ROO• | Yes | Carries on lipid peroxidation |
| Alkoxyl radical | RO• | Yes | From breakdown of lipid hydroperoxides |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H2O2 | **No** | Crosses membranes; gives •OH with iron (Fenton reaction) |
| Singlet oxygen | ¹O2 | **No** | Excited oxygen made by light with photosensitisers; quenched by carotenoids |
| Hypochlorous acid | HOCl | **No** | Made by myeloperoxidase in neutrophils |
| Ozone | O3 | **No** | Air pollutant |
| Nitric oxide | NO• | Yes (RNS) | Signal molecule, vasodilator |
| Peroxynitrite | ONOO⁻ | **No** (RNS) | NO• + O2•⁻; strong oxidant and nitrating agent |

**Oxidative stress** is the condition in which the production of ROS/RNS is **greater than the antioxidant defences** can handle, leading to damage of biomolecules. At low levels, ROS are **useful**: they kill microbes in phagocytes and act as signalling molecules (redox signalling). Problems arise only when the balance tips.

```text
       Oxidants (ROS, RNS)              Antioxidants
 ┌──────────────────────────┐    ┌─────────────────────────┐
 │ mitochondria, NADPH oxid, │ vs │ SOD, catalase, GPx,     │
 │ xanthine oxidase, CYP450, │    │ glutathione, vit C, E,  │
 │ pollution, smoke, UV      │    │ carotenoids, flavonoids │
 └──────────────────────────┘    └─────────────────────────┘
        oxidants > defences  →  OXIDATIVE STRESS → damage
```

## 3.2 Production of free radicals in cells

### A. Internal (endogenous) sources

1. **Mitochondrial electron transport chain** — the largest source. During normal respiration, a small fraction of electrons **leak** from complexes I and III and reduce oxygen to **superoxide** instead of passing to complex IV (where oxygen is fully reduced to water).
2. **NADPH oxidase (NOX)** in neutrophils and macrophages — makes large amounts of superoxide deliberately during the **respiratory burst** to kill engulfed microbes; **myeloperoxidase** then makes HOCl from H2O2 and chloride.
3. **Xanthine oxidase** — oxidises hypoxanthine and xanthine to uric acid and reduces oxygen to superoxide and H2O2; very important in **ischaemia–reperfusion injury** (Unit 4).
4. **Cytochrome P450 enzymes** in the endoplasmic reticulum — leak superoxide while metabolising drugs and toxins; some drugs and toxins (carbon tetrachloride, paracetamol overdose) are converted to radicals or reactive metabolites.
5. **Peroxisomes** — fatty-acid oxidation produces H2O2.
6. **Nitric oxide synthase** — makes NO•, which with superoxide forms peroxynitrite.
7. **Auto-oxidation** of small molecules — catecholamines, flavins, haemoglobin (forming methaemoglobin and superoxide).
8. **Transition metals** — free **iron (Fe²⁺)** and **copper (Cu⁺)** turn H2O2 into the hydroxyl radical.

### Key reactions

```text
One-electron reduction of oxygen (step by step):
 O2 ──e⁻──► O2•⁻ ──e⁻ + 2H⁺──► H2O2 ──e⁻──► •OH + OH⁻ ──e⁻ + H⁺──► H2O

Fenton reaction:
 Fe²⁺ + H2O2 → Fe³⁺ + •OH + OH⁻

Haber–Weiss reaction (iron-catalysed overall):
 O2•⁻ + H2O2 → O2 + •OH + OH⁻
 (superoxide recycles Fe³⁺ back to Fe²⁺: O2•⁻ + Fe³⁺ → O2 + Fe²⁺)

Peroxynitrite formation:
 NO• + O2•⁻ → ONOO⁻
```

### B. External (exogenous) sources

- **Ionising radiation** (X-rays, gamma rays) splits water → •OH (radiolysis); **UV light** on skin.
- **Cigarette smoke** — very rich in radicals in both tar and gas phases.
- **Air pollution** — ozone, nitrogen dioxide, particulate matter.
- **Drugs and chemicals** — paracetamol overdose, doxorubicin (redox cycling of its quinone), bleomycin, paraquat, carbon tetrachloride, alcohol (via CYP2E1).
- **Heavy metals** — iron overload, copper, cadmium, lead, mercury.
- **Diet** — oxidised and repeatedly heated frying oils, excess iron, alcohol.
- Strenuous exercise and infection temporarily raise ROS.

## 3.3 Damaging reactions of free radicals

### A. Lipids — lipid peroxidation

**Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs)** in membranes are the most easily attacked, because the **methylene (–CH2–) group between two double bonds** has weak C–H bonds. Lipid peroxidation is a **chain reaction** with three stages:

```text
1. INITIATION
   LH (PUFA) + •OH  →  L• (lipid radical) + H2O
   (hydrogen taken from the bis-allylic CH2; the double bonds shift to a conjugated diene)

2. PROPAGATION (repeats again and again)
   L•  + O2   →  LOO•  (lipid peroxyl radical)
   LOO• + LH  →  LOOH (lipid hydroperoxide) + L•   ← new radical keeps the chain going

3. TERMINATION
   LOO• + LOO•  →  non-radical products
   LOO• + vitamin E (TOH)  →  LOOH + TO• (stable tocopheroxyl radical)  ← chain-breaking antioxidant
```

- Lipid hydroperoxides break down (helped by iron) into **alkoxyl radicals** and into **aldehydes** such as **malondialdehyde (MDA)** and **4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE)**, which are toxic and cross-link proteins and DNA.
- **Consequences**: membranes become stiff and leaky, membrane enzymes and receptors fail, ion pumps stop working, organelles burst, the cell may die. **Oxidised LDL** is taken up by macrophages to form foam cells in atherosclerosis. In foods, the same process makes fats **rancid**.
- **Markers**: MDA measured as **thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS)**; **F2-isoprostanes** (from arachidonic acid; considered reliable markers); conjugated dienes; 4-HNE adducts.

### B. Proteins

- **Oxidation of amino acid side chains** — **cysteine** (thiol → disulphide, sulphenic and higher acids) and **methionine** (→ methionine sulphoxide) are most sensitive; aromatic amino acids (tyrosine, tryptophan, histidine) are also attacked.
- **Protein carbonyl formation** — oxidation of lysine, arginine, proline and threonine gives carbonyl groups; **protein carbonyls** are the most used marker of protein oxidation.
- **Nitration** of tyrosine by peroxynitrite → **3-nitrotyrosine**.
- **Cross-linking** (dityrosine bridges, links through MDA and 4-HNE) and **fragmentation** of the backbone.
- **Consequences**: loss of enzyme activity, unfolding and **aggregation**, faster breakdown by the proteasome, damaged receptors and transporters, new antigens (autoimmunity). Oxidised, cross-linked lens proteins contribute to **cataract**.

### C. Carbohydrates

- **Glycoxidation**: reducing sugars such as glucose react with protein amino groups (Maillard reaction → Schiff base → Amadori product). With oxidation, these form **advanced glycation end products (AGEs)** such as carboxymethyl-lysine and pentosidine; the process also releases more ROS. AGEs stiffen collagen and blood vessels and act on the **RAGE** receptor to cause inflammation — important in **diabetic complications** and ageing.
- **Auto-oxidation of glucose** in high-glucose states produces superoxide, H2O2 and reactive dicarbonyls (glyoxal, methylglyoxal).
- **Depolymerisation of polysaccharides** — •OH breaks **hyaluronic acid** in joint fluid, lowering its viscosity (seen in inflammatory arthritis).
- The **deoxyribose** sugar of DNA is attacked, causing strand breaks.

### D. Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA)

- **Base modification** — the hydroxyl radical adds to bases. The best-known product is **8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-oxo-dG, also 8-OHdG)**; it pairs wrongly with adenine, causing **G:C → T:A transversion mutations**. 8-oxo-dG in urine is a marker of oxidative DNA damage.
- **Sugar damage and strand breaks** — single- and double-strand breaks.
- **Abasic sites** — loss of a base.
- **DNA–protein cross-links**; MDA forms DNA adducts.
- **Mitochondrial DNA** is especially exposed (close to the electron transport chain, no histones, less repair).
- **Consequences**: mutation → **cancer**; cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis; ageing. The cell repairs much of this damage (base excision repair).

### Summary table

| Target | Main damage | Marker | Disease link |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lipids | Peroxidation chain reaction | MDA (TBARS), F2-isoprostanes, 4-HNE | Atherosclerosis, membrane injury |
| Proteins | Carbonyls, thiol oxidation, nitration, cross-links | Protein carbonyls, 3-nitrotyrosine | Cataract, neurodegeneration, ageing |
| Carbohydrates | Glycoxidation, AGEs, polysaccharide breakdown | AGEs (CML, pentosidine) | Diabetic complications, arthritis |
| Nucleic acids | Base oxidation, strand breaks | 8-oxo-dG | Cancer, ageing |

## 3.4 Dietary fibre and complex carbohydrates as functional food ingredients

### Definitions

- **Complex carbohydrates** are carbohydrates made of many sugar units — **starch** (digestible) and **non-starch polysaccharides** (mostly indigestible) — as against simple sugars.
- **Dietary fibre** is the **edible part of plants and analogous carbohydrates that resists digestion and absorption in the human small intestine**, with complete or partial **fermentation in the large intestine**. It includes polysaccharides, oligosaccharides, lignin and associated plant substances.
- **Resistant starch** is starch that escapes digestion in the small intestine (in unripe banana, cooled cooked rice and potatoes, legumes) and behaves like fibre.

### Classification by solubility

| Property | **Soluble fibre** | **Insoluble fibre** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behaviour in water | Dissolves or swells to form a **viscous gel** | Does not dissolve; holds water, adds bulk |
| Examples | **Pectin** (apple, citrus), **beta-glucan** (oats, barley), **gums** (guar, gum arabic), **mucilages** (psyllium/isabgol), **inulin, FOS**, alginate, glucomannan | **Cellulose**, **hemicellulose** (most types), **lignin** (non-carbohydrate), resistant starch (part) |
| Sources | Oats, barley, legumes, fruits, isabgol husk, flax | Wheat bran, whole grains, vegetable skins, nuts |
| Fermentation in colon | **Highly fermented** → short-chain fatty acids | Poorly fermented |
| Main effects | Lowers **LDL cholesterol**, slows **glucose** rise, satiety, prebiotic | Increases **stool bulk**, speeds **transit**, relieves **constipation** |

### Physiological effects and how they work

1. **Cholesterol lowering** (soluble, viscous fibres) — bind **bile acids** in the gut and increase their loss in faeces; the liver uses more cholesterol to make new bile acids, so blood LDL falls. Short-chain fatty acid **propionate** may also reduce cholesterol synthesis.
2. **Blood glucose control** — viscous gel slows **gastric emptying** and the contact of starch with enzymes, flattening the post-meal glucose and insulin peaks (lower **glycaemic index** of the meal). Useful in diabetes.
3. **Weight control** — fibre-rich foods are bulky and low in energy, need more chewing, and increase **satiety**.
4. **Bowel function** — insoluble fibre and psyllium increase stool weight and water, soften stools and speed transit; relief of **constipation**, **haemorrhoids** and **diverticular disease**; lower risk of **colon cancer** (dilution and faster removal of carcinogens, butyrate effects).
5. **Prebiotic effect** — fermentation by colonic bacteria produces **short-chain fatty acids** (**acetate, propionate, butyrate**). **Butyrate** is the main fuel of colon cells and has anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer actions; the colon pH falls, discouraging pathogens; mineral absorption (calcium) improves.
6. **Blood pressure** — whole-grain, high-fibre diets are associated with lower blood pressure.

### Fibres used as functional food ingredients

| Ingredient | Source | Use in foods |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Psyllium (isabgol) husk** | *Plantago ovata* seed husk | Bulk laxative, cholesterol lowering, fibre drinks |
| **Oat and barley beta-glucan** | Oats, barley | Breakfast cereals, bars; cholesterol lowering |
| **Pectin** | Citrus peel, apple pomace | Gelling agent in jams, cholesterol lowering |
| **Guar gum** | *Cyamopsis tetragonoloba* seed (galactomannan) | Thickener, glucose control |
| **Inulin and FOS** | Chicory root | Prebiotic, fat and sugar replacer |
| **Resistant starch / resistant dextrin** | Maize, tapioca, modified starch | Added fibre in breads, drinks |
| **Wheat bran** | Wheat | Breakfast cereals, high-fibre bread |
| **Glucomannan** | Konjac tuber | Weight-loss products (fullness) |
| **Cellulose derivatives** | Wood pulp, cotton | Bulking, texture |

### Possible problems with high fibre

- **Gas, bloating** and abdominal discomfort if fibre is increased too quickly — increase slowly and drink enough water.
- Bulk fibres taken with little water can cause **choking or intestinal obstruction**.
- Very high intakes of phytate-rich bran can reduce absorption of **iron, zinc, calcium**.
- Fibre supplements can **reduce absorption of some drugs** taken at the same time — leave a gap between the fibre and medicines.

## Remember

- Free radical = species with one or more unpaired electrons; ROS include radicals (O2•⁻, •OH, ROO•) and non-radicals (H2O2, ¹O2, HOCl); •OH is the most reactive.
- Oxidative stress = oxidants exceed antioxidant defences.
- Sources: mitochondrial leak (largest), NADPH oxidase respiratory burst, xanthine oxidase, CYP450, peroxisomes, Fenton reaction (Fe²⁺ + H2O2 → •OH); external — radiation, smoke, pollution, drugs, metals.
- Lipid peroxidation: initiation, propagation (L• + O2 → LOO•; LOO• + LH → LOOH + L•), termination by vitamin E; markers MDA (TBARS), F2-isoprostanes.
- Proteins: carbonyls, thiol oxidation, 3-nitrotyrosine, cross-links. Carbohydrates: AGEs, hyaluronic acid breakdown. DNA: 8-oxo-dG (G→T mutations), strand breaks.
- Dietary fibre resists digestion in the small intestine and is fermented in the colon. Soluble (pectin, beta-glucan, gums, psyllium, inulin) → lowers LDL and glucose; insoluble (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) → bulk and transit.
- Fermentation gives acetate, propionate, butyrate (butyrate fuels colon cells).
