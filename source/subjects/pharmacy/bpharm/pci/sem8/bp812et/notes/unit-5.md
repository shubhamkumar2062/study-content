# Unit 5: Stability, regulation, food safety and pharmacopoeial specifications

A nutraceutical is useful only if its active compounds survive processing and storage, and if the product is safe, honestly labelled and made to a standard. This unit covers three things: how processing, storage and the environment change the potency of nutraceuticals; the laws and systems that control food and supplement quality in India and abroad (FSSAI, FDA, FPO, MPO, AGMARK, HACCP, GMP) together with food adulteration; and the pharmacopoeial specifications used for dietary supplements.

## 5.1 Effect of processing, storage and environmental factors on nutraceuticals

### Factors that cause loss of potency

| Factor | What it does | Most sensitive nutraceuticals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Heat** (cooking, drying, pasteurisation, frying) | Speeds oxidation and breakdown; destroys enzymes and live microbes | Vitamin C, thiamine, folate, probiotics, anthocyanins, myrosinase and alliinase (so less sulforaphane and allicin), omega-3 fatty acids |
| **Oxygen** | Oxidation and rancidity | Omega-3 oils (fish oil, flax oil), carotenoids, vitamins A, C, E, polyphenols |
| **Light (especially UV)** | Photo-oxidation, isomerisation | Riboflavin, vitamin A, carotenoids (lycopene, beta-carotene), chlorophyll, vitamin D, curcumin |
| **Moisture / water activity** | Hydrolysis, microbial growth, caking; kills dry probiotics | Probiotic powders, vitamin C, effervescent tablets, hygroscopic extracts |
| **pH** | Acid or alkali breakdown, colour change | Anthocyanins (colour shifts), thiamine (unstable in alkali), folate, probiotics (stomach acid) |
| **Metal ions** (Fe, Cu) | Catalyse oxidation (Fenton chemistry) | Vitamin C, omega-3 oils, polyphenols |
| **Enzymes** in the raw material | Breakdown during storage (lipases, oxidases, polyphenol oxidase browning) | Fresh plant material, bran (rancidity unless stabilised) |
| **Time and temperature of storage** | Slow but continuous loss | All — the reason for expiry dates and overages |
| **Interactions with other ingredients** | Complexation, reduced absorption, degradation | Calcium and iron reduce each other's absorption; tannins bind iron and proteins; fibre binds minerals; reducing sugars + amino acids (Maillard browning) |

### Processing can also help

- **Lycopene**: heating tomatoes with oil breaks cell walls and forms cis isomers → **better absorption** from tomato paste than from raw tomato.
- **Beta-carotene**: light cooking of carrots improves availability.
- **Fermentation**: increases B vitamins, makes bioactive peptides, reduces anti-nutrients (phytate), produces probiotic foods (curd, idli batter).
- **Soaking, germination**: lower phytate, raise vitamin C and enzyme content.
- **Stabilisation of rice bran** by heat stops lipase and prevents rancidity.
- **Crushing garlic** before cooking allows allicin to form; **light steaming of broccoli** keeps myrosinase active.

### Ways to protect nutraceuticals

1. **Formulation**: add **antioxidants** (tocopherols, ascorbyl palmitate, rosemary extract, BHT/BHA within limits), metal **chelators** (citric acid, EDTA where permitted), use stable forms (vitamin E acetate, vitamin A palmitate, ascorbyl palmitate, esterified lutein).
2. **Encapsulation**: microencapsulation (spray drying, coacervation), liposomes, cyclodextrin complexes, emulsions — protect oils, probiotics and polyphenols, and can improve absorption (curcumin with piperine or as nanoformulations).
3. **Process control**: low-temperature drying (freeze drying, vacuum drying), short high-temperature treatment, inert gas (nitrogen) flushing, avoiding copper and iron equipment.
4. **Packaging**: light-resistant (amber glass, opaque foil), oxygen-barrier packs, blister packs, desiccants (silica gel) and **oxygen absorbers**, nitrogen-flushed soft gels and cans.
5. **Storage**: cool, dry, dark place; refrigeration for many probiotics.
6. **Overages**: adding a little more than the label amount to make up for expected loss during the shelf life (permitted within limits by FSSAI, with the label stating "appropriate overages added").
7. **Stability testing**: real-time and accelerated studies to set the **shelf life** ("best before" / expiry).

### Probiotics — a special stability case

Probiotic counts fall with **heat, moisture, oxygen, acid and time**. Manufacturers use acid- and bile-resistant strains, microencapsulation, enteric-coated capsules, low-moisture powders, cold-chain storage, and prebiotics as protectants. The label claim must be met **until the end of shelf life**, not only at manufacture.

## 5.2 Regulatory aspects

### A. FSSAI — Food Safety and Standards Authority of India

- Set up under the **Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (FSS Act)**, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. The Act brought together and **repealed** older food laws — the **Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954**, the **Fruit Products Order**, the **Meat Food Products Order**, the Vegetable Oil Products (Control) Order, the Edible Oils Packaging Order, the Milk and Milk Products Order and others — making **one integrated food law** ("One Nation, One Food Law").
- **Functions**: lays down **science-based standards** for food articles; regulates manufacture, storage, distribution, sale and **import**; **licensing and registration** of food businesses (FSSAI licence number on every pack); food **testing laboratories**; **surveillance** and enforcement; labelling and claims rules; consumer awareness (Eat Right India, food fortification "+F").
- Key regulations: **FSS (Licensing and Registration of Food Businesses) Regulations, 2011**; **FSS (Food Products Standards and Food Additives) Regulations, 2011**; **FSS (Packaging and Labelling) Regulations, 2011** (now replaced by labelling and display regulations); **FSS (Advertising and Claims) Regulations, 2018**; **FSS (Contaminants, Toxins and Residues) Regulations, 2011**.

#### FSSAI rules for nutraceuticals

- **2016 regulations**: the **FSS (Health Supplements, Nutraceuticals, Food for Special Dietary Use, Food for Special Medical Purpose, Functional Food and Novel Food) Regulations, 2016** were notified in the Gazette of India on 23 December 2016, and compliance and enforcement applied from 1 January 2018. They were amended later (a first amendment of 2021).
- **2022 regulations**: these rules were revised and consolidated as the **FSS (Health Supplements, Nutraceuticals, Food for Special Dietary Use, Food for Special Medical Purpose, and Prebiotic and Probiotic Food) Regulations, 2022**, which replace the 2016 set. Novel foods are handled separately, through FSSAI's rules for approval of non-specified foods and food ingredients. Always check the current text on fssai.gov.in, because FSSAI issues frequent amendments and directions.

**Categories covered** (in substance): health supplements; nutraceuticals; food for special dietary use (FSDU); food for special medical purpose (FSMP); foods with added probiotic ingredients; foods with added prebiotic ingredients; specialty foods containing plant or botanical ingredients.

**Main requirements:**

1. **Positive lists**: only the vitamins, minerals, amino acids, plants and botanicals (with the plant part and usage limits), nutraceutical ingredients, probiotic strains, prebiotics and additives listed in the **Schedules** of the regulations may be used; anything else needs **prior approval**.
2. **Nutrient limits**: vitamins and minerals added to health supplements and nutraceuticals must **not exceed the recommended dietary allowance (RDA)** set by the **Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR)**; where ICMR has set none, **Codex Alimentarius** standards apply.
3. **No disease claims**: labels and advertisements **must not claim to prevent, treat or cure a disease** — such products are drugs, controlled under the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**. Nutrient-function and health claims must be scientifically supported, and some claims need prior approval under the advertising and claims regulations.
4. **Not permitted**: hormones, steroids and psychotropic substances.
5. **Formats**: food formats are preferred, but tablets, capsules and syrups are allowed for these categories.
6. **Labelling** (in addition to general labelling rules): the **category name** of the product; the statement that it is **"NOT FOR MEDICINAL USE"**; recommended daily usage and a warning not to exceed it; **warnings, precautions, known side effects, contraindications and product–drug interactions**; for probiotics, the strain and the viable count; for FSDU/FSMP, whether medical advice is needed. FSMP products may not be advertised to the general public.
7. **Not for infants**: the rules do not cover foods for infants up to 24 months (separate infant nutrition rules apply).
8. **Licensing**: manufacturers need an FSSAI **central licence** for these product categories.

### B. FDA

- **United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA)** — regulates dietary supplements under the **Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA), 1994**. Supplements are treated as a category of **food**, not drugs: no pre-market approval of safety and efficacy is needed (except for **new dietary ingredients**, which require a pre-market notification); the manufacturer is responsible for safety and truthful labels; the FDA acts after a product is on the market if it is unsafe or misbranded. Labels carry a **"Supplement Facts"** panel, and **structure/function claims** must carry a disclaimer that the FDA has not evaluated the statement and that the product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure or prevent any disease. Manufacturers must follow **current Good Manufacturing Practice for dietary supplements** (21 CFR Part 111). The FDA also authorises certain health claims for foods.
- In India, **State Food and Drug Administrations** (with the Food Safety Commissioner in each state) enforce both the FSS Act and the Drugs and Cosmetics Act at state level — inspection, sampling and licensing.

### C. FPO — Fruit Products Order, 1955

- Issued under the **Essential Commodities Act, 1955**, administered earlier by the Ministry of Food Processing Industries.
- Made a **licence compulsory** for manufacturers of **fruit and vegetable products** — jams, jellies, squashes, juices, pickles, sauces, canned fruits and vegetables, vinegar.
- Laid down **minimum quality standards**, hygienic conditions of premises, water quality, permitted preservatives and colours, labelling.
- The familiar **"FPO" mark** with licence number appeared on packs.
- **Now repealed and merged into the FSS Act, 2006**; such products today carry an FSSAI licence number.

### D. MPO — Meat Food Products Order, 1973

- Also under the Essential Commodities Act; a **licence** was required to manufacture **meat food products** for sale.
- Controlled **hygiene of slaughter and processing**, ante- and post-mortem inspection, quality of raw meat, preservatives and additives, packaging and labelling.
- **Now subsumed under the FSS Act, 2006** (meat standards and hygiene are in FSSAI regulations).

### E. AGMARK

- Quality **certification mark for agricultural produce**, under the **Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act, 1937**, administered by the **Directorate of Marketing and Inspection (DMI)**, Ministry of Agriculture.
- **Voluntary** for most products (compulsory for some, and for certain exports and government purchases).
- Grades products (pulses, cereals, spices, edible oils, ghee, honey, butter, wheat flour, fruits and vegetables, etc.) by **quality grade designations** after testing in AGMARK laboratories.
- Gives the consumer assurance of **purity and quality**; still in force alongside FSSAI.

Other quality marks to know for comparison: **BIS (ISI mark)** for processed foods and packaged drinking water, **FSSAI licence**, **"+F" fortification logo**, **veg (green) / non-veg (brown) symbols**, **Jaivik Bharat** logo for organic food.

### F. HACCP — Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point

HACCP is a **preventive, science-based system** that identifies, evaluates and controls hazards that are significant for food safety, instead of relying only on testing of the final product. It was first developed for the US space programme food supply and is now recommended by the **Codex Alimentarius**.

**Hazards**: **biological** (bacteria, viruses, parasites, moulds), **chemical** (pesticide residues, heavy metals, mycotoxins, cleaning chemicals, allergens), **physical** (glass, metal, stones).

**Preliminary steps**: form the HACCP team; describe the product; identify intended use and consumers; draw a **flow diagram**; confirm it on site.

**The seven principles:**

| No. | Principle | Example (pasteurised probiotic drink) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Conduct a hazard analysis** | Pathogens in raw milk; metal pieces |
| 2 | **Determine the critical control points (CCPs)** — steps where control is essential | Pasteurisation; metal detection |
| 3 | **Establish critical limits** for each CCP | Minimum temperature–time combination for pasteurisation |
| 4 | **Establish monitoring procedures** | Continuous temperature recording |
| 5 | **Establish corrective actions** when a CCP is out of control | Re-pasteurise or discard the batch |
| 6 | **Establish verification procedures** | Calibration, record review, microbial testing |
| 7 | **Establish record-keeping and documentation** | Batch records, monitoring logs |

```text
Raw material → Receiving → Mixing → [CCP-1 Heat treatment] → Cooling
 → Inoculation with probiotic → Filling → [CCP-2 Metal detection] → Cold storage
```

**Benefits**: prevents problems rather than finding them late, reduces recalls, builds consumer confidence, meets export requirements; it is part of the **Food Safety Management System (FSMS)** required by FSSAI for many businesses and of ISO 22000.

### G. GMP on food safety

**Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)** — with **Good Hygienic Practices (GHP)** — are the basic **prerequisite programmes** on which HACCP is built. In India, the general hygienic and sanitary practices for food businesses are given in **Schedule 4** of the **FSS (Licensing and Registration of Food Businesses) Regulations, 2011**.

Main elements:

1. **Premises** — suitable location, design for easy cleaning, separation of raw and finished areas, ventilation, lighting, drainage, pest-proofing.
2. **Equipment** — food-grade materials, easy to clean, maintained and calibrated.
3. **Water** — potable water, tested regularly.
4. **Raw materials** — approved suppliers, inspection, proper storage (first in, first out).
5. **Personnel hygiene** — medical fitness, clean clothing, hand washing, no smoking or eating in production areas, training.
6. **Cleaning and sanitation** — written schedules, approved chemicals.
7. **Pest control** — regular programme, records.
8. **Process control** — written procedures, time–temperature control, prevention of cross-contamination and allergen control.
9. **Packaging and labelling** — correct, legal labels; batch number and dates.
10. **Storage and transport** — correct temperature, cold chain.
11. **Documentation and records** — batch records, traceability, **recall** procedures, complaint handling.
12. **Quality control** — testing of raw materials, in-process and finished products in a laboratory.

For supplements in tablet and capsule form, many manufacturers also follow pharmaceutical-style GMP, and in the US the specific dietary-supplement cGMP applies.

## 5.3 Adulteration of foods

**Adulteration** is the **addition or removal of any substance** to or from food, or any change, that **lowers its quality or makes it unsafe**, or that passes off a cheaper article as a better one. Under the FSS Act, food that is **unsafe**, **sub-standard** or **misbranded**, or that contains an **adulterant**, is an offence, with penalties that increase with the harm caused.

### Types

1. **Intentional** — deliberate addition of cheaper or harmful substances for profit (water in milk, colour in spices).
2. **Incidental** — accidental contamination: pesticide residues, insect fragments, stones, rodent hair, packaging migrants.
3. **Metallic contamination** — lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium from water, soil, equipment.
4. **Microbial** — spoilage and pathogens due to poor hygiene.
5. **Natural toxins** — mycotoxins (**aflatoxin** in groundnuts and maize), toxic seeds (argemone seeds in mustard).

### Common adulterants and simple detection

| Food | Adulterant | Simple test (principle) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Milk | Water | Lactometer reading falls; drop of pure milk on a slanted polished surface flows slowly leaving a trail |
| Milk | Starch | Add iodine solution → **blue** colour |
| Milk | Detergent | Shake with water → lasting foam |
| Ghee / butter | Vanaspati, mashed potato | Shake with hydrochloric acid and a little sugar → crimson colour, because vanaspati carries sesame oil (Baudouin-type test); iodine → blue for starch |
| Edible oils | **Argemone oil** (causes epidemic dropsy) | Nitric acid → red-brown colour |
| Turmeric powder | **Metanil yellow** (non-permitted colour) | Add hydrochloric acid → pink/violet colour |
| Chilli powder | Brick powder, artificial colour | Brick powder settles in water; colour streaks in water |
| Black pepper | **Papaya seeds** | Papaya seeds float in alcohol/water; shrivelled oval shape |
| Tea leaves | Used, coloured leaves | Colour leaches on wet filter paper |
| Coffee | Chicory, tamarind seed powder | Chicory sinks and colours water quickly |
| Honey | Sugar syrup, invert sugar | Pure honey does not disperse quickly in water; laboratory tests for added sugar |
| Sweets, silver leaf | Aluminium foil | Silver leaf melts to a bead on heating; aluminium leaves grey ash |
| Wheat flour | Sand, chalk | Sediment in water; chalk effervesces with acid |
| Pulses (arhar dal) | Kesari dal (*Lathyrus*) — lathyrism | Shape and laboratory tests |
| Green vegetables | Malachite green | Cotton soaked in liquid paraffin turns green when rubbed |

FSSAI publishes the **"DART" (Detect Adulteration with Rapid Test)** guide of household tests.

### Adulteration of nutraceuticals and herbal supplements

- **Undeclared drugs** added to "natural" products — sildenafil in sexual-enhancement products, sibutramine in slimming products, steroids in body-building and pain products, oral antidiabetics in "herbal" diabetes products.
- **Substitution** with a cheaper plant or a wrong species; **dilution** with fillers.
- **Fake standardisation** — adding synthetic marker compounds to meet the label claim.
- **Heavy metals and pesticide residues** in botanicals; **microbial contamination**.
- **Mislabelling** of probiotic strains or counts.

These risks are why **positive lists, licensing, GMP, testing and pharmacopoeial specifications** are needed.

## 5.4 Pharmacopoeial specifications for dietary supplements and nutraceuticals

Pharmacopoeias give **official standards** (monographs and general tests) for identity, strength, quality and purity. FSSAI requires listed nutraceutical ingredients to meet **purity criteria**, and manufacturers commonly use the **Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)**, the **United States Pharmacopeia–National Formulary (USP–NF)**, the British and European Pharmacopoeias, and Codex/food chemical standards for this.

### The USP approach to dietary supplements

The USP–NF has a separate **Dietary Supplements** section with monographs for vitamins, minerals, amino acids, botanicals (for example garlic, ginkgo, ginseng, green tea extract), probiotics and their dosage forms, and a group of **general chapters** written for supplements, including:

- disintegration and dissolution of dietary supplements (**USP <2040>**);
- weight variation of dietary supplements (**USP <2091>**);
- microbial enumeration tests, tests for absence of specified microorganisms, and microbial attributes for nutritional and dietary supplements (**USP <2021>, <2022>, <2023>**);
- manufacturing practices for dietary supplements (**USP <2750>**).

The USP also runs a voluntary **verification mark** programme for supplements that meet its standards. The **Indian Pharmacopoeia** contains monographs for vitamins, minerals, amino acids and a growing number of herbal drugs and extracts, together with the general tests applied to tablets, capsules and oral liquids.

### Typical specifications for a dietary supplement product

| Test | What it checks | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Description** | Appearance, colour, odour, taste | Orange, oblong, film-coated tablet |
| **Identification** | Right ingredient present | TLC/HPTLC fingerprint of a botanical; IR or HPLC retention time of a vitamin; microscopy of powdered herb |
| **Assay / content** | Amount of each active or marker against label claim (with permitted overage) | HPLC for vitamins, lycopene, curcuminoids, ginsenosides; titration for calcium; AAS/ICP for minerals |
| **Uniformity** | Each unit has the same amount | Weight variation; content uniformity |
| **Disintegration / dissolution** | Product releases its contents in the body | Tablets and capsules disintegrate within the set time; dissolution of key vitamins (e.g. folic acid, riboflavin) |
| **Moisture / loss on drying** | Water content (stability, microbes) | Karl Fischer or loss on drying |
| **Microbial limits** | Total aerobic count, yeast and mould count; absence of *E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Staphylococcus aureus* | Especially for botanicals and probiotics (probiotic products also need a **viable count**) |
| **Contaminants** | **Heavy metals** (lead, arsenic, cadmium, mercury), **pesticide residues**, **aflatoxins**, residual **solvents** | ICP-MS, GC, HPLC |
| **Adulterant screening** | Undeclared drugs, synthetic dyes | LC-MS |
| **Physical tests** | Hardness, friability (tablets); fill weight (capsules); pH and viscosity (liquids); particle size (powders) | As for pharmaceutical dosage forms |
| **Stability** | Content and quality over shelf life | Real-time and accelerated studies |

### Standardisation of botanicals

- **Marker-based standardisation**: extract adjusted to contain a fixed amount of a marker — ginkgo extract standardised to flavonol glycosides and terpene lactones; ginseng to ginsenosides; green tea to catechins/EGCG; curcumin to curcuminoids; garlic to alliin or allicin potential.
- **Fingerprinting**: HPTLC or HPLC fingerprint compared with a reference to confirm identity and consistency.
- **Botanical identity**: macroscopy, microscopy, DNA barcoding for substitution.
- Plant raw materials must also meet limits for **foreign matter, ash values, extractive values**.

## Remember

- Heat, oxygen, light, moisture, pH, metals, enzymes and time destroy nutraceuticals; vitamin C, folate, omega-3 oils, carotenoids, anthocyanins and probiotics are most sensitive. Cooking with oil increases lycopene absorption.
- Protect by antioxidants, chelators, stable forms, microencapsulation, light- and oxygen-barrier packs, desiccants, cool storage, overages and stability testing.
- FSSAI under the FSS Act 2006 (repealed PFA 1954, FPO, MPO etc.). Nutraceutical regulations: 2016 version (notified 23 December 2016, enforced from 1 January 2018), replaced by the 2022 regulations — positive lists, nutrients not above ICMR RDA, no disease claims, "NOT FOR MEDICINAL USE", warnings and interactions on label.
- US FDA: DSHEA 1994, supplements are foods, structure/function claims with disclaimer, 21 CFR Part 111 cGMP.
- FPO 1955 (fruit and vegetable products) and MPO 1973 (meat products) — licences under the Essential Commodities Act, now merged in the FSS Act; AGMARK — Agricultural Produce (Grading and Marking) Act 1937, DMI.
- HACCP seven principles: hazard analysis, CCPs, critical limits, monitoring, corrective action, verification, records. GMP/GHP = prerequisites (Schedule 4 of FSSAI licensing regulations).
- Adulteration: intentional, incidental, metallic, microbial, natural toxins; tests — iodine for starch, HCl for metanil yellow, nitric acid for argemone oil; supplements may hide sildenafil, sibutramine, steroids.
- Pharmacopoeial specs: identity, assay, uniformity, disintegration/dissolution, moisture, microbial limits, heavy metals, pesticides, aflatoxins; USP has a dietary supplement section (<2040>, <2091>, <2021>–<2023>, <2750>).
