# How to use this guide

Dietary Supplements and Nutraceuticals is **one of the semester-8 electives** of the PCI B.Pharm course. It has no practical; the whole subject is the 75-mark theory paper. It is a descriptive subject: marks come from clear definitions, classifications, tables of source–constituent–use, reaction schemes (free radical formation, lipid peroxidation, antioxidant enzymes) and short notes on laws. Study one unit a day: read the notes, write out each table and scheme once from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how likely that kind of question is.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus, with no splitting or joining. Two small points: the syllabus spells some names unusually — "Geebustin" is **genistein**, "Reservetrol" is **resveratrol**, "Lacto bacillum" is ***Lactobacillus*** and "leutin" is **lutein**; and the "FDA" in Unit 5 is covered both as the US FDA (dietary supplement law) and as India's state Food and Drug Administrations.

If you studied **BP603T Herbal Drug Technology** in semester 6, its Unit 2 (nutraceuticals, herbs as health foods, herb–drug interactions) is a good base. This elective goes much deeper into phytochemicals, free radicals, antioxidants and food law.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — a common question; prepare the full answer with a table or scheme.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common patterns in PCI-syllabus papers, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Begin every answer with a **definition** in one line, then a **classification**, then a **table**. Examiners in this paper reward neat tables (source → marker compound → chemical nature → use).
2. For each nutraceutical, learn the **four facts**: biological source with family, marker or active compound, its chemical class, and two or three uses. This one pattern answers most of Units 1 and 2.
3. Write **reactions with arrows** for the free radical topics: one-electron reduction of oxygen, the Fenton reaction, the three steps of lipid peroxidation, and the SOD–catalase–GPx scheme. A scheme alone earns a large part of the marks.
4. For disease questions in Unit 4 use one plan: source of extra ROS → what is damaged → result → antioxidant or dietary approach. Draw the ischaemia–reperfusion (xanthine oxidase) and oxidised-LDL schemes.
5. For laws, give the **full name, year, the authority that runs it, scope and two or three key provisions**. Do not invent dates: if you are unsure, write the name and the provision without the year.
6. Always add the **balanced view**: nutraceuticals support health but do not replace medicines; food law forbids disease claims; high-dose single antioxidants can be harmful (beta-carotene in smokers) — whole foods are preferred.
7. MCQs test small exact facts: DeFelice coined "nutraceutical" (1989); lycopene is acyclic and not provitamin A; GPx contains selenium; SOD forms Cu/Zn (cytosol) and Mn (mitochondria); •OH is the most reactive radical; H2O2 is not a radical; Harman (1956) proposed the free radical theory of ageing; FOS are β(2→1) fructans; BHT is 2,6-di-tert-butyl-4-methylphenol; HACCP has seven principles.
8. The subject gives no doses to learn. Where a question asks about limits, write that FSSAI does not allow vitamins and minerals in supplements above the ICMR recommended dietary allowance, rather than quoting numbers.
