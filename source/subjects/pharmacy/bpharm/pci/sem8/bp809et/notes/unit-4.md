# Unit 4: Principles of cosmetic evaluation; soaps and syndet bars

Claims such as "moisturises for 24 hours", "controls oil" or "makes hair 5 times stronger" must be backed by measurement. Cosmetic evaluation uses non-invasive instruments that measure skin oil, skin water, water loss, skin colour and hair mechanics on volunteers or on hair samples. The second part of the unit covers the oldest cleanser, soap, and its modern answer, the syndet bar.

## 4.1 Why and how cosmetics are evaluated

**Purposes of evaluation:**

1. Prove **efficacy claims** (moisturising, oil control, whitening, anti-ageing, conditioning).
2. Check **safety** (irritation, sensitisation) and **quality** (physical, chemical and microbial tests, see Unit 3).
3. Compare products and guide formulation changes.
4. Meet regulatory and advertising standards: claims must be truthful and supported.

**General rules for instrumental skin measurements (bioengineering methods):**

- Volunteers rest (acclimatise) for about 15–30 minutes before measurement.
- Room kept at controlled temperature (about 20–22 °C) and relative humidity (about 40–60%), because skin water and oil change with climate.
- Avoid products, washing, exercise, caffeine and smoking before the test as the protocol says.
- Measure the same marked sites at baseline and at set times after use; include an untreated control site and, where possible, a placebo product.
- Take several readings and analyse statistically.
- Volunteers give informed consent; studies follow ethical guidelines.

```text
 Typical claim study
 Recruit volunteers → baseline readings → apply product (and control) →
 readings at set times (1 h, 4 h, 24 h, 2 weeks…) → statistics → claim
```

## 4.2 Sebumeter: measurement of skin oil

**Purpose:** measures **casual sebum** (oil already on the skin surface) – for oily and dry skin typing, and claims of oil-control, mattifying and anti-acne products.

**Principle – photometric (grease-spot) method:**

- A special **opaque plastic tape** (about 0.1 mm thick) is pressed on the skin for a fixed time (about 30 seconds) with a constant pressure.
- Sebum soaks into the micro-pores of the tape and makes it **more transparent**, like an oil spot on paper.
- The tape is put into the device; light passes through it and a photocell measures the **transmission**.
- More light transmitted = more sebum. The result is shown in **µg of sebum per cm²**.

```text
 Light source → [ tape with sebum spot ] → photocell
                more sebum → more transparent → higher reading (µg/cm²)
```

**Uses:** classifying skin as oily, normal or dry; measuring sebum re-growth after washing; testing oil-control and anti-acne claims; comparing scalp oiliness for shampoos.

**Precautions:** clean, unwashed sites at a set time after washing; fixed contact time and pressure; avoid touching the tape; temperature affects sebum flow.

## 4.3 Corneometer: measurement of skin hydration

**Purpose:** measures the **water content of the stratum corneum** – the main test for moisturiser claims.

**Principle – capacitance measurement:**

- The probe head is a **capacitor**: two metal conductor tracks close together, covered by a thin glass layer so no current passes into the skin.
- When placed on the skin, the stratum corneum becomes the **dielectric** of the capacitor.
- **Water has a much higher dielectric constant (about 80) than other skin components** (below about 7), so the capacitance rises with the water content of the upper skin.
- The reading is in **arbitrary units (corneometer units)**; higher = better hydrated.
- The measurement takes about a second and measures only the upper skin depth, so it is not affected by deeper tissues; it is little affected by salts or residues because current does not flow through the skin.

**Uses:** baseline skin hydration; short-term (hours) and long-term (weeks) moisturiser efficacy; comparing humectants and emollients; dryness after cleansing products.

**Related method:** conductance or impedance meters (other devices) measure skin hydration electrically in a similar way.

## 4.4 Measurement of TEWL (transepidermal water loss)

**TEWL** is the amount of water that passes from inside the body through the epidermis and evaporates from the skin surface (not sweat). It is the main measure of **skin barrier function**: a damaged barrier (dry skin, dermatitis, after harsh surfactants or tape stripping) shows **higher TEWL**; occlusive and barrier-repair products lower it.

**Principle – open-chamber evaporimetry (Tewameter type):**

- A hollow open cylinder (probe) is held on the skin.
- Two pairs of sensors at different heights inside measure **temperature and relative humidity**.
- From these the device calculates the **water vapour pressure gradient** above the skin.
- By **Fick's law of diffusion**, the water flux is proportional to this gradient.
- Result: TEWL in **g/m²/h**.

```text
        open top
   ┌──────────────┐
   │  ● sensor 2  │  (temperature + RH)
   │              │   vapour pressure gradient ∝ water flux (Fick's law)
   │  ● sensor 1  │
   └──────────────┘
   ~~~~~~ skin ~~~~~~   water evaporating
```

**Other designs:** closed-chamber devices (measure the humidity rise in a closed cup) and condenser-chamber devices.

**Precautions:** draught-free room, controlled temperature and humidity, volunteer at rest and not sweating, probe held still and vertical, wait for readings to stabilise.

**Uses:** barrier damage by surfactants (comparing soap and syndet), efficacy of moisturisers and barrier creams, skin irritation testing, occlusivity of oils.

## 4.5 Measurement of skin colour

Skin colour depends mainly on **melanin** (brown) and **haemoglobin** (red). Measuring it supports claims for skin lightening, anti-tan, anti-redness, sunscreen (tanning protection) and self-tanning products, and is used in irritation testing (redness).

| Method | Principle | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Narrow-band reflectance meter (Mexameter type)** | Probe shines light of chosen wavelengths (green, red, infrared) on the skin; receiver measures reflected light. Melanin absorbs red strongly; haemoglobin absorbs green | **Melanin index** and **erythema index** |
| **Tristimulus colorimeter (Chromameter type) / spectrophotometer** | Measures reflected light across the visible range and converts it to the CIE **L\* a\* b\*** colour space | **L\*** = lightness (black to white), **a\*** = red–green (rises with redness), **b\*** = yellow–blue (rises with tan); the individual typology angle (ITA°) combines L\* and b\* to grade skin from very light to dark |
| Imaging and digital photography | Standardised lighting and analysis of photographs, UV photographs for hidden pigmentation | Spot area, evenness of tone |
| Visual grading | Trained graders with colour scales | Quick but subjective |

**Uses:** brightening and depigmenting creams, after-sun redness, sunscreen protection against tanning, irritation (erythema) scoring.

## 4.6 Hair tensile strength

**Purpose:** measures how strong hair is and how chemical treatments (bleaching, perming, straightening, dyeing) weaken it or how conditioners and bond-repair products protect it.

**Principle:** a single hair fibre is fixed between two clamps of a **tensile tester** (an extensometer with a load cell) and stretched at a constant rate until it breaks, usually wet or at controlled humidity. A **stress–strain (load–elongation) curve** is recorded.

```text
 Stress
   │                  ┌─── break
   │           ______/
   │  yield   /  post-yield region
   │ ______/   (keratin α-helix → β-sheet)
   │ /  Hookean (elastic) region
   └────────────────────────── Strain (elongation %)
```

**Regions of the curve:**

- **Hookean (elastic) region:** stress rises in proportion to strain; hair returns to its length when released.
- **Yield region:** large extension with little rise in stress as keratin helices unfold.
- **Post-yield region:** stress rises again until the fibre **breaks**.

**Parameters measured:** break stress (force at break, corrected for fibre cross-section), elongation at break, Young's modulus (stiffness), work to break. The **diameter** of each fibre is measured first (laser micrometer) because strength depends on cross-sectional area.

**Interpretation:** bleached or permed hair breaks at lower force (disulphide bonds damaged); a protective treatment should keep values close to untreated hair. Many fibres (often 50 or more) are tested because hair varies a lot.

**Related test – fatigue testing:** repeated small loads until the fibre breaks, closer to daily grooming stress.

## 4.7 Hair combing properties

**Purpose:** measures how easily hair can be combed, wet and dry – the key claim of conditioners and conditioning shampoos.

**Principle:** a standard **hair tress** (bundle of hair of fixed weight and length) is hung from a **load cell**; a comb is drawn through it at constant speed by an instrument. The force is recorded against distance.

- **Combing force** – peak force and the **total combing work** (area under the force–distance curve).
- Measured **before** and **after** treatment on the same tress.
- **Percentage reduction in combing work** = (before − after) ÷ before × 100. A higher reduction means better conditioning.

**Wet combing** shows detangling by conditioners (wet hair has more friction and tangles). **Dry combing** shows smoothness and is affected by static.

**Related hair tests:** shine (gloss meters, goniophotometry), friction (cuticle smoothness), static charge, curl retention for styling products, colour fastness for dyes, breakage counts during repeated combing, and sensory evaluation on half-head (salon) tests.

## 4.8 Soaps

**Soap** is the sodium or potassium salt of long-chain fatty acids, made by **saponification**: the hydrolysis of fats and oils (triglycerides) with alkali.

```text
Fat (triglyceride) + 3 NaOH  →  3 R–COONa (soap) + Glycerol
Sodium soaps → hard bars;  potassium soaps → soft or liquid soaps
```

**Evolution of soap:** soap-like materials made by heating fats with wood ash (alkali) are among the oldest cleansers known. Large-scale soap making grew with cheap industrial alkali, and in the 20th century came milled toilet soaps, transparent soaps and medicated soaps. Their high pH and poor behaviour in hard water led to the development of synthetic detergent bars.

**Raw materials:** fats and oils (tallow, palm oil, palm kernel and coconut oil – coconut gives quick lather, tallow and palm give hardness), alkali (NaOH, KOH), and additives: glycerin, perfume, colour, antioxidant, chelating agent (EDTA, to stop rancidity and spots), superfatting agents (extra oils for mildness), opacifier (titanium dioxide), medicated actives.

**Manufacture (outline):** saponification (full boiled or continuous process, or direct neutralisation of fatty acids) → separation of glycerol (salting out) → drying of soap to noodles → mixing with additives in an amalgamator → milling (roller mills) → plodding (extruding into a continuous bar) → cutting and stamping → packing.

**Types:** toilet soap, transparent soap (with glycerin, sugar, alcohol), medicated soap, baby soap, superfatted soap, laundry soap, liquid soap.

**Quality – total fatty matter (TFM):** TFM is the total fatty material (as fatty acids) obtained from a soap sample; it shows the quality of the soap. BIS grades toilet soaps: **Grade 1 – TFM at least 76%, Grade 2 – at least 70%, Grade 3 – at least 60%**. Other tests: free alkali, matter insoluble in alcohol, moisture and volatile matter, chloride, rancidity, lather, cracking and mush (sogginess) in water.

**Problems with soap:**

- **Alkaline pH (about 9–10)** – disturbs the skin's acid mantle, can swell the stratum corneum and remove lipids, causing dryness, tightness and irritation, especially in sensitive, dry or eczema-prone skin.
- **Hard water** – soap reacts with calcium and magnesium to form insoluble **soap scum**, which wastes soap and leaves deposits on skin and hair.
- High TEWL and reduced skin hydration after repeated use.

## 4.9 Syndet bars

A **syndet bar** (synthetic detergent bar) is a cleansing bar made mainly from **synthetic surfactants** instead of soap.

**Main surfactants:** sodium cocoyl isethionate (SCI, the most used), sodium lauryl sulphoacetate, disodium lauryl sulphosuccinate, alkyl glyceryl ether sulphonates, acyl glutamates; plus binders and plasticisers (stearic acid, fatty alcohols, paraffin, starch, dextrin), moisturising agents (stearic acid, glycerin, emollient oils), fillers, perfume, colour.

**Combars:** bars that combine soap and synthetic detergents (partly synthetic), giving properties between the two.

**Evolution and skin benefits:** synthetic detergents were developed in the 20th century to overcome soap's hard-water scum and high pH. Syndet bars followed as cleansing bars that clean well with less damage to the skin barrier. They are recommended by dermatologists for dry, sensitive, acne-prone and atopic skin and for babies.

| Point | Soap | Syndet bar |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Composition | Sodium salts of fatty acids | Synthetic surfactants (SCI etc.) |
| pH | Alkaline, about 9–10 | Near neutral or mildly acidic, about 5.5–7 |
| Hard water | Forms scum | No scum; lathers well |
| Effect on barrier | Removes lipids, higher TEWL, dryness | Milder, less lipid loss, lower TEWL |
| Irritation | More | Less |
| Manufacture | Simple, cheap | More difficult (needs binders and plasticisers) |
| Wear and mush | Bar lasts well | Can wear faster or turn mushy if poorly made |
| Cost | Low | Higher |
| Suits | Normal and oily skin | Dry, sensitive, acne-prone, baby, eczema-prone skin |

**Evaluation of bars:** pH of a 1% solution, lather volume and stability, cleansing, mildness (TEWL, corneometer readings, redness after repeated washing tests), rate of wear, mush, cracking, hardness, and, for soaps, TFM.

## Remember

- Instrumental tests need acclimatisation (15–30 min), controlled temperature and humidity, baseline and control sites.
- Sebumeter: opaque tape turns transparent with sebum; photometric; µg/cm².
- Corneometer: capacitance; water's dielectric constant about 80 vs below 7 for other substances; arbitrary units.
- TEWL: open-chamber evaporimeter, humidity gradient, Fick's law; g/m²/h; high TEWL = damaged barrier.
- Skin colour: Mexameter (melanin and erythema index); colorimeter (L\*a\*b\*, ITA°).
- Hair tensile: stress–strain curve (Hookean, yield, post-yield, break); combing: load cell, % reduction in combing work.
- Soap = saponification of fats with NaOH/KOH; pH 9–10; scum in hard water; BIS TFM grades 76/70/60%.
- Syndet = SCI-based bar, pH 5.5–7, milder, less TEWL; combars mix both.
