# Unit 1: Cosmetics, cosmeceuticals, cosmetic excipients and the biology of skin, hair and mouth

Cosmetic science is the study of products that clean, protect and improve the look of the skin, hair, nails, lips and teeth. A pharmacist needs it because cosmetics are made in the same way as creams, lotions and gels, use the same excipients, and are controlled by the same law in India, the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940. This unit gives the vocabulary for the rest of the subject: what a cosmetic is in law, how cosmeceuticals grew out of cosmetics, the excipients every formula is built from, and the basic biology of skin, hair and the mouth.

## 1.1 Definition of cosmetics as per Indian regulations

**Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940.** In Indian law a **cosmetic** is any article meant to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled or sprayed on, introduced into, or otherwise applied to the human body or any part of it, for **cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness or altering the appearance**. The definition also covers any article meant to be used as a **component** of a cosmetic.

Key points in the Indian definition:

- The test is the **intended use** (the purpose and the claims made), not the chemical nature of the product.
- The word "introduced into" covers products such as mouthwashes and toothpastes.
- Ingredients sold for making cosmetics are themselves cosmetics in law.
- A product that claims to **treat, cure or prevent a disease** or to affect a body function is a **drug**, not a cosmetic, and needs a drug licence.

**Cosmetics Rules, 2020.** Until 2020 cosmetics were controlled through parts of the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945. In December 2020 the Central Government notified separate **Cosmetics Rules, 2020** under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act. The main features students should know:

| Area | What the Rules cover |
| --- | --- |
| Authorities | The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO), headed by the Drugs Controller General of India, acts as the Central Licensing Authority (import registration, new cosmetics); State Licensing Authorities issue licences to manufacture |
| Import | Cosmetics must be registered before import |
| Manufacture | A licence to manufacture for sale; premises, plant and hygiene requirements; a competent technical person; testing of raw materials and finished products |
| New cosmetic | A cosmetic containing a new ingredient (not used before) needs permission from the Central Licensing Authority |
| Standards | Cosmetics must meet the Indian Standards of the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) where they exist; colours, preservatives and other ingredients must be from permitted lists and within limits |
| Labelling | Name of product, name and address of manufacturer, list of ingredients, batch number, date of manufacture, use-before date, net contents, directions and warnings where needed |
| Prohibitions | Misbranded, adulterated and spurious cosmetics; testing of cosmetics on animals (banned in India since 2014) and import of cosmetics tested on animals after that ban |

**Misbranded, adulterated and spurious cosmetics (in simple words):**

- **Misbranded:** coloured or packed to hide damage, or labelled falsely or not as the rules require.
- **Adulterated:** contains filthy or decomposed matter, prepared in insanitary conditions, has a non-permitted colour, or contains a harmful or toxic substance.
- **Spurious:** sold under another maker's name, imitates another product, or claims a manufacturer who does not exist.

Older textbooks refer to **Schedule M-II** (premises requirements for cosmetic factories) and **Schedule S** (BIS standards for cosmetics) of the 1945 Rules. These requirements now sit in the Cosmetics Rules, 2020.

## 1.2 Definition of cosmetics as per EU regulations

In the European Union cosmetics are controlled by **Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009**. A cosmetic product is any substance or mixture meant to be placed in contact with the **external parts of the body** (epidermis, hair, nails, lips, external genital organs) or with the **teeth and the lining of the mouth**, **only or mainly** to clean them, perfume them, change their appearance, protect them, keep them in good condition or correct body odour.

| Feature of EU law | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Responsible person | A named person in the EU is legally responsible for each product |
| Safety | A cosmetic product safety report (safety assessment) is required before sale |
| Notification | Every product is notified on a central EU portal before it is sold |
| Annexes | Lists of prohibited substances, restricted substances, permitted colourants, permitted preservatives and permitted UV filters |
| Animal testing | Testing finished cosmetics and ingredients on animals, and selling products so tested, is banned |
| Claims | Claims must be truthful and backed by evidence |

**Indian vs EU definition**

| Point | India (D&C Act, 1940) | EU (Regulation 1223/2009) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wording | Rubbed, poured, sprinkled, sprayed, introduced or applied to the body | Placed in contact with external parts, teeth and oral mucosa |
| Purpose | Cleansing, beautifying, promoting attractiveness, altering appearance | Cleaning, perfuming, changing appearance, protecting, keeping in good condition, correcting body odour |
| Components | Included in the definition | Raw materials controlled through annexes |
| Pre-market step | Import registration or manufacturing licence | Safety report and portal notification |

## 1.3 Evolution of cosmeceuticals from cosmetics

A **cosmeceutical** is a product that sits between a cosmetic and a drug: it is applied like a cosmetic but contains an active ingredient claimed to give a real biological benefit, such as fewer wrinkles, even skin tone or better skin barrier. The word joins *cosmetic* and *pharmaceutical*. It was popularised by the American dermatologist **Albert Kligman**.

**How cosmetics evolved into cosmeceuticals:**

```text
Ancient cosmetics      → colour and cover (kohl, henna, turmeric, oils, clay)
Modern cosmetics       → emulsions, soaps, shampoos, lipsticks; mainly appearance
Functional cosmetics   → sunscreens, antiperspirants, anti-dandruff, fluoride toothpaste
Cosmeceuticals         → "active" skin and hair care: retinoids, AHAs, vitamin C,
                         niacinamide, peptides, antioxidants, botanical actives
```

Reasons for the growth of cosmeceuticals:

1. Better understanding of skin biology (barrier, ageing, pigmentation).
2. Demand for products that show visible results against ageing and pigmentation.
3. New delivery systems (liposomes, niosomes, nanoemulsions, microsponges) that carry actives into the upper skin.
4. Ageing populations and higher spending on personal care.
5. Dermatologists and pharmacies recommending such products.

**Common cosmeceutical actives**

| Group | Examples | Claimed benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retinoids | Retinol, retinyl palmitate | Smoother skin, fewer fine lines |
| Hydroxy acids | Glycolic acid, lactic acid (AHAs); salicylic acid (BHA) | Exfoliation, brighter skin, clearer pores |
| Vitamins and antioxidants | Ascorbic acid and derivatives, tocopherol, niacinamide, green tea polyphenols | Protection from oxidative damage, even tone |
| Peptides | Signal and carrier peptides | Support of collagen |
| Skin lighteners | Kojic acid, arbutin, liquorice extract, niacinamide | Less hyperpigmentation |
| Moisturising actives | Ceramides, hyaluronic acid, urea | Barrier repair, hydration |

**Legal status:** "cosmeceutical" is **not a legal category** in India, the EU or the USA. A product is either a cosmetic or a drug depending on its ingredients and claims. If a cosmeceutical claims to treat a skin disease, it must be licensed as a drug.

## 1.4 Classification of cosmetic and cosmeceutical products

**By site of use**

| Site | Products |
| --- | --- |
| Skin | Creams, lotions, face washes, soaps, sunscreens, face packs, powders, foundations, deodorants and antiperspirants |
| Hair and scalp | Shampoos, conditioners, hair oils, hair dyes, hair setting products, depilatories |
| Nails | Nail polish, nail polish remover, cuticle softeners |
| Lips | Lipsticks, lip balms |
| Eyes | Kajal, eyeliner, mascara, eye shadow |
| Oral cavity | Toothpastes, tooth powders, mouthwashes, teeth whitening products |

**By function:** cleansing (soaps, face washes, shampoos); protective (sunscreens, barrier creams); moisturising (moisturisers, cold creams); decorative or colour (lipstick, kajal, foundation, hair dyes); deodorising (deodorants, antiperspirants); fragrance (perfumes, colognes); corrective or cosmeceutical (anti-ageing, anti-acne, skin lightening).

**By physical form:** solutions (toners, mouthwash), emulsions (creams, lotions), gels, suspensions, powders, sticks (lipstick, deodorant stick), pastes (toothpaste), aerosols and sprays, bars (soap, syndet).

**Cosmeceutical classification by action:** anti-ageing, depigmenting, anti-acne, moisturising and barrier-repair, photoprotective, hair growth and anti-hair-fall.

## 1.5 Cosmetics as quasi drugs and OTC drugs

Some products do more than a plain cosmetic but are still sold freely. Different countries handle them differently.

- **Quasi drugs (Japan):** Japanese law has a middle category, "quasi drugs" (medicated cosmetics). Examples include medicated anti-dandruff shampoos, skin whitening products, anti-acne products, deodorants and hair growth products. They contain approved active ingredients at set amounts and need approval before sale, but are less strictly controlled than medicines.
- **OTC drugs (USA):** The US Food and Drug Administration treats a product as a drug if it is meant to treat or prevent disease or affect body structure or function. So **sunscreens, antiperspirants, anti-dandruff shampoos, fluoride toothpastes and anti-acne products** are over-the-counter (OTC) drugs, often with a cosmetic side as well (a "cosmetic–drug"). They must follow OTC rules for actives, strength and labelling.
- **India:** there is no separate quasi-drug class. A product with a disease claim (for example, an anti-dandruff shampoo with ketoconazole or a medicated toothpaste for gum disease) is licensed as a drug; the same type of product without such claims, using cosmetic ingredients, is a cosmetic.

| Product | Treated as (typical) | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plain moisturiser | Cosmetic everywhere | Appearance and feel only |
| Sunscreen | Cosmetic in India and EU; OTC drug in USA | Prevents sunburn |
| Antiperspirant | Cosmetic in India and EU; OTC drug in USA; quasi drug in Japan | Reduces sweating (a body function) |
| Fluoride toothpaste | Cosmetic in India and EU; OTC drug in USA | Prevents caries |
| Ketoconazole shampoo | Drug | Treats a fungal condition |

## 1.6 Cosmetic excipients

Excipients are the "building blocks" from which every cosmetic is made. The syllabus names five groups.

### Surfactants

Surfactants (surface active agents) have a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail. They lower surface and interfacial tension and are used to clean, foam, emulsify, wet, solubilise perfume and condition.

| Class | Head group charge | Examples | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anionic | Negative | Sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS), sodium laureth sulphate (SLES), soaps (sodium stearate), sodium cocoyl isethionate | Main cleansing and foaming agents in shampoos, face washes, toothpastes, bars |
| Cationic | Positive | Cetrimonium chloride, behentrimonium chloride, benzalkonium chloride | Hair conditioners (attach to negatively charged hair), antimicrobials |
| Amphoteric | Both (depends on pH) | Cocamidopropyl betaine, sodium lauroamphoacetate | Mild co-surfactants, baby shampoos, foam boosting |
| Non-ionic | No charge | Polysorbates, sorbitan esters, polyethylene glycol ethers of fatty alcohols, alkyl polyglucosides, cocamide DEA | Emulsifiers in creams, solubilisers for perfume, mild cleansers |

Choice depends on the job: anionics clean strongly but can irritate; amphoterics and non-ionics are milder; cationics condition but do not foam well. The **HLB** (hydrophilic–lipophilic balance) guides emulsifier choice: low HLB (about 3–6) for w/o creams, high HLB (about 8–18) for o/w creams and solubilisers.

### Rheology modifiers (thickeners)

They control viscosity, flow and stability (keeping particles and droplets suspended).

| Type | Examples | Used in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural gums | Xanthan gum, guar gum, carrageenan, gum acacia | Lotions, toothpastes |
| Cellulose derivatives | Sodium carboxymethyl cellulose, hydroxyethyl cellulose, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose | Toothpaste, gels, shampoos |
| Synthetic polymers | Carbomers (crosslinked polyacrylic acid, thicken when neutralised) | Clear gels, face washes, lotions |
| Inorganic clays | Bentonite, magnesium aluminium silicate | Face packs, suspensions |
| Salt | Sodium chloride | Thickens shampoos based on ether sulphates |
| Fatty alcohols and waxes | Cetyl and cetostearyl alcohol, beeswax | Body for creams, sticks |

### Humectants

Humectants are hygroscopic substances that attract and hold water, both in the product (stop it drying out) and in the skin.

Examples: glycerin, sorbitol, propylene glycol, butylene glycol, sodium lactate, sodium PCA (pyrrolidone carboxylic acid, part of the skin's natural moisturising factor), urea, hyaluronic acid, honey. Limitation: in very dry air a humectant can pull water from the deeper skin, so it is usually combined with an occlusive or emollient.

### Emollients

Emollients soften and smooth the skin by filling gaps between surface cells and forming a thin film. Some also act as occlusives that reduce water loss.

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Hydrocarbons | Liquid paraffin (mineral oil), petrolatum, squalane |
| Vegetable oils and butters | Almond, coconut, olive, sesame oils; shea and cocoa butter |
| Esters | Isopropyl myristate, isopropyl palmitate, caprylic/capric triglyceride |
| Fatty alcohols and acids | Cetyl alcohol, stearyl alcohol, stearic acid |
| Silicones | Dimethicone, cyclomethicone |
| Lanolin and derivatives | Wool fat, lanolin alcohols |
| Waxes | Beeswax, carnauba wax |

### Preservatives

Most cosmetics contain water, nutrients and are opened many times, so they need preservatives against bacteria, yeast and mould.

| Preservative | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| Parabens (methyl, propyl) | Broad action, used together; methyl mainly against bacteria, propyl against fungi |
| Phenoxyethanol | Widely used, often with a booster |
| Benzoic acid / sodium benzoate, sorbic acid / potassium sorbate | Work best at acidic pH |
| Formaldehyde releasers (DMDM hydantoin, imidazolidinyl urea) | Effective; can sensitise some users |
| Isothiazolinones | Very active at low levels; restricted because of skin allergy |
| Benzyl alcohol, dehydroacetic acid | Milder options |

**Ideal preservative:** broad spectrum, effective at low concentration, stable at product pH, compatible with other ingredients, non-irritant, no colour or smell, partitions enough into the water phase where microbes grow. Its effect is checked by a **preservative efficacy (challenge) test**.

Other excipients (antioxidants such as BHT and tocopherol, chelating agents such as disodium EDTA, pH adjusters, colours, perfumes) are covered with the products in Unit 2.

## 1.7 Skin: basic structure and function

The skin is the largest organ of the body. It has three layers.

```text
           Surface (stratum corneum, acid mantle pH about 4.5–5.5)
 ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ EPIDERMIS  (no blood vessels)                          │
 │   stratum corneum   – flat dead cells (corneocytes)    │
 │   stratum lucidum   – only in palms and soles          │
 │   stratum granulosum– granules, lipids made            │
 │   stratum spinosum  – "prickle" cells                   │
 │   stratum basale    – dividing cells, melanocytes      │
 ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ DERMIS  papillary + reticular layers                   │
 │   collagen, elastin, fibroblasts, blood vessels,       │
 │   nerves, hair follicles, sebaceous and sweat glands   │
 ├────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ HYPODERMIS (subcutis)  fat cells, larger vessels       │
 └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Epidermis.** A stratified squamous epithelium. Cells (keratinocytes) are formed in the basal layer and move up, filling with keratin and losing their nuclei, until they become flat dead **corneocytes** that are shed. The journey takes roughly four weeks. The stratum corneum is the main **barrier**: often pictured as "bricks and mortar", with corneocytes as bricks and a lipid mortar of ceramides, cholesterol and free fatty acids between them. It also holds the **natural moisturising factor (NMF)** (amino acids, PCA, lactate, urea) that keeps it supple. **Melanocytes** in the basal layer make **melanin**, which colours the skin and absorbs UV light. Langerhans cells take part in immune defence.

**Dermis.** Gives strength and elasticity through **collagen** (strength) and **elastin** (recoil) fibres in a gel of glycosaminoglycans such as hyaluronic acid, made by fibroblasts. Loss of these fibres with age and sunlight causes wrinkles.

**Hypodermis.** Fat that insulates, cushions and stores energy.

**Appendages:**

- **Sebaceous glands** – open into hair follicles; secrete **sebum** (triglycerides, wax esters, squalene, free fatty acids), which lubricates skin and hair. Most active on face, scalp, chest and back.
- **Eccrine sweat glands** – all over the body; watery sweat for cooling.
- **Apocrine sweat glands** – armpits and groin; become active at puberty; their secretion is broken down by bacteria to cause body odour.
- **Hair follicles** and **nails**.

**Functions of skin:** protection (mechanical, chemical, microbial, UV), barrier to water loss, temperature control (sweating, blood flow), sensation (touch, pain, heat), vitamin D synthesis, excretion (sweat), immune defence, absorption of some substances, and appearance and communication.

## 1.8 Hair: basic structure and growth cycle

A hair has a **shaft** (above the skin) and a **root** in the follicle, ending in the bulb around the **dermal papilla**, which supplies nutrients. The sebaceous gland and the arrector pili muscle are attached to the follicle.

```text
 Cross-section of a hair shaft
   ┌──────────────── cuticle (overlapping flat scales, protective)
   │  ┌───────────── cortex (long keratin cells, melanin; gives strength)
   │  │   ┌───────── medulla (loose central core; may be absent in fine hair)
   ○  ◎   •
```

- **Cuticle:** 6–10 layers of overlapping scales pointing to the tip; when flat and smooth, hair shines and combs easily. Damaged cuticle (by bleaching, heat, harsh shampoo) makes hair dull, rough and tangled.
- **Cortex:** the main bulk; long cells of **keratin**, a protein rich in cystine. Its strength comes from **disulphide bonds** (broken by perming and depilatory chemicals), **hydrogen bonds** (broken by water, reformed on drying — the basis of setting) and **salt (ionic) bonds** (affected by pH). The cortex holds the **melanin** that gives hair its colour.
- **Medulla:** a soft central core.
- Hair is weakly negatively charged at normal pH, which is why cationic conditioners stick to it.

**Hair growth cycle.** Each follicle cycles on its own, so hairs are not shed together.

| Phase | What happens | Approximate length | Share of scalp hairs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Anagen (growth) | Active growth from the bulb | 2–7 years on the scalp | About 85–90% |
| Catagen (transition) | Growth stops, follicle shrinks | About 2–3 weeks | About 1% |
| Telogen (resting) | Hair rests, then a new hair pushes it out | About 3 months | About 10–15% |
| Exogen (shedding) | The old hair falls | – | – |

Scalp hair grows about 1 cm a month. Shedding of up to about 100 hairs a day is usually normal. Hair fall disorders (Unit 5) often come from a change in this cycle, for example many hairs moving into telogen at once after illness or stress.

## 1.9 Oral cavity: common problems of teeth and gums

A tooth has **enamel** (hardest tissue, mainly hydroxyapatite) over **dentine** (with tiny tubules leading to the pulp), and a **pulp** with nerves and vessels. The gums (gingiva) cover the bone around the teeth.

| Problem | What it is | Cosmetic / oral care approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dental plaque | Sticky film of bacteria on teeth | Brushing, abrasives, antiplaque agents |
| Dental caries | Acids from bacterial fermentation of sugars dissolve enamel, causing cavities | Fluoride toothpaste, less sugar |
| Calculus (tartar) | Hardened (mineralised) plaque | Anti-tartar agents (pyrophosphates, zinc salts); scaling by a dentist |
| Gingivitis | Red, swollen gums that bleed on brushing, caused by plaque | Antiplaque and antibacterial toothpastes and mouthwashes |
| Periodontitis | Gum disease spreading to the bone, loosening teeth | Needs dental treatment |
| Dentine hypersensitivity | Sharp pain to cold, hot or sweet when dentine is exposed (worn enamel or receding gums) | Desensitising toothpastes |
| Tooth discolouration | Extrinsic stains (tea, coffee, tobacco) or intrinsic changes | Whitening toothpastes and bleaching |
| Halitosis | Bad breath, mainly from sulphur compounds made by bacteria on the tongue | Tongue cleaning, mouthwash |
| Mouth ulcers, dry mouth | Soft tissue problems | Mild mouthwashes; medical advice if persistent |

## Remember

- Indian definition: articles applied to the body to cleanse, beautify, promote attractiveness or alter appearance, including components (Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940).
- Cosmetics Rules, 2020 (December 2020) now control import, manufacture, labelling and sale of cosmetics; CDSCO is the Central Licensing Authority; states licence manufacture.
- EU: Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009; safety report, responsible person, notification, annexes, animal testing ban.
- A disease claim makes a product a drug. "Cosmeceutical" is not a legal category; the term was popularised by Kligman.
- Quasi drug = Japanese category; sunscreens, antiperspirants, anti-dandruff shampoos and fluoride toothpastes are OTC drugs in the USA.
- Five excipient groups: surfactants (anionic, cationic, amphoteric, non-ionic), rheology modifiers, humectants, emollients, preservatives.
- Skin: epidermis (5 layers, stratum corneum barrier, melanocytes), dermis (collagen, elastin, glands), hypodermis; acid mantle pH about 4.5–5.5.
- Hair: cuticle, cortex, medulla; keratin with disulphide, hydrogen and salt bonds; anagen → catagen → telogen → exogen.
- Oral problems: plaque, caries, calculus, gingivitis, periodontitis, hypersensitivity, stains, halitosis.
