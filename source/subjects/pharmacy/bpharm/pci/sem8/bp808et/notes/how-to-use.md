# How to use this guide

Cell and Molecular Biology is **one of the semester-8 electives** of the PCI B.Pharm course. It has no practical; the whole subject is the 75-mark theory paper. The subject is descriptive: there are almost no calculations, and marks come from clear, well-labelled diagrams, step-by-step mechanisms and comparison tables. Study one unit a day: read the notes, draw each diagram once from memory (double helix, replication fork, tRNA cloverleaf, lac operon, cell cycle, apoptosis pathways, G protein cycle, kinase cascade), then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus papers.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus. A few textbook topics that the syllabus words point to but do not name are placed where they fit best: DNA replication, repair and mutation are under "DNA functioning" in Unit 2; gene regulation (lac and trp operons) is under "positive control of protein synthesis" in Unit 3; and apoptosis is with the cell cycle checkpoints in Unit 4.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with a labelled diagram and a table.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Topics that keep coming back |

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start each answer with a one-line definition, then a labelled diagram, then the steps in numbered order. Mechanism questions (replication, transcription, translation, signalling) are marked step by step.
2. Use comparison tables with at least six points for the classic "differentiate" questions: prokaryote vs eukaryote, DNA vs RNA, mitosis vs meiosis, apoptosis vs necrosis, prokaryotic vs eukaryotic transcription or translation, negative vs positive control.
3. Name the enzyme or factor at every step (helicase, primase, pol III, ligase; sigma factor; EF-Tu, EF-G; caspase-9, caspase-3). Missing names cost the most marks in this paper.
4. Always end with pharmaceutical relevance: antibiotics acting on bacterial replication, transcription and ribosomes; anticancer drugs acting on the cell cycle; kinase inhibitors; recombinant medicines. This links the elective to your degree and examiners reward it.
5. Learn the few exact facts that MCQs test: 3.4 nm pitch and about 10 bp per turn of B-DNA; A=T two and G≡C three hydrogen bonds; −10 TATAAT and −35 TTGACA; start AUG and stops UAA, UAG, UGA; 70S = 50S + 30S, 80S = 60S + 40S; α-helix 3.6 residues per turn; Na+/K+-ATPase 3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in; PCR temperatures.
6. Spell the history correctly with the right decade (Hooke 1665, Watson and Crick 1953, Meselson–Stahl 1958, Jacob–Monod 1961, Singer–Nicolson 1972). If you are not sure of a year, write the scientist and the finding without the date.
7. In signalling answers, draw the pathway as a chain of arrows from receptor to response and show how it is switched off; this alone earns a large part of the marks.
