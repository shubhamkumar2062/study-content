# Unit 1: Cells, membranes and the chemical foundations of life

This unit covers the first block of the syllabus: what cell and molecular biology is, its history, the properties of cells and the cell membrane, prokaryotic versus eukaryotic cells, how cells reproduce, and the chemistry that every cell is built on.

## 1.1 Definitions, basic theory and applications

**Cell biology** is the study of cells: their structure, the organelles inside them, how they interact with their surroundings, how they grow, divide and die.

**Molecular biology** is the study of life at the level of molecules, mainly DNA, RNA and proteins: how genetic information is stored, copied, read and controlled.

**Cell and molecular biology** joins the two. It explains what a cell does (cell biology) by the molecules that do it (molecular biology). For example, cell division is a cell event, but it is driven by cyclin proteins, kinases and the copying of DNA.

**The cell theory** (the basic theory of the subject):

1. All living organisms are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function of life.
3. New cells arise only from cells that already exist.

Modern additions: cells carry hereditary information (DNA) that passes to daughter cells; all cells have broadly the same chemical make-up; energy flow (metabolism) happens inside cells.

**The central dogma** (the basic theory of molecular biology): genetic information flows DNA → RNA → protein. DNA copies itself (replication), DNA is copied into RNA (transcription) and RNA is read to make protein (translation). Exceptions: retroviruses copy RNA back into DNA (reverse transcription) and some viruses copy RNA from RNA.

**Applications in pharmacy and medicine**

| Area | How cell and molecular biology is used | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug targets | Drugs act on receptors, enzymes, ion channels and transporters identified by molecular methods | Kinase inhibitors such as imatinib |
| Recombinant proteins | Human genes expressed in bacteria, yeast or animal cells | Human insulin, growth hormone, erythropoietin |
| Monoclonal antibodies | Hybridoma and recombinant cell technology | Trastuzumab, rituximab |
| Vaccines | Recombinant antigens and mRNA vaccines | Hepatitis B surface antigen vaccine |
| Diagnosis | Detecting genes and pathogens by PCR and probes | PCR test for infections, genetic screening |
| Gene therapy | Delivering a working gene into patient cells | Viral vectors for inherited disorders |
| Pharmacogenomics | Matching drug and dose to a patient's genes | CYP2C9 and VKORC1 variants and warfarin response |
| Toxicology and cancer | Understanding mutation, cell cycle loss of control, apoptosis | Anticancer drugs acting on dividing cells |

**Summation (the main lessons of the history):** the cell is the unit of life; heredity is carried by DNA; DNA is a double helix copied semiconservatively; genes are read through RNA into protein by a universal code; gene activity is regulated; and DNA can now be cut, joined, copied and read, which created biotechnology.

## 1.3 Properties of cells

All cells, from bacteria to human neurons, share these properties:

1. **Highly complex and organised** — molecules are arranged into organelles and structures in a fixed pattern.
2. **Carry a genetic program** — genes in DNA hold the instructions to build and run the cell.
3. **Reproduce** — one cell divides into two, passing a copy of the DNA to each.
4. **Acquire and use energy** — from light (plants) or food (animals); energy is stored as ATP.
5. **Carry out chemical reactions** — metabolism, speeded up by enzymes.
6. **Perform mechanical activities** — moving materials, contraction, beating of cilia and flagella, chromosome movement.
7. **Respond to stimuli** — through receptors on the surface or inside the cell.
8. **Self-regulate** — feedback keeps the internal state steady (homeostasis); damage is repaired.
9. **Evolve** — genes change over generations.

**Cell size.** Most bacteria are about 1–5 µm; most animal cells about 10–30 µm. Cells stay small because a small cell has a large surface area for its volume, which allows fast exchange of nutrients and wastes.

## 1.4 The cell membrane (plasma membrane)

The plasma membrane is the thin boundary (about 7–10 nm thick) that separates the cell from its surroundings and controls what enters and leaves.

### Fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972)

The membrane is a **fluid lipid bilayer** in which **proteins float like icebergs in a sea of lipid**. "Fluid" because lipids and many proteins move sideways; "mosaic" because many different proteins are scattered in it.

```text
        Outside the cell (extracellular fluid)
     glycoprotein      glycolipid
         |  sugar chains  |
   ooooooo|oooooooooooooooo|ooooooo   <- polar heads (hydrophilic)
   ||||||[==== integral ====]||||||   <- fatty acid tails (hydrophobic core)
   ||||||[==== protein  ====]||||||
   ooooooooooooooo[peripheral]ooooo   <- polar heads
                   protein
        Inside the cell (cytoplasm)
```

### Membrane components

| Component | Description | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phospholipids | Phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylserine, sphingomyelin; amphipathic (polar head + two fatty acid tails) | Form the bilayer; barrier to polar molecules |
| Cholesterol | Sits between phospholipids in animal membranes | Controls fluidity — stiffens at high temperature, keeps fluid at low temperature |
| Glycolipids | Lipids with sugar chains, outer face only | Cell recognition, blood group antigens |
| Integral (intrinsic) proteins | Pass through the bilayer (transmembrane) | Channels, carriers, receptors |
| Peripheral (extrinsic) proteins | Attached to the surface or to integral proteins | Enzymes, links to cytoskeleton |
| Lipid-anchored proteins | Held by a covalently attached lipid | Signalling proteins such as Ras |
| Carbohydrates | Attached to proteins and lipids on the outer face — the glycocalyx | Recognition, adhesion, protection |

**Membrane asymmetry:** the two layers differ. Phosphatidylserine faces the inside; its appearance on the outside is a signal of apoptosis. Sugar chains face only outward.

**Membrane fluidity** rises with shorter and unsaturated (kinked) fatty acid chains and with higher temperature; cholesterol buffers the change.

### Functions of the plasma membrane

1. Boundary and selective permeability barrier.
2. Transport of solutes and ions.
3. Receiving signals (receptors) and passing them inside.
4. Cell-to-cell recognition and adhesion (junctions).
5. Holding enzymes and anchoring the cytoskeleton.
6. Keeping ion gradients and the membrane potential.

### Transport across the membrane

| Type | Energy | Carrier | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simple diffusion | None (down gradient) | No | O2, CO2, small lipid-soluble drugs |
| Facilitated diffusion — channel | None | Channel protein | K+ channels, aquaporins for water |
| Facilitated diffusion — carrier | None | Carrier, shows saturation | GLUT glucose transporters |
| Primary active transport | ATP directly | Pump | Na+/K+-ATPase (3 Na+ out, 2 K+ in per ATP) |
| Secondary active transport | Ion gradient made by a pump | Symporter or antiporter | Na+–glucose symport (SGLT) in gut and kidney; Na+/Ca2+ exchanger |
| Endocytosis | ATP | Vesicles | Phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated uptake of LDL |
| Exocytosis | ATP | Vesicles | Release of hormones and neurotransmitters |

Most drugs cross membranes by passive diffusion of the un-ionised form; some use carriers (levodopa uses an amino acid carrier) or are pumped out by efflux pumps such as P-glycoprotein, which causes drug resistance in cancer cells.

## 1.5 Prokaryotic versus eukaryotic cells

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | Bacteria, archaea | Protozoa, fungi, plants, animals |
| Size | Usually 1–10 µm | Usually 10–100 µm |
| Nucleus | No true nucleus; DNA in a nucleoid region | True nucleus with a double nuclear envelope |
| DNA | Usually one circular chromosome; plasmids common; few or no histones | Several linear chromosomes wound on histones (chromatin) |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Present: mitochondria, ER, Golgi, lysosomes, chloroplasts in plants |
| Ribosomes | 70S (50S + 30S) | 80S in cytoplasm (60S + 40S); 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts |
| Cell wall | Usually present; peptidoglycan in bacteria | Absent in animals; cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi |
| Cytoskeleton | Simple protein homologues | Well developed: microfilaments, microtubules, intermediate filaments |
| Cell division | Binary fission | Mitosis and meiosis |
| Gene structure | Introns rare; genes often in operons | Introns common; one gene per mRNA usually |
| Transcription and translation | Coupled — both in cytoplasm at the same time | Separated — transcription in nucleus, translation in cytoplasm |
| Flagella | Simple, made of flagellin, rotate | Complex 9 + 2 microtubule structure, whip-like |

**Pharmaceutical importance:** antibiotics act on features found only in prokaryotes — peptidoglycan cell wall (penicillins), 70S ribosomes (tetracyclines act on 30S, macrolides and chloramphenicol on 50S), bacterial DNA gyrase (fluoroquinolones). This selective toxicity is possible because of the differences in the table.

### Main eukaryotic organelles

| Organelle | Structure | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nucleus | Double envelope with nuclear pores; nucleolus inside | Stores DNA; site of replication and transcription; nucleolus makes rRNA and ribosome subunits |
| Rough ER | Membranes studded with ribosomes | Synthesis of secretory and membrane proteins |
| Smooth ER | No ribosomes | Lipid synthesis, drug detoxification (cytochrome P450 in liver), Ca2+ storage |
| Golgi apparatus | Stacked flattened sacs (cis and trans faces) | Modifies (glycosylation), sorts and packs proteins into vesicles |
| Lysosomes | Membrane sacs with acid hydrolases (pH about 5) | Digestion of worn-out parts and engulfed material |
| Mitochondria | Double membrane; inner folded into cristae; own DNA and 70S-type ribosomes | ATP by oxidative phosphorylation; also controls apoptosis |
| Peroxisomes | Single membrane, contain catalase and oxidases | Break down fatty acids and hydrogen peroxide |
| Ribosomes | rRNA + protein, free or on ER | Protein synthesis |
| Cytoskeleton | Microfilaments (actin, about 7 nm), intermediate filaments (about 10 nm), microtubules (tubulin, about 25 nm) | Shape, movement, transport, chromosome separation |
| Centrosome | Two centrioles (animal cells) | Organises microtubules and the mitotic spindle |

## 1.6 Cellular reproduction

Cells multiply by dividing. Before dividing, the cell copies its DNA and then shares it equally.

**In prokaryotes — binary fission:**

1. The circular chromosome is copied from a single origin.
2. The two copies attach to the membrane and move apart as the cell grows.
3. A septum forms in the middle and the cell splits into two identical cells.

Under good conditions *E. coli* can divide about every 20 minutes.

**In eukaryotes — the cell cycle:**

```text
   G1 (growth) → S (DNA synthesis) → G2 (preparation) → M (mitosis + cytokinesis)
     ^                                                         |
     |_________________________________________________________|
   Cells that stop dividing leave G1 and enter G0 (resting)
```

- **Mitosis** gives two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent (diploid → diploid). It is used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
- **Meiosis** gives four cells with half the chromosome number (diploid → haploid gametes) and shuffles genes. It is used for sexual reproduction.

The phases and checkpoints of the cycle are covered in detail in Unit 4.

## 1.7 Chemical foundations of the cell

### Elements and water

About 99% of the mass of a cell is made of hydrogen, oxygen, carbon and nitrogen; phosphorus and sulphur are also important. **Water** makes up about 70% of cell mass. Water is polar, forms hydrogen bonds, dissolves ions and polar molecules, has a high specific heat (keeps temperature steady) and drives the **hydrophobic effect** — non-polar groups cluster together to avoid water, which is why membranes form and proteins fold.

### Chemical bonds in the cell

| Bond | Strength | Where it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covalent | Strong (sharing of electrons) | Backbone of all biomolecules; peptide and phosphodiester bonds |
| Ionic | Weak in water | Salt bridges between charged amino acids |
| Hydrogen bond | Weak | DNA base pairing, protein secondary structure |
| Van der Waals forces | Very weak, short range | Close packing inside proteins; drug–receptor fit |
| Hydrophobic interactions | Weak, entropy-driven | Lipid bilayer, protein folding |

Many weak bonds together make a structure stable but easy to change, which is exactly what living molecules need (DNA strands can be separated; drugs bind and leave receptors).

### The four classes of biomolecules

| Class | Building block (monomer) | Linking bond | Main roles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Monosaccharides (glucose) | Glycosidic bond | Energy (glucose, glycogen), structure (cellulose), recognition |
| Lipids | Fatty acids and glycerol (not true polymers) | Ester bond | Membranes, energy store, hormones (steroids) |
| Proteins | 20 amino acids | Peptide bond | Enzymes, structure, transport, receptors, antibodies |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotides | Phosphodiester bond | Store and transfer genetic information |

Large molecules are built by **condensation (dehydration) reactions**, which remove water, and broken down by **hydrolysis**, which adds water.

### Types of biochemical reactions

1. **Oxidation–reduction (redox)** — transfer of electrons, often with hydrogen; carriers NAD+/NADH and FAD/FADH2. Example: oxidation of glucose to CO2.
2. **Group transfer** — moving a chemical group from one molecule to another, e.g. phosphate transfer from ATP by kinases.
3. **Hydrolysis** — breaking a bond with water, e.g. digestion of proteins by proteases.
4. **Condensation** — joining molecules with release of water, e.g. peptide bond formation.
5. **Isomerisation (rearrangement)** — atoms rearranged in the same molecule, e.g. glucose 6-phosphate ⇌ fructose 6-phosphate.
6. **Making or breaking carbon–carbon bonds** — e.g. aldolase cleavage in glycolysis.
7. **Elimination and addition** — removing or adding groups across a double bond, e.g. water removal by dehydratases.

### Energy, ATP and enzymes

- **Free energy change (ΔG)** tells whether a reaction can occur on its own. ΔG negative = **exergonic** (spontaneous, releases energy); ΔG positive = **endergonic** (needs energy input).
- Cells drive endergonic reactions by **coupling** them to ATP hydrolysis: ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi, ΔG°′ about −30.5 kJ/mol (−7.3 kcal/mol).
- **Enzymes** are biological catalysts, mostly proteins (some are RNA — ribozymes). They lower the **activation energy**, speed reactions many-fold, are specific for their substrate and are not used up. They do not change ΔG or the equilibrium position.
- **Michaelis–Menten equation:** v = Vmax [S] / (Km + [S]). Km is the substrate concentration giving half Vmax; a low Km means high affinity.
- Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors: competitive inhibitors raise Km (Vmax unchanged); non-competitive inhibitors lower Vmax (Km unchanged).

## Remember

- Cell theory: all organisms are cells; the cell is the unit of life; cells come only from cells (Virchow).
- Central dogma: DNA → RNA → protein; reverse transcriptase is the main exception.
- Watson and Crick 1953 (double helix); Meselson–Stahl 1958 (semiconservative); Jacob–Monod 1961 (operon); Singer–Nicolson 1972 (fluid mosaic); Mullis 1983 (PCR).
- Membrane = fluid lipid bilayer + proteins + cholesterol + glycocalyx; about 7–10 nm thick.
- Na+/K+-ATPase moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in per ATP.
- Prokaryotes: no nucleus, 70S ribosomes, circular DNA, peptidoglycan wall, binary fission. Eukaryotes: nucleus, 80S ribosomes, linear chromosomes on histones, organelles.
- Weak bonds (hydrogen, ionic, van der Waals, hydrophobic) shape biomolecules; covalent bonds form their backbones.
- ΔG negative = exergonic; enzymes lower activation energy but not ΔG.
