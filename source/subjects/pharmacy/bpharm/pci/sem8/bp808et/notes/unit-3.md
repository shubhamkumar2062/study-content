# Unit 3: Proteins, protein synthesis and gene regulation

This unit covers proteins and amino acids, protein structure, the regular pathways a protein follows from synthesis to destruction, the cellular processes proteins carry out, and how cells control protein synthesis — including positive control of genes — and why protein synthesis matters.

## 3.1 Proteins defined

**Proteins** are large polymers of **amino acids** joined by **peptide bonds**, each folded into a definite three-dimensional shape that decides its function. The word comes from the Greek *proteios*, "of first importance". Chains below about 50 amino acids are usually called **peptides**; longer ones are **polypeptides**; a protein may have one or more polypeptide chains.

### Functions of proteins

| Function | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Catalysis (enzymes) | Amylase, DNA polymerase, cytochrome P450 |
| Structure | Collagen, keratin, elastin, tubulin, actin |
| Transport | Haemoglobin (O2), serum albumin (drugs, fatty acids), membrane carriers |
| Storage | Ferritin (iron), casein |
| Movement | Myosin, kinesin, dynein |
| Defence | Antibodies (immunoglobulins), complement, clotting factors |
| Regulation | Insulin, glucagon, growth hormone; transcription factors |
| Signalling | Receptors, G proteins, protein kinases |

### Classification of proteins

| Basis | Classes | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Fibrous (long, insoluble, structural) | Collagen, keratin, fibroin |
| | Globular (compact, soluble, functional) | Enzymes, haemoglobin, albumin |
| Composition | Simple (amino acids only) | Albumins, globulins, histones |
| | Conjugated (protein + prosthetic group) | Glycoproteins, lipoproteins, haemoglobin (haem), nucleoproteins, metalloproteins |
| | Derived (products of partial breakdown) | Peptones, denatured proteins |

## 3.2 Amino acids

An α-amino acid has a central **α-carbon** bonded to an **amino group (–NH2)**, a **carboxyl group (–COOH)**, a **hydrogen atom** and a variable **side chain (R group)**.

```text
            H
            |
     H2N — Cα — COOH        R = side chain (decides the amino acid)
            |
            R
```

- All standard amino acids except **glycine** (R = H) are chiral; proteins contain the **L-form**.
- At body pH an amino acid exists as a **zwitterion** (–NH3+ and –COO−). The pH at which the net charge is zero is the **isoelectric point (pI)**; solubility is lowest there.
- Twenty amino acids are coded by the standard genetic code. Selenocysteine (coded by a special UGA) and pyrrolysine are rarer additions.

### Classification by side chain

| Group | Amino acids |
| --- | --- |
| Non-polar, aliphatic | Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, proline |
| Aromatic | Phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan |
| Polar, uncharged | Serine, threonine, cysteine, asparagine, glutamine |
| Positively charged (basic) | Lysine, arginine, histidine |
| Negatively charged (acidic) | Aspartate, glutamate |

### Nutritional classification

- **Essential** (must come from diet; nine in adults): histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, valine.
- **Non-essential:** made in the body (alanine, glycine, serine, aspartate, glutamate and others).
- **Conditionally essential:** arginine (in growing children), tyrosine (when phenylalanine is low), cysteine.

### The peptide bond

Formed between the –COOH of one amino acid and the –NH2 of the next, with loss of water (condensation). It is **rigid and planar**, has partial double-bond character, and is almost always in the **trans** form. Rotation is possible only around the bonds on either side of the α-carbon (angles phi and psi), which limits the shapes a chain can take.

## 3.3 Protein structure

| Level | What it is | Bonds that hold it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Sequence of amino acids from N-terminus to C-terminus | Peptide (covalent) bonds; disulphide bonds |
| Secondary | Regular local folding of the backbone: α-helix, β-pleated sheet, turns | Hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H |
| Tertiary | Overall 3D folding of one chain | Hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds (salt bridges), van der Waals forces, disulphide bridges |
| Quaternary | Arrangement of two or more chains (subunits) | Same non-covalent bonds; sometimes disulphide |

**Primary structure** decides all higher levels. A single change can matter: in **sickle cell anaemia**, valine replaces glutamate at position 6 of β-globin and haemoglobin polymerises when deoxygenated. Sanger first sequenced a protein (insulin).

**α-helix:** a right-handed coil; 3.6 amino acids per turn; rise 0.54 nm per turn; each C=O hydrogen-bonds to the N–H four residues ahead; side chains point outward. Proline and glycine break helices. Found in keratin and many membrane-spanning segments.

**β-pleated sheet:** extended strands lying side by side, hydrogen-bonded between strands — **parallel** or **antiparallel**. Found in silk fibroin and in many globular proteins.

**Tertiary structure** forms mainly because non-polar side chains bury themselves inside, away from water, and polar ones stay on the surface. Compact independent folding units are called **domains** (for example the kinase domain).

**Quaternary structure:** haemoglobin has four subunits (α2β2) and shows cooperative oxygen binding; antibodies have two heavy and two light chains joined by disulphide bonds.

```text
 Primary      Secondary            Tertiary          Quaternary
 -A-G-L-K-S-   helix / sheet        folded chain      several chains
               /\/\/\  or  ===      ( compact ball )  (α2β2 haemoglobin)
```

### Folding, denaturation and chaperones

- **Anfinsen's experiment:** ribonuclease was unfolded with urea and mercaptoethanol; on removing them it refolded and regained activity — the amino acid sequence carries the information for folding.
- **Denaturation** is loss of the native 3D shape (and function) without breaking peptide bonds. Agents: heat, extreme pH, urea, guanidine, organic solvents, detergents, heavy metals. This is why protein drugs (insulin, vaccines, monoclonal antibodies) need cold storage and careful formulation.
- **Molecular chaperones** help proteins fold correctly inside the crowded cell: Hsp70 binds exposed hydrophobic regions of new chains; chaperonins (Hsp60, bacterial GroEL–GroES) give a closed chamber to fold in. Protein disulphide isomerase and peptidyl-prolyl isomerase speed slow folding steps.
- **Misfolding diseases:** misfolded proteins clump into amyloid — Alzheimer's disease (β-amyloid), Parkinson's disease (α-synuclein), prion diseases (infectious misfolded prion protein).

## 3.4 Regularities in protein pathways

Every protein follows an orderly, rule-based route from synthesis to its final location and eventual destruction.

### Protein targeting (sorting)

The "address" of a protein is written in its own sequence as a **signal**.

| Destination | Signal | Pathway |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cytosol | No signal | Stays where it was made on free ribosomes |
| ER, then Golgi, plasma membrane, lysosome or secretion | N-terminal **signal peptide** (hydrophobic stretch) | Signal recognition particle (SRP) stops translation, takes the ribosome to the SRP receptor on the ER; the chain enters through the translocon; signal peptidase removes the signal |
| ER lumen (resident proteins) | C-terminal KDEL sequence | Returned from Golgi to ER |
| Lysosome | Mannose-6-phosphate tag added in the Golgi | Bound by M6P receptors and sent in vesicles; defect causes I-cell disease |
| Nucleus | Nuclear localisation signal (short basic sequence) | Through nuclear pores with importins |
| Mitochondria | N-terminal amphipathic presequence | Through TOM and TIM import complexes |
| Peroxisome | C-terminal SKL sequence | Peroxisomal import machinery |

**The secretory pathway:**

```text
 Rough ER (synthesis, folding, N-glycosylation, disulphide bonds)
   → transport vesicles
 cis-Golgi → medial → trans-Golgi (glycan trimming, O-glycosylation, sorting)
   → secretory vesicles → plasma membrane (exocytosis)
                       → lysosomes
                       → regulated granules (e.g. insulin in β-cells)
```

### Post-translational modifications

| Modification | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Proteolytic cleavage | Preproinsulin → proinsulin → insulin + C-peptide; zymogens (trypsinogen → trypsin) |
| Phosphorylation (Ser, Thr, Tyr) | Switches enzymes and receptors on or off (protein kinases, Unit 5) |
| Glycosylation (N-linked on Asn, O-linked on Ser/Thr) | Antibodies, erythropoietin — affects activity and half-life of biologic drugs |
| Disulphide bond formation | Insulin, antibodies |
| Acetylation and methylation | Histones — control of gene expression |
| Lipid attachment | Ras (farnesyl group) anchors it to the membrane |
| Hydroxylation | Proline and lysine in collagen — needs vitamin C (lack causes scurvy) |
| Ubiquitination | Marks proteins for destruction |
| γ-Carboxylation | Glutamate in clotting factors II, VII, IX, X — needs vitamin K (blocked by warfarin) |

### Protein turnover and degradation

Proteins are broken down at very different rates; damaged, misfolded and regulatory proteins are removed quickly.

1. **Ubiquitin–proteasome pathway:** enzymes E1 (activating), E2 (conjugating) and E3 (ligase) attach a chain of the small protein **ubiquitin** to the target; the **26S proteasome** recognises the chain and cuts the protein into short peptides. It destroys cyclins at the right time in the cell cycle. **Bortezomib**, a proteasome inhibitor, is used in multiple myeloma.
2. **Lysosomal degradation and autophagy:** long-lived proteins, membrane proteins and whole organelles are delivered to lysosomes.

### Regularities in metabolic pathways

The enzymes that proteins form are arranged in pathways that follow common rules:

- Each pathway is a series of steps, each catalysed by a specific enzyme.
- Pathways are controlled at the **first committed step**, often by **feedback inhibition** — the end product inhibits an early enzyme (isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase).
- Opposing pathways (glycolysis and gluconeogenesis) use different enzymes at key steps so both are not fully on at once.
- Control is by enzyme amount (synthesis and degradation), allosteric effectors, covalent modification (phosphorylation) and compartments.

## 3.6 Control of protein synthesis — negative and positive control

Cells make only the proteins they need. In bacteria genes are controlled mainly at transcription, often in **operons** — groups of genes with one promoter and one operator, transcribed into one mRNA (Jacob and Monod).

| Type of control | Regulator | Effect when the regulator binds DNA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Negative control** | Repressor protein | Transcription is switched **off** |
| **Positive control** | Activator protein | Transcription is switched **on** or increased |

| Type of operon | Normal state | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inducible | Off; turned on by an inducer (substrate) | lac operon (catabolic) |
| Repressible | On; turned off by a corepressor (end product) | trp operon (anabolic) |

### The lac operon (both negative and positive control)

```text
  lacI          P      O       lacZ          lacY        lacA
 [repressor]--[CAP|prom]-[op]--[β-galactosidase][permease][transacetylase]
   gene        site
```

- **Structural genes:** lacZ (β-galactosidase splits lactose to glucose + galactose), lacY (permease brings lactose in), lacA (transacetylase).
- **Negative control:** the lacI gene makes a **repressor** that binds the operator and blocks RNA polymerase. When lactose is present, some is converted to **allolactose (the inducer)**, which binds the repressor and changes its shape; the repressor leaves the operator. IPTG is a synthetic inducer used in the laboratory and in recombinant protein production.
- **Positive control (catabolite activation):** when glucose is low, cAMP rises. **cAMP binds the catabolite activator protein (CAP, also called CRP)**; the CAP–cAMP complex binds a site next to the promoter, bends the DNA and helps RNA polymerase bind, increasing transcription many times. When glucose is high, cAMP falls, CAP cannot bind and the operon stays low even if lactose is present — **catabolite repression**. Glucose is therefore used first.

| Glucose | Lactose | Repressor | CAP–cAMP | lac operon |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Present | Absent | Bound | Not bound | Off |
| Present | Present | Removed | Not bound | Very low |
| Absent | Absent | Bound | Bound | Off |
| Absent | Present | Removed | Bound | Strongly on |

Positive control is the key point: removing the repressor alone is not enough — the activator is needed for strong expression.

### The trp operon (repressible)

Five genes (trpE, D, C, B, A) make tryptophan. The repressor is inactive alone; when tryptophan is plentiful it binds the repressor (**corepressor**), which then binds the operator and stops transcription. A second control, **attenuation**, ends transcription early in the leader region when tryptophan-charged tRNA is abundant.

### Gene regulation in eukaryotes

Control happens at many levels:

1. **Chromatin:** histone acetylation (open, active) and DNA methylation (closed, silent).
2. **Transcription (main level):** activators bind enhancers and recruit co-activators and the general machinery — positive control is the rule in eukaryotes. Example: steroid hormone receptors act as hormone-activated transcription factors.
3. **RNA processing:** alternative splicing.
4. **mRNA stability and transport.**
5. **Translation:** microRNAs block or degrade target mRNAs; phosphorylation of eIF2 shuts down translation during stress.
6. **Post-translational:** modification, targeting and degradation.

## 3.7 Significance of protein synthesis

1. **Expresses the genetic information** — genes act only through the proteins (and RNAs) they produce.
2. **Growth and repair** — new cells and tissues need new proteins.
3. **Enzymes, hormones, antibodies and receptors** are all made by protein synthesis; their amounts change with need (enzyme induction by phenobarbital and rifampicin increases drug metabolism).
4. **Adaptation** — inducible enzymes let bacteria use the food available.
5. **Selective drug target** — differences between 70S and 80S ribosomes allow antibiotics (tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolides, chloramphenicol) to stop bacterial protein synthesis with less harm to human cells.
6. **Disease** — errors give faulty proteins (sickle cell disease, cystic fibrosis); uncontrolled synthesis of growth proteins contributes to cancer.
7. **Biotechnology** — host cells are made to synthesise human proteins: insulin, interferons, growth hormone, erythropoietin, clotting factors, monoclonal antibodies, vaccine antigens. The lac promoter with IPTG induction is widely used for this in *E. coli*.

## Remember

- Proteins = amino acids joined by peptide bonds; peptide bond is planar, trans and rigid.
- 20 standard amino acids, L-form; glycine is not chiral; nine are essential in adults.
- Four levels of structure; α-helix has 3.6 residues per turn and 0.54 nm pitch; tertiary structure held by hydrophobic, hydrogen, ionic, van der Waals and disulphide bonds.
- Sequence decides folding (Anfinsen); chaperones help; misfolding causes amyloid diseases.
- Signal peptide + SRP → ER → Golgi → secretion; mannose-6-phosphate → lysosome; NLS → nucleus.
- Ubiquitin–proteasome removes proteins; bortezomib blocks the proteasome.
- Negative control = repressor switches off; positive control = activator switches on.
- lac operon: allolactose removes the repressor; CAP–cAMP (low glucose) activates — catabolite repression explains why glucose is used first.
- trp operon: repressible, tryptophan is the corepressor, plus attenuation.
