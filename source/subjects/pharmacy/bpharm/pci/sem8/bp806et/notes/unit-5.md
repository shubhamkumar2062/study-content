# Unit 5: Regulatory requirements, pharmacovigilance, herbal pharmacopoeias and markers

The last unit pulls the subject together. It looks at how countries regulate herbal medicines, how the safety of herbal medicines is watched after they reach patients (pharmacovigilance), how the main herbal pharmacopoeias compare, and how chemical and biological markers are used to standardise herbal products.

## 5.1 Regulatory requirements for herbal medicines

### Why regulation of herbals is difficult

- Herbal products are often seen by the public as "natural and therefore safe", and are widely self-medicated.
- The same plant may be sold as a **medicine**, a **food supplement**, a **health food** or a **cosmetic**, each under a different law.
- Traditional knowledge is passed down in texts and practice, not in modern clinical trials.
- Quality varies, and contamination or adulteration (even with synthetic drugs) occurs.

### WHO guidance on regulation

WHO has helped member states through several documents:

| WHO document | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Guidelines for the assessment of herbal medicines** | Basic criteria for assessing quality, safety and efficacy, and for labelling; long traditional use accepted as evidence of safety in the absence of contrary evidence |
| **Quality control methods for herbal materials** | Test methods (Unit 1) |
| **Guidelines on GACP** and **GMP for herbal medicines** | Good practices (Unit 2) |
| **Research guidelines for evaluating safety and efficacy** | Evidence requirements (Unit 3) |
| **Guidelines on safety monitoring in pharmacovigilance systems** | Post-marketing safety (5.2) |
| **WHO monographs on selected medicinal plants** | Model monographs for national use (5.3) |
| **WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy** | Integrating safe, effective traditional medicine into health systems |

### Basic requirements in most national systems

1. **Quality** — authenticated plant, GACP, GMP, specifications, contaminant limits, stability.
2. **Safety** — traditional-use evidence and/or toxicity data; warnings and contraindications; interaction information.
3. **Efficacy** — traditional evidence for traditional claims; clinical data for new or stronger claims.
4. **Labelling** — botanical name, part, preparation, amount, dose, warnings, batch, expiry; claims matched to evidence.
5. **Post-marketing surveillance** — adverse-reaction reporting, recalls, control of advertising.

### Regulation in different regions

| Country / region | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| **India** | Ministry of AYUSH; ASU drugs under Chapter IV-A of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940; classical and proprietary ASU licensing by state authorities; Schedule T GMP; official standards in the Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani Pharmacopoeias of India; phytopharmaceutical drugs as new drugs under CDSCO |
| **European Union** | Full, well-established-use and traditional-use routes; HMPC monographs (Unit 3) |
| **USA** | Most herbal products sold as **dietary supplements** under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA), 1994, without pre-market proof of efficacy; manufacturers follow dietary-supplement GMP; a herbal product making a disease claim must be approved as a **botanical drug** through FDA, which has issued botanical drug development guidance |
| **China** | Traditional Chinese medicine regulated by the national medical products authority; Chinese Pharmacopoeia has a separate volume for TCM materials and preparations |
| **Canada** | Natural health products need a product licence; evidence level matched to the claim |
| **Australia** | Complementary medicines either "listed" (low-risk ingredients, lower-level claims) or "registered" (higher-level claims, evaluated) by the Therapeutic Goods Administration |

## 5.2 WHO guidelines on safety monitoring of herbal medicines in pharmacovigilance systems

**Pharmacovigilance** is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. WHO published **guidelines on safety monitoring of herbal medicines in pharmacovigilance systems** (2004) to help national centres include herbal medicines in their existing systems for monitoring adverse drug reactions.

### Objectives

1. Strengthen national pharmacovigilance so that **herbal medicines are included** and monitored like other medicines.
2. Set **standard terms and definitions** for reporting herbal adverse events.
3. Provide **technical guidance on reporting, assessing and communicating** safety signals for herbal medicines.
4. Promote **safe and proper use** of herbal medicines by patients and practitioners.

### Why herbal pharmacovigilance is special

| Challenge | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Patients do not see herbs as medicines and seldom report them | Self-medication with herbal teas or supplements is not mentioned to the doctor |
| Problems of identity and naming | Same common name for different plants; substitution of a nephrotoxic *Aristolochia* species for another herb in slimming preparations caused kidney failure and urinary-tract cancer |
| Quality problems mistaken for drug effects | Contamination with heavy metals, pesticides or microbes; adulteration with undeclared synthetic drugs (steroids, sildenafil, NSAIDs) |
| Herb–drug interactions | St John's wort lowering levels of ciclosporin, warfarin, oral contraceptives and HIV protease inhibitors by enzyme induction |
| Multi-ingredient products | Hard to tell which herb caused the reaction |
| Practitioners outside the formal system | Traditional practitioners may not know about or use reporting forms |

### What a herbal adverse reaction report should contain

```text
PATIENT    age, sex, weight, relevant illness, other medicines (including other herbs)
PRODUCT    product name and manufacturer; batch number; botanical name of each herb;
           part used; type of preparation (powder, extract, decoction); source and
           where bought; dose, route, start and stop dates; reason for use
REACTION   description, onset, severity, treatment, outcome, effect of stopping
           (dechallenge) and of restarting (rechallenge)
REPORTER   name, profession, contact details
SAMPLE     a sample of the product kept for analysis, where possible
```

### Assessment and action

- **Causality assessment** — the relation between the product and the event is graded using WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre categories: certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, unassessable/unclassifiable.
- **Product analysis** — check identity and look for contaminants or adulterants, because many herbal "reactions" are really quality failures.
- **Signal detection** — reports from many countries are pooled in the WHO global database maintained by the **Uppsala Monitoring Centre** (WHO Collaborating Centre for International Drug Monitoring), which uses a herbal classification system for coding.
- **Regulatory action** — label warnings, restriction, withdrawal, recall, communication to health professionals and the public. Examples of action taken in various countries include warnings or restrictions on kava (liver injury), ephedra (cardiovascular events) and aristolochia-containing products.

### Pharmacovigilance in India

- The **Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)**, coordinated by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, collects adverse drug reaction reports for all medicines.
- The **Ministry of AYUSH** runs a pharmacovigilance programme for Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani and Homoeopathy drugs through a national coordinating centre (the All India Institute of Ayurveda, New Delhi) and peripheral centres in AYUSH institutions. It also monitors **misleading advertisements** of AYUSH products.

## 5.3 Comparison of various herbal pharmacopoeias

A **pharmacopoeia** is an official book of standards for the identity, quality, purity and strength of drugs, which has legal force in its country. Herbal pharmacopoeias give monographs for herbal materials and preparations.

### Main herbal pharmacopoeias and monograph series

| Book | Published by | Nature and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API)** | Ministry of AYUSH through the Pharmacopoeia Commission for Indian Medicine & Homoeopathy (PCIM&H), Ghaziabad | Legal standards for ASU (Ayurvedic) drugs. **Part I** — single plant drugs (and some minerals); **Part II** — compound formulations. Monographs give Sanskrit and other names, botanical source, macro/microscopy, identity, purity limits, TLC, assay where available, and Ayurvedic properties (rasa, guna, virya, vipaka, karma), important formulations, therapeutic uses and dose |
| **Unani Pharmacopoeia of India** and **Siddha Pharmacopoeia of India** | PCIM&H | Similar legal standards for Unani and Siddha drugs, with the properties of those systems |
| **Homoeopathic Pharmacopoeia of India** | PCIM&H | Standards for homoeopathic mother tinctures and potencies |
| **Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)** | Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission | Main legal pharmacopoeia for modern medicines; also contains monographs on herbs and herbal products used in modern formulations |
| **Indian Herbal Pharmacopoeia** | Indian Drug Manufacturers' Association with the Regional Research Laboratory (now CSIR-IIIM), Jammu | Non-statutory; detailed monographs with TLC/HPLC methods and markers, useful for industry |
| **British Herbal Pharmacopoeia** | British Herbal Medicine Association | Quality standards and therapeutic notes for herbs used in UK herbal practice |
| **European Pharmacopoeia** | Council of Europe (EDQM) | Legally binding in member states; general monographs on herbal drugs, preparations and extracts; strict limits for contaminants; assays and TLC/HPLC identity |
| **United States Pharmacopeia–National Formulary** | USP | Monographs for botanicals used as dietary supplements and their extracts and powders, with modern chromatographic methods |
| **Chinese Pharmacopoeia** | Chinese Pharmacopoeia Commission | A separate volume for traditional Chinese medicine materials, prepared slices, extracts and formulas |
| **Japanese Pharmacopoeia** | Japanese government | Monographs for crude drugs and Kampo extract preparations |
| **WHO monographs on selected medicinal plants** | WHO | **Not legally binding**; model monographs with quality, medicinal uses, pharmacology, safety, contraindications and dose, for countries to adapt |
| **German Commission E monographs, ESCOP monographs** | German expert commission; European Scientific Cooperative on Phytotherapy | Mainly therapeutic (uses, doses, safety), not quality standards |

### Comparison of monograph content

| Item | API | Ph. Eur. / BP | USP | WHO monographs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legal status | Statutory in India for ASU | Statutory in Europe/UK | Statutory in USA | Advisory only |
| Botanical definition and part | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Macroscopy and microscopy | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Identity by TLC | Yes (newer volumes) | Yes, detailed | Yes, often HPTLC/HPLC | Yes |
| Purity — foreign matter, ash, extractives, LOD | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Assay of active/marker | In many monographs | Usually | Usually | Where established |
| Contaminant limits | General limits for heavy metals, microbes, pesticides, aflatoxins | Detailed general limits | Detailed | Refers to WHO methods |
| Traditional / therapeutic information | Ayurvedic properties, formulations, uses, dose | No therapeutic section | No therapeutic section | Uses, pharmacology, safety, dose |

**Indian heavy-metal limits**: the limits applied to Indian ASU products are commonly quoted as **lead 10 ppm, mercury 1 ppm, arsenic 3 ppm and cadmium 0.3 ppm**. The lead and cadmium figures match the WHO-suggested limits (10 mg/kg and 0.3 mg/kg).

## 5.4 Role of chemical and biological markers in standardisation

### Chemical markers

A **marker** is a chemically defined constituent, or group of constituents, of a herbal material or product that is used for quality control, whether or not it contributes to the therapeutic effect.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Active (therapeutic) marker** | Contributes to the clinical effect | Sennosides in senna (laxative) |
| **Analytical marker** | Used only to check identity or quantity; activity not proven | Many constituents used to track extracts |
| **Negative (toxic) marker** | A harmful constituent whose absence or low level must be shown | Aristolochic acids, pyrrolizidine alkaloids, ginkgolic acids |

### Uses of chemical markers

1. **Authentication** of the plant and detection of substitution.
2. **Standardisation** — adjusting extracts to a stated marker content.
3. **Batch-to-batch consistency** of raw materials, extracts and products.
4. **Process control** — checking extraction efficiency and losses during drying or concentration.
5. **Stability** — marker loss over time sets the shelf life.
6. **Dissolution and bioavailability** studies of herbal dosage forms.
7. **Safety** — limits for toxic constituents.

### Common markers of Indian and international herbal drugs

| Plant | Marker(s) | Usual method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Senna (*Cassia angustifolia*) | Sennosides A and B | HPLC, spectrophotometry |
| Turmeric (*Curcuma longa*) | Curcuminoids (curcumin) | HPLC, HPTLC, UV |
| Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) | Withanolides, withaferin A | HPLC, HPTLC |
| Liquorice (*Glycyrrhiza glabra*) | Glycyrrhizin | HPLC, HPTLC |
| Brahmi (*Bacopa monnieri*) | Bacosides | HPLC |
| Kalmegh (*Andrographis paniculata*) | Andrographolide | HPLC, HPTLC |
| Vasaka (*Adhatoda vasica*) | Vasicine | HPLC, HPTLC |
| Guggul (*Commiphora wightii*) | Guggulsterones E and Z | HPLC |
| Black pepper (*Piper nigrum*) | Piperine | HPLC, UV |
| *Phyllanthus amarus* | Phyllanthin | HPLC, HPTLC |
| Ginkgo | Flavone glycosides, terpene lactones | HPLC |
| St John's wort | Hypericins, hyperforin | HPLC |
| Milk thistle | Silymarin | HPLC |
| Ginseng | Ginsenosides | HPLC |

### Choosing a good marker

- Specific to the plant (helps detect substitution).
- Present in measurable amount and stable during analysis.
- Ideally related to the therapeutic activity.
- A pure **reference standard** is available.
- Measurable by a **validated** method.

### Limitations of chemical markers

- A marker may not represent the whole activity; a product can pass the marker assay but fail in effect.
- Spiking with a pure marker or cheap source can disguise poor material, so a **fingerprint** should accompany the assay.
- Content varies naturally, making limits hard to set.
- Reference standards can be costly or unavailable.

### Biological markers

**Biological markers** use a biological property to identify or assess the quality of a herbal drug.

| Type | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bioassay / pharmacological markers** | Antimicrobial, antioxidant or enzyme-inhibition activity; earlier, animal assays of cardiac glycosides | Potency when chemistry cannot capture activity |
| **Protein and isoenzyme markers** | Electrophoretic patterns of seed proteins or isoenzymes | Distinguishing varieties |
| **DNA (molecular) markers** | RFLP, RAPD, AFLP, microsatellites (SSR), SCAR markers, **DNA barcoding** with standard regions (ITS2, rbcL, matK, psbA–trnH) | Authentication of species, detection of adulterants, identifying chemotypes and elite varieties |

**Advantages of DNA markers**: the DNA sequence does not change with age, season, environment, plant part or processing conditions that alter chemistry; very small samples are enough; closely related species and powdered material can be told apart.

**Limitations**: DNA shows **identity, not quality** — it cannot measure content of actives or contaminants; DNA is degraded in heavily processed products, extracts and bhasmas; equipment and reference sequence databases are needed.

```text
Complete standardisation of a herbal product
   Identity  : macroscopy + microscopy + DNA marker + fingerprint
   Quality   : chemical marker assay (active or analytical)
   Activity  : biological assay where needed
   Safety    : negative markers + contaminant limits
```

## Remember

- Regulation rests on quality, safety, efficacy, labelling and post-marketing surveillance.
- USA: most herbals are dietary supplements (DSHEA 1994); disease claims need botanical drug approval. EU: three routes. India: AYUSH, Chapter IV-A, classical vs proprietary vs phytopharmaceutical.
- WHO pharmacovigilance guidelines for herbal medicines (2004): include herbs in national systems; report botanical name, part, preparation, batch, source; analyse the product; WHO–UMC causality; Uppsala Monitoring Centre database.
- India: PvPI (IPC) for all drugs; AYUSH pharmacovigilance programme (national coordinating centre at AIIA, New Delhi) also watches misleading advertisements.
- API: Part I single drugs, Part II formulations; statutory; includes Ayurvedic properties. WHO monographs are advisory only; Ph. Eur. statutory with strict contaminant limits.
- Indian heavy-metal limits quoted: Pb 10 ppm, Hg 1 ppm, As 3 ppm, Cd 0.3 ppm (Pb and Cd as WHO).
- Markers: active, analytical, negative. Sennosides, curcumin, withaferin A, glycyrrhizin, bacosides, andrographolide, piperine.
- Biological markers: bioassays, isoenzymes, DNA markers (RAPD, RFLP, AFLP, SSR, SCAR, barcoding ITS2/rbcL/matK); DNA proves identity, not quality.
