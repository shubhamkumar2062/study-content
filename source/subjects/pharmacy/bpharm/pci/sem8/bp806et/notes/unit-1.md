# Unit 1: Basic tests, WHO guidelines for quality control and evaluation of crude drugs

A tablet of paracetamol contains one known molecule that can be assayed exactly. A herbal medicine contains a whole plant — or an extract of it — with hundreds of constituents, many of them unknown, and its make-up changes with the place it grew, the season it was collected and the way it was dried and stored. This unit explains why herbal quality control is different, the WHO "basic tests" for checking drugs when a full laboratory is not available, the WHO methods for quality control of herbal materials, and how commercial crude drugs are evaluated before use.

## 1.1 Why quality control of herbals is special

| Problem | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Natural variation** | Constituent content changes with genotype, soil, climate, altitude, age of plant, time and season of collection |
| **Complex make-up** | Many constituents act together; the active one may be unknown, so no single assay proves quality |
| **Adulteration and substitution** | Look-alike plants, exhausted drugs, cheaper species, wrong plant part, deliberate mixing |
| **Contamination** | Pesticide residues, heavy metals, microbes, aflatoxins, radioactive matter, foreign matter |
| **Processing changes** | Drying, storage and extraction alter content; volatile oils and enzymes are lost or activated |
| **Multi-ingredient products** | Classical Ayurvedic formulations often contain many herbs; identifying each one in the finished product is hard |

So herbal quality control rests on three questions, asked at every stage from field to finished pack:

```text
   IDENTITY            PURITY                  CONTENT / STRENGTH
 Is it the right   Is it free from foreign    Does it contain enough of
 plant and part?   matter and contaminants?   its marker or active constituents?
        \                  |                        /
         \_________________|_______________________/
                           |
                    SAFETY + CONSISTENCY
              (same quality batch after batch)
```

### Key definitions (WHO usage)

- **Herbs** — crude plant material: leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, stems, wood, bark, roots, rhizomes or other parts, whole, fragmented or powdered.
- **Herbal materials** — herbs plus fresh juices, gums, fixed oils, essential oils, resins and dry powders of herbs.
- **Herbal preparations** — the basis of finished products: comminuted or powdered herbal materials, extracts, tinctures and fatty oils, made by extraction, fractionation, purification, concentration or other processes.
- **Finished herbal products** — herbal preparations made from one or more herbs; may contain excipients.
- **Quality control** — the testing of materials and products against specifications, with the sampling and release procedures needed.
- **Standardisation** — setting and applying specifications (identity, purity, content) so that every batch of a herbal drug is of uniform, defined quality. For extracts it often means adjusting the extract to a stated content of constituents.

## 1.2 Basic tests for drugs

WHO published **"Basic tests for drugs"** in separate volumes for **pharmaceutical substances**, **medicinal plant materials** and **dosage forms**. They are simple tests that can be done with few reagents and simple equipment — even outside a fully equipped laboratory — by a pharmacist or trained technician.

### Purpose

1. To **confirm the identity** of a drug or plant material when a full pharmacopoeial analysis is not possible.
2. To **detect gross degradation** or obvious substandard material.
3. To **screen** suspect samples in the supply chain (warehouses, hospital stores, remote areas) and pick out those that must go to a full quality control laboratory.

### Main features

- Use readily available reagents and simple apparatus (test tubes, water-bath, hand lens, simple TLC).
- Each test is short and gives a clear yes/no result.
- They **do not replace** pharmacopoeial testing. A sample that passes a basic test is not proven to be of full pharmacopoeial quality; a sample that fails must be fully analysed before action is taken.

### What the tests look at

| Material | Typical basic tests |
| --- | --- |
| **Pharmaceutical substances** | Appearance, colour, odour; solubility; simple colour or precipitation reactions for functional groups and ions; melting behaviour; simple TLC against a reference |
| **Medicinal plant materials** | Macroscopic features (shape, size, colour, surface, fracture); odour and taste; microscopy of the powder; simple chemical tests (for alkaloids, tannins, saponins, anthraquinones); fluorescence under UV; simple TLC |
| **Dosage forms** | Visual inspection (colour, cracks, mottling, discoloration); identity of the active by colour reaction or TLC; simple disintegration check; signs of degradation such as smell of acetic acid from aspirin tablets |

## 1.3 WHO guidelines for quality control of herbal drugs

WHO first published **"Quality control methods for medicinal plant materials"** (1998); it was updated as **"Quality control methods for herbal materials"** (2011). It is **not a pharmacopoeia**. It is a collection of test methods that countries can use to build their own national standards. Alongside it, WHO issued **"Guidelines for assessing quality of herbal medicines with reference to contaminants and residues"** (2007), which gives examples of national limits.

### Tests described in the WHO manual

```text
 General notices -> Powder fineness and sieve size -> Sampling
 Foreign matter -> Macroscopy and microscopy -> Thin-layer chromatography
 Ash (total, acid-insoluble, water-soluble) -> Extractable matter
 Water and volatile matter -> Volatile oils -> Bitterness value
 Haemolytic activity -> Tannins -> Swelling index -> Foaming index
 Pesticide residues -> Arsenic and toxic metals -> Microorganisms
 Aflatoxins -> Radioactive contamination
```

### Sampling

Herbal material is not uniform, so the sample must represent the whole batch.

- **Batch of up to 5 containers** — sample every container.
- **6–50 containers** — sample 5.
- **More than 50** — sample 10%, rounding up to the nearest multiple of 10 (51 units are sampled as 60, i.e. 6 packages).
- From each selected container take **three original samples** — top, middle and bottom. In sacks the first sample is taken from not less than 10 cm deep.
- Combine to a **pooled sample**, mix, and reduce by **quartering**: spread in a square heap, divide diagonally into four, keep two opposite quarters, mix and repeat until the required amount remains.
- Divide the average sample into **final samples** for testing and keep a portion as a **retained (reference) sample**.

### Foreign matter

Herbal materials should be free from visible moulds, insects and animal contamination, and from poisonous foreign matter. Foreign matter means other parts of the same plant beyond the stated limit, other organisms, and mineral admixtures (soil, stones, sand, dust). Sample sizes: **500 g** for roots, rhizomes and bark; **250 g** for leaves, flowers, seeds and fruits; **50 g** for cut material. The sample is spread thin, sorted with a hand lens (6× or 10×), and each group weighed and expressed in g per 100 g.

### Macroscopic and microscopic examination

Identity is first checked by shape, size, colour, surface, texture, fracture, odour and taste, compared with a reference specimen. Microscopy of sections and powder shows diagnostic features — stomata type, trichomes, calcium oxalate crystals, starch grains, fibres, vessels, stone cells. Microscopy is indispensable for powders.

### Ash values

| Ash | How it is obtained | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Total ash** | 2–4 g ignited at 500–600 °C until white (carbon-free) | Physiological ash of the tissue plus non-physiological ash from adhering earth |
| **Acid-insoluble ash** | Total ash boiled with dilute HCl, insoluble matter ignited | Silica — sand and earthy matter |
| **Water-soluble ash** | Total ash boiled with water; weight lost on washing | Water-soluble inorganic matter; low in exhausted drugs |
| **Sulphated ash** | Ignition after treating with sulphuric acid | Used for some drugs and pure substances |

### Extractable matter (extractive values)

Used where no reliable chemical assay exists; it measures the amount of constituents extracted by a solvent.

- **Hot extraction** — 4 g of coarse powder refluxed with 100 ml of solvent for 1 hour, cooled, made up to the original weight, filtered; 25 ml of filtrate evaporated, dried at 105 °C for 6 hours and weighed.
- **Cold maceration** — 4 g macerated with 100 ml solvent for 6 hours with shaking, left 18 hours, filtered; 25 ml evaporated and dried at 105 °C.
- Solvents: water (water-soluble extractive), ethanol of stated strength (alcohol-soluble extractive), or others as specified. Low values point to exhausted or adulterated drug.

### Water and volatile matter

- **Azeotropic (toluene distillation)** method — the sample is distilled with water-saturated toluene; water collects in a graduated receiver. Measures water directly, so it is preferred for drugs with volatile oils.
- **Loss on drying** — drying at 100–105 °C, or in a desiccator over phosphorus pentoxide, until two weighings differ by not more than 5 mg; measures water plus volatile matter.

Excess water allows microbial growth, mould, insects and hydrolysis, so a limit is set for each material.

### Special tests

| Test | Principle | Example use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Volatile oil** | Steam distillation in a Clevenger-type apparatus; oil read in a graduated tube | Clove, cinnamon, eucalyptus |
| **Bitterness value** | Threshold bitter concentration of an extract compared with quinine hydrochloride; one unit = bitterness of 1 g quinine hydrochloride in 2000 ml | Gentian, chirata, neem |
| **Haemolytic activity** | Saponins lyse red blood cells; compared with reference saponin | Saponin drugs (liquorice, shatavari) |
| **Tannins** | Tannins bind to hide powder; difference in water-extractable matter before and after hide powder | Catechu, myrobalan |
| **Swelling index** | Volume (ml) occupied by 1 g of drug after swelling in water in a 25 ml cylinder (shaken every 10 min for 1 h, left 3 h) | Isabgol, linseed, fenugreek |
| **Foaming index** | Decoction (1 g in 100 ml) diluted in 10 tubes, shaken, foam measured after 15 min; index = 1000/a, where a = ml of decoction in the tube giving 1 cm foam | Saponin drugs |

### Contaminants

**Pesticide residues** — organochlorine, organophosphorus and pyrethroid residues are extracted, cleaned up and measured, mostly by gas chromatography. National limits are set from the **acceptable daily intake (ADI)** of the pesticide and the **mean daily intake** of the herb.

**Arsenic and toxic metals** — arsenic by limit test or atomic absorption; lead and cadmium by atomic absorption spectroscopy or ICP; a limit test for total toxic metals as lead. WHO has suggested maximum limits of **lead 10 mg/kg** and **cadmium 0.3 mg/kg** in herbal materials; other limits are set nationally.

**Microorganisms** — total viable aerobic count, yeasts and moulds, and tests for specific organisms (*Escherichia coli*, *Salmonella*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, other enterobacteria).

**Aflatoxins** — toxins (B1, B2, G1, G2) of *Aspergillus flavus* and related moulds on poorly dried or badly stored material. The WHO method uses a clean-up column and HPLC with fluorescence detection. Only materials with a history of aflatoxin contamination need routine testing.

**Radioactive contamination** — after nuclear accidents; measured by national radiation laboratories.

### Limits

WHO states that it **cannot recommend single international limits** for contaminants, because they vary too much and there is no consensus. Instead it gives test methods and **examples of national limits**, to be used as a basis for national standards. Examples from the WHO 2007 contaminants guideline:

| Category of herbal material or medicine | Aerobic bacteria (max per g) | Yeasts and moulds (max per g) | *E. coli* |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-treated with boiling water (teas, infusions) or used topically | 10^7 | 10^4 | 10^2 per g |
| Other herbal materials for internal use | 10^5 | 10^3 | 10 per g |
| Other (finished) herbal medicines | 10^5 | 10^3 | Absent in 1 g |

In all these categories *Salmonella*, *Shigella* and clostridia must be absent in 1 g. For aflatoxins, Germany's limit quoted by WHO is **2 µg/kg for aflatoxin B1** and **4 µg/kg for the sum of B1, B2, G1 and G2**.

**Setting a limit from data**: WHO suggests pooling results of about **20 successive batches** and using the grand average ± **three standard deviations** ("three sigma limits").

## 1.4 Evaluation of commercial crude drugs intended for use

**Evaluation** is the confirmation of identity and the determination of quality and purity of a crude drug. Commercial samples are bought in bulk from traders, so they must be checked before use in manufacture.

### Methods of evaluation

| Method | What is done | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Organoleptic** (morphological) | Colour, odour, taste, size, shape, surface, fracture | Fibrous fracture of quassia; aromatic odour of clove; bitter taste of chirata |
| **Microscopic** | Sections and powder; histochemical stains; quantitative microscopy | Stomatal index, palisade ratio, vein-islet and vein-termination number |
| **Physical** | Moisture, ash, extractives, swelling, foaming, solubility, viscosity, optical rotation, refractive index, melting point | Refractive index of volatile oils; viscosity of gums |
| **Chemical** | Identity tests and assays of constituents or groups | Borntrager's test (anthraquinones); alkaloid assay; acid value of resins |
| **Chromatographic / spectroscopic** | TLC/HPTLC fingerprint, HPLC assay of markers, UV, IR | Sennosides in senna, curcuminoids in turmeric |
| **Biological** | Bioassay of potency when chemistry is inadequate | Cardiac glycosides (historically), antimicrobial activity |

### Quantitative microscopy (leaf constants)

- **Stomatal number** — average number of stomata per sq mm of epidermis.
- **Stomatal index** — SI = S × 100 / (E + S), where S = stomata and E = epidermal cells per unit area; fairly constant for a species.
- **Palisade ratio** — average number of palisade cells beneath one epidermal cell.
- **Vein-islet number** — number of vein islets per sq mm of leaf surface (midway between midrib and margin).
- **Vein-termination number** — number of vein terminations per sq mm.
- **Lycopodium spore method** — used to estimate the percentage of a powdered drug, or of an adulterant, when it has a countable characteristic element (starch grains, pollen, fibres). Lycopodium spores are uniform in size and number per milligram, so they act as an internal standard.

### Adulteration of crude drugs

**Adulteration** is the debasement of a drug by admixture, substitution or removal of constituents. Types:

1. **Substitution with inferior commercial varieties** — for example, Indian senna sold as Alexandrian senna; Japanese ginger for Jamaica ginger.
2. **Substitution by exhausted drug** — already-extracted ginger, clove or coffee dried and resold; detected by low extractive or volatile oil values.
3. **Substitution with superficially similar but cheaper plants** — related species, or wrong plant parts (stems mixed with leaves).
4. **Substitution with artificially manufactured material** — moulded imitation nutmegs, artificial seeds.
5. **Adding synthetic principles** — synthetic citral to lemon oil, synthetic benzyl benzoate to balsams.
6. **Use of vegetative parts of the same plant** in place of the official part.
7. **Addition of worthless heavy materials** — sand, stones, lead shot in opium, limestone.
8. **Use of harmful or spoiled drug** — mouldy, insect-infested or decayed material.

Reasons for adulteration include scarcity, high price, confusion of vernacular names, lack of knowledge of authentic plants, and careless collection or storage.

### Workflow for a commercial crude drug

```text
Receipt -> quarantine -> sampling (WHO plan)
   -> identity: macroscopy, microscopy, TLC/HPTLC fingerprint
   -> purity: foreign matter, ash values, moisture
   -> content: extractives, volatile oil, marker assay
   -> safety: pesticides, heavy metals, microbes, aflatoxins
   -> pass: released to production    fail: rejected and recorded
```

## Remember

- Quality = identity + purity + content, plus safety and batch-to-batch consistency.
- WHO "Basic tests for drugs" — simple tests for substances, plant materials and dosage forms; confirm identity and detect gross degradation; never replace pharmacopoeial analysis.
- WHO QC methods manual (1998, updated 2011) is a set of test methods, not a pharmacopoeia.
- Sampling: up to 5 containers all; 6–50 take 5; above 50 take 10% (rounded up to a multiple of 10); top, middle, bottom; reduce by quartering.
- Foreign matter samples: 500 g roots/bark, 250 g leaves/flowers/seeds/fruits, 50 g cut material.
- Total ash 500–600 °C; acid-insoluble ash = silica/sand; water-soluble ash low in exhausted drugs.
- Water: toluene distillation (direct water) versus loss on drying (water + volatiles).
- Bitterness unit = 1 g quinine hydrochloride in 2000 ml; foaming index = 1000/a; swelling index in ml per g.
- WHO-suggested heavy-metal limits: lead 10 mg/kg, cadmium 0.3 mg/kg. WHO gives examples of national limits, not single global limits.
- Microbial examples: teas/topical 10^7 aerobic bacteria per g; other internal herbal materials 10^5; *Salmonella* and *Shigella* absent.
- Stomatal index = S × 100/(E + S); lycopodium spore method for quantitative microscopy of powders.
