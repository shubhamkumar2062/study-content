# How to use this guide

Computer Aided Drug Design is a **semester 8 elective** (BP807ET) in the PCI B.Pharm course. There is **no practical paper linked to it**, so every mark comes from the theory paper. The subject is mostly definitions, classifications, flow charts and a few equations — there is very little to calculate, so the marks go to students who can define terms precisely, name methods correctly and draw the process diagrams.

The five units here follow the five PCI syllabus units exactly; nothing has been split or joined. Unit 1 covers drug discovery and analog based design, Unit 2 all of QSAR, Unit 3 virtual screening and docking, Unit 4 the informatics and databases, and Unit 5 molecular mechanics, minimisation and conformational analysis.

Work through one unit a day: read the notes, close them and redraw the flow chart from memory, write out the equations, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with the diagram, the equation and examples.
- **Medium** — asked every few years, or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Topics that keep coming back |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Drug discovery | Stages of drug discovery and development, rational approaches to lead discovery, bioisosterism with classification and case studies |
| 2 QSAR | SAR vs QSAR, Hansch analysis with the equation, Free–Wilson analysis, partition coefficient and π, Hammett σ, Taft Es, CoMFA vs CoMSIA |
| 3 Virtual screening | Molecular docking with types and scoring functions, pharmacophore mapping, drug-likeness and Lipinski's rule of five, de novo design |
| 4 Informatics | Bioinformatics vs chemoinformatics, named databases with their contents, ADMET prediction, SMILES/InChI/fingerprints |
| 5 Molecular modelling | Molecular mechanics vs quantum mechanics, force field terms, energy minimisation methods, conformational analysis, global minimum search |

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Begin every answer with a one-line definition in the standard wording — "a pharmacophore is the ensemble of steric and electronic features necessary for…", "docking predicts the preferred pose of a ligand in a binding site and scores it". Examiners give the first mark for the definition.
2. Draw the flow chart. The discovery pipeline (Unit 1), the virtual screening funnel (Unit 3) and the drug design cycle (Unit 4) each earn marks on their own and organise the rest of the answer.
3. Learn the few equations exactly: log(1/C) = −k1(log P)² + k2 log P + k3σ + k4Es + k5; log P₀ = k2/(2k1); π_X = log P(RX) − log P(RH); σ_X = log K_X − log K_H; BA = μ + Σ aᵢXᵢ; Tc = c/(a + b − c); N2/N1 = exp(−ΔE/RT).
4. Quote the statistics with any QSAR equation — n, r or r², s, F, q². An equation written without them loses marks.
5. Use tables for comparisons. The examiner is looking for Hansch vs Free–Wilson, CoMFA vs CoMSIA, SAR vs QSAR, MM vs QM, rigid vs flexible docking, ligand-based vs structure-based screening. A clean two-column table scores faster than prose.
6. Remember the numbers that get asked as one-liners: Lipinski's 500, 5, 5, 10; Veber's 10 rotatable bonds and 140 Å²; rule of three for fragments; RMSD ≤ 2 Å for a successful re-dock; q² > 0.5 for an acceptable model; about 30 % sequence identity for homology modelling.
7. Finish a 10-mark answer with 3–4 applications or limitations. Limitations are especially valued in this subject, because every method here is an approximation — say so and name the approximation.
8. Keep ready, with spellings correct, the names of: force fields (MM2, AMBER, CHARMM, OPLS, MMFF94), semi-empirical methods (CNDO, MINDO, MNDO, AM1, PM3), minimisation methods (steepest descent, conjugate gradient, Newton–Raphson), and databases (PDB, PubChem, ChEMBL, ZINC, DrugBank, CSD, UniProt, KEGG, BindingDB).
