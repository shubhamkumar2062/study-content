# Unit 3: National Health Programmes I

The syllabus asks for each programme's **objectives, functioning and outcome**. Use the same four-part frame in every answer and add the pharmacist's role at the end:

```text
Background (when, who runs it) → Objectives → Functioning (strategies, components, structure)
     → Outcome (what has been achieved) → Role of the pharmacist
```

> **Check before you write dates and targets.** Programme names, phases and targets are revised often. The years given here were checked on Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (mohfw.gov.in), National Health Mission (nhm.gov.in), programme websites and government press releases. For your exam, confirm the current name and target on mohfw.gov.in, nhm.gov.in or the programme's own site (naco.gov.in, tbcindia.mohfw.gov.in, idsp.mohfw.gov.in, ncvbdc.mohfw.gov.in). If you are unsure of a number in the exam, describe the objective in words instead.

Most of these programmes now run under the **National Health Mission (NHM)**, which provides funds and staff through states, districts and primary health centres.

## 3.1 National AIDS Control Programme (NACP)

**Background:** HIV/AIDS control is run by the **National AIDS Control Organisation (NACO)**, set up in 1992, with **State AIDS Control Societies** in states. The programme has run in phases; **NACP phase V** covers April 2021 to March 2026.

**Objectives (phase V, in short)**

- Cut new HIV infections and AIDS-related deaths sharply (the stated aim is an 80% reduction from 2010 levels).
- Eliminate parent-to-child (vertical) transmission of HIV and syphilis.
- Reach the "95-95-95" goals: 95% of people with HIV know their status, 95% of them are on treatment, and 95% of those on treatment have suppressed virus.
- Reduce stigma and discrimination.

**Functioning — main components**

| Component | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Targeted interventions | Prevention services for high-risk groups — female sex workers, men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, transgender people, migrants and truckers: condoms, STI care, counselling |
| Harm reduction | Needle–syringe exchange and opioid substitution therapy for people who inject drugs |
| Integrated Counselling and Testing Centres (ICTC) | Free, confidential, voluntary HIV counselling and testing |
| Prevention of parent-to-child transmission (PPTCT) | Testing all pregnant women, lifelong antiretroviral therapy for positive mothers, prophylaxis and testing for babies |
| ART centres and Link ART centres | Free lifelong antiretroviral therapy, CD4 and viral load monitoring, treatment of opportunistic infections |
| STI/RTI clinics ("Suraksha Clinics") | Syndromic management of sexually transmitted and reproductive tract infections |
| Blood safety | Screening of all donated blood for HIV and other infections; voluntary blood donation |
| IEC and youth | Red Ribbon Clubs in colleges, adolescent education, mass media, toll-free helpline |
| Legal protection | HIV and AIDS (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017 — forbids discrimination, protects confidentiality and consent |
| Strategic information | Sentinel surveillance, HIV estimation, monitoring |

**Outcome:** India's adult HIV prevalence has stayed low and new infections and deaths have fallen substantially since 2010; large numbers of people receive free ART. Gaps remain in testing coverage and viral load testing.

**Pharmacist's role:** discreet supply of condoms and syringes; referral for testing; **ART adherence counselling** (missed doses lead to resistance); counselling on side effects and drug interactions (for example with anti-TB drugs); post-exposure prophylaxis awareness; strict confidentiality; fighting stigma.

## 3.2 National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP)

**Background:** The **Revised National TB Control Programme (RNTCP)** was started in 1997 (after a pilot from 1993) using the DOTS strategy. It was **renamed the National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP) in January 2020** to signal the aim of elimination. The **National Strategic Plan 2017–2025** set the goal of ending TB in India ahead of the global target year of 2030. Check tbcindia.mohfw.gov.in for the current strategic plan and target.

**Objectives**

- Sharply reduce TB incidence and deaths.
- No TB-affected family should face catastrophic costs.
- Find every case ("missing cases"), including in the private sector.

**Functioning — the four pillars: Detect – Treat – Prevent – Build (DTPB)**

1. **Detect**
   - Test every person with presumptive TB (cough for two weeks or more, fever, weight loss, night sweats).
   - Rapid molecular tests (CBNAAT/GeneXpert, Truenat) detect TB and rifampicin resistance; sputum microscopy; chest X-ray.
   - Active case finding in high-risk groups (slums, prisons, tribal areas, people with HIV or diabetes).
   - **Mandatory notification** of TB cases by private doctors, labs and chemists.
2. **Treat**
   - Free, quality-assured drugs as daily fixed-dose combinations for drug-sensitive TB.
   - Shorter oral regimens for drug-resistant TB at specialised centres.
   - Treatment supporters, adherence technologies, follow-up.
   - Patient support: **Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana** gives nutritional support by direct benefit transfer.
3. **Prevent**
   - TB preventive treatment for household contacts and people living with HIV.
   - Airborne infection control in health facilities; BCG vaccination of infants.
   - Address social determinants (nutrition, housing).
4. **Build**
   - **Ni-kshay**, the online case-based patient management system.
   - Partnerships with private sector and communities; **Pradhan Mantri TB Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan**, in which "Ni-kshay Mitras" (donors) adopt patients and give nutrition support.
   - Research and human resources.

**Outcome:** better case notification (including from the private sector), wider rapid molecular testing, free daily regimens, and a fall in incidence and deaths, though TB remains a major public health problem.

**Pharmacist's role:** refer people with cough of two weeks or more; act as treatment supporter; ensure no breaks in treatment and counsel on side effects (for example, rifampicin colours urine red-orange; report jaundice or vision changes); **follow rules on sale and recording of anti-TB drugs and notify cases** dispensed from the pharmacy; stock management of programme drugs; awareness on World TB Day (24 March).

## 3.3 Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP)

**Background:** Launched in **November 2004 with World Bank assistance** as a project, later continued as a programme under NHM. The **Integrated Health Information Platform (IHIP)** now carries IDSP's near-real-time electronic reporting.

**Objectives**

- Strengthen and maintain a **decentralised, laboratory-based, IT-enabled** disease surveillance system for epidemic-prone diseases.
- Detect early warning signals of outbreaks and **respond quickly** with trained rapid response teams.

**Functioning**

| Reporting form | Who reports | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **S** (syndromic) | Health workers (ANM, MPW) at sub-centre level | Syndromes, e.g., fever, cough > 2 weeks, loose stools, jaundice, acute flaccid paralysis |
| **P** (presumptive) | Medical officers at PHCs, CHCs, hospitals | Clinical diagnosis by a doctor |
| **L** (laboratory) | Laboratories | Laboratory-confirmed cases |

- Weekly reporting from all reporting units, plus immediate reporting of unusual events.
- Surveillance units at central, state and district levels; **rapid response teams** investigate outbreaks.
- Referral laboratory network; media scanning for health events; data analysis and feedback.
- Diseases covered include acute diarrhoeal disease and cholera, typhoid, hepatitis, malaria, dengue, chikungunya, measles, acute encephalitis syndrome, influenza-like illness, and others as decided by states.

**Outcome:** thousands of outbreaks are detected and investigated each year; the system supports response to new threats such as pandemic influenza and COVID-19.

**Pharmacist's role:** report unusual increases in sales of ORS, antidiarrhoeals, antipyretics or antimalarials (an early outbreak signal); supply during outbreaks; report notifiable diseases; take part in rapid response.

## 3.4 National Leprosy Eradication Programme (NLEP)

**Background:** A national leprosy control programme began in **1955** (dapsone monotherapy). It was redesignated the **National Leprosy Eradication Programme in 1983**, when multidrug therapy (MDT) came into use. India reached **elimination of leprosy as a public health problem (prevalence below 1 per 10,000 population) at the national level in December 2005**. Check the current strategic plan and its target year on the leprosy programme page of nhm.gov.in or mohfw.gov.in.

**Objectives**

- Reduce the leprosy burden further and interrupt transmission at district and sub-district levels.
- Prevent disabilities, especially grade 2 disabilities (visible deformities).
- Reduce stigma and discrimination.

**Functioning**

1. **Early case detection** — leprosy case detection campaigns (house-to-house search), focused campaigns in high-endemic areas, examination of contacts, the **Sparsh Leprosy Awareness Campaign**.
2. **Free MDT** in calendar blister packs for paucibacillary and multibacillary leprosy, given through all government health facilities.
3. **Post-exposure prophylaxis** — single-dose rifampicin for eligible contacts.
4. **Disability prevention and medical rehabilitation (DPMR)** — self-care of anaesthetic hands and feet, microcellular rubber footwear, splints, reconstructive surgery.
5. IEC to reduce stigma; social and economic rehabilitation.
6. Surveillance and recording through the programme information system.

**Outcome:** national elimination as a public health problem (2005); large fall in prevalence; new cases still occur, including in children, so efforts continue towards zero transmission.

**Pharmacist's role:** recognise pale or reddish **anaesthetic skin patches** and refer; counsel on regular MDT intake and expected effects (rifampicin red urine, clofazimine skin darkening that fades later); recognise lepra reactions (painful nerves, new patches, fever) and refer; fight stigma (leprosy is curable; patients on MDT quickly become non-infectious).

## 3.5 National Mental Health Programme (NMHP)

**Background:** Launched in **1982**. The **District Mental Health Programme (DMHP)** was launched under it in **1996**, based on the Bellary model. The **National Tele Mental Health Programme (Tele-MANAS)** was launched on **10 October 2022** with the toll-free number **14416** (also 1-800-891-4416). The **Mental Healthcare Act, 2017** gives rights-based legal backing.

**Objectives**

- Ensure availability and accessibility of minimum mental health care for all, especially the vulnerable and underprivileged.
- Encourage the application of mental health knowledge in general health care and social development.
- Promote community participation in developing mental health services and self-help.

**Functioning**

| Component | Activities |
| --- | --- |
| DMHP | Early detection and treatment in districts, outpatient and outreach clinics, short training of general doctors and health workers, IEC, simple record keeping |
| Tertiary care | Strengthening psychiatric departments and mental hospitals; Centres of Excellence; post-graduate training in psychiatry, clinical psychology, psychiatric social work and psychiatric nursing |
| Life-skills and suicide prevention | School and college mental health, workplace stress management, suicide prevention services |
| Tele-MANAS | 24×7 free tele-counselling in many languages, with referral to services |
| Primary care | Mental health services at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (screening for depression and other common disorders) |

**Outcome:** DMHP extended to most districts; tele-counselling widely used; but the treatment gap (people with mental disorders who receive no care) remains large, and stigma persists.

**Pharmacist's role:** recognise depression, anxiety, suicide risk and substance misuse, and refer (Tele-MANAS 14416); counsel that antidepressants take weeks to act and must not be stopped suddenly; monitor side effects; safe storage of psychotropic drugs; dispense controlled drugs as per rules; reduce stigma.

## 3.6 National Programme for Prevention and Control of Deafness (NPPCD)

**Background:** Started as a **pilot in 2006–07 (January 2007) in 25 districts** and expanded in phases. Check mohfw.gov.in for the current coverage.

**Objectives**

- Prevent avoidable hearing loss due to disease or injury.
- Early identification, diagnosis and treatment of ear problems causing hearing loss and deafness.
- Medical rehabilitation of people of all ages with deafness.
- Strengthen links with other rehabilitation programmes and build institutional capacity (district hospitals, CHCs, PHCs) for ear care.

**Functioning**

1. Training of ENT specialists, audiologists, medical officers and health workers.
2. Equipment for ENT and audiology at district hospitals; audiometry.
3. Screening of newborns and school children; camps for early detection.
4. Free hearing aids and rehabilitation (speech therapy); link with cochlear implant schemes.
5. IEC — avoid putting objects in ears, early treatment of ear discharge, protection from noise, avoid ototoxic drugs without supervision.

**Common causes of preventable deafness:** chronic ear infections (chronic suppurative otitis media), wax, noise exposure, ototoxic drugs (for example aminoglycosides), measles, mumps and meningitis, rubella in pregnancy, birth asphyxia and neonatal jaundice.

**Pharmacist's role:** counsel against self-medication with ear drops in a discharging ear; warn about ototoxic drugs and refer for hearing checks when used long term; advise ear protection in noisy work; refer children with ear discharge or poor speech development.

## 3.7 Universal Immunization Programme (UIP)

**Background:** Immunisation began as the **Expanded Programme of Immunization (EPI) in 1978**; it was expanded as the **Universal Immunization Programme in 1985**. It is a key part of NHM. **Mission Indradhanush** and its intensified rounds are catch-up drives for children and pregnant women who have missed doses. The electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network (**eVIN**) tracks vaccine stocks and cold-chain temperatures. **U-WIN** records vaccinations digitally.

**Objectives**

- Rapidly increase immunisation coverage and keep it high.
- Improve quality of services; maintain a reliable **cold chain**.
- Monitor performance district-wise; achieve self-sufficiency in vaccine production.
- Protect children and pregnant women against vaccine-preventable diseases.

**Vaccines in the national schedule (main ones)**

| Age/group | Vaccines |
| --- | --- |
| Pregnant women | Td |
| Birth | BCG, OPV zero dose, hepatitis B birth dose |
| Infancy (6, 10, 14 weeks) | OPV, pentavalent (DPT + hepatitis B + Hib), rotavirus vaccine, fractional IPV, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV) |
| 9–12 months | Measles–rubella (MR) first dose, PCV booster, JE first dose (endemic districts), vitamin A |
| 16–24 months | MR second dose, DPT first booster, OPV booster, JE second dose (endemic districts) |
| 5–6 years | DPT second booster |
| 10 and 16 years | Td |

> The schedule is revised from time to time (new vaccines and doses are added). Check the current National Immunization Schedule on mohfw.gov.in before writing an exam table.

**Functioning:** routine immunisation sessions at sub-centres, Anganwadi centres and outreach sites on fixed days; ASHAs mobilise beneficiaries; cold chain from national stores down to ice-lined refrigerators and vaccine carriers; vaccine vial monitors; **AEFI (adverse events following immunisation) surveillance**; open-vial policy; supportive supervision.

**Outcome:** polio-free certification (2014); elimination of maternal and neonatal tetanus; marked rise in full immunisation coverage; addition of newer vaccines (rotavirus, PCV, MR).

**Pharmacist's role:** maintain cold chain (2–8 °C for most vaccines; never freeze freeze-sensitive vaccines such as DPT, hepatitis B, Td, PCV); check vaccine vial monitors and expiry; remind parents of due dates; counsel about common mild reactions; report AEFI; counter vaccine myths.

## 3.8 National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment (NPCB&VI)

**Background:** Launched in **1976** as a 100% centrally sponsored scheme (the earlier Trachoma Control Programme of 1963 was merged into it). It was **renamed the National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment in 2017** to cover all kinds of visual impairment, not only blindness. Its stated goal (government statement, 2022) is to reduce the prevalence of avoidable blindness to **0.25% by 2025**; check the current target on the programme page of mohfw.gov.in.

**Objectives**

- Reduce the backlog of avoidable blindness by identifying and treating the blind at primary, secondary and tertiary levels.
- Develop comprehensive eye care facilities in every district.
- Develop human resources for eye care; improve service quality.
- Secure participation of voluntary organisations and the private sector.

**Major causes of blindness in India:** cataract (the leading cause), refractive errors, glaucoma, corneal blindness, diabetic retinopathy, childhood blindness (including vitamin A deficiency).

**Functioning**

1. Free **cataract surgery** with intraocular lens implantation.
2. **School eye screening** and free spectacles for children with refractive errors; spectacles for elderly people with presbyopia.
3. Eye banks, eye donation awareness and corneal transplantation.
4. Treatment of glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy and other eye diseases; vision centres at PHCs; mobile ophthalmic units.
5. Training of eye surgeons and ophthalmic assistants; funding of NGOs.
6. National Eye Donation Fortnight and other IEC.

**Outcome:** large numbers of cataract operations every year and a fall in the prevalence of blindness, though cataract and refractive errors remain major causes.

**Pharmacist's role:** encourage eye checks for diabetics and the elderly; counsel on correct eye-drop use and avoiding steroid eye drops without prescription (risk of glaucoma and cataract); vitamin A awareness; promote eye donation.

## 3.9 Pulse Polio Programme

**Background:** The **Pulse Polio Immunization (National Immunization Day) programme was launched in 1995** to eradicate poliomyelitis, as part of the global polio eradication effort. India's **last case of wild poliovirus was reported in January 2011** (Howrah, West Bengal), and the WHO South-East Asia Region, including India, was **certified polio-free on 27 March 2014**.

**Objectives**

- Interrupt transmission of wild poliovirus.
- Reach every child under five years with extra doses of oral polio vaccine, in addition to routine immunisation, to raise population immunity.
- Maintain polio-free status until global eradication.

**Functioning**

1. **National and sub-national immunisation days** — all children under five get OPV drops on the same day(s), regardless of previous doses ("pulse" = simultaneous mass dosing).
2. Booth day followed by **house-to-house** visits to reach missed children; transit teams at bus and railway stations; mobile teams for migrants, brick kilns and construction sites.
3. Marking of the child's finger and the house to track coverage.
4. **Acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance** — every case of AFP in a child under 15 years is reported and stool samples are tested; environmental (sewage) surveillance.
5. Introduction of injectable polio vaccine in routine immunisation; cross-border vaccination posts; preparedness to respond to any imported case.

**Outcome:** India, once one of the countries with the highest polio burden, has been polio-free since 2011 and certified in 2014.

**Pharmacist's role:** publicise pulse polio dates, act as a vaccination booth and help with cold chain, counter rumours (for example that drops cause infertility), report AFP cases.

## 3.10 Common role of the pharmacist in national health programmes

1. **Logistics** — forecasting, storage, cold chain, stock records, avoiding stock-outs and expiry of programme drugs and vaccines.
2. **Dispensing and adherence counselling** — TB, HIV, leprosy, malaria and mental illness need long or complete courses.
3. **Health education** — on health days (World TB Day, World AIDS Day, World Mental Health Day, World Leprosy Day).
4. **Screening and referral** — cough, anaesthetic patches, ear discharge, poor vision, fever, depression.
5. **Surveillance and reporting** — TB notification, AEFI, adverse drug reactions to the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India, unusual drug sales.
6. **Fighting stigma and keeping confidentiality.**
