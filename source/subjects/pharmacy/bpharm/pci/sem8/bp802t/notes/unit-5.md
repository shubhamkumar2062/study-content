# Unit 5: Community Services in Rural, Urban and School Health

This unit is about **where** people get preventive and primary care: primary health centres in villages, urban health services under the National Urban Health Mission, and schools. It also covers rural sanitation and the methods of health promotion and education that link everything together.

> Population norms and scheme names below were checked on National Health Mission sources (nhm.gov.in) and Ministry of Health documents. Names change (for example, Health and Wellness Centres are now called **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs**); check the current terms on nhm.gov.in.

## 5.1 Primary health care — the concept

**Primary health care (PHC)** was defined at the **Alma-Ata Conference (1978)** as essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound and socially acceptable methods, made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community, through their full participation, at a cost the community and country can afford. It is the **first level of contact** with the health system and brings care **as close as possible to where people live and work**.

**Principles of primary health care**

1. **Equitable distribution** — services for all, especially the rural poor and underserved.
2. **Community participation** — people plan, run and monitor their own health care.
3. **Intersectoral coordination** — health works with agriculture, education, water, housing, food and communication.
4. **Appropriate technology** — methods that are scientifically sound, acceptable, affordable and locally maintainable (for example, ORS rather than IV fluids for most diarrhoea).
5. Focus on prevention and health promotion.

**Essential elements of primary health care (Alma-Ata) — memory aid "ELEMENTS"**

```text
E  Education about prevailing health problems and their prevention
L  Locally endemic disease prevention and control
E  Expanded immunisation against major infectious diseases
M  Maternal and child health, including family planning
E  Essential drugs provision
N  Nutrition and food supply
T  Treatment of common diseases and injuries
S  Safe water and basic sanitation
```

## 5.2 Rural health infrastructure in India

```text
                 District hospital
                        ↑ referral
        Community Health Centre (CHC) — first referral unit, specialists
                        ↑
        Primary Health Centre (PHC) — medical officer
                        ↑
   Sub-centre / Ayushman Arogya Mandir — ANM, multipurpose worker, community health officer
                        ↑
   Village: ASHA, Anganwadi worker, Village Health, Sanitation and Nutrition Committee
```

**Population norms (Indian Public Health Standards / NHM)**

| Facility | Plain areas | Hilly, tribal, difficult areas |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sub-centre | 5,000 | 3,000 |
| Primary Health Centre | 30,000 | 20,000 |
| Community Health Centre | 1,20,000 | 80,000 |

A CHC is a 30-bedded hospital with specialists (physician, surgeon, obstetrician–gynaecologist, paediatrician) and serves as a referral unit for about four PHCs. Many sub-centres and PHCs have been upgraded into **Ayushman Arogya Mandirs**, which give comprehensive primary care including screening for non-communicable diseases, mental health, elderly care, eye and ENT care, free essential medicines and diagnostics, and wellness activities such as yoga.

## 5.3 Functions of the Primary Health Centre (PHC)

The PHC is the first contact point between the village community and a medical officer. It usually has about six beds and is supported by sub-centres.

**Functions**

1. **Medical care** — outpatient treatment of common illnesses and injuries; basic inpatient care; 24×7 delivery services at many PHCs; emergency care and referral to CHC/district hospital.
2. **Maternal and child health** — antenatal, natal and postnatal care; newborn care; growth monitoring; management of diarrhoea and pneumonia; high-risk pregnancy referral.
3. **Family planning** — counselling, contraceptives, IUCD insertion, sterilisation camps or referral.
4. **Immunisation** — routine immunisation, cold chain point, outreach sessions.
5. **Control of communicable diseases** — TB, leprosy, malaria and vector-borne diseases, HIV testing and referral, IDSP reporting.
6. **Non-communicable diseases** — screening for hypertension, diabetes and common cancers; follow-up and free drugs.
7. **Environmental sanitation and safe water** — chlorination of wells, sanitary latrines, health inspection.
8. **Health education** — individual counselling, group talks, campaigns, health days.
9. **School health services** — check-ups, deworming, iron–folic acid, eye screening, referral.
10. **Basic laboratory services** — haemoglobin, urine tests, blood sugar, malaria tests, sputum, pregnancy test.
11. **Collection of vital statistics and records** — births, deaths, disease reports, health management information system.
12. **Supervision and training** of sub-centre staff, ASHAs and village committees.
13. **Implementation of national health programmes** at the local level.
14. **Drug supply** — free essential medicines through the PHC pharmacy.

**Staff of a typical PHC:** medical officer(s), **pharmacist**, staff nurses, lady health visitor, health assistants / multipurpose workers, laboratory technician, clerk and support staff. (Exact staffing follows IPHS norms; check nhm.gov.in.)

**Role of the pharmacist at the PHC:** indenting, storage and issue of drugs and vaccines (cold chain), dispensing and counselling, maintaining stock and expiry records, supporting national programme drug supply (TB, leprosy, malaria), health education, assisting in camps, reporting adverse drug reactions.

## 5.4 Improvement in rural sanitation

**Environmental sanitation** is the control of all factors in the physical environment that harm health. In villages the main problems are open defecation, unsafe water, poor drainage, cattle near houses, refuse heaps and breeding of flies and mosquitoes.

**Components of rural sanitation and how to improve them**

| Component | Improvement |
| --- | --- |
| **Excreta disposal** | Sanitary latrines in every household: **pour-flush water-seal latrine**, **twin-pit latrine** (one pit used while the other matures into manure), septic tank where suitable; community and school toilets; end open defecation |
| **Safe water supply** | Protected sources (sanitary wells with parapet and cover, hand pumps, piped water); regular **chlorination** with bleaching powder; household methods — boiling, chlorine tablets, filtration; protection of sources from latrines and cattle |
| **Liquid waste (sullage)** | Soak pits, kitchen gardens using wastewater, covered drains |
| **Solid waste** | Collection and segregation; composting (pits), vermicomposting; biogas plants from cattle dung |
| **Housing** | Ventilated houses, separate cattle sheds, smokeless chulhas or clean cooking fuel |
| **Food hygiene** | Clean food handling, covered food, safe milk |
| **Vector control** | Removing stagnant water, larvivorous fish, drainage, clean surroundings (reduces malaria, dengue, filariasis, flies) |
| **Health education** | Hand washing with soap, use of toilets by all family members, menstrual hygiene |

**Chlorination of water (textbook principle):** enough chlorine is added to satisfy the chlorine demand (**break-point chlorination**) and leave a free residual chlorine of about **0.5 mg per litre after one hour of contact**, which protects against later contamination. Wells are disinfected with **bleaching powder** (about one-third available chlorine), mixed into a paste and added to the well.

**Programmes**

- **Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen)**, launched on **2 October 2014**, aimed at making rural India open-defecation free (ODF) through household toilets, behaviour change and community action; its later phase focuses on sustaining ODF status and solid and liquid waste management ("ODF Plus").
- Piped tap water to rural households is being extended under the **Jal Jeevan Mission** (check current progress on the mission's official site).
- Village Health, Sanitation and Nutrition Committees use NHM untied funds for local sanitation.

**Health benefits of good sanitation:** fewer diarrhoeal diseases, cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A and E, polio, worm infestations and trachoma; less malnutrition and stunting; dignity and safety for women.

## 5.5 National Urban Health Mission (NUHM)

**Background:** The Union Cabinet approved NUHM on **1 May 2013** as a **sub-mission of the National Health Mission**, alongside NRHM. It covers cities and towns with a population of more than **50,000**, and district and state headquarters.

**Why it was needed:** rapid urban growth; large slum populations with poor water and sanitation; health indicators of the urban poor often worse than rural averages; scarcity of public primary care in cities (most urban primary care was private and costly); high NCD, TB and vector-borne disease burden.

**Objectives**

- Meet the health needs of the **urban poor**, especially slum dwellers and other vulnerable groups, by making essential primary health care available and reducing out-of-pocket spending.
- Strengthen the public health system in cities.
- Partner with urban local bodies, NGOs and the private sector.

**Vulnerable groups focused on:** slum dwellers, homeless people, rag pickers, street children, rickshaw pullers, construction and brick-kiln workers, sex workers, temporary migrants.

**Functioning — structure**

| Level | Facility / mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Community | **ASHA** for urban slums; **Mahila Arogya Samiti** (a women's group of the slum that works on health, nutrition, water and sanitation with an untied fund) |
| Outreach | Urban health and nutrition days, special outreach camps in slums, mobile medical units |
| Primary | **Urban Primary Health Centre (U-PHC)**, generally one for about **50,000 population**, located in or near a slum, with OPD in convenient hours (afternoon/evening), free drugs and tests, immunisation, family planning, NCD screening; many now run as urban Ayushman Arogya Mandirs |
| Secondary | **Urban Community Health Centre (U-CHC)** and district hospitals for referral |

**Convergence:** with urban local bodies (water, sanitation, waste), ICDS, school health, housing and livelihood missions.

**Outcome:** expanded free primary care in urban slums, better reach of immunisation, family planning and NCD screening, though staff shortages and slum coverage remain challenges.

**Pharmacist's role:** dispensing at U-PHCs; community pharmacists in cities support the mission by counselling, referral, screening (BP, sugar), promoting Jan Aushadhi generics and taking part in slum health camps.

## 5.6 Health promotion and education

**Health promotion** (WHO, Ottawa Charter 1986): the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health.

**Five action areas of the Ottawa Charter**

1. Build healthy public policy (for example, taxes on tobacco, ban on smoking in public places).
2. Create supportive environments (safe water, parks, healthy school canteens).
3. Strengthen community action.
4. Develop personal skills (health education, life skills).
5. Reorient health services towards prevention and promotion.

**Health education** is a process that informs, motivates and helps people adopt and maintain healthy practices and lifestyles. It aims at changes in **knowledge, attitude and practice (KAP)**.

**Principles of health education**

| Principle | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Interest | Start with what people are interested in ("felt needs") |
| Participation | Active involvement — discussion, demonstration |
| Proceed from known to unknown | Build on existing knowledge |
| Comprehension | Simple local language, level of the audience |
| Reinforcement | Repeat the message in different ways |
| Motivation | Create a desire to change; use incentives |
| Learning by doing | Demonstrations, practice (ORS preparation, hand washing) |
| Credibility | Message from a trusted source, consistent with facts |
| Good human relations | Friendly, respectful communicator |
| Leaders | Use opinion leaders, teachers, religious leaders |
| Soil, seed and sower | The people (soil), the message (seed) and the educator (sower) must all be right |

**Methods (approaches) of health education**

| Approach | Examples | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individual | Counselling at the pharmacy counter, home visit | Personal problems, adherence, contraception |
| Group | Lecture, demonstration, group discussion, panel discussion, symposium, role play, workshop | Mothers' groups, school classes |
| Mass | Television, radio, newspapers, posters, hoardings, social media, health exhibitions, street plays | Wide awareness (polio, tobacco, dengue) |

**Communication process**

```text
Communicator (source) → Message → Channel → Audience (receiver) → Feedback
```

Barriers: language, literacy, cultural beliefs, noise, poor timing, lack of trust.

## 5.7 Health promotion and education in schools (school health services)

**Why school health matters:** school children form a large part of the population; they are at a learning stage when habits form; they carry messages home; many problems (anaemia, worms, poor vision, dental caries, malnutrition, mental stress) can be found and corrected early.

**Objectives of school health services**

- Promote positive health and healthy habits.
- Prevent disease; detect and correct defects early.
- Provide a healthy school environment.
- Create health awareness among children, parents and teachers.

**Components (aspects) of school health services**

1. **Health appraisal** — periodic medical examination of students (and teachers): height, weight, vision, hearing, teeth, anaemia, skin; daily morning observation by teachers.
2. **Remedial measures and follow-up** — treatment, spectacles, dental care, referral.
3. **Prevention of communicable diseases** — immunisation checks, Td vaccination, isolation of sick children, deworming.
4. **Healthful school environment** — safe site, adequate classrooms with light and ventilation, proper desks, safe drinking water, separate clean toilets for girls and boys, hand-washing stations, waste disposal, playground.
5. **Nutritional services** — hot cooked school meal (PM POSHAN), weekly iron–folic acid supplementation, nutrition education, healthy canteens.
6. **First aid and emergency care** — first-aid box, trained teachers.
7. **Mental health** — counselling, prevention of bullying and exam stress, life-skills.
8. **Dental, eye and ear health** — screening and education.
9. **Health education** — personal hygiene, hand washing, menstrual hygiene, nutrition, road safety, substance abuse prevention, reproductive and sexual health appropriate to age.
10. **Education of children with special needs** (inclusive education).
11. **School health records** — cumulative health card for each child.

**Programmes in schools (India)**

- **Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK)** — mobile health teams screen children for the 4 Ds (defects, deficiencies, diseases, developmental delays) in Anganwadis and government schools.
- **School Health and Wellness Programme** under Ayushman Bharat — trained teachers ("health and wellness ambassadors") teach health topics in a weekly session; health screening through RBSK teams.
- **Weekly Iron and Folic Acid Supplementation (WIFS)** for adolescents; **National Deworming Day** (twice a year).
- **Tobacco-free educational institutions**; COTPA bans tobacco sale near schools.
- Adolescent education and RKSK peer educators; school eye screening under NPCB&VI.

**Health-promoting school (WHO idea):** a school that constantly strengthens its capacity as a healthy setting for living, learning and working — health is built into the curriculum, environment, services and community links.

**Role of the pharmacist in school health:** talks on hand hygiene, safe medicine use, antibiotic misuse, dangers of tobacco and drugs; first-aid training; checking school first-aid kits; supporting deworming and IFA days (explaining that mild stomach upset can occur); anaemia and menstrual hygiene awareness.

## 5.8 The community pharmacist in rural and urban health

1. First point of contact for many people — triage and referral to PHC/U-PHC.
2. Screening — BP, blood sugar, BMI, peak flow.
3. Rational medicine use — no antibiotics without prescription, generic substitution where allowed, Jan Aushadhi.
4. Supply and counselling for programme drugs, vaccines and contraceptives.
5. Health education on health days and in schools.
6. Surveillance — notifying TB cases, reporting adverse drug reactions, spotting unusual sales during outbreaks.
7. Emergency and disaster support.
