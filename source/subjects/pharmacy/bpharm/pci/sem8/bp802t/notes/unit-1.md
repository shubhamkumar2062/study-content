# Unit 1: Concept of Health and Disease, Nutrition, Sociology and Hygiene

This unit lays the base for the whole subject. It answers four questions: **What is health? What causes disease and how do we prevent it? How does food affect health? How do society, poverty, cities and personal habits affect health?** Almost every later unit (diseases, national programmes, community services) builds on these ideas.

## 1.1 Concept of health

**WHO definition (1948):** "Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity."

Points to note when you explain the definition:

- It is **positive**: health is more than "no disease".
- It is **multidimensional**: body, mind and social life all count.
- It is an **ideal**: "complete" well-being is hard to reach, so it is better seen as a goal.
- A fourth dimension, **spiritual health**, is often added. Many textbooks also mention emotional, vocational and environmental dimensions.

**Changing concepts of health**

| Concept | Main idea | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biomedical | Health = absence of disease; disease is caused by germs or body faults | Ignores mind, society and environment |
| Ecological | Health = balance between person and environment (agent, host, environment) | Hard to measure |
| Psychosocial | Social, psychological, cultural and economic factors shape health | Can underplay biology |
| Holistic | Combines all the above; looks at the whole person and the community | Broad, needs many sectors to work together |

**Dimensions of health**

1. **Physical** — organs work normally; normal pulse, BP, weight, sleep, appetite.
2. **Mental** — free from mental illness, able to cope with stress, think clearly, work productively.
3. **Social** — good relations with family and society; able to fit into a community.
4. **Spiritual** — sense of purpose, values and inner peace.
5. **Emotional, vocational, environmental** — feeling in control of emotions, satisfaction from work, a safe surrounding.

**Spectrum of health:** health and disease are not two boxes but a continuous line.

```text
Positive health → Better health → Freedom from sickness → Unrecognised sickness → Mild sickness → Severe sickness → Death
```

**Determinants of health:** heredity (genes), environment (water, air, housing), lifestyle (diet, exercise, tobacco, alcohol), socio-economic conditions (income, education, occupation), health services (availability, quality, cost), and other factors (gender, culture, politics).

**Indicators of health** (how we measure the health of a community): mortality indicators (crude death rate, infant mortality rate, maternal mortality ratio, life expectancy), morbidity indicators (incidence, prevalence), disability rates, nutritional status (low birth weight, stunting), health-care delivery (doctor–population ratio), utilisation (immunisation coverage, institutional deliveries), social and economic indicators (literacy, income), environmental indicators (safe water, sanitation).

## 1.2 Concept of disease

**Disease** is a departure from normal structure or function that produces signs and symptoms. Three related words:

- **Disease** — the biological abnormality (doctor's view).
- **Illness** — how the person feels about being unwell (patient's view).
- **Sickness** — the social role of being unwell (society's view; e.g., leave from work).

**Epidemiological triad**

```text
            AGENT
     (germ, chemical, deficiency)
           /      \
          /        \
       HOST ------ ENVIRONMENT
 (age, sex, immunity,   (physical, biological,
  genes, habits)          social)
```

Disease results when the balance among agent, host and environment is disturbed. Prevention aims to restore that balance (kill the agent, protect the host, improve the environment).

**Multifactorial causation** applies to non-communicable diseases such as coronary heart disease: smoking, diet, obesity, high BP, stress, lack of exercise and genes all act together. There is no single "agent".

**Natural history of disease** has two periods:

```text
PRE-PATHOGENESIS (before the person is affected)      PATHOGENESIS (inside the host)
Agent, host and environment interact      →   Early changes → Signs and symptoms → Recovery / Disability / Death
```

**Iceberg of disease:** the cases seen in hospitals are only the tip. Below the surface lie undiagnosed, mild and carrier cases. Screening and surveillance try to find this hidden part (for example, undiagnosed diabetes and hypertension).

## 1.3 Public health and its evolution

**Public health** (Winslow's idea, in short): the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organised efforts of society.

**How public health developed ("evolution of public health")**

| Phase | Key events |
| --- | --- |
| Ancient | Indus valley drains and baths; Ayurveda's rules on diet and daily routine; Hippocrates linked disease with air, water and place |
| Disease-control phase | John Snow linked cholera to a contaminated water pump in London (1854); sanitation reforms in England |
| Germ theory | Pasteur and Koch proved that microbes cause disease; vaccines developed (Jenner's smallpox vaccination, 1796) |
| Health-promotion phase | Focus on lifestyle and behaviour; Alma-Ata Declaration on primary health care (1978); Ottawa Charter for health promotion (1986) |
| Modern | Global goals (Sustainable Development Goals), universal health coverage, surveillance for new infections |

**"Evaluation of public health"** in the syllabus can be answered by describing how public health action is judged: by health indicators (falling IMR and MMR, rising life expectancy), by coverage (immunisation, safe water, institutional delivery), by programme outcomes (polio eliminated, leprosy eliminated as a public health problem) and by equity (whether the poor benefit).

## 1.4 Prevention and control of disease

**Levels of prevention**

| Level | Stage of disease | Aim | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primordial | Before risk factors appear | Stop risk factors from arising in a population | Healthy school food, ban on tobacco advertising, safe play areas |
| Primary | Risk factors present, no disease | Prevent the disease | Immunisation, safe water, condoms, mosquito nets, iodised salt |
| Secondary | Early disease | Early diagnosis and prompt treatment | Screening for BP, diabetes, cervical cancer; sputum test for TB |
| Tertiary | Established disease | Limit disability, rehabilitate | Physiotherapy after stroke, artificial limbs, leprosy footwear |

Some books add **quaternary prevention**: protecting patients from over-medicalisation and unnecessary tests or drugs.

**Modes of intervention (Leavell and Clark)**

1. Health promotion — health education, nutrition, housing, lifestyle change.
2. Specific protection — vaccines, chemoprophylaxis, protection against occupational hazards.
3. Early diagnosis and treatment — screening, case finding.
4. Disability limitation — complete treatment to stop complications.
5. Rehabilitation — medical, vocational, social and psychological.

**Control, elimination and eradication**

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control | Reduce incidence or prevalence to a level where it is no longer a major problem | Malaria control |
| Elimination | Stop transmission in a defined area, or reduce below a set level ("as a public health problem") | Leprosy eliminated as a public health problem in India |
| Eradication | Remove the infection worldwide; no more cases anywhere | Smallpox |

## 1.5 Social causes of disease and social problems of the sick

**Social causes of disease**

- **Poverty** — poor food, overcrowded housing, no safe water.
- **Illiteracy** — less awareness of hygiene, late care seeking.
- **Overcrowding and slums** — spread of TB, respiratory and skin infections.
- **Unemployment** — stress, depression, alcohol misuse.
- **Occupation** — dust diseases, injuries, pesticide exposure.
- **Customs and beliefs** — child marriage, food taboos in pregnancy, belief that illness is a curse.
- **Gender inequality** — girls fed last, less care for women.
- **Addictions** — tobacco, alcohol, drugs.

**Social problems of the sick person**

- Loss of income, cost of treatment, debt (out-of-pocket spending).
- Stigma and discrimination (TB, leprosy, HIV, mental illness).
- Isolation, broken relationships, divorce.
- Loss of job or schooling.
- Dependence on others; burden on family carers.
- Psychological problems: fear, anxiety, depression.

The pharmacist helps by kind counselling, keeping confidentiality, guiding patients to free government schemes and support groups, and fighting myths.

## 1.6 Food in relation to nutrition and health

**Nutrition** is the science of food, its nutrients and their use by the body for growth, repair and energy.

| Nutrient group | Main role | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Main energy source | Cereals, millets, potatoes, sugar |
| Proteins | Growth and repair, enzymes, antibodies | Pulses, milk, eggs, meat, fish, soya |
| Fats | Concentrated energy, fat-soluble vitamins, essential fatty acids | Oils, ghee, nuts, seeds |
| Vitamins | Regulate body processes | Fruits, vegetables, milk, liver |
| Minerals | Bones, blood, nerve and muscle function | Milk (calcium), green leaves and jaggery (iron), iodised salt |
| Water | Medium for all body reactions, temperature control | Drinking water, food |
| Dietary fibre | Bowel movement, lowers cholesterol and blood sugar rise | Whole grains, vegetables, fruits |

**Energy values:** carbohydrate and protein about 4 kcal per gram; fat about 9 kcal per gram.

**Quality of protein:** cereals are low in lysine, pulses are low in methionine. Eating them together (dal-rice, khichdi) gives a better protein mix — the **supplementary action** of proteins.

## 1.7 Balanced diet

A **balanced diet** contains all nutrients in the right amounts and proportions to meet the body's needs, with a small extra for lean periods. It depends on age, sex, body weight, activity and physiological state (pregnancy, lactation).

**Principles**

- Include foods from all groups: cereals and millets; pulses, eggs, meat, fish; milk and milk products; vegetables and fruits; oils, fats and nuts.
- Prefer whole grains and millets; plenty of vegetables and fruits of different colours.
- Limit sugar, salt, refined foods, fried and ultra-processed foods.
- Use iodised salt; use oils in moderation and vary them.
- Drink safe water; be physically active.

> The National Institute of Nutrition (ICMR-NIN) publishes the Recommended Dietary Allowances and Dietary Guidelines for Indians. Check the latest edition for exact amounts.

**Special groups:** pregnant and lactating women need extra energy, protein, iron, folic acid and calcium; infants need exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, then complementary foods with continued breastfeeding; elderly need soft, fibre-rich, protein-adequate food with calcium and vitamin D.

## 1.8 Nutritional and vitamin deficiencies

**Vitamin deficiency diseases**

| Vitamin | Deficiency disease / signs |
| --- | --- |
| A (retinol) | Night blindness, conjunctival xerosis, Bitot's spots, keratomalacia (can cause blindness), poor immunity |
| D | Rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults |
| E | Nerve problems, haemolysis in premature infants |
| K | Bleeding tendency (haemorrhagic disease of the newborn) |
| B1 (thiamine) | Beriberi (dry, wet), Wernicke's encephalopathy in alcoholics |
| B2 (riboflavin) | Angular stomatitis, cheilosis, glossitis |
| B3 (niacin) | Pellagra: dermatitis, diarrhoea, dementia (death) |
| B6 (pyridoxine) | Peripheral neuropathy, convulsions in infants (may follow isoniazid use) |
| B12 | Megaloblastic anaemia, nerve damage |
| Folic acid | Megaloblastic anaemia; neural tube defects in the baby |
| C | Scurvy: bleeding gums, poor wound healing |

**Mineral deficiencies**

| Mineral | Deficiency |
| --- | --- |
| Iron | Iron-deficiency anaemia (fatigue, pallor, low work capacity, low birth weight) |
| Iodine | Goitre, cretinism, mental retardation, stillbirths — iodine deficiency disorders |
| Calcium | Poor bone growth, osteoporosis |
| Zinc | Poor growth, delayed wound healing, diarrhoea |
| Fluoride | Too little → dental caries; too much → fluorosis |

## 1.9 Malnutrition and its prevention

**Malnutrition** means either **undernutrition** (deficiency) or **overnutrition** (obesity, excess). India faces a "double burden" — undernutrition in children and women, and rising obesity and diabetes.

**Protein–energy malnutrition (PEM)**

| Feature | Marasmus | Kwashiorkor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main deficiency | Energy (and protein) | Mainly protein, with infection |
| Oedema | Absent | Present |
| Look | "Skin and bones", old man face | Moon face, puffy |
| Hair, skin | Less change | Flag sign in hair, flaky-paint skin |
| Liver | Normal | Fatty, enlarged |
| Appetite | Good | Poor |

**Anthropometric terms (children)**

- **Stunting** — low height for age (long-term undernutrition).
- **Wasting** — low weight for height (recent, acute undernutrition).
- **Underweight** — low weight for age (both).
- **Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)** (WHO criteria): weight-for-height below −3 SD, or mid-upper arm circumference below 115 mm, or bilateral pitting oedema (age 6–59 months).

**Causes:** poverty, poor feeding practices, early stopping of breastfeeding, repeated infections (diarrhoea, measles, worms), large families, ignorance, food taboos, poor sanitation.

**Prevention**

1. Exclusive breastfeeding to six months; timely, adequate complementary feeding.
2. Growth monitoring; nutrition education of mothers.
3. Supplementary nutrition through Anganwadis (ICDS) and school meals.
4. Food fortification (iodised salt, fortified rice, oil and milk).
5. Vitamin A supplementation, iron–folic acid, deworming.
6. Immunisation, safe water, sanitation and hand washing to cut infections.
7. Family planning and women's education.
8. Kitchen gardens and income support for poor families.

## 1.10 Sociology and health

**Sociology** studies human society and behaviour in groups. Medical sociology looks at how social factors affect health and illness.

**Socio-cultural factors related to health and disease**

- **Culture and beliefs** — traditional healers, belief in evil spirits, use of home remedies, delay in seeking care.
- **Customs** — early marriage, early pregnancy, dietary taboos, open defecation habits.
- **Family** — joint or nuclear family, family support for sick and elderly, family size.
- **Religion** — fasting, diet rules, attitude to contraception and blood transfusion.
- **Social class and caste** — access to schools, safe water and health care.
- **Gender roles** — women's control over money and decisions on care.
- **Education** — mother's education is linked strongly with child survival.

**Impact of urbanisation on health**

Urbanisation is the movement of people from villages to cities.

| Positive effects | Negative effects |
| --- | --- |
| Better access to hospitals, specialists and pharmacies | Slums, overcrowding, poor drainage and water |
| More education and jobs | Air, noise and water pollution |
| Better transport and communication | Road accidents, injuries |
| | Sedentary life, fast food → obesity, diabetes, hypertension |
| | Stress, mental illness, drug and alcohol misuse, crime |
| | Spread of TB, dengue, HIV; breakdown of family support |

**Poverty and health — the vicious cycle**

```text
Poverty → poor food, housing, water, education
   ↑                         ↓
Loss of earnings  ←  Ill health and disability
```

Poverty causes disease, and disease (cost of treatment, loss of work) pushes families deeper into poverty. Breaking the cycle needs free primary care, insurance cover against hospital costs, nutrition support and safe water and sanitation.

## 1.11 Hygiene and health

**Hygiene** is the practice of keeping oneself and one's surroundings clean to prevent disease.

**Personal hygiene**

| Area | Good practice |
| --- | --- |
| Skin and bathing | Daily bath, clean clothes; prevents scabies and fungal infections |
| Hands | Wash with soap and water before eating, after toilet, after handling waste — the single most useful habit against diarrhoea and respiratory infections |
| Hair and nails | Clean hair, short nails; prevents lice and worm eggs under nails |
| Teeth and mouth | Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste; avoid tobacco and too much sugar |
| Eyes, ears, nose | Do not share towels or kajal; no sharp objects in ears |
| Feet | Wear footwear outdoors (hookworm); keep feet dry |
| Clothing | Clean, seasonal, loose clothes |
| Sleep, rest, exercise | Adequate sleep, daily physical activity |
| Menstrual hygiene | Clean pads or cups, change regularly, safe disposal |
| Food hygiene | Wash raw foods, cook well, cover food, safe water |
| Respiratory etiquette | Cover coughs and sneezes, no spitting in public |

**Health care at home:** first-aid box, safe storage of medicines away from children, checking expiry dates, proper disposal of unused medicines.

**Avoidable habits** (harmful habits that people can choose to stop)

| Habit | Health effect |
| --- | --- |
| Smoking and chewing tobacco (gutka, khaini) | Oral, lung and throat cancer, heart disease, COPD, poor pregnancy outcomes |
| Alcohol misuse | Liver cirrhosis, accidents, violence, family breakdown |
| Drug misuse | Dependence, overdose deaths, HIV and hepatitis through shared needles |
| Junk food, overeating | Obesity, diabetes, heart disease |
| Sedentary life | Obesity, hypertension, diabetes |
| Self-medication, especially antibiotics | Drug resistance, adverse reactions |
| Open defecation, spitting | Spread of worms, diarrhoea, TB |
| Unsafe sex | HIV and other sexually transmitted infections |
| Excess screen time, poor sleep | Eye strain, obesity, poor mental health |

**Role of the pharmacist in this unit:** health education on diet, hygiene and habits; screening for anaemia signs, obesity, high BP; supplying and explaining iron, folic acid, ORS and supplements; tobacco cessation advice; referring malnourished children to health services.

## Remember

- WHO (1948): complete physical, mental and social well-being, not merely absence of disease.
- Triad = agent, host, environment; NCDs are multifactorial.
- Levels of prevention: primordial → primary → secondary → tertiary (+ quaternary).
- Leavell and Clark: promotion, specific protection, early diagnosis, disability limitation, rehabilitation.
- Elimination ≠ eradication; smallpox is the eradicated example.
- Kwashiorkor has oedema; marasmus does not. SAM: WHZ < −3 SD, MUAC < 115 mm, or bilateral oedema.
- Vitamin A → night blindness, Bitot's spots; niacin → pellagra (3 Ds); C → scurvy; folate → neural tube defects.
- Poverty and disease form a vicious cycle; urbanisation brings both benefits and NCD/slum problems.
- Hand washing with soap is the most valuable personal-hygiene habit.
