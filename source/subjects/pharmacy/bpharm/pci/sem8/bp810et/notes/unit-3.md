# Unit 3: Bioassays of Classical Autacoids

Autacoids (from the Greek autos = self, akos = remedy) are locally produced signalling molecules that act near their site of release. This unit covers the biology of the main autacoids and their bioassay on specific tissues or intact animal preparations.

## 3.1 Overview of classical autacoids

| Autacoid | Source | Key biological actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Histamine | Mast cells, basophils, ECL cells | H1: bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, oedema, itch; H2: gastric acid; H3: CNS autoreceptor |
| 5-Hydroxytryptamine (serotonin) | Enterochromaffin cells of gut, platelets, raphe nuclei | 5-HT2: smooth muscle contraction, vasoconstriction; 5-HT3: depolarisation, emesis; 5-HT4: GI motility |
| Bradykinin | Plasma kininogens by kallikrein | Pain, vasodilation, oedema, bronchoconstriction (B2 receptor) |
| Prostaglandins | Membrane phospholipids (arachidonic acid via COX) | PGE2: fever, pain, vasodilation; PGF2α: uterine/bronchial contraction; PGI2: vasodilation, antiplatelet |
| Platelet-activating factor (PAF) | Platelets, macrophages, endothelium | Platelet aggregation, bronchoconstriction, hypotension |
| Angiotensin II | Circulating substrate by renin and ACE | Vasoconstriction, aldosterone release, cardiac remodelling |

## 3.2 Bioassay of histamine

### 3.2.1 On guinea pig ileum (graded, in-vitro)

The guinea pig ileum is the classic preparation for histamine bioassay because it has abundant H1 receptors in the longitudinal smooth muscle and shows a large, reproducible contraction.

**Principle**: histamine contracts the ileum in a dose-related, graded fashion. Atropine does not block the response but mepyramine (an H1 blocker) does, confirming H1 mediation.

**Procedure**:
1. Prepare a 2–3 cm segment of ileum from a guinea pig.
2. Suspend in Krebs solution at 37°C with constant oxygenation; apply 1 g tension.
3. Equilibrate for 30–45 minutes.
4. Construct a log-dose response curve with standard histamine (doses 1–1000 ng/mL, usually 2–3 doses per decade).
5. Test the unknown preparation at an appropriate volume.
6. Read potency from the standard curve by interpolation.

**Sensitivity**: 1–10 ng/mL of histamine produces a reproducible contraction. This is far more sensitive than most chemical methods.

### 3.2.2 On cat blood pressure (fall in BP, in-vivo)

In an anaesthetised cat, intravenous histamine causes a fall in blood pressure through H1-mediated vasodilation and increased capillary permeability, followed by reflex tachycardia.

**Procedure**: the cat is anaesthetised (pentobarbitone 35 mg/kg i.v.), the carotid artery is cannulated and connected to a pressure transducer. Standard histamine doses are given intravenously, and the fall in mean arterial blood pressure is measured in mmHg.

This preparation is used for studying antihistamines and for bioassay of histamine in complex mixtures.

## 3.3 Bioassay of 5-hydroxytryptamine (serotonin)

### 3.3.1 Rat fundus strip

The fundal strip (from the stomach fundus of the rat) responds to 5-HT with a **contraction** mediated primarily by 5-HT2 receptors. It is insensitive to histamine and atropine, which helps distinguish 5-HT from these autacoids.

**Procedure**:
1. Prepare a longitudinal muscle strip from the fundus of a fasted rat.
2. Suspend in Krebs at 37°C; apply 0.5–1 g tension.
3. Equilibrate 30 min.
4. Add 5-HT doses (0.1–100 ng/mL) and record contractions.
5. Ketanserin (5-HT2 blocker) confirms the mediation.

**Uses**: bioassay of serotonin in blood or biological extracts; study of 5-HT2 receptor antagonists.

### 3.3.2 Clam heart preparation

The heart of a clam (Venus mercenaria or Mercenaria mercenaria) is very sensitive to 5-HT at concentrations as low as 0.5 ng/mL, with an increase in rate and force of contraction. It is relatively insensitive to histamine and catecholamines, making it selective for 5-HT bioassay. Used historically; now largely replaced by HPLC methods.

## 3.4 Bioassay of bradykinin

Bradykinin is a 9-amino-acid peptide formed by kallikrein-catalysed cleavage of plasma kininogens. It acts on B2 receptors to produce pain, vasodilation, oedema and bronchoconstriction.

### 3.4.1 On guinea pig ileum
Bradykinin contracts the guinea pig ileum through B2 receptors. The contraction is slower in onset and more sustained than the histamine response. It is not blocked by mepyramine (antihistamine) or atropine, but is blocked by icatibant (B2 receptor antagonist).

### 3.4.2 On rat uterus
Bradykinin produces a sustained contraction of the rat uterus. The uterus is sensitised by oestrogen. Bradykinin is distinguished from oxytocin by resistance to atosiban (oxytocin antagonist) but sensitivity to icatibant.

## 3.5 Bioassay of angiotensin

Angiotensin I (a decapeptide) and angiotensin II (an octapeptide) both raise blood pressure, but angiotensin II is 10–50 times more potent because it directly activates AT1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle.

### On rat blood pressure (rise in BP, in-vivo)

**Procedure**: the rat is anaesthetised (urethane 1.25 g/kg i.p.), the femoral vein is cannulated for injection and the carotid artery for pressure recording. Angiotensin I or II (10–100 ng/kg i.v.) produces a dose-related rise in mean arterial blood pressure.

**Comparison**: the pressor response to angiotensin I is abolished by captopril (ACE inhibitor), confirming that conversion to angiotensin II is necessary for the full effect. Angiotensin II's response is blocked by losartan (AT1 antagonist). This preparation distinguishes ACE inhibitors from AT1 antagonists.

## 3.6 Bioassay of prostaglandins

Prostaglandins are 20-carbon lipid mediators derived from arachidonic acid by cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2). They are extremely potent (active at pg/mL concentrations) but are rapidly metabolised in the circulation (half-life < 2 min), making in-vivo bioassay difficult unless a cascade system is used.

### Rat fundus strip
PGE2 and PGF2α both contract the rat fundus strip (through EP2/EP3 and FP receptors respectively). The contraction with prostaglandins is slower to develop than with 5-HT and is not blocked by methysergide or mepyramine.

### Rat colon
PGE2 relaxes the rat colon longitudinal muscle (EP2 receptor), while PGF2α contracts it (FP receptor). This opposite response pattern allows the two prostaglandin types to be distinguished in a cascade system.

## 3.7 Bioassay of acetylcholine

### 3.7.1 On frog heart (cardio-inhibitory response)
The isolated frog heart (perfused with frog Ringer) responds to acetylcholine with a slowing of rate and reduction in force (muscarinic M2 response). Atropine blocks this response. The preparation is highly sensitive (1–10 ng/mL). The response is measured as the number of beats per minute or as the amplitude of the phasic contractions.

### 3.7.2 On cat blood pressure (depressor effect)
Intravenous ACh in the anaesthetised cat produces a brief fall in blood pressure (muscarinic vasodilation) followed by a slight rise from nicotinic stimulation of sympathetic ganglia. After atropine, the fall disappears and a rise is unmasked (Dale's vasomotor reversal). Used to demonstrate muscarinic vs nicotinic effects and atropine antagonism.

## 3.8 Estimation from a standard curve (worked example)

```text
Problem: Estimate the histamine content of a plasma extract using the guinea pig ileum.

Standard curve data (histamine):
  Log conc (ng/mL)  Response (mm)
  0.5 (3.16 ng/mL)  8
  1.0 (10 ng/mL)   18
  1.5 (31.6 ng/mL) 29
  2.0 (100 ng/mL)  38

The standard curve is linear from log dose 0.5 to 2.0.
Slope b = (38 - 8) / (2.0 - 0.5) = 30 / 1.5 = 20 mm per log unit

Unknown plasma extract (0.5 mL added to bath) → response = 22 mm

From the curve: log(dose) = 0.5 + (22 - 8) / 20 = 0.5 + 0.70 = 1.20
Equivalent histamine dose = antilog(1.20) = 15.8 ng per bath

Bath volume = 25 mL; therefore concentration in bath = 15.8 / 25 = 0.63 ng/mL

Extract added = 0.5 mL; therefore concentration in extract = 15.8 / 0.5 = 31.6 ng/mL
```

## 3.9 In-vitro vs in-vivo bioassays

| Feature | In-vitro | In-vivo |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System used | Isolated tissue in organ bath | Whole anaesthetised or conscious animal |
| Complexity | Low (single tissue, defined receptor) | High (reflex loops, counter-regulation, metabolism) |
| Variables controlled | Temperature, pH, ionic composition | Many uncontrolled (anaesthesia, blood flow, etc.) |
| Drug access | Direct; no absorption or distribution step | Dependent on route, absorption, distribution |
| Variability | Lower | Higher |
| Information gained | Receptor type, mechanism, potency | Integrated pharmacological effect |
| Animal use | Fewer animals (one animal → multiple tissues) | More animals needed |
| Limitations | May miss systemic effects, pharmacokinetics | Cannot isolate a single receptor or tissue |

## 3.10 Cascade superfusion for multiple autacoid bioassay

In a **cascade superfusion** system, the effluent from one tissue bath (or from a reservoir containing the test sample) flows sequentially over a series of isolated tissue preparations, each selective for a different autacoid. All preparations are exposed simultaneously to the same concentration of the test substance.

```text
Sample perfusate → Rat fundus strip (5-HT)
                 → Guinea pig ileum (histamine, bradykinin)
                 → Rat colon (PGE2 vs PGF2α distinguished by direction)
                 → Rat uterus (oxytocin)
```

Specific antagonists are added to individual tissues to confirm identification. The relative heights of responses on the different tissues, together with selective pharmacological blockers, allow simultaneous identification and rough quantification of multiple autacoids released from one experiment (e.g., from antigen-challenged lung tissue).

## 3.11 Relevance to drug standardisation and research

1. **Standardisation**: biological medicines (insulin, heparin, oxytocin, vaccines) cannot be characterised fully by chemical means alone. Bioassay assigns potency in International Units, ensuring clinical equivalence between batches.
2. **Drug discovery**: isolated tissue preparations provide the initial pharmacological profile of a new compound, identifying receptor selectivity and potency before the compound is tested in vivo.
3. **Mechanism studies**: the use of selective antagonists on isolated tissues dissects the receptor type responsible for an agonist's response (e.g., the Schild pA2 identifies a histamine H1 antagonist's potency precisely).
4. **Quality control**: batch-to-batch variation in biological extracts is detected by bioassay even when chemical content appears unchanged.

## Remember

1. Autacoids act locally; their plasma concentrations are extremely low and they are metabolised rapidly.
2. Guinea pig ileum: responds to histamine (H1), bradykinin (B2), 5-HT; mepyramine blocks histamine, icatibant blocks bradykinin, methysergide blocks 5-HT.
3. Rat fundus strip: selective for 5-HT2 (serotonin) and prostaglandins; not histamine-sensitive.
4. Rat uterus (oestrogen-primed): selective for oxytocin and bradykinin; atosiban blocks oxytocin.
5. Angiotensin I and II both raise BP in rat; captopril blocks angiotensin I response; losartan blocks both.
6. ACh on frog heart = cardio-inhibitory, muscarinic, atropine-sensitive.
7. ACh on cat BP = fall (muscarinic vasodilation); after atropine, rise unmasked (Dale's vasomotor reversal).
8. In-vitro assays: simpler, less variable, one receptor type; in-vivo assays: integrated whole-animal response.
9. Cascade superfusion: same perfusate over different selective tissues simultaneously identifies multiple substances.
10. Standard curve interpolation: read the response, find the equivalent log-dose from the linear regression, back-calculate the concentration in the original sample using the dilution factor.
