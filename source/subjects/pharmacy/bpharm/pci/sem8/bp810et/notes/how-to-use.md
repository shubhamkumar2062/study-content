# How to use this guide

Experimental Pharmacology (BP810ET) is a Semester 8 elective worth 3 theory credits. Coming at the end of the B.Pharm programme, it expects you to already know receptor pharmacology, biochemistry, and the major drug classes from Pharmacology I, II and III. What it adds is the **experimental dimension**: how pharmacologists actually measure drug activity, test safety and develop a PK profile before a drug reaches clinical trials. These skills are directly relevant to careers in pharmaceutical R&D, regulatory affairs and academic research.

There is no practical paper for this elective in the standard PCI scheme, so all learning is examined in writing. The marks come from: understanding the **principles** and **calculations** of bioassay, knowing the **animal models** in detail (purpose, procedure, endpoint, control drug), and applying **toxicological and pharmacokinetic concepts** to drug development scenarios.

## Exam pattern

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 20 objective / MCQ questions (attempt all) | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Total: 75 marks, 180 minutes.** Confirm the exact paper pattern with your college, as universities may vary Section B and C counts.

## Probability ratings

- **High** — asked in most sittings of this elective; know the full answer with procedures, calculations and reference drugs.
- **Medium** — appears every few years or as part of a broader question.
- **Low** — rare; useful for the MCQ section.

## High-probability topics to know cold

1. **3Rs and CPCSEA** — definitions with examples; IAEC composition; humane endpoints.
2. **Dose-response curve and bioassay calculation** — log-dose vs response, slope, interpolation, four-point parallel-line assay calculation, index of precision λ, validity tests.
3. **Isolated guinea pig ileum** — preparation, what it detects, receptor types, Schultz-Dale reaction.
4. **Carrageenan paw oedema** — phases with mediators, plethysmometry, % inhibition calculation, reference drug.
5. **Acetic acid writhing test** — procedure, opioids vs NSAIDs comparison, sensitivity vs selectivity.
6. **Lorke's LD50 method** — stage 1 and stage 2 animal numbers, calculation formula.
7. **PK parameter calculation** — AUC by trapezoidal rule, CL, Vd, t½, bioavailability.
8. **ICH toxicity guidelines** — categories Q, S, E; specific S-series guidelines for genotoxicity, reproductive toxicity, repeat-dose.
9. **Formalin test phases** — Phase 1 (neurogenic, opioid-sensitive) vs Phase 2 (inflammatory, NSAID-sensitive).
10. **Four-point parallel-line assay** — why parallelism matters, what it means if slopes differ.

## Scoring tips for this subject

1. **Draw a labelled diagram for the organ bath in every question that involves an isolated tissue preparation.** A clean diagram with the labels (jacketed bath, Krebs solution, gas inlet, transducer, tissue hooks, drain tap) earns one to two marks and signals that you understand the setup. Even a rough sketch is better than no diagram.

2. **Write a worked calculation step by step.** In bioassay questions (three-point assay, interpolation, AUC, bioavailability) the examiner awards marks for the method and each step, not just the final number. Write out the formula, substitute values, show the arithmetic.

3. **State the reference drug and the direction of change.** For every screening model answer, say which reference drug you are comparing with and whether the endpoint increases or decreases. For example: "Morphine at 5 mg/kg i.p. increased the tail-flick latency from 3 s (vehicle) to 9 s; the test compound at 10 mg/kg increased it to 7 s, suggesting moderate central analgesic activity."

4. **Classify before you describe.** In a question on toxicity studies or bioassay types, start with a classification (quantal vs graded, in-vitro vs in-vivo, acute vs sub-acute vs chronic) and then discuss each class. Examiners count the classes and their examples.

5. **Distinguish similar-sounding models precisely.** The tail flick test and the hot plate test both use thermal stimuli, but the tail flick is a spinal reflex and the hot plate is supraspinal — write this distinction explicitly. The MES test and PTZ test both screen antiepileptics, but they predict different seizure types — state which is which.

6. **In ethics questions, use the exact terminology.** Replacement, Reduction and Refinement are capitalised. CPCSEA is not just "an animal ethics body" — it operates under the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Act, 1960, and requires an IAEC with a specified composition.

7. **Link PK to dose design.** When you write about PK parameter calculation, finish with one sentence connecting NOAEL or AUC to what it means for clinical dose selection or for the drug discovery pipeline.
