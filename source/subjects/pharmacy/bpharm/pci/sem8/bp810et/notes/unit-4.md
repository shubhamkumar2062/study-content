# Unit 4: Screening Models for CNS, Anti-inflammatory and Analgesic Drugs

Animal models in pharmacology cannot fully replicate complex human conditions such as schizophrenia, depression or rheumatoid arthritis, but they can detect behaviours or physiological changes that are modified by drugs with known clinical efficacy. The models in this unit are those accepted for preclinical drug screening by regulatory agencies.

## 4.1 CNS screening

### 4.1.1 Locomotor activity — actophotometer

**Principle**: the actophotometer (or photoactometer) uses an infrared beam across a cage. When the animal moves through a beam it interrupts it, and the number of beam interruptions per unit time is the **locomotor activity score**.

```text
Procedure:
1. Place a rat or mouse in the actophotometer cage for a 5-minute habituation.
2. Administer the test drug (i.p. or p.o.) or vehicle.
3. Count beam interruptions for 10 minutes at defined intervals (10, 20, 30, 60 min post-dose).
4. CNS stimulant: interruption count INCREASES above vehicle control (e.g., amphetamine, caffeine).
5. CNS depressant: interruption count DECREASES below vehicle control (e.g., phenobarbitone, diazepam).
```

A simple, rapid screen to flag whether a compound stimulates or sedates the CNS.

### 4.1.2 Pentobarbitone (thiopentone) sleeping time — sedative/hypnotic activity

**Principle**: a sub-hypnotic dose of pentobarbitone sodium does not by itself induce sleep, but when a CNS depressant is given before it, the combined depressant action exceeds the threshold and sleep follows. The enhancement of sleeping time is the assay endpoint.

```text
Procedure:
1. Administer test drug (or vehicle) to mice 30 min before pentobarbitone.
2. Give pentobarbitone sodium 35 mg/kg i.p.
3. Record the onset of sleep (loss of righting reflex) and the duration until the righting reflex returns.
4. CNS depressant: prolongs sleeping time and shortens onset.
5. CNS stimulant: reduces or abolishes sleeping time.
```

Diazepam significantly increases sleeping time; caffeine abolishes it. The model is used to screen sedative-hypnotic, anxiolytic and general CNS depressant drugs.

### 4.1.3 Motor coordination — rotarod test

**Principle**: a mouse or rat is placed on a motor-driven rotating drum (10–25 rpm). A well-coordinated animal walks on the drum and does not fall off. Drugs that impair motor coordination (sedatives, muscle relaxants, drugs causing ataxia) increase the frequency of falls or reduce the time the animal remains on the rod.

```text
Procedure:
1. Train animals on the rotarod until they can stay on for 2–3 min without falling (3 training sessions).
2. Administer drug or vehicle.
3. Test on the rotarod at 15, 30, 60 min post-dose.
4. Record: time before falling (seconds), or number of falls in a fixed period.
5. Neurological impairment: fall time DECREASES.
```

**Used for**: distinguishing sedation from true anxiolysis (a true anxiolytic such as buspirone does not impair rotarod performance at anxiolytic doses).

### 4.1.4 Muscle relaxant tests

**Inclined plane test**: a mouse is placed on a smooth inclined board at 45°. A normal mouse grips and does not slide for at least 10 seconds. Muscle relaxants reduce the ability to grip.

**Traction test**: a mouse is hung by its forepaws from a horizontal wire. It normally grips the wire and climbs onto it within 5 seconds. Failure (inability to grip with hind limbs within 5 seconds) indicates muscle relaxation.

### 4.1.5 Anxiolytic screening — elevated plus maze (EPM)

**Principle**: the EPM exploits the conflict between the natural exploratory drive of rodents and their innate fear of open, elevated, brightly lit spaces. The maze has four arms (two open, two enclosed with walls), all elevated 50 cm from the floor, arranged in a plus (+) shape.

```text
Procedure:
1. Place the mouse at the central junction, facing an open arm.
2. Record for 5 minutes: time spent in open arms, closed arms, and central junction.
3. Count arm entries.
4. Normal animal: spends ~30% of time in open arms (thigmotaxis = preference for closed arms).
5. Anxiolytic drug: INCREASES time in open arms and open arm entries.
6. Anxiogenic drug: DECREASES time in open arms.
```

Diazepam (1 mg/kg) doubles open arm time. Buspirone (5-HT1A partial agonist) also increases open arm time without impairing motor coordination. Caffeine (anxiogenic) decreases it.

**Light-dark box**: similar principle; a mouse can move between a brightly lit compartment and a dark box. An anxiolytic increases time in the lit compartment.

**Social interaction test**: pairs of unfamiliar rats are placed together and the time spent in active social contact (sniffing, grooming, following) is measured. Anxiety reduces social interaction; anxiolytics restore it.

### 4.1.6 Antidepressant screening

**Forced swim test (Porsolt despair test)**

```text
Procedure:
1. Place a mouse or rat in a cylinder of water from which it cannot escape.
2. After an initial period of vigorous swimming (2 min for mice, 5 min for rats), the animal assumes
   an immobile floating posture ("behavioural despair").
3. The immobility time in a second exposure (24 h later) is the endpoint.
4. Antidepressants REDUCE immobility time.
```

Tricyclic antidepressants (imipramine), SSRIs (fluoxetine) and MAO inhibitors all reduce immobility. Swim speed is measured to distinguish anti-immobility from locomotor stimulation.

**Tail suspension test**

```text
Procedure:
1. Suspend a mouse by its tail from a hook for 6 minutes.
2. Record the time the mouse is immobile (hanging passively without attempts to escape).
3. Antidepressants REDUCE immobility time.
```

This test does not involve swimming and is easier to automate. SSRIs and TCAs reduce immobility with less confounding from locomotion than the FST. It is not usable in rats (they climb their own tails).

### 4.1.7 Antiepileptic screening

**Maximal electroshock seizure (MES) test**

```text
Procedure:
1. Deliver a supramaximal electroshock (50 mA, 60 Hz, 0.2 s) through corneal or ear-clip electrodes.
2. The seizure shows: tonic flexion → tonic extension → clonic convulsions → postictal depression.
3. TONIC HIND LIMB EXTENSION (THE) is the endpoint: its abolition indicates protection.
4. Drugs active in MES: phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, lamotrigine.
5. Drugs inactive in MES: ethosuximide (absence seizure drug; active in PTZ).
```

The MES model mimics grand mal (tonic-clonic) seizures and predicts clinical activity against partial and generalised tonic-clonic seizures.

**Pentylenetetrazol (PTZ) test**

```text
Procedure:
1. Administer PTZ (85–90 mg/kg i.p.) to mice.
2. Observe for 30 minutes for clonic convulsions (whole-body jerking), tonic convulsions and death.
3. Endpoint: absence of clonic convulsions (CD97 = dose that abolishes clonics in 97% of animals).
4. Drugs active in PTZ: ethosuximide, valproate, benzodiazepines.
5. Phenytoin is inactive in this test.
```

PTZ acts by blocking GABA-A receptor Cl- channels. This model predicts activity against absence and myoclonic seizures.

### 4.1.8 Antipsychotic screening

**Apomorphine-induced stereotypy**: apomorphine (dopamine agonist, 1–2 mg/kg s.c.) produces repetitive gnawing, licking and head-bobbing in rats and mice. D2 receptor antagonists (antipsychotics such as haloperidol, chlorpromazine) block this stereotypy in a dose-related fashion.

**Catalepsy bar test**: the mouse or rat is placed with its forepaws on a horizontal bar 10 cm above the bench, and the time before it moves off is measured. Dopamine D2 antagonists produce catalepsy (rigid posture, akinesia). Positive: typical antipsychotics (haloperidol). Atypical antipsychotics (clozapine) produce less catalepsy at equivalent antipsychotic doses.

**Conditioned avoidance response (CAR)**: rats are trained to jump onto a platform in response to a warning signal (light or tone) to avoid a foot shock. Antipsychotics selectively suppress the avoidance response (conditioned) at doses lower than those needed to block the escape response (unconditioned), because the dopaminergic motivation to avoid is reduced.

## 4.2 Anti-inflammatory screening

### 4.2.1 Carrageenan-induced paw oedema (rat)

**Principle**: injection of carrageenan (1% solution, 0.1 mL) into the right hind paw sub-plantar region causes a reproducible, biphasic inflammatory oedema. The paw volume is measured by water displacement plethysmometry.

```text
Time course of carrageenan-induced oedema:
  Phase 1 (0–1 h): release of HISTAMINE and 5-HT from local mast cells
  Phase 2 (1–3 h): release of PROSTAGLANDINS (arachidonic acid pathway)
  Phase 3 (3–6 h): production of BRADYKININ and further prostanoids; peak oedema

The 3-hour time point is used to screen for NSAID activity (prostaglandin phase).
```

**Procedure**:
1. Measure baseline paw volume with plethysmometer.
2. Administer test drug or vehicle p.o. 30 min before carrageenan.
3. Inject carrageenan into sub-plantar region.
4. Measure paw volume at 1, 2, 3 and 4 hours.
5. Calculate % oedema inhibition = [(Vc - Vt) / Vc] × 100.

**Controls**: indomethacin or diclofenac (25 mg/kg) as the reference anti-inflammatory.

### 4.2.2 Cotton pellet granuloma (chronic inflammation)

**Principle**: sterile cotton pellets implanted subcutaneously induce a foreign body granuloma over 7 days. The pellet mass increases from: (a) **transudate** (fluid accumulation, early), and (b) **granulation tissue proliferation** (proliferative phase, late). The dried pellet weight minus the original pellet weight reflects total granuloma weight.

**Procedure**:
1. Under ether anaesthesia, implant two sterile cotton pellets (10 mg each) subcutaneously in the axillary region of rats.
2. Administer test drug daily for 7 days.
3. On day 8, remove the granulomas under anaesthesia.
4. Dry to constant weight (60°C, 24 h).
5. Increased pellet weight indicates inflammation.
6. Calculate inhibition of granuloma formation.

**What is detected**: NSAIDs inhibit primarily the exudative phase; corticosteroids inhibit both exudation and the proliferative phase (more complete inhibition).

### 4.2.3 Acetic acid-induced peritonitis (leukocyte count)

**Principle**: intraperitoneal injection of acetic acid (1% in saline, 10 mL/kg) induces peritoneal inflammation with chemotaxis of leucocytes.

```text
Procedure:
1. Administer test drug or vehicle 30 min before acetic acid injection.
2. Inject acetic acid i.p.
3. After 4 hours, sacrifice the animal and wash the peritoneal cavity with phosphate-buffered saline.
4. Count total and differential leucocytes in the peritoneal lavage.
5. NSAIDs and COX inhibitors reduce leucocyte influx.
```

### 4.2.4 Freund's adjuvant arthritis model

**Principle**: complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA, containing heat-killed Mycobacterium tuberculosis in mineral oil) is injected into one hind paw of a rat. It induces an immune-mediated polyarthritis that peaks at 14–28 days and resembles rheumatoid arthritis more closely than carrageenan oedema.

**Primary lesion**: at the injection site (day 0–5).
**Secondary lesion**: contralateral paw and systemic joint swelling (day 14–21) — immune-mediated.

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) such as methotrexate and leflunomide suppress the secondary lesion; NSAIDs mainly suppress the primary lesion.

## 4.3 Analgesic screening

### 4.3.1 Acetic acid-induced writhing test (visceral pain)

**Principle**: intraperitoneal injection of dilute acetic acid (0.6%, 10 mL/kg) irritates the peritoneum and causes a characteristic stretching response — **writhing** — consisting of contraction of the abdominal muscles, drawing in of the flanks, and extension of the hind limbs. Each writh is counted.

```text
Procedure:
1. Administer drug or vehicle 30 min before acetic acid.
2. Inject acetic acid i.p.
3. Count writhes for 20 minutes.
4. Analgesics REDUCE the number of writhes.
5. Both opioids AND NSAIDs are active (visceral pain is prostanoid-mediated).
```

**Comparison**: morphine > aspirin > paracetamol in potency in this model. The model detects mild analgesics that the hot plate test may miss. It is very sensitive but non-selective — many drugs with non-analgesic mechanisms (sedatives, anticonvulsants) also reduce writhes.

### 4.3.2 Tail flick test (thermal nociception, centrally acting)

**Principle**: the base of the mouse tail is placed on a focused beam of radiant heat (or immersed in hot water at 55°C). The normal animal flicks its tail within 2–4 seconds (baseline latency). Opioids increase the latency to tail flick by acting at spinal cord dorsal horn μ-receptors.

```text
Procedure:
1. Measure baseline latency (3 measurements, mean).
2. Administer drug or vehicle.
3. Measure latency at 15, 30, 60 min post-dose.
4. Set a CUT-OFF TIME (usually 10 seconds) to prevent tissue damage from prolonged exposure.
5. Opioids: significantly INCREASE latency.
6. NSAIDs: minimal effect in this model (peripheral analgesics are less active on spinal nociception).
```

The tail flick is primarily a **spinal reflex**; central analgesics (opioids) are detected, but hypnotic doses of drugs can also increase latency by sedation. Use in combination with the rotarod to distinguish true analgesia from sedation.

### 4.3.3 Hot plate test (supraspinal thermal nociception)

**Principle**: the mouse is placed on a metal plate maintained at 55°C. It responds by licking the paws or jumping. The latency to the first lick or jump is the endpoint. This response is **supraspinal** (requires an intact brain), so drugs must cross the blood-brain barrier to be active.

```text
Procedure:
1. Measure baseline latency for licking or jumping.
2. Administer drug; measure latency at 15, 30 and 60 min.
3. CUT-OFF TIME: 60 seconds to prevent burns.
4. Opioids: increase latency significantly.
5. NSAIDs: largely inactive at clinical doses.
```

The hot plate test is used together with the tail flick test to confirm central analgesic action. Morphine increases both; a compound that increases hot plate latency but not tail flick latency is likely acting supraspinally.

### 4.3.4 Formalin test (neurogenic and inflammatory pain)

**Principle**: a small volume of dilute formalin (1–5%, 20 μL) is injected into the sub-plantar region of the mouse paw. The animal shows pain behaviour (licking and biting the injected paw) in two distinct phases:

```text
Phase 1 (0–5 min): NEUROGENIC pain — direct stimulation of C fibres by formalin.
  Opioids reduce phase 1; NSAIDs do not.

Phase 2 (20–40 min): INFLAMMATORY pain — prostaglandins, bradykinin from the inflamed tissue.
  Both opioids and NSAIDs reduce phase 2.

The gap between phases 1 and 2 (5–20 min) is called the interphase (quiescent period).
```

The formalin test distinguishes drugs acting on neurogenic pain from those acting on inflammatory pain, and is therefore more informative than the writhing test.

### 4.3.5 Randall-Selitto test (mechanical hyperalgesia)

**Principle**: a pressure is applied to the rat paw by an analgesiometer (Randall-Selitto apparatus). The pressure at which the rat vocalises or withdraws its paw is the **paw pressure threshold (PPT)**.

Carrageenan is first injected to produce inflammatory hyperalgesia (lowered threshold). Analgesic drugs restore the threshold toward normal. The model detects both NSAIDs and opioids and is used to study mechanical hyperalgesia in inflammatory pain.

## Remember

1. Actophotometer: more beam breaks = CNS stimulation; fewer = depression.
2. Pentobarbitone sleeping time: prolonged by depressants (diazepam); abolished by stimulants (caffeine).
3. Rotarod: fall time decreases with sedation; anxiolytics (buspirone) do not impair rotarod at anxiolytic doses.
4. Elevated plus maze: more time in open arms = anxiolytic; diazepam is the reference.
5. Forced swim test and tail suspension test: antidepressants reduce immobility; SSRIs and TCAs both active.
6. MES test: abolition of tonic hind limb extension; predicts grand mal (tonic-clonic) activity.
7. PTZ test: absence of clonic convulsions; predicts absence seizure activity; ethosuximide active here but not in MES.
8. Carrageenan paw oedema: measure at 3 hours for NSAIDs (prostaglandin phase).
9. Writhing test: visceral pain; both opioids and NSAIDs active but very sensitive and non-selective.
10. Formalin test: Phase 1 = neurogenic (opioid-sensitive), Phase 2 = inflammatory (NSAID-sensitive).
11. Hot plate test: supraspinal reflex; opioids active, NSAIDs not. Always use a cut-off time to prevent tissue damage.
