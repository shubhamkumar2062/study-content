# Unit 4: Evaluation of herbal drugs, patenting and regulatory requirements

A herbal product is only as good as the evidence that it is the right plant, free from harmful matter, contains enough of its active principles, and stays that way until the expiry date. This unit covers the WHO and ICH guidelines used to assess herbal drugs, stability testing, the patenting of traditional knowledge and natural products (with the famous turmeric and neem cases), and the Indian law that controls Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani (ASU) drugs.

## 4.1 Why evaluation of herbal drugs is difficult

- A herbal drug is a **mixture** of many compounds; often the active principle is not known.
- Content varies with species, variety, place, climate, season, harvest time, drying and storage.
- **Adulteration and substitution** are common, deliberate or accidental.
- **Contaminants**: pesticides, heavy metals, microbes, aflatoxins, residual solvents, foreign matter.
- Polyherbal formulations contain many plants, so one test cannot prove the presence of each.

Evaluation therefore uses a set of tests that together prove **identity, purity, quality (content), safety and stability**.

## 4.2 WHO guidelines for the assessment of herbal drugs

The World Health Organization has issued a series of guidelines that member countries, including India, follow or adapt:

| WHO guideline | What it covers |
| --- | --- |
| Guidelines for the assessment of herbal medicines | Principles for judging quality, safety and efficacy, including the use of long traditional use as evidence |
| Quality control methods for herbal materials (earlier "for medicinal plant materials") | Standard test methods: macroscopy, microscopy, ash values, extractives, moisture, swelling and foaming index, bitterness value, pesticide residues, heavy metals, microbial contamination, aflatoxins |
| Good agricultural and collection practices (GACP) for medicinal plants | Cultivation, collection and primary processing (see Unit 1) |
| Good manufacturing practices (GMP) for herbal medicines | Factory standards specific to herbal products |
| Guidelines on safety monitoring (pharmacovigilance) of herbal medicines | Reporting and assessment of adverse reactions |
| Guidelines on methodologies for research and evaluation of traditional medicine | Clinical and pre-clinical study design |

### Parameters in WHO quality-control methods

```text
          Evaluation of a herbal material
                       │
   ┌───────────────┬───┴──────────────┬───────────────────┐
 IDENTITY        PURITY            CONTENT/ASSAY       SAFETY
   │               │                    │                  │
 Macroscopy     Foreign matter       Extractive values   Pesticide residues
 Microscopy     Total ash            Volatile oil        Heavy metals
 Chromatographic Acid-insoluble ash  Marker assay        Microbial load
 fingerprint    Water-soluble ash    (HPLC, HPTLC,       Aflatoxins
 (TLC, HPTLC)   Moisture / LOD       titration, UV)      Radioactive
 Chemical tests  Swelling index                          contamination
                 Foaming index,
                 bitterness value
```

**Details worth writing in an answer**

1. **Macroscopic and organoleptic evaluation** — shape, size, colour, surface, fracture, odour, taste.
2. **Microscopic evaluation** — characteristic tissues and cell contents; quantitative microscopy (stomatal number and index, vein-islet number, palisade ratio, lycopodium spore method for powder purity).
3. **Foreign matter** — other plant parts, stones, soil, insects removed and weighed; limit given in the monograph.
4. **Ash values** — total ash (inorganic matter, earthy adulteration), acid-insoluble ash (silica, sand), water-soluble ash, sulphated ash.
5. **Extractive values** — water-soluble and alcohol-soluble matter; low values suggest exhausted or adulterated drug.
6. **Moisture content / loss on drying**; for drugs with volatile oil, the azeotropic toluene distillation method.
7. **Special values** — swelling index (mucilaginous drugs such as isabgol), foaming index (saponin drugs), bitterness value (bitter drugs such as gentian), haemolytic activity (saponins), volatile oil content.
8. **Chromatographic fingerprint** — TLC or HPTLC profile compared with a reference sample; HPLC and GC for markers.
9. **Pesticide residues** — organochlorine, organophosphorus and other pesticides by GC; limits set by the monograph or national law.
10. **Heavy metals** — lead, cadmium, arsenic and mercury by atomic absorption spectroscopy or ICP. WHO has suggested maximum limits for lead (10 mg/kg) and cadmium (0.3 mg/kg); national pharmacopoeias set their own limits.
11. **Microbial contamination** — total aerobic microbial count, yeasts and moulds, and absence of specified pathogens (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*); limits differ for material used as such, for teas boiled before use, and for oral products.
12. **Aflatoxins** — mycotoxins from *Aspergillus flavus* on poorly stored drugs; tested by TLC/HPLC.
13. **Radioactive contamination** — where there is a risk.

### WHO approach to safety and efficacy

- **Long-term traditional use** with no documented harm is accepted as an indication of safety, but toxicity studies are needed when a product is new, used in a new way or at a higher dose.
- **Efficacy** may be supported by traditional use for traditional indications; new claims need pharmacological and clinical evidence.
- Products must carry clear **labels** with plant name, part, preparation, dose, warnings and storage.

## 4.3 ICH guidelines and herbal drugs

The **International Council for Harmonisation (ICH)** writes technical guidelines for registration of pharmaceuticals in member regions. ICH has **no guideline written only for herbal products**, but its quality guidelines are applied to herbal products and extracts, especially by industry exporting to regulated markets.

| ICH guideline | Topic | Relevance to herbal drugs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Q1A(R2)** | Stability testing of new drug substances and products | Storage conditions, test intervals and shelf life for extracts and finished products |
| **Q1B** | Photostability testing | Many plant pigments and actives are light-sensitive |
| **Q2** | Validation of analytical procedures | Validating HPLC/HPTLC marker assays: specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, LOD, LOQ, robustness |
| **Q3C** | Residual solvents | Solvents used to make extracts (hexane, methanol, acetone) |
| **Q6** | Specifications | Setting acceptance criteria |
| **Q7** | GMP for active ingredients | Manufacture of extracts and isolated phytochemicals |
| **Q9, Q10** | Quality risk management, pharmaceutical quality system | Managing raw-material variability |
| **M4 (CTD)** | Common Technical Document | Dossier format for registration |

The European Medicines Agency has a separate committee and guidelines for herbal medicinal products, and these are often consulted alongside ICH.

## 4.4 Stability testing of herbal drugs

**Stability** is the ability of a product to keep its physical, chemical, microbiological, therapeutic and toxicological properties within limits throughout its shelf life, in the container in which it is sold.

### Why herbal stability testing is special

- Many constituents, of which the active one may be unknown.
- Constituents interact with each other and with excipients.
- Moisture, light, oxygen and heat cause hydrolysis, oxidation, loss of volatile oil, colour change and microbial growth.
- Hence stability is judged by **several markers plus the whole fingerprint**, not by one compound.

### Factors affecting stability

| Factor | Effect | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moisture | Hydrolysis of glycosides, enzyme action, mould | Churnas cake and grow fungi |
| Temperature | Faster degradation, volatile oil loss | Clove, mint lose aroma |
| Light | Photodegradation, colour fading | Curcumin, chlorophyll, hypericin |
| Oxygen | Oxidation, rancidity of oils | Fixed oils, polyphenols |
| pH | Hydrolysis, isomerisation | Anthocyanins change colour |
| Microbes | Spoilage, toxin formation | Syrups, aqueous extracts |
| Container | Interaction, permeation | Plastic absorbing volatile oils |

### Design of a stability study

1. **Select batches** — at least three, made as in production, in the final pack.
2. **Choose parameters**:
   - *Physical*: appearance, colour, odour, taste, pH, viscosity, moisture, disintegration, dissolution, phase separation.
   - *Chemical*: content of selected markers (HPLC/HPTLC), chromatographic fingerprint (no new or lost peaks), degradation products.
   - *Microbiological*: microbial limits, preservative efficacy.
3. **Storage conditions** as in ICH Q1A(R2):

| Study | Condition | Minimum period at submission |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long-term | 25 °C ± 2 °C / 60% RH ± 5% RH, or 30 °C ± 2 °C / 65% RH ± 5% RH | 12 months |
| Intermediate | 30 °C ± 2 °C / 65% RH ± 5% RH | 6 months |
| Accelerated | 40 °C ± 2 °C / 75% RH ± 5% RH | 6 months |

India lies in a **hot and very humid climatic zone (zone IVb)**, for which **30 °C / 75% RH** is the long-term condition used in WHO guidance.

4. **Testing frequency** — for long-term studies typically every 3 months in the first year, every 6 months in the second year and yearly thereafter; for accelerated studies at least 0, 3 and 6 months.
5. **Evaluation** — a "significant change" (marked loss of marker, failure of any specification) during accelerated testing triggers intermediate testing. The **shelf life** is the period during which the product stays within specification under the labelled storage conditions. **Photostability** testing is added for light-sensitive products.

The Drugs Rules in India also lay down shelf lives (date of expiry) for different categories of ASU medicines, and classical products such as Asava and Arishta are traditionally regarded as improving with age.

## 4.5 Patenting and regulatory requirements of natural products

### Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Patent** | An exclusive right granted by the government to an inventor for a limited period (20 years from filing under the TRIPS standard, followed by India) in exchange for full public disclosure of the invention |
| Criteria for a patent | **Novelty** (new), **inventive step** (not obvious to a skilled person), **industrial applicability** (useful), and the subject matter must not be excluded by law |
| **Traditional knowledge (TK)** | Knowledge, practices and innovations of indigenous and local communities, developed over generations and passed on orally or in classical texts — for example, the use of turmeric for wounds |
| **Prior art** | All knowledge available to the public before the filing date, anywhere in the world, in any form; anything in prior art cannot be patented |
| **Biopiracy** | The commercial use or patenting of biological resources or traditional knowledge from a country or community **without its permission and without sharing benefits** with it |
| **Bioprospecting** | The legitimate search for useful products from biological resources, done with permission and benefit sharing |

### Patenting of traditional knowledge and natural products

Under the **Indian Patents Act, 1970** (as amended):

- **Section 3(c)**: the mere discovery of a living thing or of a non-living substance occurring in nature is **not** an invention.
- **Section 3(p)**: an invention that is in effect **traditional knowledge**, or an aggregation or duplication of known properties of traditionally known components, is **not** patentable.
- A **new process** of extraction, a new formulation with real improvement, or a new use proved scientifically may be patentable if it meets the criteria.
- An application based on biological material must **disclose the source and geographical origin** of the material.

Related laws and tools:

- **Biological Diversity Act, 2002** — access to Indian biological resources and associated knowledge for research or commercial use needs approval from the **National Biodiversity Authority**; seeking a patent or other IPR on an invention based on Indian biological resources also needs its approval; benefits must be shared with the communities that conserved them.
- **Convention on Biological Diversity** (international) — countries have sovereign rights over their biological resources; access with prior informed consent and fair sharing of benefits (detailed in the Nagoya Protocol).
- **TRIPS agreement** of the WTO — minimum standards for patents, including the 20-year term.
- **Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL)** — set up by the CSIR with the AYUSH department; it translates formulations from classical Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and Yoga texts into patent-office languages and formats, and is shared with major patent offices so that examiners can find the prior art and refuse patents on Indian TK.

### Case study 1: Turmeric (*Curcuma longa*)

1. Turmeric has been used in India for centuries to heal wounds and skin conditions; this is described in classical texts and household practice.
2. In 1995 the **US Patent Office granted a patent** to two researchers of the University of Mississippi Medical Center for the **use of turmeric powder to heal wounds**, given by mouth or applied on the skin.
3. India's **Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR)** filed a request for **re-examination**, arguing that the claimed use was not new.
4. CSIR submitted **documentary evidence of prior art** — including old Sanskrit texts and a paper in the *Journal of the Indian Medical Association* — showing that turmeric had long been used for wound healing.
5. In **1997 the US Patent Office revoked the patent**, holding that the claims were anticipated by prior art (not novel).
6. **Lessons**: traditional knowledge must be **documented** in a form patent examiners can search; oral knowledge is hard to prove as prior art. This case led directly to the creation of the **TKDL**.

### Case study 2: Neem (*Azadirachta indica*)

1. Neem has long been used in India as a pesticide, fungicide, antiseptic and in oral hygiene (neem twigs).
2. The **European Patent Office (EPO) granted a patent** to the US company **W. R. Grace** and the **US Department of Agriculture** for a method of controlling fungi on plants using a **hydrophobic extract of neem oil**.
3. The patent was opposed by a group including Vandana Shiva (Research Foundation for Science, Technology and Ecology), the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements and a member of the European Parliament.
4. The opponents showed that the **fungicidal use of neem extracts was already known and practised in India** — including evidence of earlier use and trials by Indian workers — so the invention lacked novelty and inventive step.
5. The **EPO revoked the patent in 2000**, and the decision was upheld on appeal.
6. **Lessons**: the neem case is the best-known example of a successful fight against biopiracy at the EPO; it showed the need for documentation and for laws such as the Biological Diversity Act. Other examples often quoted are basmati rice and *Hoodia* (from southern Africa).

| Point | Turmeric | Neem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patent office | US Patent and Trademark Office | European Patent Office |
| Claimed invention | Use of turmeric for wound healing | Fungicidal use of a neem oil extract |
| Challenged by | CSIR, India | Group of NGOs and individuals |
| Ground | Lack of novelty — prior art in old texts | Lack of novelty and inventive step — prior use in India |
| Outcome | Revoked (1997) | Revoked (2000), upheld on appeal |

## 4.6 Regulatory issues — regulation of ASU drugs in India

ASU drugs are regulated under **Chapter IV-A of the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940** and the ASU parts of the **Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945**. The administration at the centre is under the **Ministry of AYUSH**; licences are given by the **State Licensing Authority** (state AYUSH/ISM department).

### Definitions in the Act

- **Ayurvedic, Siddha or Unani drug**: all medicines meant for internal or external use for the diagnosis, treatment, mitigation or prevention of disease in humans or animals, manufactured exclusively according to the formulae described in the authoritative books of these systems listed in the **First Schedule** of the Act.
- **Patent or proprietary medicine** (ASU): a formulation containing only ingredients mentioned in the formulae of the authoritative books, but not itself a formula described in those books; given by mouth or applied externally, not parenterally.

### ASU DTAB — Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani Drugs Technical Advisory Board

- Constituted by the Central Government under **Section 33C** of the Act.
- **Function**: to advise the Central and State Governments on **technical matters** arising out of the administration of Chapter IV-A, and to carry out other functions given to it by the Act.
- **Members**: senior officers of the AYUSH ministry and health departments, the Drugs Controller, heads of laboratories, and experts from the teaching, research and practice of Ayurveda, Siddha and Unani, and from pharmacognosy and pharmaceutical chemistry, some nominated and some elected.
- It recommends changes to the rules, standards and the list of authoritative books.

### ASU DCC — Ayurvedic, Siddha and Unani Drugs Consultative Committee

- Constituted under **Section 33D** of the Act.
- **Function**: to advise the Central and State Governments and the ASU DTAB on any matter that is needed to secure **uniformity throughout India in the administration** of the Act as it applies to ASU drugs.
- **Members**: representatives of the Central Government and of each State Government, with experts in these systems.

| Point | ASU DTAB | ASU DCC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Section | 33C | 33D |
| Nature | Technical advisory board | Consultative committee |
| Main role | Technical matters — standards, rules | Uniform administration across states |

### Regulation of manufacture of ASU drugs

1. **Licence to manufacture**: application to the State Licensing Authority (application in Form 24-D; licence granted in Form 25-D), with details of premises, staff and products. Loan licences are also provided for.
2. **Conditions of licence**:
   - Manufacture under the supervision of a **competent technical person** qualified in the concerned system or in pharmacy.
   - Compliance with **Good Manufacturing Practices in Schedule T** (Unit 5).
   - Raw materials and finished products tested in the firm's own laboratory or an **approved drug-testing laboratory**; records kept.
   - Each product made only as per the formula approved by the licensing authority.
3. **Standards**: products must meet the **Ayurvedic, Siddha or Unani Pharmacopoeias** (prepared by the Pharmacopoeia Commission for Indian Medicine and Homoeopathy) and the formularies.
4. **Labelling**: name of product, list of ingredients with quantities, reference to the authoritative book (for classical drugs), manufacturing licence number, batch number, dates of manufacture and expiry, "Ayurvedic/Siddha/Unani medicine", and the caution "To be taken under medical supervision" for drugs with poisonous ingredients listed in **Schedule E(1)**.
5. **Prohibitions**: no manufacture or sale of misbranded, adulterated or spurious ASU drugs; misleading advertisements are controlled under the Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954.
6. **Enforcement**: drug inspectors and Government analysts for ASU drugs; penalties for violations.

### Schedule Z — requirements for licensing ASU drugs

The syllabus names **Schedule Z** as the part of the Drugs and Cosmetics law covering the requirements and guidelines for permission to manufacture ASU drugs. It was brought out by the Central Government as a proposed (draft) schedule; the licensing guidelines that operate today are in the Drugs Rules for ASU drugs (Rule 158-B). Whichever form your college follows, the scheme is the same: **the more a product departs from the classical texts, the more evidence the manufacturer must submit.**

| Category | Description | Typical requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Classical ASU drugs** | Formulations exactly as described in the authoritative books of the First Schedule | Reference to the text, standards of raw materials and product; no new safety or efficacy studies |
| **Patent or proprietary ASU drugs** | New combinations of ingredients drawn from the classical texts | Rationale from the texts, safety data, evidence of effectiveness (published literature or pilot clinical study), stability, quality standards |
| **New ASU drugs / new indications / new dosage forms** | A classical or known drug proposed for a new use, in a new dosage form, or as an extract, or a new ingredient | Full quality data, acute and sub-acute toxicity studies, clinical studies as required, stability |

The aim is to bring ASU drugs closer to modern requirements of **quality, safety and efficacy** without demanding unnecessary trials for drugs with a long documented use.

## Remember

- Evaluation = identity (macro, micro, fingerprint) + purity (foreign matter, ash, moisture) + content (extractives, markers) + safety (pesticides, heavy metals, microbes, aflatoxins).
- WHO guidelines: assessment of herbal medicines, QC methods, GACP, GMP, pharmacovigilance. WHO-suggested limits: lead 10 mg/kg, cadmium 0.3 mg/kg.
- ICH has no herbal-only guideline; Q1A(R2) stability, Q1B photostability, Q2 validation, Q3C residual solvents applied to herbals.
- Stability: long-term 25 °C/60% RH or 30 °C/65% RH; accelerated 40 °C/75% RH for 6 months; India is zone IVb (30 °C/75% RH); judge by markers plus fingerprint.
- Biopiracy = use or patenting of biological resources/TK without consent or benefit sharing.
- Patents Act: Section 3(c) natural substances and 3(p) traditional knowledge are not inventions; Biological Diversity Act, 2002 — NBA approval and benefit sharing; TKDL as searchable prior art.
- Turmeric: US patent on wound healing, challenged by CSIR, revoked 1997. Neem: EPO patent (W. R. Grace and USDA) on fungicidal neem extract, revoked 2000.
- ASU drugs: Chapter IV-A; ASU DTAB (Section 33C, technical advice); ASU DCC (Section 33D, uniform administration); licence from State Licensing Authority; GMP under Schedule T; poisonous ingredients in Schedule E(1).
- Schedule Z / licensing guidelines: classical → patent/proprietary → new ASU drugs, each needing more evidence of safety and efficacy.
