# Unit 1: Herbs as raw materials and Indian systems of medicine

Every herbal medicine starts as a plant growing in a field or a forest. The quality of the finished product can never be better than the quality of the plant that went into it. This unit follows the herb from its source, through cultivation, collection and processing, to the point where it is ready for the factory. The second half introduces the Indian systems of medicine (Ayurveda, Siddha, Unani and Homoeopathy) and the preparation and standardisation of the classical Ayurvedic dosage forms: Asava and Arishta, Gutika, Churna, Lehya and Bhasma.

## 1.1 Basic definitions

The words below are used in the WHO guidelines on herbal medicines and in most textbooks. Learn the difference between them, because a definition question is a common 5-mark or 2-mark item.

| Term | Meaning (in plain words) | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Herb** | Crude plant material — leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, stems, wood, bark, roots, rhizomes or other parts — whole, cut or powdered | Dried senna leaf, ginger rhizome |
| **Herbal material** | Herbs plus other plant products such as fresh juices, gums, fixed oils, volatile oils, resins and dry powders of herbs, sometimes processed by local methods (steaming, roasting, baking with honey) | Aloe juice, acacia gum, clove oil |
| **Herbal preparation** (herbal drug preparation) | Herbal material processed further by extraction, distillation, expression, fractionation, purification, concentration or fermentation; the starting point of finished products | Powders, tinctures, dry extracts, fatty oils, expressed juices |
| **Finished herbal product** (herbal medicinal product) | Herbal preparation(s) made into a dosage form ready for the patient; may contain excipients | Tablets of ashwagandha extract, Triphala churna, cough syrup with tulsi |
| **Herbal medicine** | The overall group: herbs, herbal materials, herbal preparations and finished herbal products whose active ingredients are plant parts or plant materials | — |

Two more terms appear in exam questions:

- **Phytopharmaceutical**: a purified and standardised fraction of a plant extract with defined marker compounds, studied like a modern drug.
- **Marker compound**: a chemically defined constituent used to check identity and quality, whether or not it is the active compound (for example, withaferin A in ashwagandha, curcumin in turmeric).

A product that contains an isolated, chemically pure constituent (morphine, digoxin, quinine) is **not** a herbal medicine; it is a drug of natural origin.

## 1.2 Sources of herbs

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Wild collection** (forests, waste lands) | Cheap, no farming cost, plants grow in their natural habitat | Variable quality, wrong species collected, adulteration, over-harvesting and loss of species, no records of origin, contamination |
| **Cultivation** (farms, plantations) | Known species and variety, uniform quality, planned harvest, records kept, GAP possible, pests controlled, protects wild stock | Cost of land and labour, some plants give less active content in cultivation, needs know-how |
| **Plant tissue culture** | Year-round production, no soil or climate dependence, free from pests | Expensive; few products made commercially this way |
| **Imports** | Plants not grown in India | Price, quality and supply depend on other countries |

In India most medicinal plants used by industry are still collected from the wild. The National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB) supports cultivation and conservation so that pressure on wild plants falls.

Plants sourced for industry are grouped as:

- **Cultivated drugs**: ginger, turmeric, senna, isabgol, ashwagandha, aloe, mentha, opium poppy (licensed).
- **Wild-collected drugs**: many forest roots and barks, for example *Rauwolfia*, kutki, jatamansi, guggul.
- **Endangered or restricted drugs**: species whose collection is controlled to prevent extinction; using cultivated material is preferred.

## 1.3 Selection, identification and authentication of herbal materials

### Selection

The right plant must be chosen for a product. Factors are:

1. **Correct species and plant part** as given in the official text or formula (root, not stem; seed, not leaf).
2. **Chemotype or variety** — some varieties of the same species have far more active constituent than others.
3. **Geographical source, season and time of collection** — content changes with age, season and even time of day (for example, volatile oils are highest before flowering in many herbs).
4. **Freedom from contamination** — pesticides, heavy metals, microbes, aflatoxins.
5. **Sustainable supply** and legal permission to collect.

### Identification and authentication

Authentication means proving that the material really is the plant claimed and is of acceptable quality. Mistakes happen because of similar-looking plants, the same vernacular name used for different plants, and deliberate substitution.

| Method | What is checked |
| --- | --- |
| **Taxonomic identity** | Voucher specimen compared with herbarium sheet; identification by a botanist; a voucher kept with the batch record |
| **Macroscopy / organoleptic** | Shape, size, colour, surface, fracture, odour, taste |
| **Microscopy** | Tissues and cell contents: trichomes, stomata, calcium oxalate crystals, starch grains, vessels, fibres; quantitative microscopy (stomatal index, palisade ratio, vein-islet number) |
| **Physicochemical** | Ash values, extractive values, moisture, swelling index, foaming index, bitterness value |
| **Chemical** | Identification tests for constituent groups; TLC or HPTLC fingerprint against a reference; marker assay by HPLC |
| **DNA-based methods** | DNA barcoding, RAPD, AFLP — used when plant parts look alike or the material is powdered |

Authentication is done for every batch on receipt, and the material stays in **quarantine** until it passes.

## 1.4 Processing of herbal raw material

After harvest, the plant passes through a series of steps that decide its final quality.

```text
Harvest (right part, right stage, right season, dry weather)
      │
      ▼
Primary processing on the farm
  cleaning → washing (only if allowed) → removal of foreign matter
      │
      ▼
Drying (sun, shade, or artificial dryer at controlled temperature)
      │
      ▼
Garbling (sorting out stems, other plant parts, stones, insects)
      │
      ▼
Cutting / size reduction (if specified)
      │
      ▼
Packing (clean, dry, labelled containers)  →  Storage (cool, dry, pest-free)
      │
      ▼
Transport to manufacturer  →  quarantine  →  testing  →  release
```

Points to explain in an answer:

- **Collection**: by hand or machine; only the specified part; dust and soil avoided; damaged or diseased plants rejected; collectors trained.
- **Drying**: the most important step. It stops enzyme action and microbial growth. Leaves and flowers are dried in shade or at low temperature to save colour and volatile oil; roots and barks can take higher temperatures. Moisture is brought low enough to prevent mould. Some drugs need special treatment — for example, *fermentation* (curing) of vanilla pods and *sweating* of some barks to develop aroma or colour.
- **Garbling**: removing unwanted matter by hand or by sieving and winnowing.
- **Special processing**: blanching, peeling (ginger), boiling (turmeric is boiled before drying), slicing, and in Ayurveda *shodhana* (purification) of toxic drugs such as *Strychnos* (kupilu) or *Aconitum* (vatsanabha) before use.
- **Packing and storage**: in clean containers that keep out moisture, light and pests; labelled with name, part, source, batch and date; stored away from finished products.

## 1.5 Biodynamic agriculture

Biodynamic agriculture is a form of organic farming based on the lectures given by **Rudolf Steiner** in 1924. It treats the farm as one self-sustaining living organism in which soil, plants, animals and the farmer are linked.

Main features:

1. **No synthetic fertilisers or pesticides**; fertility comes from compost and farm manure.
2. **Biodynamic preparations** added in very small amounts to soil, compost and crops:
   - **Preparation 500 (horn manure)**: cow dung packed in a cow horn and buried in soil over winter; stirred in water and sprayed on soil to improve soil structure and root growth.
   - **Preparation 501 (horn silica)**: finely ground quartz packed in a horn and buried over summer; sprayed on leaves to improve light use and ripening.
   - **Compost preparations 502–507**: made from yarrow, chamomile, stinging nettle, oak bark, dandelion and valerian; added to the compost heap.
3. **Sowing calendar**: sowing, cultivation and harvest are timed according to the positions of the moon and planets.
4. Crop rotation, mixed cropping, green manure and integration of livestock.

Value for medicinal plants: high-quality, residue-free raw material and better soil health. The scientific basis of some practices (such as the planetary calendar) is not proven, and an answer may say so.

## 1.6 Good agricultural practices (GAP / GACP)

**Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP)** are guidelines that make sure medicinal plants are grown and collected in a way that gives safe, good-quality and consistent raw material. WHO published GACP guidelines for medicinal plants, and in India the National Medicinal Plants Board has issued GACP guidelines based on them.

| Area | What GACP asks for |
| --- | --- |
| **Identity of plant** | Correct botanical identity of seeds and planting material; voucher specimens kept |
| **Site selection** | Soil and water free from heavy metals, pesticide residues and sewage; history of the land known; suitable climate |
| **Seeds and propagation material** | Certified, disease-free, traceable to source |
| **Cultivation** | Proper spacing, irrigation with clean water, fertilisers (preferably organic) applied only as needed, crop rotation |
| **Crop protection** | Minimum use of pesticides; only approved ones, in permitted amounts; integrated pest management preferred |
| **Harvest** | Correct season, stage and time; dry weather; clean tools; damaged plants removed |
| **Primary processing** | Prompt washing/drying; clean drying areas; protection from animals, birds and insects |
| **Packing, storage, transport** | Clean, labelled containers; dry, ventilated store; no mixing with other materials |
| **Personnel** | Trained, healthy workers with protective clothing; hygiene facilities |
| **Documentation** | Records of every step (seed, field, inputs, harvest, processing) so each batch can be traced back |
| **Collection from the wild** | Permission to collect; sustainable amounts; species not endangered; collectors trained |

### Organic farming

Organic farming grows crops **without synthetic fertilisers, synthetic pesticides, growth regulators or genetically modified organisms**. Fertility is maintained by compost, farmyard manure, vermicompost, green manure, crop rotation and biofertilisers (*Rhizobium*, *Azotobacter*, phosphate-solubilising bacteria, mycorrhiza). Pests are managed by biological and cultural means.

Why it matters for herbal drugs: no pesticide residue in the final product, better acceptance in export markets, and long-term soil health. Organic produce is certified by accredited bodies; in India the **National Programme for Organic Production (NPOP)** provides the certification framework.

## 1.7 Pests and pest management in medicinal plants

Pests that damage medicinal plants include insects (aphids, caterpillars, beetles, whiteflies), mites, nematodes, fungi (rusts, wilts, root rot, mildews), bacteria, viruses, weeds, rodents and birds. Stored drugs are attacked by beetles, moths, mites and moulds.

**Pest management** methods:

| Method | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Cultural | Crop rotation, clean seed, proper spacing, removal of infected plants, correct sowing time, trap crops |
| Mechanical / physical | Hand picking, traps (light traps, pheromone traps, sticky traps), barriers, heat or cold treatment of stored drugs |
| Biological | Natural enemies — predators (ladybird beetle against aphids), parasitoids (*Trichogramma*), microbial agents |
| Chemical | Synthetic pesticides — used only when needed and within permitted limits, because residues enter the drug |
| **Integrated pest management (IPM)** | A planned combination of the above that keeps pests below damaging levels with the least chemical use |

### Biopesticides and bioinsecticides

Biopesticides are pest-control agents from living organisms or natural products. They are specific, break down quickly and leave little residue, which makes them the preferred choice for medicinal crops.

| Group | Source and active principle | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Botanical insecticides | Neem (*Azadirachta indica*, Meliaceae) seed — **azadirachtin**: antifeedant, repellent and growth regulator | Wide range of insects |
| | Pyrethrum (*Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium*, Asteraceae) flowers — **pyrethrins** | Quick knock-down of flying insects |
| | Derris and *Lonchocarpus* roots — **rotenone** | Insecticide, fish poison |
| | Tobacco — **nicotine** | Sucking insects (old use) |
| Microbial insecticides | ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** — protein crystal toxic to caterpillar gut | Lepidopteran larvae |
| | Fungi: *Beauveria bassiana*, *Metarhizium anisopliae* | Insects, termites |
| | Nuclear polyhedrosis viruses (NPV) | *Helicoverpa*, *Spodoptera* larvae |
| Biofungicides | *Trichoderma viride*, *T. harzianum*; *Pseudomonas fluorescens* | Soil-borne fungal diseases, seed treatment |
| Pheromones | Sex pheromone traps | Monitoring and mass trapping |

## 1.8 Indian systems of medicine — basic principles

India officially recognises Ayurveda, Yoga and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa and Homoeopathy, under the Ministry of AYUSH. The syllabus covers four of them.

### Ayurveda

"Ayurveda" means the science (*veda*) of life (*ayu*). Its main texts are the *Charaka Samhita* (medicine), *Sushruta Samhita* (surgery) and *Ashtanga Hridaya*.

| Principle | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Panchamahabhuta** | Everything, including the body and drugs, is made of five elements: *prithvi* (earth), *jala* (water), *agni/tejas* (fire), *vayu* (air) and *akasha* (space) |
| **Tridosha** | Three functional forces: **vata** (air + space; movement, nerve action), **pitta** (fire + water; digestion, metabolism, heat), **kapha** (earth + water; structure, lubrication, stability). Health = balance of the three; disease = imbalance |
| **Sapta dhatu** | Seven tissues: rasa (plasma), rakta (blood), mamsa (muscle), meda (fat), asthi (bone), majja (marrow), shukra (reproductive tissue) |
| **Mala** | Waste products: faeces, urine, sweat |
| **Agni** | Digestive and metabolic fire; weak agni produces *ama* (undigested toxic matter) |
| **Prakriti** | Individual constitution decided at conception by the dominant dosha |
| **Drug action** | Explained by *rasa* (taste — six tastes), *guna* (properties), *virya* (potency — hot or cold), *vipaka* (post-digestive effect) and *prabhava* (special action) |

Treatment includes *shodhana* (purification, such as Panchakarma), *shamana* (palliation with medicines), diet (*pathya*) and lifestyle, and *rasayana* (rejuvenation).

### Siddha

Siddha is practised mainly in Tamil Nadu and is traditionally linked to the Siddhars, especially **Agastya**.

- Also accepts five elements and **three humours** — *vatham*, *pitham*, *kapham* — similar to the doshas.
- Describes the body in terms of **96 basic principles (thathuvas)**.
- Diagnosis relies heavily on **eight methods (envagai thervu)**, including pulse (*naadi*), tongue, colour, voice, eyes, touch, urine and stools.
- Makes great use of **metals and minerals** (*parpam*, *chendooram*) and of herbs, besides diet and yoga.

### Unani

Unani (Unani-Tibb) comes from Greek medicine (Hippocrates, Galen), developed by Arab and Persian physicians such as **Ibn Sina** (Avicenna), and came to India with the Arabs and Persians.

- **Four humours (akhlat)**: *dam* (blood), *balgham* (phlegm), *safra* (yellow bile), *sauda* (black bile). Health is their balance.
- **Temperament (mizaj)**: hot, cold, moist, dry and their combinations, for persons and drugs.
- **Six essentials (asbab-e-sitta zarooriya)** for health: air, food and drink, bodily movement and rest, mental movement and rest, sleep and wakefulness, retention and evacuation.
- **Quwwat-e-mudabbira-e-badan** — the body's own power of self-healing.
- Treatment: regimental therapy (*ilaj bil tadbeer* — cupping, massage, exercise), diet therapy (*ilaj bil ghiza*), pharmacotherapy (*ilaj bil dawa*) and surgery.

### Homoeopathy

Founded by **Samuel Hahnemann** (Germany, late 18th century).

| Principle | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Law of similars** (*similia similibus curentur*) | A substance that produces symptoms in a healthy person can cure similar symptoms in a sick person |
| **Drug proving** | Drugs are tested on healthy volunteers to record the symptoms they produce |
| **Single remedy** | One medicine at a time |
| **Minimum dose** | The smallest amount that can produce a cure |
| **Potentisation (dynamisation)** | Serial dilution with vigorous shaking (succussion) or grinding with lactose (trituration); decimal (X) and centesimal (C) scales |
| **Individualisation** | The whole person, not just the disease, is treated |

Starting materials are **mother tinctures** (Ø) made mostly from plants, and also from minerals and animal products.

## 1.9 Preparation and standardisation of Ayurvedic formulations

The official Ayurvedic texts list hundreds of classical formulations. Their preparation is described in the *Ayurvedic Formulary of India* (AFI), and their quality standards in the *Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India* (API).

### Asava and Arishta (fermented liquid preparations)

Both are liquid preparations in which **self-produced alcohol**, produced by fermentation of sugar, extracts the active constituents and also preserves the product.

| Point | Asava | Arishta |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How the herbs are used | Raw drugs or their juices/powders added **without boiling** (cold process) | A **decoction** (*kwatha*) of the drugs is made first, then fermented (hot process) |
| Examples | Drakshasava, Kumaryasava, Kanakasava, Lohasava | Dashamularishta, Ashokarishta, Arjunarishta, Abhayarishta |

**General method**

1. Prepare the decoction (Arishta) or the cold liquid (Asava) as given in the formula; strain.
2. Dissolve the sweetening agent — **jaggery, sugar or honey** — which supplies the sugar for fermentation.
3. Add the **fermentation initiator**, traditionally the flowers of **dhataki (*Woodfordia fruticosa*)**, which carry natural yeasts, and the powdered *prakshepa dravya* (flavouring and aromatic drugs).
4. Fill into a clean, fumigated wooden vat or earthen/glazed vessel, leaving some space; seal the mouth.
5. Keep in a warm, quiet place for the fermentation period stated in the text (usually about a month). Fermentation is complete when bubbling stops, a lit match held near the mouth no longer goes out, and the liquid has a characteristic aromatic odour and turns clear.
6. Decant, filter, allow to settle, and fill into bottles. The product keeps well because of the alcohol.

**Standardisation of Asava/Arishta**

- Organoleptic characters: colour, odour, taste, clarity.
- pH, specific gravity, total solids.
- **Alcohol content** (self-produced) by distillation — must be within the monograph's limits; no added alcohol.
- Methanol must be absent (safety).
- Reducing and non-reducing sugars.
- TLC/HPTLC fingerprint for identity of ingredients.
- Microbial limits, heavy metals and pesticide residues.

### Gutika (vati) — pills and tablets

Gutika are pills or tablets made from fine powders of drugs bound with a liquid (water, juice, decoction, honey, jaggery or guggul). Examples: Chandraprabha vati, Sanjivani vati, Arogyavardhini vati, Lasunadi vati.

**Method**: powder and sieve the drugs → triturate with the binding liquid (*bhavana*) as directed → roll into pills of the prescribed weight by hand or pill machine, or compress as tablets → dry in shade or a low-temperature dryer → pack.

**Standardisation**: description, uniformity of weight, hardness, disintegration time, moisture (loss on drying), ash values, extractive values, TLC fingerprint, microbial limits, heavy metals.

### Churna — fine powders

Churna is a fine powder of one or more drugs, dried and finely sieved, and mixed uniformly. Examples: Triphala churna, Sitopaladi churna, Hingvashtaka churna, Avipattikar churna.

**Method**: clean and dry the ingredients → powder separately → pass through a fine sieve (the formulary specifies the fineness) → mix in the proportions given → store in airtight containers. Salts and sugar, where included, are powdered and added at the end.

**Standardisation**: colour, odour, taste; particle size (sieve test); loss on drying; total ash and acid-insoluble ash; water- and alcohol-soluble extractives; pH of an aqueous solution; bulk density; microscopy of each ingredient (useful for detecting omission or substitution); TLC; microbial load, aflatoxins, heavy metals.

Churnas absorb moisture and grow mould, so moisture control and good packing are critical.

### Lehya (avaleha) — semisolid preparations

Lehya are semisolid, jam-like preparations of drugs cooked with **jaggery, sugar or sugar candy**, usually with ghee or oil and finished with honey. Examples: **Chyawanprash**, Agastya haritaki rasayana, Kushmanda avaleha.

**Method**: prepare the decoction or juice → dissolve jaggery/sugar and filter → heat on a mild fire with constant stirring until the right consistency (*paka*) is reached — the mass sinks in water without spreading, or forms a thread between two fingers → add the fine powders and ghee/oil, mix thoroughly → cool and add honey when lukewarm → store in clean, dry, wide-mouthed containers.

**Standardisation**: description, consistency, moisture, total ash, acid-insoluble ash, pH, reducing and non-reducing sugars, fat content where ghee is used, TLC, microbial limits and heavy metals.

### Bhasma — calcined metallic and mineral preparations

Bhasma are fine powders obtained by repeated incineration of purified metals, minerals or animal products (shells, pearls) with herbal juices. Examples: Swarna (gold) bhasma, Abhraka (mica) bhasma, Lauha (iron) bhasma, Yashada (zinc) bhasma, Shankha (conch shell) bhasma, Praval (coral) bhasma.

```text
Raw metal/mineral
   │ Shodhana (purification): heating and quenching in
   │ liquids such as oil, buttermilk, cow's urine, decoctions
   ▼
Purified material
   │ Bhavana (trituration with herbal juices), made into small cakes (chakrika), dried
   ▼
Marana (incineration): cakes sealed in earthen saucers (sarava samputa),
heated in a pit with cow-dung cakes ("puta"); repeated many times
   ▼
Fine, lustreless, tasteless powder = Bhasma
```

**Classical tests for a properly made bhasma**

| Test | Expected result |
| --- | --- |
| **Rekhapurnatva** | Powder enters and fills the lines of the fingertips (very fine) |
| **Varitara** | A small amount floats on still water (fineness, lightness) |
| **Nishchandratva** | No metallic lustre seen in bright light |
| **Nirutthatva / Apunarbhava** | Does not revert to the metal when heated with specified substances |
| **Nirdhuma** | No fumes on heating |
| Taste and colour | Tasteless (no metallic taste); characteristic colour |

**Modern standardisation**: particle size, X-ray diffraction (chemical form of the metal), scanning electron microscopy, elemental analysis (atomic absorption, ICP), loss on drying, acid-insoluble ash, and safety studies. Bhasmas contain metals by design, so their safety depends entirely on correct processing.

## Remember

- Herb → herbal material → herbal preparation → finished herbal product: learn the four definitions with examples.
- Wild collection is cheap but variable and harmful to species; cultivation gives uniform, traceable material.
- Authentication: voucher specimen, macroscopy, microscopy, physicochemical values, chemical tests, TLC/HPTLC fingerprint, DNA methods; quarantine until released.
- Drying is the key processing step; garbling removes foreign matter.
- Biodynamic farming: Rudolf Steiner; preparations 500 (horn manure), 501 (horn silica), 502–507 (compost herbs); sowing calendar.
- GACP covers identity, site, seed, cultivation, pesticides, harvest, processing, storage, personnel and documentation.
- Biopesticides: azadirachtin (neem), pyrethrins, rotenone, *Bacillus thuringiensis*, *Beauveria*, *Trichoderma*, NPV.
- Ayurveda: panchamahabhuta, tridosha (vata, pitta, kapha), sapta dhatu, rasa–guna–virya–vipaka–prabhava.
- Siddha: three humours, 96 thathuvas, eight-fold diagnosis, metals and minerals. Unani: four humours, mizaj, six essentials. Homoeopathy: similia, proving, single remedy, minimum dose, potentisation.
- Asava = cold, Arishta = decoction; both ferment with jaggery/sugar and dhataki flowers to self-produced alcohol.
- Churna = fine powder; Gutika = pills; Lehya = jam with jaggery, ghee, honey (Chyawanprash); Bhasma = shodhana → bhavana → marana, tested by rekhapurnatva, varitara, nishchandratva, apunarbhava.
