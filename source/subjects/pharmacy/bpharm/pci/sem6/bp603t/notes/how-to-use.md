# How to use this guide

Herbal Drug Technology is a sixth-semester subject that takes you from the medicinal plant in the field to the finished, legally made herbal product. Unit 1 is about raw herbs and the Indian systems of medicine, Unit 2 about nutraceuticals and interactions, Unit 3 about cosmetics, excipients and formulations, Unit 4 about evaluation, patents and law, and Unit 5 about the herbal industry and Schedule T. Marks come from **neat definitions**, **classifications with examples**, **biological sources with families and key constituents**, **flow charts of processes**, and **correct legal names** (sections, schedules, bodies).

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Unit 1 is the largest (11 of the 45 teaching hours) and has two halves — raw materials (definitions, sourcing, GAP, organic and biodynamic farming, pests) and Indian systems of medicine with the Ayurvedic dosage forms. Study each half on a separate day. Unit 4 mixes science (WHO/ICH, stability) and law (patents, ASU regulation); keep a separate one-page table for the legal points.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

The question bank has long essay (10-mark), short answer (5-mark) and very short (2-mark) questions. Very short questions help with Section A and with quick revision.

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with tables, examples and a diagram where one fits.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common patterns in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start every long answer with a **one-line definition**, then a **classification or flow chart**, then the details, then **examples** and **uses**. Examiners in this subject reward structure more than length.
2. For every herb write **biological source with family → key constituent with its class → use → caution or interaction**: "Garlic, bulbs of *Allium sativum*, Liliaceae; alliin → allicin by alliinase; lowers lipids; bleeding with warfarin."
3. Learn the **Ayurvedic dosage forms as pairs of method and tests**: Asava (cold) versus Arishta (decoction), both fermented with dhataki flowers and jaggery; Bhasma = shodhana → bhavana → marana, tested by rekhapurnatva, varitara, nishchandratva, apunarbhava.
4. In interaction questions, always say whether the interaction is **pharmacokinetic** (CYP3A4 or P-gp induction or inhibition) or **pharmacodynamic** (additive or opposing), and give the clinical result.
5. Get the **legal names exactly right**: Drugs and Cosmetics Act 1940, Chapter IV-A; ASU DTAB (Section 33C); ASU DCC (Section 33D); Schedule T (GMP for ASU); Schedule E(1) (poisonous ASU drugs); Patents Act Section 3(p); Biological Diversity Act 2002; TKDL. Where you are unsure of a number or date, describe the rule without it rather than guessing.
6. For the patent cases, use a **five-step story**: traditional use → patent granted → who challenged → evidence of prior art → outcome and lesson.
7. For Schedule T, list the **components as headings** (premises, water, waste, stores, working space, machinery, SOPs, health and hygiene, documentation, quality control) with one or two lines each — this alone earns most marks.
8. Do not confuse: herbal preparation versus finished herbal product; nutraceutical versus functional food versus dietary supplement; probiotic versus prebiotic; phytosome versus liposome; ASU DTAB versus ASU DCC; biopiracy versus bioprospecting; Schedule T (GMP) versus Schedule Z (licensing requirements).
9. For practical examinations, revise the alcohol determination of asava/arishta, the cassia-flask phenol method, the hydroxylamine aldehyde method, the total alkaloid shake-out with back-titration, and the preliminary phytochemical tests — these are the usual spotting and viva questions.
