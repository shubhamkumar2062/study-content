# Unit 3: Herbal cosmetics, herbal excipients and herbal formulations

Plants give the cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries much more than active drugs. Oils, waxes, gums, colours, perfumes and antioxidants of plant origin go into creams, shampoos and toothpastes; starches, gums and sugars go into tablets and syrups as excipients. This unit covers these raw materials, how they are used in skin, hair and oral products, the role of natural excipients, and how herbal extracts are made into conventional dosage forms (syrups, mixtures, tablets) and into a novel form, the phytosome.

## 3.1 Herbal cosmetics — introduction

A **cosmetic** is a product meant to be rubbed, poured, sprinkled or sprayed on the body to cleanse, beautify, make attractive or change the appearance, without affecting the body's structure or function. A **herbal cosmetic** uses ingredients of plant origin — extracts, oils, waxes, gums, colours or perfumes — either as the base or as the active part. In India cosmetics are regulated under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and the Cosmetics Rules.

Why herbal cosmetics are popular: consumer belief that natural is mild and safe, fewer synthetic chemicals, traditional use (turmeric, sandalwood, henna, neem, amla), and multiple benefits in one ingredient (aloe moisturises and soothes). They still need preservatives, stability testing and patch testing, because plant materials can cause allergy and grow microbes.

## 3.2 Raw materials of herbal origin

### Fixed oils

Fixed oils are esters of glycerol with fatty acids (triglycerides). They soften the skin (emollients), form a film that reduces water loss, and act as the oily phase of creams and lotions.

| Oil | Source and family | Cosmetic use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coconut oil | Kernel of *Cocos nucifera*, Arecaceae | Hair oil, soaps (lauric acid gives good lather), creams |
| Olive oil | Fruit of *Olea europaea*, Oleaceae | Emollient in creams, massage oils, baby products |
| Almond oil | Seeds of *Prunus amygdalus*, Rosaceae | Emollient, skin and baby oils, under-eye creams |
| Castor oil | Seeds of *Ricinus communis*, Euphorbiaceae | Lipsticks (gloss, dissolves dyes), hair oils |
| Sesame (til) oil | Seeds of *Sesamum indicum*, Pedaliaceae | Massage oils, base for Ayurvedic oils |
| Sunflower, groundnut, jojoba* | — | Skin creams, hair conditioners |

*Jojoba "oil" from *Simmondsia chinensis* is really a **liquid wax**, very stable and close to skin sebum.

### Waxes

Waxes are esters of long-chain fatty acids with long-chain alcohols. They give body, hardness and gloss, and raise the melting point of sticks and creams.

| Wax | Source | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Beeswax** | Honeycomb of *Apis* species | Cold creams (with borax forms an emulsifier), lipsticks, lip balms |
| **Carnauba wax** | Leaves of the palm *Copernicia cerifera* (*C. prunifera*) | Hardest natural wax; gives gloss and stiffness to lipsticks, mascara; tablet polishing |
| **Candelilla wax** | Stems of *Euphorbia cerifera* | Lipsticks, a substitute for beeswax |
| Jojoba wax | Seeds of *Simmondsia chinensis* | Skin and hair products |
| Lanolin (wool fat)* | Sheep wool | Emollient — animal origin, often grouped here |

### Gums and mucilages

Gums are polysaccharides that swell or dissolve in water to form viscous solutions or gels. In cosmetics they thicken lotions and gels, stabilise emulsions and form films.

- **Acacia** (gum arabic) — *Acacia senegal*, Fabaceae: emulsifier, film former in face masks.
- **Tragacanth** — *Astragalus gummifer*, Fabaceae: thickener for lotions and gels, hair-setting lotions.
- **Karaya** — *Sterculia urens*, Sterculiaceae (Malvaceae): denture adhesives, hair-setting.
- **Guar gum** — seed endosperm of *Cyamopsis tetragonolobus*, Fabaceae: thickener, conditioning agent (as guar derivatives) in shampoos.
- **Xanthan gum** — produced by the bacterium *Xanthomonas campestris*: stable thickener over wide pH.
- **Agar, alginates, carrageenan** — from seaweeds: gels and thickeners.
- **Aloe gel** — *Aloe vera* (*A. barbadensis*), Asphodelaceae/Liliaceae: soothing, moisturising.

### Colours

| Colour | Source and pigment | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orange-red (hair, nails) | **Henna** — leaves of *Lawsonia inermis*, Lythraceae; **lawsone** (a naphthoquinone) binds keratin | Hair dye, mehendi |
| Yellow | **Turmeric** — *Curcuma longa*, Zingiberaceae; **curcumin** | Creams, fairness and anti-acne products |
| Orange-yellow | **Annatto** — seeds of *Bixa orellana*; **bixin** (carotenoid) | Lipsticks, soaps |
| Yellow | **Saffron** — stigmas of *Crocus sativus*, Iridaceae; **crocin** | Premium creams |
| Red-violet | **Beetroot** — *Beta vulgaris*; **betanin** | Lip and cheek tints |
| Blue / black | **Indigo** — *Indigofera tinctoria*; indigotin (with henna for black hair) | Hair dyes |
| Green | **Chlorophyll** (and copper chlorophyllin) | Soaps, toothpastes |
| Red | Carmine from cochineal insect (animal origin) | Lipsticks |
| Red | Red sandalwood — *Pterocarpus santalinus*; santalins | Face packs |

### Perfumes

Natural perfumes are mostly **volatile oils** obtained by steam distillation, expression, solvent extraction or enfleurage.

| Perfume | Source | Main constituents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rose oil | Petals of *Rosa damascena*, Rosaceae | Geraniol, citronellol, phenylethyl alcohol |
| Jasmine | Flowers of *Jasminum grandiflorum*, Oleaceae (absolute) | Benzyl acetate, jasmone |
| Sandalwood oil | Heartwood of *Santalum album*, Santalaceae | Santalols |
| Lavender oil | *Lavandula angustifolia*, Lamiaceae | Linalool, linalyl acetate |
| Vetiver (khus) | Roots of *Vetiveria zizanioides*, Poaceae | Vetiverols — fixative |
| Lemongrass | *Cymbopogon flexuosus*, Poaceae | Citral |
| Patchouli | *Pogostemon cablin*, Lamiaceae | Patchouli alcohol — fixative |

### Protective agents

Protective agents shield the skin from sun, water loss, irritation and microbes:

- **Sun protection**: extracts rich in polyphenols and flavonoids (green tea, aloe, turmeric) give some antioxidant and UV-protective support; vegetable oils with natural tocopherols. Mineral filters such as zinc oxide are often combined.
- **Moisturisers and humectants**: aloe gel, honey, glycerin of vegetable origin.
- **Barrier agents**: vegetable butters (cocoa butter from *Theobroma cacao*, shea butter from *Vitellaria paradoxa*), waxes.
- **Soothing and healing agents**: aloe, calendula, chamomile, liquorice, neem (antibacterial).

### Bleaching (skin-lightening) agents

These reduce dark spots and uneven pigment by blocking the enzyme **tyrosinase**, which makes melanin, or by gentle exfoliation:

- **Liquorice** root extract (*Glycyrrhiza glabra*) — glabridin.
- **Arbutin** — from bearberry (*Arctostaphylos uva-ursi*) leaves.
- **Kojic acid** — from fungi (*Aspergillus*) fermentation.
- **Lemon juice** — citric acid and vitamin C; **papaya** (papain) and **pineapple** (bromelain) — enzymatic exfoliation.
- **Turmeric** (curcumin), **saffron**, **mulberry** extract, **vitamin C** derivatives.

### Antioxidants

Antioxidants stop oils going rancid and protect skin from free radicals.

- **Tocopherols** (vitamin E) — wheat germ, sunflower, soybean oils.
- **Ascorbic acid** (vitamin C) — amla, citrus.
- **Rosemary extract** — carnosic acid, carnosol, rosmarinic acid.
- **Green tea** — catechins (EGCG).
- **Grape seed** — proanthocyanidins; **curcumin**; **sesamol** from sesame oil.

| Product | Herbal ingredients and their role |
| --- | --- |

In formulation, abrasives (calcium carbonate), humectants (sorbitol, glycerin), thickeners (gums), foaming agents and sweeteners are added to the herbal actives.

## 3.4 Herbal excipients

An **excipient** is any substance other than the active ingredient that is added to a dosage form to help in manufacture, protect the product, or improve patient acceptance, release or absorption.

### Significance of natural excipients

Advantages:

- **Biocompatible and non-toxic**; many are eaten as food.
- **Biodegradable** and eco-friendly.
- **Cheap and widely available**, renewable.
- Can be **chemically modified** (pregelatinised starch, sodium starch glycolate, carboxymethyl derivatives) to improve properties.
- Patient acceptance and suitability for "natural" products.

Disadvantages:

- **Batch-to-batch variation** with source, season and processing.
- **Microbial contamination** — need tight limits.
- **Uncontrolled hydration and viscosity changes** on storage.
- Possible **heavy-metal and pesticide** contamination.
- Slow, variable supply and the need for standardisation.

### Classes and examples

| Function | Herbal examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Colorants** | Curcumin, chlorophyll, caramel, carotenoids (beta-carotene, annatto), anthocyanins, beetroot red | Improve appearance and identification; less stable to light and pH than synthetic dyes |
| **Sweeteners** | Sucrose (sugarcane, beet), honey, glucose (from starch), **stevioside/rebaudioside** (*Stevia rebaudiana* leaves, many times sweeter than sugar, non-caloric), **glycyrrhizin** (liquorice), **thaumatin** (protein from *Thaumatococcus daniellii* fruit), **mogrosides** (monk fruit), sorbitol and mannitol (from glucose/mannose) | Natural non-caloric sweeteners suit diabetics |
| **Binders** | Acacia, tragacanth, **starch paste**, pregelatinised starch, gelatin (animal), alginates, guar gum, cellulose derivatives | Hold tablet granules together |
| **Diluents (fillers)** | Starch (maize, potato, rice), **microcrystalline cellulose** (from wood pulp), lactose (from milk), sucrose, mannitol, calcium carbonate | Give bulk to small-dose tablets |
| **Viscosity builders** | Acacia, tragacanth, guar, xanthan, sodium alginate, pectin, agar, carrageenan, methylcellulose | Thicken liquids, suspend particles, stabilise emulsions |
| **Disintegrants** | Starch, **sodium starch glycolate**, alginic acid, MCC, agar, guar gum, isabgol husk | Swell in water and break the tablet |
| **Flavours and perfumes** | Peppermint, spearmint, lemon, orange, cinnamon, clove, cardamom, vanilla, anise oils | Mask taste, improve acceptance |
| Others | Lubricants (vegetable stearates, talc), coating agents (shellac — insect resin; zein — maize protein), gelling agents (pectin, agar) | — |

A natural excipient must be **standardised** like any raw material: identity, moisture, ash, swelling index, viscosity, pH, particle size, microbial limits and heavy metals.

## 3.5 Herbal formulations

Herbal extracts are difficult to formulate: they are mixtures, often coloured, bitter or hygroscopic, may be sticky and may contain constituents that degrade. Steps common to all formulations:

1. **Prepare and standardise the extract** — fixed solvent, ratio of drug to extract (DER), marker content, solvent residue, microbial and heavy-metal limits.
2. **Pre-formulation** — solubility, hygroscopicity, stability to heat, light and pH, compatibility with excipients.
3. **Formulation and process** design.
4. **Evaluation** as per pharmacopoeia, plus marker assay and fingerprint.
5. **Stability testing** in the final pack.

### Herbal syrups

A syrup is a concentrated, viscous aqueous solution of sugar (or sugar substitute) containing the extract or a decoction. Example: cough syrups with vasaka, tulsi, mulethi; tonic syrups with ashwagandha.

**Method**: dissolve sucrose in purified water with heat (simple syrup) → cool → add the aqueous or hydroalcoholic extract (or fluid extract) gradually with stirring → add preservatives (methyl/propyl paraben, sodium benzoate), flavour and colour → make up to volume → filter → fill into clean amber bottles.

**Evaluation**: appearance and clarity, pH, specific gravity, viscosity, sugar content, marker assay, alcohol content (if any), microbial limit, preservative efficacy, fill volume, stability.

### Herbal mixtures

A mixture is a liquid oral preparation in which the drugs are dissolved or **suspended** in an aqueous vehicle. Herbal mixtures often contain insoluble powders or extracts that need a suspending agent.

**Method**: wet the powder or extract with a little vehicle → add a suspending agent (tragacanth, acacia, xanthan or sodium CMC) → add the rest of the vehicle, preservative and flavour → homogenise → make up to volume → label "Shake well before use".

**Evaluation**: sedimentation volume (F = Hu/Ho, ratio of settled-sediment height to original height), ease of redispersion, viscosity, pH, particle size, marker assay, microbial limits.

### Herbal tablets

**Method** (wet granulation, the usual route for extracts):

```text
Standardised dry extract + diluent (MCC, starch) + disintegrant
          │ mix
          ▼
Granulate with binder (starch paste, PVP, acacia)  →  sieve wet mass
          │
          ▼
Dry granules (controlled temperature)  →  sieve dry granules
          │
          ▼
Add lubricant and glidant (magnesium stearate, talc)  →  compress
          │
          ▼
(Film-coat to mask taste, odour and protect from moisture)
```

Problems with extracts: **hygroscopic, sticky** extracts cause picking and sticking — use adsorbents (colloidal silica), dry granulation or coating; **high dose** of extract makes large tablets — use concentrated extracts.

**Evaluation (pharmacopoeial)**: appearance, uniformity of weight, hardness, friability, disintegration time, dissolution (where marker can be measured), content of marker, moisture, microbial limits.

## 3.6 Novel dosage forms — phytosomes

Many plant actives, especially **polyphenols and flavonoids**, are water-soluble but too polar to cross the lipid membranes of the gut, and others are poorly soluble in water. Both give poor oral bioavailability. **Phytosomes** solve this.

**Definition**: a phytosome is a **complex between a natural active constituent and a phospholipid**, usually **phosphatidylcholine** from soy lecithin, in which the active is bonded to the polar head of the phospholipid. The term and the technology were developed by the Italian company **Indena**.

```text
       Phosphatidylcholine molecule
   Polar head (choline-phosphate) ── H-bonds ── Polyphenol / flavonoid
            │
   Two fatty-acid tails (lipophilic)  → wrap the complex, make it
                                        compatible with cell membranes
```

**Preparation**: the phospholipid and the active (or extract) are reacted in a fixed molar ratio (often 1:1 or 2:1) in an aprotic solvent such as dioxane or acetone (or by modern methods like solvent evaporation, anti-solvent precipitation or freeze-drying); the complex is recovered by precipitation with a non-solvent or by drying.

**Phytosome versus liposome**

| Feature | Phytosome | Liposome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Binding | Active is chemically bonded (H-bonds) to phospholipid — part of the membrane | Active is enclosed in the aqueous core or bilayer, not bonded |
| Phospholipid : active | Low, defined molar ratio | Large excess of phospholipid |
| Stability | Better; less leakage | Leakage of contents possible |
| Bioavailability gain | Large, for polar polyphenols | Variable |

**Advantages**: much better absorption and bioavailability of polar polyphenols; lower dose needed; phosphatidylcholine itself is hepatoprotective; good skin penetration in cosmetics; stable complex.

**Examples**: silybin phytosome (from milk thistle, *Silybum marianum*), ginkgo phytosome, green tea (EGCG) phytosome, curcumin phytosome, grape seed phytosome, ginseng phytosome, hawthorn phytosome.

**Characterisation**: complex formation by IR, NMR, DSC and X-ray diffraction; particle size and zeta potential; solubility and partition coefficient; drug content; in vitro release and in vivo bioavailability.

Other novel herbal delivery systems often mentioned: liposomes, niosomes, ethosomes (skin), nanoparticles, solid lipid nanoparticles, microspheres, nanoemulsions and self-emulsifying systems — all aim to improve solubility, absorption, stability and targeting of herbal actives.

## Remember

- Herbal cosmetic raw materials: fixed oils (coconut, olive, almond, castor), waxes (beeswax, carnauba, candelilla; jojoba is a liquid wax), gums (acacia, tragacanth, karaya, guar, xanthan), colours (lawsone, curcumin, bixin, crocin, betanin, indigo, chlorophyll), perfumes (rose, jasmine, sandalwood, lavender, vetiver).
- Bleaching agents block tyrosinase: liquorice (glabridin), arbutin, kojic acid, vitamin C.
- Antioxidants: tocopherols, ascorbic acid, rosemary (carnosic acid), green tea catechins.
- Hair: shikakai and reetha (saponins), amla, bhringraj, henna. Oral: neem, babool, clove (eugenol), peppermint (menthol), miswak.
- Natural excipients: biocompatible, cheap, biodegradable — but variable and microbe-prone; must be standardised.
- Sweeteners: stevioside, glycyrrhizin, thaumatin, mogrosides; disintegrants: starch, sodium starch glycolate, alginic acid.
- Syrups, mixtures (sedimentation volume) and tablets (wet granulation; sticky extracts need adsorbents) are evaluated as per pharmacopoeia plus marker assay.
- Phytosome = phosphatidylcholine–polyphenol complex (Indena); better bioavailability than free extract; differs from liposome in bonding and ratio; silybin phytosome is the classic example.
