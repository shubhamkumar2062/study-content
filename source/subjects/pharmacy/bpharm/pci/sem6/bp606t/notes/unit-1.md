# Unit 1: Quality assurance and quality management concepts

A medicine cannot be checked into quality at the end of the line. A tablet that passes every test on a sample may still be one of thousands made under poor conditions. Quality assurance is the whole system that makes sure each batch is right every time. This unit sets out the basic ideas (QC, QA, GMP, TQM), the international guidelines that shape them (ICH, QbD) and the management standards a company or laboratory can be certified against (ISO 9000, ISO 14000, NABL).

## 1.1 Quality, quality control, quality assurance and GMP

**Quality** of a medicine means it is fit for its intended use: it has the right identity, strength, purity and performance, it is safe, and it stays that way until the expiry date. Put simply, a quality product consistently meets its specification and the needs of the patient.

| Term | Meaning | Focus | Example of activity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quality control (QC) | The part of GMP concerned with sampling, specifications, testing, and release or rejection of materials and products | Detecting defects in what has been made (product-oriented, reactive) | Assay of a tablet batch, dissolution test, sterility test |
| Quality assurance (QA) | The sum of all organised arrangements made to ensure that products are of the quality needed for their intended use | Preventing defects by controlling the whole system (process-oriented, proactive) | Approving SOPs, validation, change control, audits, batch release decision |
| Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) | The part of QA that ensures products are consistently produced and controlled to quality standards suited to their use and as required by the licence | Rules for people, premises, equipment, materials, processes and records | Following Schedule M, line clearance, cleaning records |

```text
Relationship (each is part of the next):

   +-------------------------------------------------+
   | Quality management / Pharmaceutical quality      |
   |   system                                         |
   |   +-------------------------------------------+  |
   |   | Quality assurance                         |  |
   |   |   +-----------------------------------+   |  |
   |   |   | GMP                               |   |  |
   |   |   |   +---------------------------+   |   |  |
   |   |   |   | Quality control (testing) |   |   |  |
   |   |   |   +---------------------------+   |   |  |
   |   |   +-----------------------------------+   |  |
   |   +-------------------------------------------+  |
   +-------------------------------------------------+
```

**Key differences between QA and QC**

| QA | QC |
| --- | --- |
| Prevents defects | Finds defects |
| Covers the whole process from design to distribution | Covers testing of samples |
| Responsibility of everyone in the company, led by the QA department | Responsibility of the QC laboratory |
| Tools: SOPs, validation, audits, training, change control, deviation and CAPA systems | Tools: specifications, test methods, instruments, sampling plans |
| Asks "is the system right?" | Asks "is this batch right?" |

**Basic principles of GMP** (common to WHO GMP and Schedule M)

1. Manufacturing processes are clearly defined, validated and reviewed.
2. Changes are controlled and evaluated before they are made.
3. Instructions are written as clear, unambiguous SOPs.
4. Operators are trained to follow them.
5. Records are made during manufacture, showing every step was done; deviations are investigated.
6. Records of manufacture and distribution allow the full history of a batch to be traced.
7. Storage and distribution do not damage quality.
8. A system exists to recall any batch from sale.
9. Complaints are examined, causes found and repeats prevented.

**GMP in India.** GMP is legally required under the Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945, through **Schedule M** (Good Manufacturing Practices and requirements of premises, plant and equipment for pharmaceutical products). Schedule M was revised by a notification dated 28 December 2023. The revised text brings in a pharmaceutical quality system, quality risk management, product quality review, validation and computerised systems, close to WHO GMP. Compliance dates were phased by company size. Always check the current Schedule M text and deadlines on the CDSCO website, because dates have been extended more than once.

## 1.2 Total Quality Management (TQM)

**Definition.** TQM is a management approach in which the whole organisation, from top management to the shop floor, works together to improve processes, products, services and culture continuously, with customer satisfaction as the aim.

**Elements of TQM**

| Element | What it means in a pharmaceutical company |
| --- | --- |
| Customer focus | The patient, doctor, pharmacist and regulator are the customers; their needs define quality |
| Top management commitment and leadership | Management sets the quality policy, provides resources and leads by example |
| Total employee involvement | Every worker owns the quality of their work; teams, quality circles, suggestion schemes |
| Process-centred approach | Work is seen as linked processes with inputs and outputs that can be measured |
| Integrated system | Departments (production, QC, QA, stores, engineering) share one system and goals |
| Strategic and systematic approach | Quality goals are part of the business plan |
| Continual improvement | Small steady improvements (kaizen), PDCA cycle |
| Fact-based decision making | Data, statistics, trend analysis, control charts |
| Communication | Policies and results reach everyone |
| Training and education | Skills and quality awareness are built up |

**Philosophies of the quality pioneers ("quality gurus")**

| Thinker | Main ideas |
| --- | --- |
| W. Edwards Deming | 14 points for management; the PDCA (Plan–Do–Check–Act) cycle; reduce variation; cease dependence on mass inspection; drive out fear; constant improvement |
| Joseph M. Juran | Quality is "fitness for use"; the quality trilogy: quality planning, quality control, quality improvement; the Pareto principle (the vital few causes produce most problems) |
| Philip B. Crosby | Quality is conformance to requirements; "zero defects"; prevention, not appraisal; the cost of quality is the price of non-conformance; "quality is free" |
| Kaoru Ishikawa | Cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram; quality circles; company-wide quality control; the seven basic quality tools |
| Armand V. Feigenbaum | Total quality control: quality is everyone's job across all departments; cost of quality categories |
| Genichi Taguchi | Robust design; the quality loss function (any departure from the target value is a loss, even inside limits) |

```text
PDCA cycle (Deming cycle)

        PLAN  -> set objectives, find causes, plan the change
         ^                      |
         |                      v
        ACT   <- standardise    DO -> carry out the change on a small scale
         ^      or adjust        |
         |                      v
         +--------------- CHECK -> study the results against the plan
```

**Seven basic quality tools:** check sheet, histogram, Pareto chart, cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram, scatter diagram, control chart, and flow chart (or stratification, in some lists).

**Cost of quality** is grouped as prevention costs (training, validation), appraisal costs (testing, inspection), internal failure costs (rework, rejected batches) and external failure costs (complaints, recalls). TQM spends on prevention to cut the failure costs.

## 1.3 ICH guidelines

**ICH** is the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use. It began in 1990 as the International Conference on Harmonisation, bringing together the drug regulators and the research-based industry of the European Union, Japan and the USA. In 2015 it was reorganised as an international association with its present name, and its membership has since grown to many more regulators and industry bodies.

**Purpose**

- Make technical requirements for registering medicines the same across regions, so one set of data can serve many countries.
- Avoid repeating human and animal studies needlessly.
- Reduce development cost and time, and bring safe, effective, good-quality medicines to patients faster.
- Keep high standards for quality, safety and efficacy.

**Participants**

| Group | Who (examples) |
| --- | --- |
| Founding regulatory members | European Commission; FDA (USA); MHLW/PMDA (Japan) |
| Founding industry members | EFPIA (Europe), PhRMA (USA), JPMA (Japan) |
| Standing regulatory members and other members | Health Canada, Swissmedic and many other national regulators added later |
| Observers | WHO and a number of national regulators and organisations |

Membership changes often; see the "Members and Observers" page on ich.org for the current list, including India's status.

**Process of harmonisation (the ICH steps)**

| Step | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Step 1 | Consensus building: an expert working group drafts a technical document |
| Step 2 | 2a: the Assembly confirms consensus on the technical document; 2b: regulatory members adopt it as a draft guideline |
| Step 3 | Regulatory consultation: each region publishes the draft for public comment; the working group revises it |
| Step 4 | Adoption of the final ICH harmonised guideline by the regulatory members |
| Step 5 | Implementation: each regulator brings it into its own national rules |

A revised guideline gets an "(R)" with a number, for example Q1A(R2) is the second revision of Q1A.

**QSEM: the four groups of ICH guidelines**

| Letter | Area | Examples of topics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q | Quality (chemical and pharmaceutical quality) | Stability, analytical validation, impurities, GMP, development, risk management |
| S | Safety (non-clinical studies) | Carcinogenicity, genotoxicity, reproductive toxicology, safety pharmacology |
| E | Efficacy (clinical studies) | Good Clinical Practice (E6), clinical trial design, pharmacovigilance |
| M | Multidisciplinary | Common Technical Document (M4), MedDRA terminology, electronic standards |

**Q-series guidelines (titles in brief)**

| Code | Title (short form) |
| --- | --- |
| Q1A(R2) | Stability testing of new drug substances and products |
| Q1B | Photostability testing of new drug substances and products |
| Q1C | Stability testing for new dosage forms |
| Q1D | Bracketing and matrixing designs for stability testing |
| Q1E | Evaluation of stability data |
| Q2(R2) | Validation of analytical procedures |
| Q3A(R2) | Impurities in new drug substances |
| Q3B(R2) | Impurities in new drug products |
| Q3C | Residual solvents (revised several times; check the current R number) |
| Q3D | Elemental impurities (check the current R number) |
| Q4 / Q4A / Q4B | Pharmacopoeias; pharmacopoeial harmonisation; evaluation of pharmacopoeial texts for use in ICH regions |
| Q5A–Q5E | Quality of biotechnological products (viral safety, genetic stability, stability, cell substrates, comparability) |
| Q6A | Specifications: test procedures and acceptance criteria for new drug substances and products (chemical substances) |
| Q6B | Specifications for biotechnological and biological products |
| Q7 | GMP for active pharmaceutical ingredients |
| Q8(R2) | Pharmaceutical development (basis of QbD) |
| Q9(R1) | Quality risk management |
| Q10 | Pharmaceutical quality system |
| Q11 | Development and manufacture of drug substances |
| Q12 | Product lifecycle management |
| Q13 | Continuous manufacturing |
| Q14 | Analytical procedure development |

The trio **Q8, Q9 and Q10** is often learned together: Q8 tells how to develop a product with understanding, Q9 gives the risk-based way of thinking, and Q10 describes the quality system that keeps the product right through its life.

ICH revises its guidelines often. ICH has also been working on a single consolidated stability guideline to replace Q1A–Q1E. Check the "Quality Guidelines" page on ich.org for the current version and step of each guideline before quoting an R number in an exam answer.

## 1.4 ICH stability testing guidelines

**Stability** is the ability of a drug substance or product to stay within its specification for identity, strength, quality and purity throughout its shelf life, in its container. Stability testing gives the evidence for the **shelf life (expiry date)**, **retest period** (for a drug substance) and **storage conditions** on the label.

**Main points of Q1A(R2)**

- **Batches:** data from at least three primary batches; for a drug product, at least two of the three should be at least pilot scale, made by a process that simulates the final one.
- **Container:** the same container-closure system proposed for marketing.
- **Tests:** attributes likely to change and affect quality, safety or efficacy (assay, degradation products, dissolution, water content, appearance, microbial limits, and so on), with validated stability-indicating methods.
- **Stress testing** of the drug substance (heat, humidity, oxidation, light, pH) shows the likely degradation products and pathways and proves that the method is stability-indicating.

**General storage conditions for a drug product**

| Study | Storage condition | Minimum period covered at submission |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long-term | 25 °C ± 2 °C / 60% RH ± 5% RH, or 30 °C ± 2 °C / 65% RH ± 5% RH | 12 months |
| Intermediate | 30 °C ± 2 °C / 65% RH ± 5% RH | 6 months |
| Accelerated | 40 °C ± 2 °C / 75% RH ± 5% RH | 6 months |

Intermediate testing is needed only if long-term testing is at 25 °C/60% RH and a "significant change" occurs at the accelerated condition.

| Product stored in | Long-term | Accelerated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refrigerator | 5 °C ± 3 °C | 25 °C ± 2 °C / 60% RH ± 5% RH |
| Freezer | −20 °C ± 5 °C | (case by case) |

**Testing frequency (long-term):** every 3 months in the first year, every 6 months in the second year, then once a year. **Accelerated:** at least three time points including the start and the end, for example 0, 3 and 6 months.

**"Significant change" for a drug product** (any one of these):

1. A 5% change in assay from the initial value.
2. Any degradation product exceeding its acceptance criterion.
3. Failure of appearance, physical attributes or functionality test (colour, phase separation, hardness, dose delivery per actuation).
4. Failure of pH, where relevant.
5. Failure of dissolution for 12 dosage units, where relevant.

**Climatic zones** decide the long-term condition for a given market:

| Zone | Climate | Typical long-term condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Temperate | 21 °C / 45% RH |
| II | Subtropical and Mediterranean | 25 °C / 60% RH |
| III | Hot and dry | 30 °C / 35% RH |
| IVa | Hot and humid | 30 °C / 65% RH |
| IVb | Hot and very humid | 30 °C / 75% RH |

India is placed in zone IVb in WHO's list, so long-term stability for the Indian market is generally done at 30 °C / 75% RH. Confirm the current CDSCO requirement in its guidance.

**Other Q1 guidelines in brief**

- **Q1B photostability:** samples are exposed to an overall illumination of not less than 1.2 million lux hours and an integrated near-ultraviolet energy of not less than 200 watt hours per square metre, first the drug substance, then the product outside and inside its pack, to see if light-resistant packaging or a "protect from light" label is needed.
- **Q1C:** stability for a new dosage form of an already approved drug substance (for example, a new injection of an approved tablet drug); a reduced data package may be accepted.
- **Q1D bracketing:** only the extremes of a design factor (highest and lowest strength, or largest and smallest container) are tested at every time point. **Matrixing:** only a chosen subset of all samples is tested at each time point, with different subsets at different times.
- **Q1E:** how to evaluate stability data, including statistical analysis (regression with 95% confidence limits) and limited extrapolation to propose a shelf life beyond the real-time data.

## 1.5 Quality by Design (QbD)

**Definition (ICH Q8(R2)).** A systematic approach to development that begins with predefined objectives and emphasises product and process understanding and process control, based on sound science and quality risk management.

The old approach fixed a process and then tested the product ("quality by testing"). QbD builds quality in from the start, so a product made within a well-understood design space will meet its quality targets.

**Elements of a QbD programme**

| Element | Meaning | Example (immediate-release tablet) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality target product profile (QTPP) | A prospective summary of the quality characteristics the product should have | Oral tablet, 500 mg, dissolution above a set limit in 30 min, 24-month shelf life |
| Critical quality attributes (CQAs) | Physical, chemical, biological or microbiological properties that must stay within limits to ensure quality | Assay, content uniformity, dissolution, impurities |
| Risk assessment | Links material attributes and process parameters to CQAs | Fishbone diagram, FMEA |
| Critical material attributes (CMAs) and critical process parameters (CPPs) | Inputs whose variation affects a CQA | Drug particle size (CMA); granulation time, compression force (CPPs) |
| Design space | The multidimensional combination of input variables and process parameters shown to assure quality; moving within it is not a change needing approval | A defined range of granulation water and drying temperature |
| Control strategy | A planned set of controls from product and process understanding | In-process tests, PAT probes, specifications |
| Product lifecycle management and continual improvement | Monitoring and updating knowledge over the product's life | Trend review, ICH Q10 system |

```text
QbD flow

QTPP -> CQAs -> risk assessment -> DoE to link CMAs/CPPs to CQAs
     -> design space -> control strategy -> lifecycle management
```

**Tools of QbD**

1. **Design of experiments (DoE):** factorial, fractional factorial, response surface designs (for example central composite, Box–Behnken) to study many factors with few runs.
2. **Risk assessment tools (ICH Q9):** Ishikawa (fishbone) diagram, FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis), FMECA, fault tree analysis, HACCP, HAZOP, preliminary hazard analysis, risk ranking and filtering.
3. **Process analytical technology (PAT):** real-time measurement during processing, for example near-infrared (NIR) for blend uniformity or moisture.
4. **Multivariate data analysis** and prior knowledge.

**Benefits:** fewer batch failures, a robust process, more regulatory flexibility, less end-product testing (real-time release is possible), and easier continual improvement.

## 1.6 ISO 9000 and ISO 14000

**ISO** (International Organization for Standardization, Geneva) publishes voluntary international standards. A company can be **certified** against a requirements standard by an independent certification body.

**ISO 9000 family (quality management)**

| Standard | Content |
| --- | --- |
| ISO 9000 | Quality management systems: fundamentals and vocabulary |
| ISO 9001 | Quality management systems: requirements (the only one used for certification) |
| ISO 9004 | Quality management: guidance for sustained success |
| ISO 19011 | Guidelines for auditing management systems |

The current editions in recent years have been ISO 9000:2015 and ISO 9001:2015, and a revision of ISO 9001 has been in progress. Check iso.org for the edition in force before quoting a year.

**Seven quality management principles (ISO 9000:2015):** customer focus; leadership; engagement of people; process approach; improvement; evidence-based decision making; relationship management.

**Elements (main clauses of ISO 9001:2015):** context of the organisation (4), leadership (5), planning, including risks and opportunities (6), support: resources, competence, awareness, communication, documented information (7), operation (8), performance evaluation: monitoring, internal audit, management review (9), improvement: nonconformity, corrective action (10). Clauses 1–3 are scope, references and terms.

**ISO 14000 family (environmental management)**

- **ISO 14001:** Environmental management systems: requirements with guidance for use (the certifiable standard; edition 2015 in recent years, check for amendments).
- **ISO 14004:** general guidelines on implementing an environmental management system.
- Others cover environmental auditing, environmental performance evaluation (ISO 14031), environmental labels, and life cycle assessment (ISO 14040 and ISO 14044).

ISO 14001 helps a pharmaceutical plant control effluent, solvent emissions, hazardous waste and energy use, comply with pollution laws, and show this to buyers.

**Benefits of ISO certification**

| ISO 9001 | ISO 14001 |
| --- | --- |
| Consistent products, fewer rejections | Lower pollution and waste |
| Clear responsibilities and documented processes | Compliance with environmental law |
| Better customer confidence and export access | Saving on raw materials and energy |
| Culture of measurement and continual improvement | Better public image and relations with regulators |

Note: ISO 9001 certification does not replace GMP. GMP is a legal requirement for a drug licence; ISO 9001 is voluntary and general.

**Steps for registration (certification)**

1. Top management decides, appoints a management representative and sets the quality policy.
2. Gap analysis against the standard.
3. Training of staff.
4. Preparation of documented information: policy, objectives, procedures, records.
5. Implementation for a few months, generating records.
6. Internal audit and management review; correction of gaps.
7. Selection of an accredited certification body (in India, certification bodies are accredited by NABCB under the Quality Council of India).
8. Stage 1 audit (readiness and document review) and Stage 2 audit (on-site implementation).
9. Closure of nonconformities with corrective action.
10. Grant of certificate, usually valid for three years with surveillance audits in between, then recertification.

## 1.7 NABL accreditation

**NABL** is the National Accreditation Board for Testing and Calibration Laboratories, a constituent board of the Quality Council of India. It accredits laboratories after checking their technical competence and management system.

**Standards used**

| Laboratory type | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| Testing and calibration laboratories (including drug testing labs) | ISO/IEC 17025 |
| Medical (clinical) laboratories | ISO 15189 |
| Proficiency testing providers | ISO/IEC 17043 |
| Reference material producers | ISO 17034 |

Check nabl-india.org for the edition of each standard currently applied.

**Accreditation vs certification:** certification (ISO 9001) confirms that a management system exists; accreditation (NABL) confirms that a laboratory is technically competent to produce valid results for a defined scope of tests.

**Principles of NABL accreditation**

- Impartiality and independence of the laboratory's results.
- Technical competence of staff, methods and equipment for a defined scope.
- Measurement traceability to national or international standards (through calibrated equipment and certified reference materials).
- Validated or verified methods and estimation of measurement uncertainty.
- Participation in proficiency testing or inter-laboratory comparison.
- Confidentiality and a working management system with internal audits and management review.
- International recognition: NABL is a signatory to the mutual recognition arrangements of ILAC and APAC, so accredited results are accepted abroad.

**Procedure for NABL accreditation**

```text
Prepare (ISO/IEC 17025 system, quality manual, internal audit, management review)
   |
Apply online with fee, quality manual and scope of tests
   |
Adequacy (document) review by NABL
   |
Pre-assessment visit (where applicable)
   |
Final on-site assessment by lead assessor and technical assessors
   (witness of tests, records, staff competence)
   |
Laboratory closes non-conformities with corrective action
   |
Accreditation committee recommends --> NABL grants accreditation for the scope
   |
Surveillance assessments, then reassessment at the end of the cycle
```

The validity period and surveillance schedule are set by NABL's current documents; check the latest NABL procedure.

## Remember

- QC tests the product, QA builds the system, GMP is the part of QA that controls manufacture; in India GMP = Schedule M (revised by the notification of 28 December 2023).
- TQM: customer focus, leadership, everyone involved, process approach, continual improvement (PDCA), decisions on facts. Deming = PDCA and 14 points; Juran = trilogy and fitness for use; Crosby = zero defects; Ishikawa = fishbone.
- ICH: founded 1990, reorganised 2015; QSEM; five steps from consensus to implementation.
- Stability: long-term 25/60 or 30/65 (12 months), intermediate 30/65 (6 months), accelerated 40/75 (6 months); India zone IVb, 30 °C/75% RH.
- QbD: QTPP → CQA → risk assessment → CMA/CPP → design space → control strategy → lifecycle.
- ISO 9001 = QMS (certifiable), ISO 14001 = EMS (certifiable); seven principles; 10 clauses.
- NABL accredits labs to ISO/IEC 17025 (testing and calibration) and ISO 15189 (medical); it is part of QCI.
- Check ich.org, iso.org, nabl-india.org and cdsco.gov.in for current versions before quoting edition numbers.
