# Test for pyrogens (rabbit method)

**Aim:** To test a given parenteral preparation for the presence of pyrogens by the rabbit method as described in the Indian Pharmacopoeia.

> Demonstrated by simulation or video as per the PCI note. Live animal work needs Institutional Animal Ethics Committee approval under CCSEA (earlier CPCSEA) rules. Follow the official monograph and your lab manual.

## Principle

**Pyrogens** are fever-producing substances. The most important are **bacterial endotoxins**, the lipopolysaccharides of the Gram-negative cell wall, which are heat stable and are not removed by ordinary sterilisation or by bacterial filtration. Injected into the blood, they act on monocytes and macrophages, which release **interleukin-1, interleukin-6 and TNF-α**; these act on the hypothalamic thermoregulatory centre through prostaglandin E2 and raise body temperature.

```text
 Endotoxin in injection --> monocytes/macrophages
            --> IL-1, IL-6, TNF-alpha --> hypothalamus (PGE2)
            --> rise in body temperature (fever)
```

Every injection of more than a small volume, and every large-volume parenteral, must be free from pyrogens. In the rabbit test, the preparation is injected into the ear vein of rabbits and the rise in rectal temperature is measured. The **LAL (Limulus amoebocyte lysate) test** is the in vitro alternative, which uses the clotting reaction of amoebocyte lysate from the horseshoe crab with endotoxin and is now widely used because it needs no animals.

## Requirements

Healthy rabbits of either sex, not used for a pyrogen test in the preceding days as the monograph directs; rabbit holders; clinical or electronic thermometer with a probe for rectal temperature; sterile pyrogen-free syringes, needles and glassware; the test preparation warmed to about 37 °C; pyrogen-free saline; a quiet room at constant temperature; or simulation software.

**Depyrogenation:** glassware is made pyrogen-free by dry heat (for example 250 °C for 30 minutes or as the monograph states); pyrogens are destroyed by dry heat, not by autoclaving alone.

## Procedure

1. Select healthy rabbits of the weight range given in the monograph; keep them in the test room for the stated time before the test so that they become used to the surroundings.
2. Withhold food overnight as directed; water is allowed until the test starts.
3. Place each rabbit in a holder and insert the thermometer probe into the rectum to the stated depth.
4. Record the **initial (control) temperature** of each rabbit as the mean of readings taken at the intervals given in the monograph. Reject any rabbit whose temperature varies by more than the permitted amount, or whose temperature is outside the stated range.
5. Warm the test preparation to about 37 °C and inject the stated volume per kg slowly into the marginal ear vein of each of three rabbits, completing each injection within the time the monograph allows.
6. Record the rectal temperature at the intervals stated (commonly every 30 minutes for 3 hours).
7. For each rabbit, find the **difference** between the highest temperature recorded after injection and its own control temperature. A fall is counted as zero rise.
8. Add the individual rises to get the **summed response** of the group and compare it with the limits in the monograph. If the result is inconclusive, repeat with more rabbits as the monograph directs.

## Result

## Precautions

1. Use only apparatus and solutions that are pyrogen-free; wash and depyrogenate all glassware by dry heat.
2. Keep the animals in the test room beforehand so that handling and surroundings do not themselves raise the temperature.
3. Keep the room temperature steady and the room quiet; avoid excitement.
4. Insert the thermometer to the same depth and for the same time in every reading.
5. Warm the preparation to about 37 °C before injection; a cold injection changes body temperature by itself.
6. Inject slowly into the marginal ear vein within the permitted time.
7. Do not reuse a rabbit before the rest period stated in the monograph.
8. Reject any animal with an unsteady baseline temperature.

## Viva questions

1. **What are pyrogens?** Substances that produce fever when injected; the most important are bacterial endotoxins (lipopolysaccharides of Gram-negative bacteria).
2. **Why are pyrogens not removed by autoclaving?** They are heat stable; they are destroyed by dry heat at high temperature.
3. **How does an endotoxin cause fever?** It makes macrophages release IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α, which raise the hypothalamic set point through prostaglandin E2.
4. **Which animal is used for the official pyrogen test?** The rabbit, because its fever response resembles that of humans.
5. **Name the in vitro alternative to the rabbit test.** The LAL (Limulus amoebocyte lysate) test, also called the bacterial endotoxin test.
6. **Why is the rabbit kept in the test room before the test?** So that it is used to the surroundings and its temperature is steady.
7. **Which preparations must be tested for pyrogens?** Large-volume parenterals and other injections as directed by the pharmacopoeial monograph.
8. **How is glassware made pyrogen-free?** By dry heat sterilisation at the temperature and time given in the monograph.
9. **What is measured in this test?** The rise in rectal temperature above each rabbit's own control temperature, summed for the group.
