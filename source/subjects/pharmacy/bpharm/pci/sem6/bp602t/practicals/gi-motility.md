# Effect of drugs on gastrointestinal motility

**Aim:** To study the effect of a prokinetic and an antimotility drug on gastrointestinal motility in mice using the charcoal meal (intestinal transit) method.

> Demonstrated by simulation or video as per the PCI note. Live animal work needs Institutional Animal Ethics Committee approval under CCSEA (earlier CPCSEA) rules. Use the drugs and doses from your lab manual.

## Principle

Gut motility is controlled by the enteric nervous system with vagal (cholinergic, M3) excitation and sympathetic inhibition. Drugs can increase motility (**prokinetics** such as metoclopramide and domperidone, and cholinergic agonists) or decrease it (**antimotility** drugs such as loperamide and atropine).

In the **charcoal meal test**, a marker of charcoal suspended in gum acacia is given by mouth. After a fixed time the small intestine is removed and the distance travelled by the leading edge of the charcoal is measured and expressed as a percentage of the total length of the small intestine.

```text
 pylorus |=========charcoal front=========>............| caecum
         |<-------- distance moved ------->|
         |<--------- total length of small intestine ------->|

 % intestinal transit = distance moved / total length x 100
```

A prokinetic increases the percentage; an antimotility drug decreases it.

## Requirements

Mice, charcoal meal (activated charcoal in gum acacia suspension), oral feeding needle, test drug, standard prokinetic (metoclopramide) and standard antimotility drug (loperamide or atropine), dissecting set, measuring scale or thread, syringes, balance; or simulation software.

## Procedure

1. Fast the mice for the time stated in the manual with water available. Weigh and group them: control (vehicle), standard prokinetic, standard antimotility drug and test drug.
2. Give the drugs by the route and at the dose in the manual.
3. After the stated interval, give each mouse the charcoal meal orally with a feeding needle.
4. Wait exactly the time fixed in the manual (commonly 20–30 minutes in this model).
5. Humanely euthanise the animal as per the manual, open the abdomen and carefully remove the small intestine from the pylorus to the caecum without stretching it.
6. Lay the intestine on a flat surface and measure its total length and the distance from the pylorus to the leading edge of the charcoal.
7. Calculate the percentage of intestinal transit for each animal and the group mean.

## Observations and calculation

**Specimen readings (illustrative only, not real data).**

| Group | Total length (cm) | Distance moved (cm) | % transit | Change vs control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Control (vehicle) | 55 | 30 | 54.5 | — |
| Metoclopramide (prokinetic) | 54 | 42 | 77.8 | increased |
| Loperamide (antimotility) | 56 | 14 | 25.0 | decreased |
| Test drug | 55 | 36 | 65.5 | increased |

% transit = distance moved ÷ total length × 100.
% change vs control = (test − control) ÷ control × 100.

## Result

The test drug increased the percentage intestinal transit of the charcoal meal compared with the control (specimen: 65.5 % against 54.5 %), showing a prokinetic effect; loperamide reduced transit, confirming the sensitivity of the method.

## Precautions

1. Fast the animals for the same time and keep water available.
2. Give the charcoal meal gently with a feeding needle so that none enters the airway.
3. Shake the charcoal suspension before each dose so that the concentration stays uniform.
4. Keep the interval between the meal and the dissection exactly equal in every animal; this is the commonest source of error.
5. Remove the intestine without pulling, and lay it out without stretching.
6. Measure to the leading edge of the charcoal only.
7. Use animals of similar weight and the same sex within a group.

## Viva questions

1. **What does the charcoal meal measure?** The distance travelled by a marker in the small intestine in a fixed time, that is, intestinal transit.
2. **Name two prokinetic drugs and their mechanism.** Metoclopramide (D2 block plus 5-HT4 agonism) and domperidone (peripheral D2 block); both increase acetylcholine release in the myenteric plexus.
3. **How does loperamide reduce motility?** It is a μ-opioid agonist acting on the gut; it does not cross the blood-brain barrier.
4. **How would atropine affect transit?** It blocks M3 receptors and reduces motility.
5. **Why is gum acacia used?** It keeps the charcoal in uniform suspension so the marker moves as a front.
6. **Which nerve increases gut motility?** The vagus, through acetylcholine on muscarinic receptors.
7. **Name a drug that would increase transit by a cholinergic mechanism.** Neostigmine (or bethanechol).
8. **Why must the time between the meal and dissection be fixed?** Transit distance depends directly on time, so any difference would be read as a drug effect.
