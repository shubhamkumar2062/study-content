# Unit 2: Chemotherapy — general principles, sulfonamides and antibiotics

**Chemotherapy** is the treatment of infections (and cancers) with drugs that act selectively on the invading organism or abnormal cells while sparing the host. Selective toxicity is the key idea of this whole unit. Penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones are the most frequent 10-mark questions.

## 2.1 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Antibiotic | Substance produced by a microorganism that kills or inhibits other microorganisms (penicillin from *Penicillium*, streptomycin from *Streptomyces*) |
| Antimicrobial agent (AMA) | Any natural, semisynthetic or synthetic drug that acts against microbes (includes sulfonamides, quinolones) |
| Bactericidal | Kills bacteria (penicillins, cephalosporins, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin) |
| Bacteriostatic | Stops multiplication; host defence clears infection (sulfonamides, tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, macrolides, clindamycin) |
| Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) | Lowest concentration that prevents visible growth |
| Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) | Lowest concentration that kills 99.9% of the inoculum |
| Post-antibiotic effect (PAE) | Suppression of growth that persists after the drug level falls below MIC (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones) |
| Superinfection | New infection from organisms that overgrow when normal flora is suppressed by a broad-spectrum drug |

**Time- vs concentration-dependent killing**

- **Time-dependent:** effect depends on how long the level stays above MIC (β-lactams, vancomycin) → frequent dosing or infusion.
- **Concentration-dependent:** effect depends on the peak level (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones) → once-daily dosing of aminoglycosides.

## 2.2 Classification of antimicrobial agents by mechanism

| Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Inhibit cell wall synthesis | Penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, monobactams, vancomycin, bacitracin, cycloserine |
| Damage cell membrane | Polymyxin B, colistin; amphotericin B and azoles (fungi) |
| Inhibit protein synthesis (30S) | Aminoglycosides, tetracyclines |
| Inhibit protein synthesis (50S) | Chloramphenicol, macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid |
| Inhibit nucleic acid synthesis/function | Fluoroquinolones (DNA gyrase), rifampicin (RNA polymerase), metronidazole |
| Antimetabolites (folate pathway) | Sulfonamides, trimethoprim, pyrimethamine, dapsone |
| Inhibit viral enzymes | Acyclovir (DNA polymerase), zidovudine (reverse transcriptase), protease inhibitors |

Other classifications: by **spectrum** (narrow: penicillin G; broad: tetracyclines, chloramphenicol) and by **type of organism** (antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, antiprotozoal, anthelmintic).

## 2.3 General principles: choosing an antimicrobial

1. **Diagnosis and the likely organism** — clinical features, Gram stain, culture and sensitivity. Start **empirical** therapy in serious infections after taking samples, then switch to **definitive** therapy.
2. **Drug factors** — spectrum, bactericidal or static, tissue penetration (CSF, bone, urine, intracellular), kinetics, toxicity, cost, route.
3. **Patient factors** — age (no tetracyclines in children under 8; no fluoroquinolones in children routinely; chloramphenicol causes grey baby syndrome in neonates), pregnancy (avoid tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones; penicillins and cephalosporins are safe), renal and liver function, allergy, host defence, genetic factors (G6PD deficiency with sulfonamides).
4. **Dose and duration** adequate to eradicate infection; stop when no longer needed.
5. **Antimicrobial stewardship** — the right drug, dose and duration, to slow resistance.

### Combination of antimicrobials

**Reasons:** to prevent resistance (TB, HIV), to treat mixed infections, for synergism (penicillin + aminoglycoside in enterococcal endocarditis; sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim), for empirical treatment of serious infection, and to reduce toxicity of each drug.

**Drawbacks:** antagonism (a bacteriostatic drug can reduce the effect of a bactericidal drug that needs growing cells, such as tetracycline with penicillin), more adverse effects, superinfection, cost.

### Resistance

**Types:** natural (innate) or acquired. **Acquired** by **mutation** (vertical transfer) or by gene transfer — **conjugation** (plasmid, R factor, via sex pilus; commonest in Gram-negatives), **transduction** (bacteriophage; staphylococcal penicillinase) and **transformation** (free DNA taken up).

**Mechanisms:**

1. Drug-inactivating enzymes — β-lactamases, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes, chloramphenicol acetyltransferase.
2. Altered target — penicillin-binding protein 2a in MRSA, mutated DNA gyrase, methylated ribosome (macrolides).
3. Reduced permeability — loss of porins.
4. Efflux pumps — tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones.
5. Alternative metabolic pathway — sulfonamide resistance.

**Cross-resistance:** resistance to one drug carries resistance to related drugs (between tetracyclines; between macrolides and clindamycin).

### Problems of antimicrobial use

Toxicity, hypersensitivity (penicillin anaphylaxis), drug resistance, superinfection (oral thrush, *Clostridioides difficile* colitis after clindamycin or broad-spectrum drugs), masking of infection (partial treatment of syphilis or TB), and nutritional deficiency (vitamin K and B complex from loss of gut flora).

## 2.4 Sulfonamides

Sulfonamides are synthetic derivatives of sulfanilamide, structurally similar to **para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)**.

**Classification**

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Short-acting | Sulfadiazine |
| Intermediate-acting | Sulfamethoxazole |
| Long-acting | Sulfadoxine (with pyrimethamine in malaria) |
| Poorly absorbed (gut) | Sulfasalazine (ulcerative colitis, rheumatoid arthritis) |
| Topical | Silver sulfadiazine, mafenide (burns), sulfacetamide sodium (eye drops) |

**Mechanism:** bacteria must make their own folic acid. Sulfonamides **competitively inhibit dihydropteroate synthase**, preventing incorporation of PABA → no dihydrofolic acid → no purines and DNA → **bacteriostatic**. Humans use preformed folate, so they are spared. Pus contains PABA, so sulfonamides fail in pus; procaine (gives PABA on hydrolysis) antagonises them.

**Adverse effects:** crystalluria (keep good urine output and alkalinise), hypersensitivity (rash, fever, Stevens–Johnson syndrome), haemolysis in G6PD deficiency, kernicterus in newborns (they displace bilirubin from albumin), blood dyscrasias. Avoided in late pregnancy and neonates.

**Uses:** mainly as cotrimoxazole; sulfadiazine with pyrimethamine for toxoplasmosis; topical uses above.

## 2.5 Cotrimoxazole (sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim)

**Trimethoprim** inhibits **bacterial dihydrofolate reductase** selectively (much higher affinity for the bacterial than the human enzyme). The combination produces a **sequential block** of two steps of the same pathway.

```text
 PABA + pteridine
       |  dihydropteroate synthase   <-- blocked by sulfamethoxazole
       v
 Dihydrofolic acid
       |  dihydrofolate reductase    <-- blocked by trimethoprim
       v
 Tetrahydrofolic acid --> purines, thymidine --> DNA
```

**Why combine:** synergism (the pair is bactericidal although each alone is static), lower chance of resistance, a wider spectrum. The ratio is **1 part trimethoprim : 5 parts sulfamethoxazole**, which gives an optimal **1 : 20** ratio in plasma, as the two drugs have similar half-lives.

**Uses:** urinary tract infections, respiratory infections, typhoid (resistance now common), shigellosis, *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia (drug of choice, treatment and prophylaxis in HIV), toxoplasmosis, nocardiosis.

**Adverse effects:** those of sulfonamides plus folate deficiency (megaloblastic anaemia), hyperkalaemia, rashes (more frequent in HIV patients).

## 2.6 β-Lactam antibiotics: penicillins

All β-lactams have a **β-lactam ring**. Penicillins have 6-aminopenicillanic acid (a thiazolidine ring fused to the β-lactam ring).

**Mechanism:** bacteria build their wall from peptidoglycan cross-linked by **transpeptidases**, which are **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)**. β-Lactams bind PBPs and block the final **transpeptidation** step → a weak wall → autolysins act → the cell bursts in a hypotonic medium. **Bactericidal**, best on actively growing cells. Human cells have no cell wall, hence low toxicity.

```text
 NAM-NAG units + peptide side chains
        |
 transpeptidase (PBP) cross-links chains  <-- β-lactams block here
        |
 rigid peptidoglycan wall
```

**Classification**

| Group | Examples | Key point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural | Benzylpenicillin (penicillin G), procaine and benzathine penicillin (depot) | Narrow spectrum, acid-labile, penicillinase-sensitive |
| Acid-resistant | Phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) | Oral |
| Penicillinase-resistant | Methicillin, cloxacillin, dicloxacillin, flucloxacillin | Staphylococci producing penicillinase |
| Extended spectrum: aminopenicillins | Ampicillin, amoxicillin | Also Gram-negatives (*E. coli*, *H. influenzae*) |
| Antipseudomonal | Piperacillin, carbenicillin, ticarcillin | *Pseudomonas* |
| With β-lactamase inhibitors | Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid, ampicillin + sulbactam, piperacillin + tazobactam | Inhibitor protects the penicillin |

**Penicillin G uses:** streptococcal infections (pharyngitis, rheumatic fever prophylaxis with benzathine penicillin), pneumococcal infections, meningococcal meningitis, syphilis (drug of choice), gas gangrene, tetanus, diphtheria (with antitoxin), actinomycosis, leptospirosis.

**Adverse effects:**

- **Hypersensitivity** — the main problem: rash, urticaria, fever, angioedema, and anaphylaxis (rare but fatal). Treated with adrenaline. Ask about past reactions; skin testing is used in some settings. Cross-allergy among all penicillins and partly with cephalosporins.
- Jarisch–Herxheimer reaction when treating syphilis (release of spirochaetal products).
- Ampicillin rash (common in infectious mononucleosis), diarrhoea.
- Very high levels: seizures; sodium or potassium overload with large IV amounts of salts.

**Probenecid** blocks tubular secretion of penicillin and prolongs its action.

### β-Lactamase inhibitors

Clavulanic acid, sulbactam and tazobactam have a β-lactam structure but little antibacterial action of their own. They bind β-lactamase irreversibly ("suicide inhibitors") and protect the partner drug.

## 2.7 Cephalosporins

Derived from *Cephalosporium acremonium*; nucleus 7-aminocephalosporanic acid. Same mechanism as penicillins; more resistant to many β-lactamases.

| Generation | Examples | Spectrum trend |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First | Cefazolin, cephalexin, cefadroxil | Mainly Gram-positive; surgical prophylaxis |
| Second | Cefuroxime, cefaclor, cefoxitin | Better Gram-negative, *H. influenzae*; cefoxitin covers anaerobes |
| Third | Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, cefoperazone, cefixime, cefpodoxime | Strong Gram-negative; ceftriaxone enters CSF; ceftazidime/cefoperazone anti-*Pseudomonas* |
| Fourth | Cefepime, cefpirome | Broad; resistant to many β-lactamases; *Pseudomonas* |
| Fifth | Ceftaroline, ceftobiprole | Active against MRSA |

**Uses:** meningitis (ceftriaxone/cefotaxime), typhoid (ceftriaxone, cefixime), gonorrhoea (ceftriaxone), hospital infections, surgical prophylaxis (cefazolin), respiratory and urinary infections.

**Adverse effects:** hypersensitivity (cross-reactivity with penicillin is low but real; avoided after penicillin anaphylaxis), pain at injection site, diarrhoea, superinfection; some with an N-methylthiotetrazole side chain (cefoperazone) cause hypoprothrombinaemia and a **disulfiram-like reaction** with alcohol; ceftriaxone may form biliary sludge.

**Other β-lactams:** **carbapenems** (imipenem with cilastatin — cilastatin blocks renal dehydropeptidase; meropenem) have the widest spectrum and are reserved for resistant hospital infections; imipenem can cause seizures. **Monobactam:** aztreonam (Gram-negative aerobes only, no cross-allergy with penicillin).

## 2.8 Chloramphenicol

Broad-spectrum, **bacteriostatic**, originally from *Streptomyces venezuelae*.

**Mechanism:** binds the **50S** ribosomal subunit and inhibits **peptidyl transferase**, blocking peptide bond formation.

**Kinetics:** well absorbed orally, enters CSF and the eye well; inactivated in the liver by **glucuronidation**; an enzyme inhibitor.

**Adverse effects:**

1. **Bone marrow depression** — (a) dose-related, reversible suppression of red cell maturation; (b) idiosyncratic, irreversible **aplastic anaemia**, not related to dose, often fatal. This is why use is restricted.
2. **Grey baby syndrome** — newborns lack glucuronyl transferase and have poor renal excretion; the drug accumulates → vomiting, cyanosis (ashen grey colour), hypothermia, circulatory collapse.
3. Hypersensitivity, superinfection, optic neuritis.
4. Interactions: inhibits metabolism of phenytoin, warfarin and tolbutamide.

**Uses (restricted):** bacterial meningitis and brain abscess when safer drugs cannot be used, typhoid (where sensitive), rickettsial infections, anaerobic infections, and topically in eye infections.

## 2.9 Macrolides

Erythromycin, clarithromycin, azithromycin, roxithromycin (large lactone ring).

**Mechanism:** bind the **50S** subunit (23S rRNA) and block **translocation** of the growing peptide chain. Bacteriostatic (bactericidal at high levels). Resistance by efflux or ribosomal methylation (*erm* genes).

**Uses:** atypical pneumonia (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydia*, *Legionella*), whooping cough, diphtheria carriers, streptococcal infection in penicillin-allergic patients, *H. pylori* (clarithromycin), *Mycobacterium avium* complex (clarithromycin, azithromycin), chlamydial urethritis (single-dose azithromycin), trachoma, typhoid (azithromycin).

**Adverse effects:** gastric upset (erythromycin is a **motilin agonist**), cholestatic hepatitis (erythromycin estolate), QT prolongation, ototoxicity at high levels.

**Interactions:** erythromycin and clarithromycin **inhibit CYP3A4** → raise levels of theophylline, carbamazepine, warfarin, statins and some antihistamines (arrhythmias). Azithromycin inhibits the enzyme least.

**Azithromycin advantages:** acid-stable, high tissue and intracellular levels, very long half-life (once-daily, short courses), fewer interactions.

## 2.10 Quinolones and fluoroquinolones

**Nalidixic acid** is the first-generation quinolone (urinary antiseptic, mostly Gram-negative). Adding fluorine gave **fluoroquinolones (FQs)**.

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| First generation FQs | Ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin, pefloxacin |
| Respiratory FQs (newer) | Levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, gemifloxacin |

**Mechanism:** inhibit **DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II)** — the main target in Gram-negatives — and **topoisomerase IV** — the main target in Gram-positives. Gyrase introduces negative supercoils needed for DNA replication; blocking it causes DNA breaks → **bactericidal**, concentration-dependent killing with a post-antibiotic effect.

**Uses:** urinary tract infections, gonorrhoea (resistance now high), typhoid (resistance common), bacterial diarrhoea, bone and joint infections, respiratory infections (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), multidrug-resistant TB (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), anthrax, prophylaxis in neutropenia.

**Adverse effects:** GI upset, CNS effects (dizziness, headache, seizures in predisposed patients), **tendinitis and tendon rupture** (Achilles tendon, more in the elderly and with steroids), damage to growing cartilage (avoided in children and pregnancy except when necessary), photosensitivity, QT prolongation (moxifloxacin), dysglycaemia, peripheral neuropathy.

**Interactions:** absorption reduced by antacids, iron, calcium and zinc (chelation); ciprofloxacin inhibits CYP1A2 → theophylline toxicity; with NSAIDs, a higher risk of seizures.

## 2.11 Tetracyclines

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Older | Tetracycline, oxytetracycline |
| Newer, long-acting | Doxycycline, minocycline |
| Glycylcycline | Tigecycline |

**Mechanism:** actively taken into bacteria; bind the **30S** subunit and prevent attachment of aminoacyl-tRNA to the acceptor (A) site → protein synthesis stops. Broad-spectrum, **bacteriostatic**. Resistance mainly by efflux pumps and ribosomal protection proteins.

**Kinetics:** form insoluble chelates with Ca2+, Mg2+, Al3+ and Fe2+ (milk, antacids, iron reduce absorption, except doxycycline is affected less); deposit in growing bones and teeth. Doxycycline is excreted mainly in faeces, so it is the tetracycline safe in renal failure.

**Uses:** rickettsial infections (typhus, scrub typhus — doxycycline is the drug of choice), *Mycoplasma* and *Chlamydia* infections, cholera, brucellosis (with streptomycin or rifampicin), plague, leptospirosis prophylaxis, acne (doxycycline, minocycline), malaria prophylaxis and treatment (doxycycline), *H. pylori* (quadruple therapy), Lyme disease.

**Adverse effects:**

- GI irritation; oesophageal ulcers (take with plenty of water, sitting up).
- **Teeth and bones:** brown discolouration and enamel hypoplasia of teeth; reduced bone growth → avoided in pregnancy, lactation and children under 8.
- Superinfection (candidiasis, *C. difficile*).
- Phototoxicity (demeclocycline, doxycycline).
- Hepatotoxicity (high doses, pregnancy); renal: expired (degraded) tetracycline causes Fanconi syndrome; worsens renal failure (except doxycycline).
- Vestibular toxicity (minocycline); raised intracranial pressure in infants.

## 2.12 Aminoglycosides

Streptomycin, gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin, kanamycin, neomycin, netilmicin, sisomicin.

**Mechanism:** enter bacteria by an **oxygen-dependent** transport, so they are inactive against anaerobes. They bind the **30S** subunit → (1) block the initiation complex, (2) **misreading of mRNA** producing faulty proteins that insert into the membrane and increase permeability, (3) break up polysomes → **bactericidal**, concentration-dependent with a post-antibiotic effect. β-Lactams help their entry → synergy.

**Common properties**

1. Highly polar, **not absorbed orally** (neomycin used orally for gut action); given IM or IV.
2. Do not enter CSF or cells well; excreted unchanged by the kidney.
3. Narrow therapeutic index; levels monitored.
4. Active mainly against aerobic Gram-negative bacilli; streptomycin and amikacin against *M. tuberculosis*.
5. Less effective in acidic and anaerobic sites (pus, abscess).

**Adverse effects**

| Toxicity | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Ototoxicity | Damage to hair cells: vestibular (streptomycin, gentamicin) or cochlear (amikacin, neomycin, kanamycin); may be permanent; worse with loop diuretics |
| Nephrotoxicity | Proximal tubular damage; usually reversible; worse with vancomycin, amphotericin B, cephalosporins, NSAIDs |
| Neuromuscular blockade | Reduce acetylcholine release (calcium antagonism) → respiratory paralysis risk with neuromuscular blockers, in myasthenia gravis; reversed by calcium |
| Others | Hypersensitivity (streptomycin) |

Avoided in pregnancy (fetal ear damage). **Once-daily dosing** gives a high peak (better killing, longer PAE) with lower trough levels (less toxicity).

**Uses:** gentamicin/amikacin for serious Gram-negative infections (septicaemia, hospital infections, often with a β-lactam), with penicillin in enterococcal and streptococcal endocarditis; streptomycin in TB, plague and tularaemia; amikacin in MDR-TB; neomycin topically and orally for bowel preparation and hepatic encephalopathy; tobramycin in *Pseudomonas* infections (inhaled in cystic fibrosis).

## Remember

- Selective toxicity; bactericidal (β-lactams, aminoglycosides, FQs) vs bacteriostatic (sulfonamides, tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, macrolides).
- Resistance: mutation, conjugation (R plasmid), transduction, transformation; mechanisms: enzymes, altered target, permeability, efflux.
- Sulfonamide: PABA analogue, inhibits dihydropteroate synthase; trimethoprim inhibits bacterial DHFR; cotrimoxazole = sequential block, 1:5 tablet → 1:20 plasma; drug of choice in *Pneumocystis* pneumonia.
- Penicillins block transpeptidase (PBP); anaphylaxis → adrenaline; benzathine penicillin for rheumatic fever prophylaxis; drug of choice for syphilis.
- Clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam: suicide β-lactamase inhibitors.
- Cephalosporin generations: 3rd ceftriaxone (meningitis, typhoid, gonorrhoea); 5th ceftaroline (MRSA); disulfiram-like reaction with cefoperazone.
- Chloramphenicol: 50S, peptidyl transferase; aplastic anaemia, grey baby syndrome.
- Macrolides: 50S translocation; erythromycin a CYP3A4 inhibitor and motilin agonist; azithromycin long half-life.
- FQs: DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV; tendon rupture, cartilage damage, QT; chelate with antacids.
- Tetracyclines: 30S A-site; teeth/bone staining; doxycycline drug of choice in rickettsial infections, safe in renal failure.
- Aminoglycosides: 30S misreading, oxygen-dependent entry; ototoxic, nephrotoxic, neuromuscular block; once-daily dosing.
