# Unit 4: UTI and STD, cancer chemotherapy and immunopharmacology

This unit closes chemotherapy with drugs for urinary tract infections, sexually transmitted diseases and cancer, and then turns to drugs that boost or suppress the immune system, including biological drugs such as monoclonal antibodies and biosimilars. Classification of anticancer drugs and immunosuppressants are the classic long questions.

## 4.1 Urinary tract infections (UTI)

**UTI** is infection anywhere in the urinary tract: lower (cystitis, urethritis) or upper (pyelonephritis). The commonest organism is ***Escherichia coli***; others are *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* and, in hospital and catheter infections, *Pseudomonas*. Women get UTI more often because of the short urethra.

### Drugs for UTI

| Group | Examples | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Urinary antiseptics | Nitrofurantoin, methenamine (hexamine) mandelate, nalidixic acid | Act only in urine; no systemic antibacterial levels |
| Fosfomycin | Fosfomycin trometamol | Single-dose oral for uncomplicated cystitis; inhibits an early step of cell wall synthesis (MurA) |
| Cotrimoxazole, trimethoprim | | Cystitis where local resistance is low; prophylaxis of recurrent UTI |
| Fluoroquinolones | Ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin, levofloxacin | Complicated UTI, pyelonephritis; avoid for simple cystitis to spare them |
| β-Lactams | Amoxicillin–clavulanate, cephalexin, cefixime, ceftriaxone; carbapenems for resistant organisms | Safe in pregnancy |
| Aminoglycosides | Gentamicin, amikacin | Severe pyelonephritis, hospital UTI |

**Nitrofurantoin:** reduced by bacterial flavoproteins to reactive intermediates that damage DNA and ribosomes; reaches effective levels only in urine, so used for lower UTI (not pyelonephritis). Adverse effects: nausea, brown urine, peripheral neuropathy, pulmonary reactions (acute pneumonitis; fibrosis on long use), haemolysis in G6PD deficiency. Not used when kidney function is poor (fails to reach urine and toxicity rises).

**Methenamine:** in **acidic urine** it releases **formaldehyde**, which is antibacterial. Urine must be acidic (pH below about 5.5), so it is given with an acidifier; ineffective with urea-splitting *Proteus* (alkaline urine). Used only for prophylaxis.

**Urinary analgesic:** phenazopyridine relieves burning (colours urine orange-red); it has no antibacterial action.

**General measures:** plenty of fluids, frequent voiding, treat obstruction or stones, remove catheters when possible. **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** is treated in pregnancy (risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth) but usually not in others.

## 4.2 Sexually transmitted diseases (STD)

The National AIDS and STI Control Programme in India uses a **syndromic approach**: treat all likely causes of a syndrome (urethral discharge, genital ulcer, vaginal discharge) at the first visit, because laboratory diagnosis may not be available and patients may not return. Colour-coded STI kits are used.

| Disease (organism) | Treatment of choice (drug classes) |
| --- | --- |
| Syphilis (*Treponema pallidum*) | **Benzathine penicillin G**; doxycycline in penicillin allergy (not in pregnancy) |
| Gonorrhoea (*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*) | **Ceftriaxone** (with azithromycin or doxycycline for possible chlamydial co-infection) |
| Chlamydial urethritis/cervicitis, lymphogranuloma venereum | Azithromycin or doxycycline |
| Chancroid (*Haemophilus ducreyi*) | Azithromycin, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin |
| Donovanosis (*Klebsiella granulomatis*) | Azithromycin, doxycycline |
| Trichomoniasis | Metronidazole or tinidazole (treat partner) |
| Bacterial vaginosis | Metronidazole, clindamycin |
| Vulvovaginal candidiasis | Clotrimazole (topical), fluconazole (oral) |
| Genital herpes | Acyclovir, valacyclovir |
| Genital warts (HPV) | Podophyllotoxin, imiquimod; prevented by HPV vaccine |
| HIV | Antiretroviral therapy (Unit 3) |

Principles: treat sexual partners, counsel on condom use, test for HIV and syphilis, follow up.

## 4.3 Cancer chemotherapy: principles

Cancer cells divide without control. Most anticancer drugs act on **dividing cells**, so they also damage normal fast-dividing tissues: bone marrow, gut lining, hair follicles, gonads and the fetus.

### Cell cycle and drug specificity

```text
            G1 (growth)  -->  S (DNA synthesis)
            ^                        |
            |                        v
     M (mitosis)  <------------  G2 (preparation)
            |
            v
     G0 (resting cells, insensitive to most drugs)

 S phase:  antimetabolites (methotrexate, 5-FU, cytarabine, 6-MP)
 G2 phase: bleomycin, etoposide, topotecan
 M phase:  vinca alkaloids, taxanes
 Cell-cycle non-specific: alkylating agents, platinum, anthracyclines
```

- **Cell-cycle specific (CCS)** drugs act in a particular phase; effective on tumours with a high growth fraction (leukaemias, lymphomas).
- **Cell-cycle non-specific (CCNS)** drugs kill both cycling and resting cells; useful in solid tumours with a low growth fraction.

### Log-kill hypothesis

Each course kills a **constant fraction** (not a constant number) of tumour cells. A course that kills 99.9% (3-log kill) reduces 10^12 cells to 10^9. So repeated courses are needed, and treatment continues after the tumour is no longer detectable.

### Principles of use

1. **Combination chemotherapy** — drugs with different mechanisms and non-overlapping toxicities, each active alone (e.g. CHOP for lymphoma, ABVD for Hodgkin's lymphoma). It raises cell kill and delays resistance.
2. **Intermittent courses (cycles)** allow the bone marrow and gut to recover between courses.
3. **Adjuvant** chemotherapy after surgery or radiation removes micrometastases; **neoadjuvant** chemotherapy shrinks a tumour before surgery.
4. **Supportive care:** anti-emetics (ondansetron, aprepitant, dexamethasone), growth factors (filgrastim for neutropenia), allopurinol or rasburicase for **tumour lysis syndrome** (hyperuricaemia), mesna with cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide, folinic acid (leucovorin) rescue after high-dose methotrexate.

### Common toxicities

Bone marrow suppression (neutropenia, infection, bleeding), nausea and vomiting, mucositis and diarrhoea, alopecia, sterility, teratogenicity, hyperuricaemia, delayed wound healing, and secondary cancers (alkylating agents).

**Drug-specific toxicities** (frequently asked): cyclophosphamide — haemorrhagic cystitis (acrolein; prevented by mesna); cisplatin — nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, severe vomiting; doxorubicin — cardiotoxicity; bleomycin — pulmonary fibrosis; vincristine — peripheral neuropathy; methotrexate — mucositis, hepatotoxicity; busulfan — pulmonary fibrosis, hyperpigmentation; L-asparaginase — pancreatitis, hypersensitivity.

### Mechanisms of the main groups

- **Alkylating agents** form reactive carbonium ions that attach alkyl groups to the **N7 of guanine** in DNA → cross-linking of DNA strands, abnormal base pairing, strand breaks → cell death. **Cyclophosphamide** is a prodrug activated by liver CYP enzymes; its metabolite **acrolein** causes haemorrhagic cystitis. Also used as an immunosuppressant. **Cisplatin** forms intrastrand DNA cross-links; used in testicular, ovarian, lung, head and neck cancers.
- **Methotrexate** inhibits **dihydrofolate reductase** → no tetrahydrofolate → no thymidylate and purines → S-phase arrest. Used in acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, choriocarcinoma, osteosarcoma; at low doses in rheumatoid arthritis and psoriasis. **Folinic acid (leucovorin)** bypasses the block and rescues normal cells.
- **5-Fluorouracil** is converted to 5-FdUMP, which inhibits **thymidylate synthase**; used in colorectal, breast and skin cancers. **6-Mercaptopurine** inhibits purine synthesis; it is metabolised by **xanthine oxidase**, so with allopurinol its dose must be reduced.
- **Vinca alkaloids** bind tubulin and **prevent microtubule assembly** → metaphase arrest. Vincristine → neuropathy (spares marrow); vinblastine → marrow suppression.
- **Taxanes** do the opposite: they **stabilise microtubules** and prevent their breakdown → mitotic arrest. Paclitaxel → hypersensitivity, neuropathy, neutropenia.
- **Etoposide** inhibits **topoisomerase II**; **topotecan/irinotecan** inhibit **topoisomerase I**.
- **Anthracyclines** (doxorubicin) intercalate DNA, inhibit topoisomerase II and generate free radicals → cumulative **cardiotoxicity** (dexrazoxane protects). Red urine.
- **Bleomycin** forms iron-oxygen complexes that break DNA (G2 phase); little marrow toxicity; pulmonary fibrosis.
- **L-Asparaginase** breaks down asparagine; leukaemic cells cannot make their own asparagine.
- **Tamoxifen** blocks oestrogen receptors in breast (used in receptor-positive breast cancer) but acts as an agonist in the uterus (endometrial cancer risk) and bone.

### Drug resistance in cancer

Mechanisms: reduced uptake, increased efflux by **P-glycoprotein** (multidrug resistance gene MDR1), increased target enzyme (DHFR amplification), increased DNA repair, altered target, failure of apoptosis. Combination therapy and adequate intensity reduce it.

## 4.5 Immunopharmacology: introduction

The immune response has innate and adaptive arms. Adaptive immunity involves **T cells** (cell-mediated; CD4 helper and CD8 cytotoxic) and **B cells** (antibodies). Antigen-presenting cells show antigen to T cells; activated T cells make **interleukin-2 (IL-2)**, which drives T-cell proliferation. Many immunosuppressants act on this pathway.

```text
 Antigen + APC --> T-cell receptor --> calcineurin activated
                                             |
                    cyclosporine/tacrolimus  X  (block calcineurin)
                                             v
                         NFAT --> IL-2 gene transcription --> IL-2
                                             |
                       basiliximab X (blocks IL-2 receptor)
                                             v
                        IL-2 receptor --> mTOR --> T-cell proliferation
                                    sirolimus, everolimus X (block mTOR)
                                             |
      azathioprine, mycophenolate X (block purine synthesis in dividing T cells)
```

## 4.6 Immunosuppressants

**Uses:** prevention and treatment of organ transplant rejection (kidney, liver, heart, bone marrow), graft-versus-host disease, autoimmune diseases (rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, autoimmune haemolytic anaemia), nephrotic syndrome.

| Class | Examples | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Calcineurin inhibitors | Cyclosporine, tacrolimus | Cyclosporine binds cyclophilin, tacrolimus binds FKBP-12 → complex inhibits calcineurin → no IL-2 transcription |
| mTOR inhibitors | Sirolimus (rapamycin), everolimus | Bind FKBP-12 → inhibit mTOR → block IL-2-driven proliferation |
| Antiproliferative / antimetabolites | Azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, leflunomide | Azathioprine → 6-MP (purine synthesis); mycophenolate inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase (guanine synthesis in lymphocytes) |
| Glucocorticoids | Prednisolone, methylprednisolone | Reduce cytokine gene expression (IL-1, IL-2, TNF), lymphocyte redistribution, anti-inflammatory |
| Antibodies | Antithymocyte globulin (polyclonal); basiliximab (anti-IL-2 receptor α); rituximab (anti-CD20); belatacept (blocks co-stimulation) | Deplete or block lymphocytes |
| Anti-cytokine biologicals | Infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept (anti-TNF-α); tocilizumab (anti-IL-6 receptor) | Block inflammatory cytokines |

**Cyclosporine:** narrow therapeutic index, blood levels monitored; metabolised by CYP3A4 (levels raised by ketoconazole, erythromycin, diltiazem, grapefruit juice; lowered by rifampicin, phenytoin). Adverse effects: **nephrotoxicity** (main), hypertension, hyperkalaemia, hepatotoxicity, **hirsutism**, **gum hyperplasia**, tremor, diabetes. It does not suppress the bone marrow. **Tacrolimus** is more potent; causes nephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity and diabetes but less hirsutism and gum hyperplasia.

**Azathioprine:** bone marrow suppression; dose reduced with allopurinol (xanthine oxidase inhibition); TPMT enzyme deficiency increases toxicity. **Mycophenolate:** GI upset, leucopenia, teratogenic.

**General risks of immunosuppression:** opportunistic infections (CMV, fungi, *Pneumocystis*), reactivation of TB and hepatitis B, and higher risk of lymphoma and skin cancer. Combinations of drugs with different mechanisms (triple therapy, e.g. tacrolimus + mycophenolate + prednisolone) allow lower amounts of each.

## 4.7 Immunostimulants

Drugs that enhance immune responses in immunodeficiency, chronic infections and some cancers.

| Example | Use |
| --- | --- |
| BCG (live attenuated *M. bovis*) | Vaccine against TB; intravesical BCG in superficial bladder cancer |
| Levamisole | Originally an anthelmintic; restores T-cell function (older use in colon cancer adjuvant therapy, nephrotic syndrome in children) |
| Interferons: α (hepatitis B and C, hairy cell leukaemia, Kaposi sarcoma), β (multiple sclerosis), γ (chronic granulomatous disease) | Antiviral, antiproliferative, immunomodulatory; cause flu-like symptoms, depression, marrow suppression |
| Interleukin-2 (aldesleukin) | Metastatic renal cell carcinoma and melanoma |
| Colony-stimulating factors: filgrastim (G-CSF), sargramostim (GM-CSF) | Raise neutrophil counts after chemotherapy |
| Thymic peptides (thymosin), inosine pranobex, imiquimod | Immunomodulators; imiquimod stimulates local interferon (genital warts, skin cancers) |
| Immunoglobulins | Passive immunity, immunodeficiency |
| Vaccines | Active immunisation |
| Immune checkpoint inhibitors: pembrolizumab, nivolumab (anti-PD-1), ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) | Release the "brakes" on T cells in cancer; immune-related adverse effects (colitis, hepatitis, thyroiditis) |
| Herbal immunomodulators | *Withania somnifera* (ashwagandha), *Tinospora cordifolia* (guduchi), *Echinacea* — traditional use |

## 4.8 Protein drugs, monoclonal antibodies, targeted drugs and biosimilars

### Protein drugs

Proteins made by **recombinant DNA technology** in bacteria, yeast or mammalian cells: insulin, growth hormone, erythropoietin, filgrastim, interferons, tissue plasminogen activator (alteplase), clotting factors, enzymes for enzyme replacement (imiglucerase), and hormones such as FSH.

**Features:** large molecules, destroyed in the gut, so given by injection; may be immunogenic (form antibodies); need cold storage (2–8 °C); **pegylation** (attaching polyethylene glycol) prolongs action (peginterferon, pegfilgrastim).

### Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs)

Identical antibodies from a single clone of cells, originally made by **hybridoma technology** (fusion of an antibody-producing B cell with a myeloma cell). The suffix of the name tells its origin and target:

| Suffix | Source | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| -omab | Mouse | Muromonab-CD3 (older) |
| -ximab | Chimeric (mouse variable + human constant) | Rituximab, infliximab, basiliximab, cetuximab |
| -zumab | Humanised | Trastuzumab, bevacizumab, omalizumab, tocilizumab |
| -umab | Fully human | Adalimumab, denosumab, nivolumab |

**Mechanisms:** neutralise a ligand (bevacizumab binds VEGF; infliximab binds TNF-α), block a receptor (trastuzumab on HER2; cetuximab on EGFR), mark cells for destruction by complement and immune cells (rituximab on CD20 of B cells), or deliver a toxin/drug (**antibody–drug conjugates** such as trastuzumab emtansine).

| mAb | Target | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rituximab | CD20 | Non-Hodgkin lymphoma, CLL, rheumatoid arthritis |
| Trastuzumab | HER2/neu | HER2-positive breast cancer (cardiotoxicity) |
| Bevacizumab | VEGF | Colorectal cancer, age-related macular degeneration (off-label; ranibizumab is the eye-specific form) |
| Cetuximab | EGFR | Colorectal, head and neck cancers |
| Infliximab, adalimumab | TNF-α | Rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's disease, psoriasis (screen for latent TB first) |
| Omalizumab | IgE | Severe allergic asthma |
| Denosumab | RANKL | Osteoporosis |
| Basiliximab | IL-2 receptor | Transplant rejection prophylaxis |

**Adverse effects:** infusion reactions, hypersensitivity, infections (TB reactivation with anti-TNF drugs), formation of anti-drug antibodies, target-specific effects.

### Targeted drugs (drugs aimed at a specific molecular target)

Small molecules that block signals cancer cells depend on — usually **kinase inhibitors** (suffix **-tinib**):

| Drug | Target | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Imatinib | **BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase** (Philadelphia chromosome), c-KIT | Chronic myeloid leukaemia (first "magic bullet" success), GIST |
| Gefitinib, erlotinib | EGFR tyrosine kinase | Non-small cell lung cancer with EGFR mutation |
| Sorafenib, sunitinib | VEGFR and other kinases | Renal cell and liver cancer |
| Bortezomib | Proteasome | Multiple myeloma |

Targeted drugs (small molecules and mAbs) are more selective than cytotoxic drugs, so they cause less marrow suppression, but they have their own effects (skin rash, diarrhoea, hypertension, fluid retention) and resistance develops through target mutation. "Targeting drugs to an antigen" also includes **antibody–drug conjugates** and radio-labelled antibodies, which carry a cytotoxic or radioactive payload to cells bearing the antigen.

### Biosimilars

A **biosimilar** is a biological product that is **highly similar** to an already approved reference biological, with **no clinically meaningful differences** in safety, purity and potency. Because biologicals are made in living cells by complex processes, an exact copy cannot be made (unlike generic small-molecule drugs, which are chemically identical).

| Generic drug | Biosimilar |
| --- | --- |
| Small, simple, chemically defined molecule | Large, complex protein |
| Identical to the reference | Similar, not identical |
| Bioequivalence study is enough | Stepwise comparability: analytical, non-clinical, clinical (PK/PD, immunogenicity, efficacy) |
| Low development cost | Higher cost, but cheaper than the original biological |

In India, biosimilars are approved under the **"Similar Biologics" guidelines** of CDSCO and the Department of Biotechnology. Examples: biosimilar trastuzumab, rituximab, adalimumab, insulin glargine, filgrastim, erythropoietin. India was among the early countries to market biosimilars, which has improved access to costly biological therapy.

## Remember

- UTI: *E. coli* commonest; nitrofurantoin acts only in urine (avoid in renal failure); methenamine needs acidic urine (releases formaldehyde); fosfomycin single oral dose.
- STD: syndromic approach; syphilis → benzathine penicillin; gonorrhoea → ceftriaxone; chlamydia → azithromycin/doxycycline; trichomonas → metronidazole.
- Log-kill: a fixed fraction killed per course; combination and cyclic therapy.
- Alkylating agents → guanine N7; cyclophosphamide → acrolein → haemorrhagic cystitis → mesna.
- Methotrexate → DHFR, rescue with folinic acid; 5-FU → thymidylate synthase; 6-MP with allopurinol needs less.
- Vinca prevents microtubule assembly, taxanes prevent disassembly; etoposide topo II, topotecan topo I.
- Doxorubicin → cardiotoxic; bleomycin → lung fibrosis; cisplatin → kidney, ear, vomiting; vincristine → neuropathy.
- Cyclosporine/tacrolimus → calcineurin → IL-2; sirolimus → mTOR; mycophenolate → IMP dehydrogenase; cyclosporine nephrotoxic, hirsutism, gum hyperplasia.
- Immunostimulants: BCG, levamisole, interferons, IL-2, G-CSF, checkpoint inhibitors.
- mAb suffixes: -ximab chimeric, -zumab humanised, -umab human; rituximab CD20, trastuzumab HER2, bevacizumab VEGF, infliximab TNF.
- Imatinib → BCR-ABL in CML. Biosimilar = highly similar, not identical; India's Similar Biologics guidelines.
