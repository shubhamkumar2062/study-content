# Unit 5: Principles of toxicology and chronopharmacology

The last unit is short in teaching hours but easy to score in. It has two parts: **toxicology** (types of toxicity, the special toxicities tested before a drug is marketed, and the general plan for managing a poisoned patient) and **chronopharmacology** (how the body's daily rhythms change drug action, and how drug timing is planned around them).

> Scope of these notes: the poisoning topics are covered at the level of definitions, the general principles of management as a textbook chapter lists them, and the named antidotes by class. Treatment of an actual poisoning is always decided by the treating doctor and the Poison Information Centre. For the clinical features of each named poisoning, read the chapter in your prescribed textbook.

## 5.1 Basic terms in toxicology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Toxicology | Science that studies the harmful effects of chemicals (drugs, industrial and household chemicals, natural toxins) on living organisms, their detection, mechanisms and management |
| Poison | Any substance that can cause harm or death when it enters the body in sufficient amount ("the dose makes the poison" — Paracelsus) |
| Toxicity | The degree to which a substance can harm an organism |
| Hazard vs risk | Hazard is the capacity to cause harm; risk is the probability that harm occurs under given exposure |
| LD50 (median lethal dose) | Dose that kills 50% of the test animals in a defined period; a measure of acute toxicity (determined statistically in the laboratory; see the BP608P practical) |
| ED50 | Dose that produces the desired effect in 50% of animals |
| Therapeutic index | LD50 ÷ ED50 (or TD50 ÷ ED50 in humans); higher means safer |
| NOAEL | No-observed-adverse-effect level: highest dose in a study with no harmful effect |
| Branches | Clinical, forensic, environmental, occupational, regulatory and analytical toxicology |

**Routes of exposure:** oral, inhalation, skin and eye contact, injection. **Types by cause:** accidental (children, occupational), suicidal, homicidal, iatrogenic (drug-induced), and environmental.

## 5.2 Acute, subacute and chronic toxicity

Toxicity studies are classified by how long animals are exposed. They are required before human trials and marketing (Schedule Y / New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules 2019 in India; OECD test guidelines internationally).

| Type | Definition | Exposure and observation | What it tells |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acute toxicity | Harmful effects after a **single** exposure (or several within 24 h) | Observed for 14 days | LD50 or acute toxic class, signs of toxicity, target organs; guides dose selection for later studies |
| Subacute (repeated-dose 28-day) toxicity | Effects of **repeated daily** exposure for a short period | 14–28 days | Target organs, reversibility, dose for longer studies |
| Subchronic toxicity | Repeated exposure for part of the lifespan | Usually 90 days | NOAEL, cumulative effects |
| Chronic toxicity | Repeated exposure for a **long** part of the lifespan | 6 months (rodents) to 9–12 months (non-rodents) or longer | Long-term organ damage, accumulation, safety margin for long-term human use |

**Parameters examined:** clinical signs and behaviour, body weight, food and water intake, haematology, serum biochemistry (liver and kidney tests), urine analysis, organ weights, and histopathology of organs at the end. Usually done in a rodent (rat) and a non-rodent (dog, rabbit) of both sexes at three dose levels with a control group.

**Acute vs chronic toxicity in humans**

| Feature | Acute | Chronic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exposure | Single or short | Repeated over months or years |
| Onset | Quick | Slow, gradual |
| Effects | Often reversible | Often cumulative, may be irreversible (organ damage, cancer) |
| Example pattern | Accidental single exposure | Occupational exposure to heavy metals |

**Modern approach (3Rs):** reduction, refinement and replacement of animal use. The classical LD50 test needing many animals has been replaced by methods using fewer animals: the **fixed dose procedure** (OECD 420), **acute toxic class method** (OECD 423) and **up-and-down procedure** (OECD 425). In India, animal studies follow **CCSEA** (formerly CPCSEA) guidelines and need approval of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee.

## 5.3 Genotoxicity, mutagenicity, carcinogenicity and teratogenicity

| Term | Definition | Common tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Genotoxicity** | Any damage to the genetic material (DNA, chromosomes), whether or not it is inherited — strand breaks, adducts, chromosome damage | Comet assay, chromosomal aberration test, micronucleus test, unscheduled DNA synthesis |
| **Mutagenicity** | Ability to cause a **permanent, heritable change** (mutation) in the DNA sequence — point mutations, frameshifts, chromosome changes. All mutagens are genotoxic, not all genotoxic agents cause lasting mutation | **Ames test** (reverse mutation in histidine-dependent *Salmonella typhimurium* strains, with liver S9 fraction for metabolic activation), mouse lymphoma assay, in vivo micronucleus test |
| **Carcinogenicity** | Ability to cause **cancer** (malignant tumours). Genotoxic carcinogens damage DNA directly; non-genotoxic (epigenetic) carcinogens act by hormonal effects, chronic irritation or cell proliferation | Long-term rodent bioassay (usually 2 years in rats and mice), transgenic mouse models; needed for drugs used long term |
| **Teratogenicity** | Ability to cause **structural or functional defects in the embryo or fetus** when given during pregnancy | Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies (fertility, embryo-fetal development in two species, pre- and postnatal development) |

**Carcinogenesis in stages:** initiation (irreversible DNA change) → promotion (clonal growth of initiated cells) → progression (malignancy and spread). Examples of drugs/chemicals linked to cancer: alkylating agents (secondary leukaemia), oestrogens (endometrial cancer), tamoxifen (endometrium), cyclophosphamide (bladder), benzene, aflatoxin, tobacco smoke, asbestos.

**Teratogenesis — key points:**

- The most sensitive period is **organogenesis** (about the 3rd to 8th week after conception). Before that, damage is "all or none"; later, it causes growth and functional defects.
- Classic example: **thalidomide** (1950s–60s) → phocomelia (short, flipper-like limbs). This disaster led to modern drug regulation and compulsory reproductive toxicity testing.
- Other known teratogens: isotretinoin and other retinoids, warfarin (fetal warfarin syndrome), valproate (neural tube defects), phenytoin (fetal hydantoin syndrome), ACE inhibitors (fetal kidney damage in later pregnancy), tetracyclines (teeth and bones), methotrexate, lithium (Ebstein's anomaly), diethylstilboestrol (vaginal cancer in daughters), alcohol (fetal alcohol syndrome).
- Drugs in pregnancy are grouped by risk categories in many references; the principle is to use a drug only when clearly needed, choose the one with the longest safety record, and avoid the first trimester where possible.

## 5.4 General principles of treatment of poisoning

Management of a poisoned patient is a medical emergency handled by doctors and Poison Information Centres. For the exam, learn the headings in order, as textbook chapters list them:

1. **Resuscitation and stabilisation** — airway, breathing and circulation first.
2. **Diagnosis** — history, examination and laboratory analysis to identify the substance.
3. **Decontamination** — measures that limit further exposure.
4. **Enhanced elimination** — measures that increase removal of an absorbed substance, used only for selected cases.
5. **Specific antidote** — where one exists.
6. **Supportive care and monitoring** — the backbone of treatment, because most poisons have no antidote.
7. **Prevention and follow-up** — counselling, safe storage, reporting to a Poison Information Centre.

**Antidote** means a substance that counteracts the effect of a poison. Antidotes are grouped by how they work: receptor antagonists, enzyme reactivators, chelating agents (bind metals into a complex the body can excrete), and agents that restore a protective body substance.

## 5.5 Named antidotes for the syllabus poisonings

| Poisoning named in syllabus | Antidote or approach (by name and class) |
| --- | --- |
| Barbiturates | No specific antidote; supportive care |
| Morphine (opioids) | Naloxone — opioid receptor antagonist |
| Organophosphorus compounds | Atropine — muscarinic antagonist; pralidoxime — oxime cholinesterase reactivator |
| Lead | Chelating agents: calcium disodium edetate, dimercaprol (BAL), succimer (DMSA), penicillamine |
| Mercury | Chelating agents: dimercaprol, succimer, unithiol (DMPS) |
| Arsenic | Chelating agents: dimercaprol, succimer, unithiol |

Other antidote pairs often asked as MCQs: N-acetylcysteine (paracetamol), flumazenil (benzodiazepines), protamine (heparin), vitamin K (warfarin), desferrioxamine (iron), fomepizole (methanol), digoxin-specific antibody fragments (digoxin).

## 5.6 Chronopharmacology: definitions

**Chronopharmacology** is the study of how the effects and kinetics of drugs vary with biological time (the time of day, month or year), and of how drugs themselves change biological rhythms.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Biological rhythm | A regular, self-sustained, repeating change in a body function |
| Cycle | One complete repetition of a rhythm |
| Period | Time taken for one cycle |
| Amplitude | Half the difference between the highest and lowest value |
| Acrophase | Time at which the rhythm peaks |
| MESOR | Rhythm-adjusted mean value |
| Circadian rhythm | Period of about 24 h (Latin *circa diem*): sleep–wake, body temperature, cortisol, blood pressure |
| Ultradian rhythm | Period shorter than 24 h: heartbeat, breathing, pulsatile hormone release |
| Infradian rhythm | Period longer than 24 h: menstrual cycle (circamensual), seasonal or yearly (circannual) |
| Zeitgeber | External time cue that sets the clock; light–dark cycle is the main one |

**Branches:**

- **Chronopharmacokinetics** — rhythmic changes in absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (gastric emptying, liver blood flow, kidney function vary through the day).
- **Chronopharmacodynamics (chronesthesy)** — rhythmic changes in the sensitivity of target tissues and receptors.
- **Chronotoxicology** — variation of adverse effects with the time of drug use.
- **Chronotherapy** — timing drug use to match the body's rhythms, for better effect and fewer adverse effects.

## 5.7 Biological clock and its significance

```text
 Light --> retina --> retinohypothalamic tract
                            |
                            v
          Suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), hypothalamus  = master clock
                            |
          +-----------------+------------------+
          v                 v                  v
     Pineal gland      Cortisol rhythm    Peripheral clocks
     melatonin (night) (peak early morning) liver, heart, gut, kidney
```

- The **master clock** is the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** of the hypothalamus. It is reset daily by light and keeps peripheral clocks in organs in step.
- At the molecular level, **clock genes** (CLOCK, BMAL1, Period, Cryptochrome) form feedback loops of transcription and translation lasting about 24 h.
- The **pineal gland** releases **melatonin** in darkness; melatonin and its agonists (ramelteon, agomelatine) are used for sleep and jet-lag problems.
- Disturbance of the clock (shift work, jet lag) is linked with sleep disorders, metabolic and mood problems.

**Significance leading to chronotherapy — examples**

| Condition / rhythm | Chronotherapy point |
| --- | --- |
| Asthma worsens at night and early morning (low cortisol and adrenaline, high vagal tone) | Evening doses of inhaled steroids or long-acting bronchodilators; theophylline timed for night cover |
| Blood pressure surges in the early morning; heart attacks and strokes more common then | Timing of antihypertensives to cover the morning rise |
| Cholesterol synthesis is highest at night | Short-acting statins (simvastatin) given in the evening |
| Gastric acid secretion peaks at night | Bedtime H2-blocker for ulcer healing |
| Cortisol peaks in the early morning | Corticosteroids given in the morning reduce suppression of the adrenal axis |
| Rheumatoid arthritis stiffness is worst in the morning | Delayed-release preparations taken at bedtime |
| Cancer cells and normal cells differ in rhythm | Timed anticancer drug use to reduce toxicity |

**Chronotherapeutic drug delivery systems** (pulsatile or delayed-release dosage forms) release drug after a lag time so that the peak level comes when the disease is most active, for example a bedtime dose releasing in the early morning.

## Remember

- Acute = single exposure, observed 14 days; subacute = repeated 28 days; subchronic = 90 days; chronic = 6 months or more.
- Genotoxic = any DNA damage; mutagenic = heritable change (Ames test); carcinogenic = cancer (2-year rodent study); teratogenic = birth defects (thalidomide → phocomelia; organogenesis weeks 3–8).
- 3Rs and fewer-animal acute methods (OECD 420, 423, 425); CCSEA approval.
- Poisoning management headings: stabilise → diagnose → decontaminate → enhance elimination → antidote → support → prevent.
- Antidotes: naloxone (opioids), atropine + pralidoxime (organophosphates), chelators for lead, mercury, arsenic; barbiturates have no specific antidote.
- Circadian ≈ 24 h, ultradian < 24 h, infradian > 24 h; SCN is the master clock; melatonin at night.
- Chronotherapy: evening statin, morning steroid, night cover in asthma, bedtime H2-blocker.
