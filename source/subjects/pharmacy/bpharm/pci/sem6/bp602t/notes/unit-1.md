# Unit 1: Drugs acting on the respiratory system and the gastrointestinal tract

This unit has two halves. The first covers drugs for asthma, COPD, cough, nasal congestion and respiratory depression. The second covers the gut: peptic ulcer, constipation, diarrhoea, appetite, digestion and vomiting. Questions on anti-asthmatic drugs, peptic ulcer drugs and anti-emetics appear in almost every paper.

## 1.1 Bronchial asthma: what the drugs must do

**Asthma** is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways with recurrent, reversible bronchoconstriction, airway hyper-responsiveness and mucus plugging. Triggers include allergens, infection, exercise, cold air and drugs such as aspirin and β-blockers.

```text
 Allergen + IgE on mast cell
            |
            v
 Mast cell degranulation ---> histamine, leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4),
            |                  prostaglandin D2, PAF, cytokines
            v
 Early phase: bronchospasm (minutes)
            |
            v
 Late phase: eosinophil and T-cell inflammation, oedema,
             mucus, hyper-responsiveness (hours)
```

So the two aims are **bronchodilation** (relief) and **suppression of inflammation** (control).

| 1. Bronchodilators | β2-agonists: short-acting (SABA) | Salbutamol, terbutaline |

| | Methylxanthines | Theophylline, aminophylline, doxofylline |
| | Anticholinergics | Ipratropium, tiotropium |
| 2. Leukotriene antagonists | Receptor (CysLT1) blockers | Montelukast, zafirlukast |
| | 5-lipoxygenase inhibitor | Zileuton |
| 3. Mast cell stabilisers | | Sodium cromoglycate, ketotifen |
| 4. Corticosteroids | Inhaled | Beclomethasone, budesonide, fluticasone, ciclesonide |
| | Systemic | Prednisolone (oral), hydrocortisone (IV) |
| 5. Anti-IgE antibody | | Omalizumab |
| 6. Anti-IL-5 antibody | | Mepolizumab |

### β2-adrenergic agonists

**Mechanism:** stimulate β2 receptors on bronchial smooth muscle → Gs → adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP → protein kinase A → relaxation. They also reduce mediator release from mast cells and improve mucociliary clearance.

- **Salbutamol (SABA):** quick onset (minutes) by inhalation, action 4–6 h; the reliever of choice for acute symptoms.
- **Salmeterol (LABA):** slow onset, action about 12 h; used only with an inhaled steroid for maintenance, never alone. **Formoterol** is long-acting but fast in onset.
- **Adverse effects:** fine tremor (β2 on skeletal muscle), palpitation and tachycardia, hypokalaemia (K+ moves into cells), tolerance on regular use, metabolic effects such as hyperglycaemia.

### Methylxanthines (theophylline)

**Mechanism:** (1) inhibition of phosphodiesterase (PDE) → ↑ cAMP; (2) blockade of adenosine receptors (adenosine constricts airways); (3) increased histone deacetylase activity, which may add an anti-inflammatory effect.

**Actions:** bronchodilation, CNS stimulation, cardiac stimulation, mild diuresis, increased gastric secretion, improved diaphragm contractility.

**Problems:** a **narrow therapeutic index**, so plasma levels are monitored; metabolised by CYP1A2. Adverse effects include nausea, vomiting, restlessness, insomnia, tachycardia and arrhythmias, and seizures at high levels.

**Interactions:** enzyme inhibitors (erythromycin, ciprofloxacin, cimetidine) raise theophylline levels; enzyme inducers (rifampicin, phenytoin) and smoking lower them.

Aminophylline is theophylline with ethylenediamine (more water-soluble, given IV). Doxofylline has less adenosine block, so fewer cardiac and CNS effects.

### Anticholinergics

**Ipratropium** (short) and **tiotropium** (long-acting) are quaternary compounds. They block M3 receptors in bronchial smooth muscle and reduce vagal bronchoconstriction and mucus secretion. Being quaternary, they are poorly absorbed after inhalation, so systemic atropine-like effects are few (dry mouth is common). They are more useful in COPD than in asthma.

### Leukotriene modifiers

Cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) cause bronchoconstriction, mucus and oedema. **Montelukast** and **zafirlukast** block the CysLT1 receptor; **zileuton** inhibits 5-lipoxygenase. Used orally for mild persistent asthma, aspirin-sensitive asthma and exercise-induced asthma. Montelukast may cause headache and, rarely, mood changes; zileuton can raise liver enzymes.

### Mast cell stabilisers

**Sodium cromoglycate** prevents degranulation of mast cells (it stabilises the membrane, probably through chloride channels). It is **prophylactic only**: no bronchodilator action, useless in an acute attack. Poorly absorbed orally, so given by inhalation, nasal spray or eye drops. **Ketotifen** also has H1-blocking action and is given orally.

### Corticosteroids

**Mechanism:** bind the cytoplasmic glucocorticoid receptor → complex enters the nucleus → reduced transcription of inflammatory genes (cytokines, COX-2, adhesion molecules) and increased lipocortin (annexin-1), which inhibits phospholipase A2. Result: fewer eosinophils and mast cells, less oedema and mucus, less hyper-responsiveness. They also **up-regulate β2 receptors**.

- **Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)** are the mainstay of controller therapy. Local adverse effects: oropharyngeal candidiasis and hoarseness (dysphonia); reduced by a spacer and by rinsing the mouth after use.
- **Systemic steroids** (oral prednisolone, IV hydrocortisone) are used for severe exacerbations and acute severe asthma. Long-term use causes Cushingoid features, osteoporosis, hyperglycaemia and adrenal suppression.

### Biologicals

**Omalizumab** binds free IgE and stops it attaching to mast cells; given by subcutaneous injection in severe allergic asthma. **Mepolizumab** blocks IL-5 and lowers eosinophils in severe eosinophilic asthma.

### Treatment approach (stepwise)

```text
Mild intermittent  -> inhaled SABA (or low-dose ICS-formoterol) when needed
Mild persistent    -> add regular low-dose ICS (or montelukast)
Moderate           -> low/medium ICS + LABA
Severe             -> high ICS + LABA, add tiotropium / montelukast /
                      theophylline; biologicals; oral steroid if needed
Acute severe asthma -> oxygen, nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium,
                      systemic corticosteroid, IV magnesium sulphate
                      in selected cases, ventilatory support if needed
```

**Status asthmaticus (acute severe asthma)** is a medical emergency: a severe attack not relieved by usual bronchodilators. Sedatives are avoided because they depress breathing.

## 1.3 Drugs used in COPD

**Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)** is a persistent, mostly irreversible airflow limitation from chronic bronchitis and emphysema, usually due to smoking.

| Drug group | Role in COPD |
| --- | --- |
| Long-acting anticholinergics (LAMA): tiotropium, glycopyrronium | First-line maintenance bronchodilators |
| LABA: salmeterol, formoterol, indacaterol | Maintenance, often combined with LAMA |
| SABA / ipratropium | Relief of symptoms |
| Inhaled corticosteroids | Added in frequent exacerbators, used with LABA |
| PDE-4 inhibitor: roflumilast | Reduces exacerbations in severe chronic bronchitis |
| Theophylline | Add-on when others are not enough |
| Mucolytics: N-acetylcysteine, carbocisteine | Thin sputum |
| Antibiotics, oxygen therapy, vaccines (influenza, pneumococcal), smoking cessation | Infective exacerbations, hypoxaemia and prevention |

**Asthma vs COPD:** asthma is reversible and steroid-responsive with eosinophilic inflammation; COPD is largely irreversible, neutrophilic, and responds better to anticholinergics.

## 1.4 Cough: expectorants and antitussives

Cough is a protective reflex. A **productive** (wet) cough should not be suppressed; a **dry**, irritating cough may be suppressed.

| Class | Examples | How they act |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pharyngeal demulcents | Lozenges, liquorice, honey, glycerine | Coat the throat, reduce afferent impulses |
| Expectorants (secretion enhancers) | Guaiphenesin, ammonium chloride, potassium iodide, ipecacuanha (low strength), vasaka | Increase volume of bronchial secretion, making it easier to cough out |
| Mucolytics | Bromhexine, ambroxol, N-acetylcysteine, carbocisteine, dornase alfa | Break mucus structure, reduce viscosity |
| Antitussives: opioids | Codeine, pholcodine, ethylmorphine | Depress the cough centre in the medulla |
| Antitussives: non-opioids | Dextromethorphan, noscapine, chlophedianol | Central cough suppression, no analgesia, low dependence |
| Antihistamines | Diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine, promethazine | Sedative and anticholinergic, reduce postnasal drip |
| Bronchodilators | Salbutamol | When cough is due to bronchospasm |

- **Bromhexine** is a vasicine derivative; it depolymerises mucopolysaccharides. **Ambroxol** is its active metabolite.
- **N-acetylcysteine** opens disulfide bonds in mucoproteins (free –SH group). It is also the antidote for paracetamol poisoning (see Unit 5).
- **Codeine** causes constipation, drowsiness and has dependence potential. **Dextromethorphan** is the d-isomer of a morphinan; it lacks analgesic action. It should not be combined with MAO inhibitors.
- Many irrational fixed-dose combinations of cough syrups exist; combining an expectorant with a cough suppressant is illogical.

## 1.5 Nasal decongestants

Nasal congestion is due to dilated blood vessels in the nasal mucosa. α-Adrenergic agonists constrict them.

| Type | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topical (nasal drops/spray) | Oxymetazoline, xylometazoline, naphazoline, phenylephrine | Rapid relief; use for a few days only |
| Oral | Pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine | Often in cold combinations |

- **Rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa)** follows prolonged topical use.
- Oral decongestants can raise blood pressure, cause insomnia and urinary retention; avoided in hypertension and with MAO inhibitors.
- Naphazoline drops are avoided in infants (CNS depression).
- Allergic rhinitis is also treated with antihistamines, intranasal steroids and cromoglycate.

## 1.6 Respiratory stimulants (analeptics)

These stimulate the respiratory centre directly or through carotid chemoreceptors.

| Example | Point to remember |
| --- | --- |
| Doxapram | Acts on carotid body chemoreceptors and the medulla; used in post-anaesthetic respiratory depression |
| Nikethamide, lobeline | Older agents, now obsolete |
| Caffeine (as citrate) | Apnoea of prematurity |
| Theophylline | Also stimulates breathing |

They have a narrow margin between respiratory stimulation and convulsions, so mechanical ventilation and specific antagonists (naloxone for opioids) are preferred today.

## 1.7 Peptic ulcer: the balance

A **peptic ulcer** is a break in the mucosa of the stomach or duodenum exposed to acid and pepsin. It forms when **aggressive factors** (acid, pepsin, *Helicobacter pylori*, NSAIDs, smoking) overcome **defensive factors** (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, blood flow, cell renewal).

```text
 Control of acid secretion at the parietal cell

 ACh (M3) ----\
 Gastrin (CCK2)---> ↑ Ca2+ --\
 Histamine (H2) --> ↑ cAMP ---> H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump) --> H+ into lumen
 (from ECL cells)             ^
 PGE2 (EP3) ----> ↓ cAMP (inhibits)
```

The proton pump is the final common step, which is why proton pump inhibitors are the most effective acid suppressants.

## 1.8 Classification of anti-ulcer drugs

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Reduce acid secretion | |
| H2-receptor antagonists | Cimetidine, ranitidine, famotidine, roxatidine |
| Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) | Omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole |
| Potassium-competitive acid blocker | Vonoprazan |
| Anticholinergics | Pirenzepine (M1-selective) |
| Prostaglandin analogue | Misoprostol |
| 2. Neutralise acid (antacids) | |
| Systemic | Sodium bicarbonate |
| Non-systemic | Magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide, calcium carbonate, magaldrate |
| 3. Ulcer protectives | Sucralfate, colloidal bismuth subcitrate |
| 4. Anti-*H. pylori* drugs | Amoxicillin, clarithromycin, metronidazole, tinidazole, tetracycline, bismuth |

### H2-receptor antagonists

Competitively block H2 receptors on parietal cells → ↓ cAMP → ↓ basal and nocturnal acid secretion (and partly food-stimulated). **Cimetidine** inhibits CYP450 (raises levels of warfarin, theophylline, phenytoin) and has antiandrogenic effects (gynaecomastia, impotence) by blocking androgen receptors and raising prolactin. **Famotidine** is more potent and lacks these problems. Ranitidine products were withdrawn in many countries because of an NDMA impurity concern.

### Proton pump inhibitors

**Mechanism:** PPIs are prodrugs. They are weak bases that concentrate in the acidic canaliculus of the parietal cell, convert to an active sulfenamide, and bind **covalently and irreversibly** to cysteine groups of H+/K+ ATPase. Acid secretion returns only when new pump molecules are made, so a once-daily dose works although plasma half-life is short. Given before breakfast, as enteric-coated forms, because acid destroys them before absorption.

**Uses:** peptic ulcer, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD, drug of choice), Zollinger–Ellison syndrome (drug of choice), *H. pylori* eradication, NSAID-ulcer prevention, stress ulcer prophylaxis.

**Adverse effects:** headache, diarrhoea; long-term: vitamin B12 deficiency, hypomagnesaemia, increased risk of fractures and enteric infections (including *Clostridioides difficile*), rebound acid hypersecretion on stopping. **Omeprazole** inhibits CYP2C19 and reduces activation of clopidogrel; pantoprazole is preferred with clopidogrel.

### Antacids

Weak bases that neutralise gastric acid. Quick relief but short action.

| Antacid | Points |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium bicarbonate | Fast, systemic; CO2 belching, alkalosis, sodium load; avoid in hypertension and heart failure |
| Magnesium hydroxide | Laxative (osmotic) |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Constipating; binds phosphate (hypophosphataemia) |
| Calcium carbonate | Constipation, acid rebound, milk-alkali syndrome |

Mg and Al salts are combined so that laxative and constipating effects cancel. Antacids reduce absorption of tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, iron and digoxin (chelation or adsorption), so these drugs are spaced apart.

### Ulcer protectives

- **Sucralfate:** aluminium salt of sucrose octasulfate. In acid it polymerises to a sticky gel that binds to the ulcer base and protects it from acid, pepsin and bile; it also stimulates prostaglandin and mucus. Needs acid to work, so not given with antacids or PPIs at the same time. Causes constipation; may bind other drugs.
- **Colloidal bismuth subcitrate:** forms a protective coat, increases prostaglandin and bicarbonate, and is toxic to *H. pylori*. Blackens stools and tongue.
- **Misoprostol** (PGE1 analogue): inhibits acid and increases mucus and bicarbonate; mainly for **NSAID-induced ulcers**. Causes diarrhoea and abdominal cramps; contraindicated in pregnancy because it contracts the uterus.

### *H. pylori* eradication

*H. pylori* is a Gram-negative spiral bacterium that makes urease, survives in acid and is a major cause of duodenal ulcer and recurrence. Eradication uses combinations to avoid resistance:

- **Triple therapy:** PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin (or metronidazole), usually 14 days.
- **Quadruple therapy:** PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole (when clarithromycin resistance is likely).

## 1.9 Drugs for constipation (laxatives / purgatives)

**Laxatives** soften stools gently; **purgatives** (cathartics) cause stronger, more fluid evacuation. Same drugs often act as both depending on amount.

| Class | Examples | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk-forming | Ispaghula (psyllium), methylcellulose, bran | Absorb water, swell, distend colon → peristalsis |
| Stool softeners | Docusate sodium, liquid paraffin | Lower surface tension / lubricate |
| Osmotic | Lactulose, magnesium sulphate, magnesium hydroxide, sodium sulphate, polyethylene glycol | Retain water in the lumen by osmosis |
| Stimulant | Bisacodyl, senna, sodium picosulfate, castor oil | Stimulate the myenteric plexus and secretion |
| Newer | Lubiprostone (Cl− channel activator), prucalopride (5-HT4 agonist), linaclotide | Increase fluid secretion or motility |

- **Lactulose:** broken down by colonic bacteria to lactic and acetic acid; also used in **hepatic encephalopathy** (acid traps ammonia as NH4+ in the gut).
- **Liquid paraffin:** long use causes lipoid pneumonia (if aspirated), loss of fat-soluble vitamins and anal seepage.
- **Saline purgatives** (MgSO4, Na2SO4) act within hours and are used for bowel preparation.
- Laxatives are avoided in undiagnosed abdominal pain and intestinal obstruction. Chronic stimulant abuse can cause hypokalaemia and a sluggish ("cathartic") colon.

## 1.10 Drugs for diarrhoea

The first priority in acute diarrhoea is **replacing water and salts**. Drugs come second.

**Oral rehydration solution (ORS):** glucose and sodium are absorbed together by the sodium–glucose cotransporter (SGLT1) in the small intestine, and water follows. This mechanism works even in cholera because cAMP-driven secretion does not damage it. WHO recommends a **reduced osmolarity ORS** (about 245 mOsm/L), which reduces stool output and vomiting compared with the older formula. Zinc supplements are added for children.

| Class | Examples | Mechanism and points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antimotility | Loperamide, diphenoxylate (with atropine), codeine | μ-opioid agonists in the gut → ↓ peristalsis, ↑ segmentation. Loperamide does not cross the BBB. Not for infective dysentery with fever/blood or small children |
| Antisecretory | Racecadotril | Enkephalinase inhibitor → ↓ secretion without slowing transit |
| Adsorbents / bulk | Kaolin, pectin, ispaghula | Bind fluid and toxins (weak benefit) |
| Antimicrobials | Ciprofloxacin, azithromycin, metronidazole, doxycycline | Only for specific infections (cholera, shigellosis, amoebiasis, giardiasis) |
| Probiotics | *Lactobacillus*, *Saccharomyces boulardii* | Restore normal flora; antibiotic-associated diarrhoea |
| Others | Octreotide (secretory tumours), bismuth subsalicylate | Special situations |

## 1.11 Appetite stimulants and suppressants

**Appetite stimulants (orexigenics):** cyproheptadine (5-HT2 and H1 blocker), megestrol acetate and medroxyprogesterone (cancer and AIDS cachexia), corticosteroids, dronabinol (cannabinoid agonist), mirtazapine; also traditional bitters and vitamin B complex.

**Appetite suppressants (anorexiants)** for obesity:

| Drug | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Phentermine | Releases noradrenaline in the hypothalamus |
| Orlistat | Inhibits gastric and pancreatic lipase → less fat absorbed (not a true anorectic); causes oily stools, steatorrhoea, loss of fat-soluble vitamins |
| GLP-1 agonists: liraglutide, semaglutide | Delay gastric emptying and act on satiety centres |
| Naltrexone–bupropion, topiramate–phentermine (combinations) | Central appetite control |

Older anorectics such as fenfluramine (valvular heart disease) and sibutramine (cardiovascular events) were withdrawn.

## 1.12 Digestants and carminatives

**Digestants** help digestion of food: pepsin, pancreatin (lipase, amylase, protease), papain, diastase, bromelain, dilute hydrochloric acid and ursodeoxycholic acid (bile acid). **Pancreatin** is used in exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (cystic fibrosis, chronic pancreatitis); it is given as enteric-coated granules because acid destroys the enzymes.

**Carminatives** relieve flatulence by relaxing the lower oesophageal sphincter and helping expulsion of gas: volatile oils of peppermint, dill, cardamom, ginger, clove, fennel (saunf) and ajwain. **Simethicone (activated dimethicone)** is an antifoaming agent that breaks gas bubbles, often added to antacids.

## 1.13 Emetics and anti-emetics

### Vomiting reflex

```text
 Gut irritation (vagal 5-HT3) ----\
 Chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) --> Vomiting centre (medulla) --> vomiting
   [D2, 5-HT3, NK1; outside BBB]      [M1, H1, NK1, 5-HT3]
 Vestibular apparatus (M1, H1) ----/
 Higher centres (smell, sight, fear)/
```

### Emetics

**Emetics** induce vomiting: apomorphine (dopamine agonist on the CTZ) and syrup of ipecacuanha (emetine; gastric irritation and central action). They were used to remove swallowed poisons but are **no longer recommended** in routine poisoning management (Unit 5), because evidence of benefit is poor and the risk of aspiration is real.

### Classification of anti-emetics

| Class | Examples | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5-HT3 antagonists | Ondansetron, granisetron, palonosetron | Chemotherapy and postoperative vomiting (drug of choice) |
| D2 antagonists (prokinetic) | Metoclopramide, domperidone | Gastric stasis, drug and postoperative vomiting |
| D2 antagonists (neuroleptic) | Prochlorperazine, chlorpromazine, haloperidol | Drug- and disease-induced vomiting, vertigo |
| NK1 antagonists | Aprepitant, fosaprepitant | Delayed vomiting of cisplatin-type chemotherapy (with 5-HT3 antagonist and dexamethasone) |
| H1 antihistamines | Promethazine, cyclizine, meclizine, doxylamine (with pyridoxine) | Motion sickness, morning sickness |
| Anticholinergic | Hyoscine (scopolamine), including transdermal patch | Motion sickness (most effective) |
| Corticosteroids | Dexamethasone | Adjuvant in chemotherapy vomiting |
| Cannabinoids | Dronabinol, nabilone | Resistant chemotherapy vomiting |
| Benzodiazepines | Lorazepam | Anticipatory vomiting |

### Metoclopramide vs domperidone

| Feature | Metoclopramide | Domperidone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Action | D2 block (CTZ and gut) + 5-HT4 agonism → prokinetic | Peripheral D2 block; CTZ lies outside the BBB |
| Crosses BBB | Yes | Hardly |
| Extrapyramidal effects | Yes (dystonia, especially in children and young adults) | Rare |
| Other | Hyperprolactinaemia | Hyperprolactinaemia; QT prolongation risk |

**Prokinetic drugs** increase gastric emptying and lower oesophageal sphincter tone: metoclopramide, domperidone, itopride, mosapride; erythromycin acts as a motilin agonist.

### Ondansetron

Blocks 5-HT3 receptors on vagal afferents in the gut and in the CTZ. Highly effective against chemotherapy-induced and postoperative vomiting, weak in motion sickness. Adverse effects: headache, constipation, QT prolongation. Palonosetron is long-acting.

## Remember

- Asthma: SABA (salbutamol) relieves; ICS controls; LABA only with ICS; cromoglycate only prevents.
- Theophylline: PDE inhibition + adenosine block; narrow therapeutic index; CYP1A2 interactions (ciprofloxacin, erythromycin raise levels).
- Tiotropium: first-line maintenance in COPD; roflumilast is a PDE-4 inhibitor.
- Productive cough: expectorant/mucolytic; dry cough: dextromethorphan or codeine. N-acetylcysteine breaks S–S bonds.
- Topical decongestants cause rebound congestion; doxapram is the respiratory stimulant still in use.
- PPIs: irreversible H+/K+ ATPase block, prodrug activated in acid, drug of choice in GERD and Zollinger–Ellison syndrome.
- Cimetidine: enzyme inhibitor and antiandrogenic. Sucralfate needs acid. Misoprostol for NSAID ulcer, not in pregnancy.
- *H. pylori*: PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin (triple) or bismuth quadruple therapy.
- Lactulose also treats hepatic encephalopathy; Mg salts purge, Al salts constipate.
- ORS works through Na+-glucose cotransport; reduced osmolarity ORS ~245 mOsm/L; loperamide does not enter the brain.
- Ondansetron: chemotherapy vomiting; hyoscine: motion sickness; aprepitant: delayed vomiting; metoclopramide causes extrapyramidal effects, domperidone rarely.
