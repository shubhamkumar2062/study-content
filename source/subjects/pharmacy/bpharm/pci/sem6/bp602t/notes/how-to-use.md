# How to use this guide

Pharmacology III is a classification-and-mechanism subject, like Pharmacology I. Nearly every answer follows one shape: definition → classification with examples → mechanism of action → pharmacological actions → uses → adverse effects → interactions or contraindications. Study one unit at a time: read the notes, write each classification table once from memory, draw the key diagrams (acid secretion at the parietal cell, folate pathway with sulfonamide and trimethoprim blocks, malarial life cycle, cell cycle with anticancer drugs, calcineurin–IL-2 pathway), then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Unit 1 joins the respiratory and gastrointestinal chapters, as the syllabus does; no unit has been split.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with classification, mechanism and a diagram.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scope of Unit 5.** The poisoning topics are covered at the level of definitions, the general headings of management and the named antidotes by class. The clinical features of each named poisoning are in your prescribed textbook chapter.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start with a one-line definition, then a **classification table with two examples per class**. Examiners give a large share of marks for a clean classification.
2. For a drug essay use the fixed order: mechanism, actions, uses, adverse effects, interactions. For antimicrobials add spectrum and resistance.
3. Draw what can be drawn: parietal cell and proton pump, vomiting pathways, peptidoglycan cross-linking, folate pathway, HIV life cycle with drug targets, malarial life cycle, cell cycle, calcineurin–IL-2 pathway, SCN and the biological clock.
4. Learn the "drug of choice" list (syphilis → benzathine penicillin; *Pneumocystis* → cotrimoxazole; rickettsia → doxycycline; amoebiasis → metronidazole; severe malaria → artesunate; CML → imatinib; chemotherapy vomiting → ondansetron; motion sickness → hyoscine).
5. Pair each drug with its signature adverse effect (isoniazid neuropathy, ethambutol optic neuritis, chloramphenicol grey baby, aminoglycoside ototoxicity, cyclophosphamide haemorrhagic cystitis, doxorubicin cardiotoxicity, cyclosporine nephrotoxicity). These become MCQs and short notes.
6. Comparisons score well in short answers: H2-blocker vs PPI, metoclopramide vs domperidone, bactericidal vs bacteriostatic, generic vs biosimilar, vinca vs taxane, mutagenicity vs genotoxicity.
7. No doses are needed in this paper; write mechanism and use rather than a number.
