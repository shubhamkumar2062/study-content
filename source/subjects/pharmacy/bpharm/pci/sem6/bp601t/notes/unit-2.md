# Unit 2: Macrolides, chloramphenicol, clindamycin, prodrugs and antimalarials

This unit finishes the antibiotics (the 50S-ribosome group), introduces the **prodrug** idea, and then covers the **antimalarials**. Three drugs are starred in the syllabus: **chloramphenicol, chloroquine and pamaquine**; for their preparations work from the schemes in your prescribed textbook (Lednicer, or Wilson and Gisvold) and practise writing them as numbered steps. These notes carry the rest of the examinable chemistry. The SAR of 4-aminoquinolines and the chemistry of erythromycin's acid instability are the most likely theory questions.

## 2.1 Macrolide antibiotics

### History and nomenclature

The term **macrolide** describes a **large lactone ring (12–16 atoms) carrying one or more sugars**, at least one of them an amino sugar. **Erythromycin** was isolated in 1952 from *Streptomyces erythreus* (now *Saccharopolyspora erythraea*), obtained from a soil sample from the Philippines. Its semi-synthetic successors are **clarithromycin** and **azithromycin**; roxithromycin is another useful derivative.

### Structure of erythromycin

```text
   14-membered lactone ring = ERYTHRONOLIDE (aglycone)
      │
      ├─ C3–O– L-CLADINOSE    (neutral sugar, 3-O-methyl)
      ├─ C5–O– D-DESOSAMINE   (amino sugar, 3'-N(CH3)2 → basic, pKa ≈ 8.8)
      ├─ C6–OH, C11–OH, C12–OH
      ├─ C9 = O  (ketone)
      └─ C1 = O  (lactone carbonyl), ethyl group at C13
```

- **Stereochemistry:** the aglycone has ten chiral carbons; with the two sugars erythromycin has **eighteen**. Only the natural isomer is active, which is why macrolides are made by fermentation and modified, not totally synthesised.
- The dimethylamino group of desosamine makes erythromycin a **base**; it forms salts (stearate, lactobionate) and esters (ethylsuccinate, propionate).

### Chemical degradation: why erythromycin is acid-labile

In gastric acid the **6-OH attacks the C9 ketone**, forming a hemiketal, which loses water to give **8,9-anhydro-6,9-hemiketal**; the **12-OH** then adds as well, giving the **6,9:9,12-spiroketal (anhydroerythromycin)**. Both products are inactive, and the hemiketal stimulates **motilin receptors** in the gut, causing cramps and diarrhoea.

```text
 erythromycin (9-ketone, 6-OH, 12-OH)
       │ H+ (stomach)
 6,9-hemiketal → 8,9-anhydro-6,9-hemiketal → 6,9:9,12-spiroketal
       (all inactive; motilin agonist → GI upset)
```

The answer to this problem drives the SAR of the newer drugs:

1. **Block the 6-OH:** methylating it gives **clarithromycin (6-O-methylerythromycin)** — acid stable, better absorbed, more potent.
2. **Remove the 9-ketone:** converting it to an **oxime ether** gives **roxithromycin**; converting the oxime by **Beckmann rearrangement**, reduction and N-methylation inserts a nitrogen into the ring to give **azithromycin**, a **15-membered azalide** (9-deoxo-9a-aza-9a-methyl-9a-homoerythromycin A).
3. **Protect by formulation:** enteric coating, or salts and esters (stearate, ethylsuccinate, **estolate** = lauryl sulphate salt of the 2'-propionyl ester). The estolate gives the highest blood levels but can cause **cholestatic hepatitis**.

### SAR of macrolides

1. The macrocyclic lactone (14-, 15- or 16-membered) is needed; ring size decides the subclass.
2. **The basic desosamine sugar with its dimethylamino group is essential** for ribosome binding; the 2'-OH can be esterified as a prodrug (hydrolysed in the body).
3. Removing **cladinose** and oxidising C3 to a ketone gives the **ketolides** (telithromycin), active against macrolide-resistant pneumococci.
4. Changes at **C6 and C9** give acid stability (above).

### Mechanism, uses and interactions

Macrolides bind the **23S rRNA of the 50S subunit** in the peptide exit tunnel and **block translocation**, so the growing chain cannot move along; they are **bacteriostatic** (bactericidal at high levels). Uses: atypical pneumonia (*Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydophila*, *Legionella*), whooping cough, diphtheria carriers, streptococcal infections in penicillin allergy, *Campylobacter*; clarithromycin in ***H. pylori*** regimens and *Mycobacterium avium* complex; azithromycin as a single dose for chlamydial urethritis and in trachoma, and in typhoid. Erythromycin and clarithromycin **inhibit CYP3A4** (interactions with theophylline, warfarin, statins, some antihistamines); azithromycin hardly does. Azithromycin is concentrated in tissues and phagocytes and has a very long half-life.

## 2.2 Chloramphenicol*

### History, structure and stereochemistry

Isolated in 1947 from ***Streptomyces venezuelae***, chloramphenicol was the **first antibiotic produced commercially by total synthesis**, because its structure is simple.

Chemical name: **D-(−)-threo-2-dichloroacetamido-1-(4-nitrophenyl)propane-1,3-diol**.

```text
        OH   NH–CO–CHCl2
        │    │
 O2N–C6H4–C1H–C2H–C3H2–OH
        (1R, 2R) = D-(−)-threo
```

It has **two chiral carbons, C1 and C2**, so four stereoisomers exist (D-threo, L-threo, D-erythro, L-erythro). **Only the D-(−)-threo isomer (1R,2R) is active.** It is one of the few natural products with a **nitro group** and a **dichloroacetyl** group.

### SAR of chloramphenicol

1. The **D-(−)-threo (1R,2R)** configuration is essential; the other three stereoisomers are inactive.
2. The **p-nitro group** on the benzene ring gives the best activity, but it is not unique — replacing it by methylsulphonyl gives **thiamphenicol**, which keeps activity and does not cause aplastic anaemia; a fluorine in place of the C3 hydroxyl gives **florfenicol** (veterinary).
3. The **dichloroacetamide** side chain gives maximum potency; other acyl groups reduce it.
4. Both the **C1 hydroxyl** and the **C3 primary hydroxyl** are needed for ribosome binding. The C3-OH is the esterification site used for prodrugs: the tasteless **palmitate** for children's suspensions and the water-soluble **succinate** for injection, both hydrolysed in the body.

### Mechanism, uses and toxicity

Chloramphenicol binds the **50S subunit** and inhibits **peptidyl transferase**, so the peptide bond is not formed; it is **bacteriostatic and broad spectrum**. Its use is now restricted by toxicity: **dose-independent, idiosyncratic aplastic anaemia** (rare but often fatal), dose-dependent reversible bone marrow suppression, and **grey baby syndrome** in neonates, who cannot glucuronidate it. It is reserved for typhoid in resistant cases, anaerobic brain abscess and severe rickettsial disease when tetracyclines cannot be used, and is widely used **topically in eye drops and ointments**, where systemic toxicity is not a concern.

## 2.3 Clindamycin

Clindamycin is **7(S)-chloro-7-deoxylincomycin**, a semi-synthetic **lincosamide** derived from **lincomycin** (*Streptomyces lincolnensis*). The molecule is an amide of a **propylhygric acid** (a 4-propyl-substituted proline) with an amino sugar, **methyl thiolincosaminide**.

SAR points: replacing the **7(R)-hydroxyl of lincomycin by 7(S)-chlorine** inverts that centre, raises lipophilicity and gives better absorption and potency; lengthening the alkyl chain on the pyrrolidine ring to propyl or butyl improves activity; the sulphur-linked methyl group on the sugar is needed. The 2-hydroxyl of the sugar is esterified to make **clindamycin palmitate** (oral suspension) and **phosphate** (injection, topical).

Mechanism: binds the **50S subunit** at the same site as the macrolides and blocks peptide bond formation — so macrolides and clindamycin are **mutually antagonistic** and share cross-resistance. Uses: anaerobic infections (*Bacteroides fragilis*), serious staphylococcal bone and joint infection, topical acne, and with primaquine in *Pneumocystis* pneumonia; it also suppresses toxin production in streptococcal toxic shock. Main hazard: **pseudomembranous colitis** from *Clostridioides difficile*.

## 2.4 Prodrugs

### Concept

A **prodrug** is a chemically modified, pharmacologically inactive (or much less active) form of a drug that is converted in the body — by enzymes or by chemical means — to the active drug. It is a design tool for **delivery problems, not potency problems**.

```text
  Prodrug ── (esterase / amidase / CYP / phosphatase / gut flora) ──► Active drug + carrier
```

**Types**

| Type | Idea | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carrier-linked | Active drug joined to a carrier by a bond the body cleaves; most prodrugs are esters | Chloramphenicol palmitate, erythromycin estolate, enalapril |
| Bioprecursor | No carrier; the molecule itself is metabolised to the active form | Levodopa → dopamine, cyclophosphamide, sulindac |
| Site-specific / targeted | Cleaved only where the enzyme is | Sulfasalazine (split by colonic bacteria), olsalazine |
| Mutual (codrug) | Two active drugs joined, each acting as carrier for the other | Sultamicillin (ampicillin + sulbactam), benorilate (aspirin + paracetamol) |
| Macromolecular | Drug bound to a polymer, antibody or protein | Antibody–drug conjugates |

### Applications (the examinable list)

1. **Better absorption / oral use:** pivampicillin and bacampicillin (ampicillin esters), cefuroxime axetil, enalapril (ester of enalaprilat), dipivefrine in the eye.
2. **Water solubility for injection:** chloramphenicol succinate, prednisolone sodium phosphate, fosphenytoin.
3. **Taste masking:** chloramphenicol palmitate, clindamycin palmitate.
4. **Longer action:** fluphenazine and haloperidol decanoates in oil.
5. **Reduced irritation or toxicity:** sulindac (inactive until reduced, so less gastric damage), aspirin as benorilate.
6. **Site-specific delivery:** sulfasalazine releasing 5-aminosalicylic acid in the colon; capecitabine converted to fluorouracil mainly in tumour tissue; levodopa crossing the blood–brain barrier on the amino acid carrier.
7. **Improved stability:** erythromycin as the estolate against gastric acid.
8. **Antiviral activation:** acyclovir and ganciclovir are bioprecursors activated by viral thymidine kinase (Unit 3), which is also what makes them selective.

**Ideal properties of a prodrug:** no activity of its own, a predictable and quantitative conversion in the body, a non-toxic carrier that is quickly excreted, chemical stability on the shelf, and an economical preparation.

## 2.5 Antimalarials

### Aetiology and life cycle

Malaria is caused by the protozoan ***Plasmodium***: *P. falciparum* (dangerous, cerebral malaria), *P. vivax*, *P. ovale*, *P. malariae* and *P. knowlesi*. The vector is the female ***Anopheles*** mosquito.

```text
Mosquito bite → sporozoites → LIVER (pre-erythrocytic schizogony)
          │                      │ (vivax/ovale: dormant HYPNOZOITES → relapse)
          │                 merozoites
          ▼
      BLOOD: ring → trophozoite → schizont → merozoites (fever with each cycle)
          │  some become GAMETOCYTES → taken up by the mosquito → sporogony
```

The parasite digests haemoglobin in its food vacuole and releases toxic **haem (ferriprotoporphyrin IX)**, which it detoxifies by polymerising it into insoluble **haemozoin (malaria pigment)**. Most blood schizonticides interfere with this step.

**Classification by stage of action**

| Class | Acts on | Drugs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tissue schizonticides (causal prophylactic) | Liver forms | Primaquine, pyrimethamine, proguanil |
| Hypnozoitocides (radical cure) | Dormant liver forms of vivax/ovale | **Primaquine**, tafenoquine |
| Blood schizonticides (clinical cure) | Erythrocytic stage | Chloroquine, quinine, mefloquine, amodiaquine, artemisinins, sulphadoxine–pyrimethamine |
| Gametocytocides | Sexual forms | Primaquine, artemisinins (also chloroquine for vivax) |
| Sporontocides | Development in the mosquito | Proguanil, pyrimethamine |

### Quinolines: chemistry and SAR

Quinoline is a benzene ring fused to pyridine. The antimalarials are built on three patterns: **4-quinolinemethanols** (quinine, mefloquine), **4-aminoquinolines** (chloroquine, amodiaquine) and **8-aminoquinolines** (primaquine, pamaquine).

**SAR of 4-aminoquinolines (chloroquine type)** — a favourite question:

```text
            Cl ─┐ 7-position (essential)
                ▼
        ┌───────────────┐
        │   quinoline   │
        └──4──┬─────────┘
              NH–CH(CH3)–CH2CH2CH2–N(C2H5)2
              │        side chain of 4 carbons
         4-amino nitrogen essential
```

1. The **quinoline ring** is the core; the **4-amino group** is essential.
2. A **chlorine at position 7** gives maximum activity; removing it or moving it lowers potency sharply.
3. The side chain should be a **diamine with about four carbons between the two nitrogens**; shortening or lengthening it reduces activity.
4. The **terminal nitrogen must be tertiary** and reasonably basic; this is the group that lets the drug accumulate in the acidic food vacuole.
5. Branching with a methyl on the chain (as in chloroquine) slows metabolism; the drug is used as the **racemate**, because both enantiomers are nearly equally active.
6. Replacing one ethyl of the terminal amine by a hydroxyethyl group gives **hydroxychloroquine** (less toxic, also used in rheumatoid arthritis and lupus); replacing the chain by a **p-hydroxyanilino-methyl group** gives **amodiaquine**, whose quinoneimine metabolite causes agranulocytosis and liver injury.

**Mechanism of 4-aminoquinolines.** The drug is a **weak diprotic base**; it diffuses in as the free base and becomes **protonated and trapped** in the parasite's acidic food vacuole, reaching very high concentrations. There it **binds haem and prevents its polymerisation to haemozoin**, so free haem accumulates and kills the parasite by membrane damage and oxidative stress. Resistance in *P. falciparum* is mainly due to a transporter (PfCRT) that moves the drug out of the vacuole.

Chloroquine is used as the **phosphate**. Besides malaria it is used in extra-intestinal amoebiasis, rheumatoid arthritis and lupus. Long high-dose use can cause **retinopathy**; it also causes itching (common in dark-skinned patients) and, in overdose, serious cardiac depression.

### 8-Aminoquinolines

**Primaquine** and the older **pamaquine** carry the amino side chain at **position 8** and a **methoxy group at position 6**. They are the only drugs that kill the **hypnozoites** of *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* and so prevent relapse; they are also strong **gametocytocides**. They are activated by metabolism to quinoline-quinones that generate reactive oxygen species and disturb the parasite's mitochondria.

SAR: the **6-methoxy group raises activity**; a **primary amine at the end of the chain (primaquine)** is less toxic than the **diethylamino end of pamaquine**, which is why primaquine replaced it in practice; the side chain works best with four to six carbons. Both cause **haemolysis in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency** and methaemoglobinaemia, so G6PD status matters before use. Pamaquine (plasmoquine) was the first synthetic antimalarial of this class, introduced in the 1920s, and is of historical and chemical interest rather than current use.

### Other quinoline antimalarials

| Drug | Chemistry | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quinine sulphate** | Cinchona alkaloid: quinoline with a 6-methoxy group, a secondary alcohol at C9 linked to a **quinuclidine** ring carrying a vinyl group; four chiral centres | Blood schizonticide, used for chloroquine-resistant and severe malaria; **cinchonism** (tinnitus, headache, blurred vision) and hypoglycaemia; quinidine is its diastereoisomer and an antiarrhythmic |
| **Mefloquine** | 4-Quinolinemethanol with two CF3 groups | Long half-life, weekly prophylaxis; neuropsychiatric effects |
| **Amodiaquine** | 4-Aminoquinoline with a p-hydroxyanilino side chain | Used in combination with artesunate; agranulocytosis risk |
| **Quinacrine (mepacrine) hydrochloride** | **Acridine** rather than quinoline: 6-chloro-2-methoxyacridine with the chloroquine-type side chain | Historic antimalarial, later used for giardiasis and tapeworm; stains the skin yellow |

### Biguanides and dihydrotriazines

**Proguanil (chlorguanide)** is an **aryl biguanide** with a chlorophenyl ring and an isopropyl group. It is a **bioprecursor prodrug**: the liver cyclises it to the dihydrotriazine **cycloguanil**, the active form, which inhibits **plasmodial dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)**. **Cycloguanil pamoate** is the preformed triazine as a depot salt. Because they act on folate metabolism they are slow-acting and are used mainly for prophylaxis, often with atovaquone.

### Miscellaneous antimalarials

- **Pyrimethamine** — a **2,4-diaminopyrimidine** with a chlorophenyl and an ethyl group; a selective inhibitor of plasmodial DHFR. Combined with the sulphonamide **sulphadoxine** (Unit 4) it gives **sequential blockade** of folate synthesis; also used with sulphadiazine in toxoplasmosis.
- **Artemisinin group** — **artesunate** (water-soluble hemisuccinate ester, used intravenously in severe malaria) and **artemether** (oil-soluble methyl ether, given intramuscularly or orally with lumefantrine) are derivatives of artemisinin, a **sesquiterpene lactone from *Artemisia annua*** carrying an **endoperoxide bridge**. Iron or haem in the parasite cleaves this peroxide to carbon-centred radicals that alkylate parasite proteins. They act faster than any other antimalarial and clear gametocytes; they are always given as **artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT)** to protect against resistance. India's national programme uses artesunate-based combinations for falciparum malaria.
- **Atovaquone** — a **hydroxynaphthoquinone** that blocks the parasite's mitochondrial **cytochrome bc1 complex**; given with proguanil for prophylaxis and treatment, and used in *Pneumocystis* pneumonia.

## Remember

- Erythromycin = 14-membered erythronolide + cladinose + desosamine; acid gives the inactive anhydro-hemiketal and spiroketal; clarithromycin blocks the 6-OH, azithromycin is a 15-membered azalide.
- Macrolides, chloramphenicol and clindamycin all act on the 50S subunit; macrolides block translocation, chloramphenicol blocks peptidyl transferase, clindamycin competes at the macrolide site.
- Chloramphenicol is D-(−)-threo (1R,2R); palmitate for taste, succinate for injection; aplastic anaemia and grey baby syndrome.
- Clindamycin = 7(S)-chloro-7-deoxylincomycin; anaerobes; *C. difficile* colitis.
- Prodrug types: carrier-linked, bioprecursor, site-specific, mutual, macromolecular — learn one example of each.
- 4-Aminoquinoline SAR: 4-amino group, 7-chloro, four-carbon diamine chain, basic tertiary terminal nitrogen; the drug is trapped in the food vacuole and blocks haemozoin formation.
- 8-Aminoquinolines (primaquine, pamaquine) kill hypnozoites and gametocytes; haemolysis in G6PD deficiency.
- Proguanil → cycloguanil (DHFR); pyrimethamine + sulphadoxine = sequential blockade; artemisinins work through an endoperoxide bridge and are used only in combinations.
