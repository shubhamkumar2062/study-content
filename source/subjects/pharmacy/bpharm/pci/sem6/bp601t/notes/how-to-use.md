# How to use this guide

Medicinal Chemistry III is the **anti-infective** paper plus an introduction to **drug design**. Almost every mark comes from five kinds of answer: **classification with examples**, **structure–activity relationship (SAR)**, **mechanism of action with uses**, **chemical degradation** (for the antibiotics), and the **preparation of the starred drugs**. Work unit by unit — read the notes, write the classification table from memory, draw the nucleus and number the SAR points against it, then finish the unit with the question bank and the MCQs.

**How the units here match the syllabus.** The PCI syllabus has five units and these notes keep one file per unit, in the same order and with the same contents. Unit 1 and Unit 2 both carry the heading "Antibiotics" in the syllabus; they are split exactly as the syllabus splits them (β-lactams, aminoglycosides and tetracyclines in Unit 1; macrolides, chloramphenicol and clindamycin in Unit 2), with prodrugs and antimalarials following in Unit 2 as the syllabus places them. Unit 4 is the longest in content though it carries fewer hours, because it holds four separate drug families.

**One thing these notes deliberately do not give.** Seventeen drugs carry an asterisk in this syllabus and each asterisk means "synthesis may be asked": **chloramphenicol, chloroquine, pamaquine, isoniazid, para-aminosalicylic acid, ciprofloxacin, nitrofurantoin, acyclovir, miconazole, tolnaftate, metronidazole, diethylcarbamazine citrate, mebendazole, sulfacetamide, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim and dapsone**. The notes cover their chemistry, SAR, mechanism and uses in full, but the **step-by-step preparation schemes are not reproduced here** — take them from your prescribed textbook (Lednicer's *Organic Chemistry of Drug Synthesis*, or Wilson and Gisvold), copy each as a numbered scheme into your own notebook and practise writing it from memory. That is the one part of this paper you must rehearse with a pen.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; learn the full answer with the nucleus, the SAR points and the classification table.
- **Medium** — asked every few years, or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for the objective section and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common patterns in pharmacy papers, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. **Draw the nucleus first, then number the SAR points against it.** The five that carry the most marks are the **penam and cephem nuclei**, the **tetracycline four-ring system with its upper and lower faces**, the **4-oxoquinoline-3-carboxylic acid** core, the **4-aminoquinoline** skeleton and the **sulphanilamide** skeleton.
2. **Classify by mechanism or by chemistry, never by adjectives.** Antifungals by site of action, antimalarials by stage of the parasite's life cycle, anthelmintics by worm type and mechanism, antitubercular drugs by first and second line. Examiners count the classes and the named examples.
3. **Degradation is a Unit 1 and 2 speciality.** Penicillin → penicilloic, penillic, penicillenic acid; tetracycline → 4-epi, anhydro and iso products; erythromycin → the anhydro hemiketal. Each is a ready-made five-mark answer, and each explains a storage or formulation rule.
4. **Attach one toxicity tag to each important drug.** Aminoglycoside → ear and kidney; tetracycline → teeth; chloramphenicol → aplastic anaemia and grey baby; isoniazid → neuropathy; ethambutol → optic neuritis; rifampicin → orange urine and enzyme induction; quinolone → tendon rupture; amphotericin B → kidney; metronidazole → alcohol reaction; dapsone and primaquine → haemolysis in G6PD deficiency; sulphonamide → crystalluria and Stevens–Johnson syndrome. These are one-mark gifts in Section A.
5. **Say why a prodrug exists, not just that it is one.** Chloramphenicol palmitate for taste, its succinate for injection, erythromycin estolate for acid stability, proguanil for activation to cycloguanil, isoniazid and metronidazole for selective activation inside the organism, acyclovir for activation only in infected cells.
6. **Unit 5 is the easiest place to gain marks.** Definitions (lead, pharmacophore, bioisostere, QSAR, docking), the four Lipinski numbers, the meaning of π, σ and Es with their signs, and the split-and-mix diagram are short, exact answers. Do not skip this unit because it has no drugs in it.
7. **Do not mix up look-alike SARs.** Quinolones need the 3-COOH and 4-oxo pair; sulphonamides need the free para-amino group; 4-aminoquinolines need the 7-chloro and the four-carbon diamine chain; azoles need a free basic azole nitrogen. Writing one for another loses the whole question.
8. **Link chemistry to the clinic in every answer.** Say *why* omeprazole-style enteric protection is needed for erythromycin, *why* tetracyclines stain teeth, *why* nitrofurantoin cannot treat pyelonephritis, *why* methenamine needs acidic urine, *why* ethambutol must be the (S,S) isomer. One such sentence per answer lifts the grade.
9. **This subject has a practical paper, BP607P.** Its guides are in this pack. The preparations (sulphanilamide, 7-hydroxy-4-methylcoumarin, chlorobutanol, triphenylimidazole, tolbutamide, hexamine) and the assays (isoniazid, chloroquine, metronidazole, dapsone, chlorpheniramine maleate, benzylpenicillin) also supply ready-made theory answers — the reaction type of each preparation and the titration principle of each assay are fair game in the written paper too.
