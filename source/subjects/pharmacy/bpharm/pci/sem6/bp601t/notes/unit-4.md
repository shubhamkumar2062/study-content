# Unit 4: Antifungal, antiprotozoal and anthelmintic agents; sulphonamides and sulfones

This is the busiest unit for classification questions. It holds four separate families — **antifungals, antiprotozoals, anthelmintics and the sulphonamides/sulfones** — and seven starred drugs: miconazole, tolnaftate, metronidazole, diethylcarbamazine citrate, mebendazole, sulfacetamide, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim and dapsone. Learn each family by its classification table, one mechanism line per class, and the SAR where the syllabus names it (azoles and sulphonamides). Preparations of the starred drugs come from your prescribed textbook; everything else is here.

## 4.1 Antifungal agents

### Why selective toxicity is hard

Fungi are eukaryotes, so their cells resemble ours. Only three differences are exploited: the **cell wall** (chitin and glucan, absent in man), **ergosterol** in the fungal membrane instead of cholesterol, and fungal nucleic acid enzymes.

```text
   Fungal cell wall  ← echinocandins (glucan synthase), griseofulvin (mitosis)
   Fungal membrane   ← polyenes bind ergosterol (pores)
        │               azoles block 14α-demethylase (no ergosterol)
        │               allylamines/thiocarbamates block squalene epoxidase
   Nucleus / DNA     ← flucytosine (converted to 5-fluorouracil)
```

### Antifungal antibiotics

**Amphotericin B** is a **polyene macrolide** from *Streptomyces nodosus*: a large lactone ring with a **rigid polyene (seven conjugated double bonds) on one side and a polyhydroxyl chain on the other**, plus the amino sugar **mycosamine** and a carboxyl group — so it is **amphoteric** (hence the name) and **amphipathic**. Several molecules insert into the membrane with their polyene faces against **ergosterol** and their hydroxyl faces inward, forming a **pore** through which potassium and small molecules leak out; the fungus dies. It binds cholesterol much more weakly, which gives its (limited) selectivity.

It is insoluble in water and is given intravenously as a **sodium deoxycholate colloidal dispersion** or in **liposomal and lipid-complex forms**, which reduce toxicity. It is still the standard for severe systemic mycoses — cryptococcal meningitis, disseminated candidiasis, aspergillosis, mucormycosis, and kala-azar (leishmaniasis). Toxicity is the problem: **infusion reactions** (fever, rigors) and **dose-related nephrotoxicity with potassium and magnesium loss**, so renal function and electrolytes are watched.

**Nystatin** is a similar polyene (from *Streptomyces noursei*) that is too toxic for injection; it is used **topically and orally for candidiasis of the mouth, gut and vagina** — the oral dose is not absorbed. **Natamycin** is a polyene used mainly as **5% eye drops for fungal keratitis**. 

**Griseofulvin** (from *Penicillium griseofulvum*) is a **benzofuran** derivative, not a polyene. It is **fungistatic against dermatophytes only**. It binds **tubulin and disrupts the mitotic spindle**, and it is deposited in newly formed **keratin**, so the drug works only as fresh, protected skin, hair and nail grow out — treatment therefore lasts weeks for skin and months for nails. Absorption is better from the **microsize or ultramicrosize powders with a fatty meal**. It **induces CYP enzymes** (so it lowers the effect of oral contraceptives and warfarin) and causes headache and a disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.

### Azoles: structure, SAR and mechanism

All azoles share a pattern:

```text
   Ar (halogenated phenyl) ─ C ─ CH2 ─ N  [imidazole or 1,2,4-triazole]
                             │
                        O or further aryl / heterocycle
   • The basic nitrogen (N3 of imidazole, N4 of triazole) binds the haem iron
   • 2,4-dichlorophenyl or 2,4-difluorophenyl ring gives the best potency
```

**Mechanism.** The unshared electron pair of the azole nitrogen binds the **iron of the haem** in the fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme **14α-demethylase (CYP51)**. Lanosterol is then not demethylated, **ergosterol is not made**, and toxic methylated sterols build up, so the membrane becomes leaky and growth stops. They are mainly **fungistatic**.

**SAR points:** a **weakly basic imidazole or triazole ring** is essential and must not be substituted at the nitrogen that binds iron; **halogens (Cl, F) at the 2 and 4 positions** of the attached phenyl ring raise potency and metabolic stability; replacing **imidazole by triazole** lowers affinity for *human* P450 enzymes, which is why triazoles cause fewer endocrine effects and interactions; increasing lipophilicity (itraconazole) improves tissue and nail penetration but reduces water solubility, while fluconazole's two triazole rings make it **water-soluble, well absorbed and able to enter the CSF**.

| Drug | Points |
| --- | --- |
| **Clotrimazole** | Topical for ringworm, candidiasis; oral troches for thrush |
| **Econazole, butoconazole, oxiconazole, tioconazole, terconazole** | Topical and vaginal preparations |
| **Miconazole*** | Topical cream, powder and vaginal forms; also an oral gel; a starred drug |
| **Ketoconazole** | The first oral azole; now limited by **hepatotoxicity** and by blocking human steroid synthesis (gynaecomastia, low testosterone); still used topically and in shampoo, and occasionally in Cushing's syndrome for its enzyme block |
| **Fluconazole** | Water-soluble, long half-life, excellent CSF levels; candidiasis and cryptococcal meningitis |
| **Itraconazole** | Broad, including *Aspergillus*; needs acid for absorption; negative inotropic effect |
| **Voriconazole, posaconazole** | Reserved for invasive aspergillosis and mucormycosis |

**Interactions to quote:** azoles inhibit CYP3A4, raising levels of statins, warfarin, cyclosporin and some antihistamines; rifampicin lowers azole levels.

### Allylamines and thiocarbamates

**Naftifine hydrochloride** and **terbinafine** are **allylamines**; **tolnaftate*** is a **thiocarbamate** (O-2-naphthyl N-methyl-N-(3-tolyl)thiocarbamate). Both groups inhibit **squalene epoxidase**, the first enzyme of ergosterol synthesis. Two things follow: ergosterol is not made, **and squalene accumulates**, which is itself toxic to the fungal membrane — so these drugs are **fungicidal** against dermatophytes, unlike the fungistatic azoles. Tolnaftate and naftifine are topical (ringworm, athlete's foot); terbinafine is also oral and is the drug of choice for **onychomycosis**. Tolnaftate does not act on *Candida*.

## 4.2 Antiprotozoal agents

### 5-Nitroimidazoles

**Metronidazole***, **tinidazole** and **ornidazole** share a **5-nitroimidazole** nucleus with a 1-(2-hydroxyethyl) or similar side chain and a 2-methyl group.

```text
       CH3 ─┐  2-position
   ┌────────┴──┐
   │ imidazole │ ── N1 – CH2CH2OH   (metronidazole)
   └────┬──────┘
       NO2   5-position (essential)
```

**Mechanism:** these are **prodrugs activated by anaerobic metabolism**. Inside anaerobic protozoa and bacteria, low-redox-potential proteins such as **ferredoxin** reduce the **5-nitro group** to a nitro radical anion and other reactive species, which break DNA strands. Aerobic human cells cannot carry out this reduction, so the drug is selective. Activity therefore needs the **nitro group at position 5**; substituting position 1 changes only the pharmacokinetics (tinidazole and ornidazole act longer).

**Uses:** amoebiasis (intestinal and liver abscess), giardiasis, trichomoniasis, bacterial vaginosis, anaerobic bacterial infections (*Bacteroides*, *Clostridioides difficile*), and as part of ***H. pylori*** regimens. **Adverse effects:** metallic taste, nausea, dark urine, peripheral neuropathy on long use, and a **disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol**, which patients must be warned about.

## 4.3 Anthelmintics

Worms are classified as **nematodes (roundworms)**, **cestodes (tapeworms)** and **trematodes (flukes)**. Anthelmintics either **paralyse** the worm (so it is expelled) or **starve** it by blocking glucose uptake or energy metabolism.

| Drug | Class | Mechanism | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Diethylcarbamazine citrate*** | Piperazine derivative (1-diethylcarbamoyl-4-methylpiperazine) | Alters the microfilarial surface so the host's own immune cells destroy them; also blocks arachidonic acid metabolism in the worm | **Filariasis** — the drug of choice; also tropical pulmonary eosinophilia and loiasis. Dying microfilariae cause fever and itching (Mazzotti-type reaction) |
| **Thiabendazole** | Benzimidazole | Blocks fumarate reductase and microtubules | Strongyloidiasis, larva migrans; largely replaced because of side effects |
| **Mebendazole*** | Benzimidazole carbamate | Binds **β-tubulin** of the worm, stops microtubule assembly and **glucose uptake**, so the worm is starved of energy | Roundworm, hookworm, whipworm, pinworm; poorly absorbed, so it acts in the gut |
| **Albendazole** | Benzimidazole carbamate | Same; better absorbed, active metabolite albendazole sulphoxide | Same worms plus **tissue cysts** (neurocysticercosis, hydatid disease); used in mass deworming programmes |
| **Niclosamide** | Salicylanilide | Uncouples oxidative phosphorylation in the tapeworm's mitochondria; kills the scolex | **Tapeworms**; not absorbed |
| **Praziquantel** | Pyrazinoisoquinoline | Opens calcium channels in the worm's membrane → sustained contraction and tegument damage | **Schistosomiasis (flukes)** and tapeworms, including neurocysticercosis |
| **Oxamniquine** | Tetrahydroquinoline | Alkylates worm DNA after activation by a worm sulphotransferase | *Schistosoma mansoni* |
| **Ivermectin** | Semi-synthetic **macrocyclic lactone** (avermectin) | Opens **glutamate-gated chloride channels** of invertebrates → paralysis; mammals are spared because these channels are confined to the CNS behind the blood–brain barrier | Onchocerciasis, strongyloidiasis, filariasis, scabies and head lice |

**Benzimidazole SAR in one line:** the **2-carbamate group** on the benzimidazole ring is essential for tubulin binding, and a **lipophilic substituent at position 5** (benzoyl in mebendazole, propylthio in albendazole) governs potency and absorption.

## 4.4 Sulphonamides

### Historical development

In 1932 Domagk found that the red azo dye **Prontosil** cured streptococcal infection in mice but did nothing to bacteria in a test tube. French workers then showed that the body **reduces the azo bond** to release **sulphanilamide**, the real drug — the first clear example of a **bioprecursor prodrug** and the beginning of modern antibacterial chemotherapy.

```text
   Prontosil (azo dye, inactive in vitro)
        │  azoreductase in the body
   SULPHANILAMIDE  =  p-aminobenzenesulphonamide
        H2N ─C6H4─ SO2NH2
```

Woods and Fildes then explained how it works: sulphanilamide is an **antimetabolite of p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA)**, which they showed by demonstrating that added PABA reverses the drug's effect — **competitive antagonism**.

### Mechanism

Bacteria must **make their own folic acid**; man takes it in the diet. Sulphonamides resemble PABA closely in size and charge distribution, so they compete for **dihydropteroate synthase** and are even incorporated to give a useless pteroate analogue. Folate, and then thymidine and purines, are not made, and the bacteria stop dividing — the action is **bacteriostatic**. Because the block can be overcome by excess PABA, pus and tissue breakdown products (rich in PABA) reduce their effect; procaine, which releases PABA, antagonises them.

**Sequential blockade.** **Trimethoprim*** blocks the *next* enzyme, **dihydrofolate reductase**, with a very high selectivity for the bacterial enzyme (thousands of times greater affinity than for the human one). Giving the two together blocks two consecutive steps of the same pathway, which turns a bacteriostatic effect into a **bactericidal** one and slows resistance. The combination of **sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim (1 part trimethoprim to 5 parts sulphonamide in the tablet) is co-trimoxazole**; the two were paired because their half-lives match.

### SAR of sulphonamides

```text
          H2N ─┤ benzene ├─ SO2 ─ NH ─ R
           N4                S     N1
```

1. The **para-amino group (N4) must be free** or easily converted to a free amine in the body. If it is blocked the drug is inactive — this is why **prontosil** and **sulfasalazine** work only after cleavage, and why N4-acetylated metabolites are inactive.
2. The **amino and sulphonyl groups must be para** to each other; ortho and meta isomers are inactive.
3. The **benzene ring must not carry other substituents**; any extra group lowers activity.
4. The **sulphonamide nitrogen (N1) should carry one substituent**, best of all a **heterocyclic ring**; disubstitution abolishes activity.
5. The **N1 substituent controls acidity, solubility and duration**. The N1-H must be acidic (pKa about 6–7.5) so that the drug is largely ionised at blood pH — the ionised form binds the enzyme; too acidic or too weak and activity falls.
6. **Sulphur can be replaced by other groups only with loss of potency**; the SO2NH link is optimal.

### Classification and products

| Group | Members | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short acting | **Sulfisoxazole**, **sulphamethizole**, **sulphadiazine** | Urinary infections; sulphadiazine with pyrimethamine in toxoplasmosis |
| Intermediate acting | **Sulfamethoxazole*** | Partner of trimethoprim in co-trimoxazole |
| Long acting | **Sulphadoxine**, sulfamethopyrazine | With pyrimethamine for malaria |
| Topical | **Sulfacetamide*** (eye drops), **mafenide acetate** and silver sulphadiazine (burns) | Sulfacetamide is very water-soluble as the sodium salt and non-irritant to the eye; mafenide penetrates eschar but can cause acidosis because it inhibits carbonic anhydrase |
| Gut-acting / special | **Sulfasalazine** (sulphapyridine linked by an azo bond to 5-aminosalicylic acid) | Split by colonic bacteria; the 5-ASA part treats ulcerative colitis — a site-specific prodrug |
| Non-antibacterial sulphonamide relatives | Acetazolamide, thiazides, sulphonylureas, celecoxib | Same SO2NH2 group, different targets — worth one line in an essay |

**Uses:** urinary tract infection, nocardiosis, toxoplasmosis, *Pneumocystis* pneumonia (high-dose co-trimoxazole), trachoma and conjunctivitis (sulfacetamide), burns (silver sulphadiazine), ulcerative colitis (sulfasalazine), and in malaria combinations.

**Adverse effects:** **crystalluria** (the older, poorly soluble, acetylated metabolites crystallise in acid urine — prevented by plenty of fluid and alkalisation), hypersensitivity rashes including **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**, blood dyscrasias, **haemolysis in G6PD deficiency**, and **kernicterus in neonates** because sulphonamides displace bilirubin from albumin — so they are avoided in late pregnancy and in the newborn.

### Trimethoprim*

**2,4-Diamino-5-(3,4,5-trimethoxybenzyl)pyrimidine.** The **2,4-diaminopyrimidine** head mimics the pteridine of dihydrofolate; the **trimethoxybenzyl** group fits a pocket that differs between bacterial and human dihydrofolate reductase, giving its selectivity. Related compounds: pyrimethamine (antimalarial, Unit 2) and methotrexate (an anticancer drug that blocks the *human* enzyme). Adverse effects reflect folate antagonism — megaloblastic anaemia with prolonged use, prevented or treated with folinic acid.

## 4.5 Sulfones

Sulfones have the general structure **Ar–SO2–Ar**, in contrast to the **Ar–SO2–N** of sulphonamides.

**Dapsone*** = **4,4'-diaminodiphenylsulfone (DDS)**: two p-aminophenyl rings joined by a sulphone bridge. Like the sulphonamides it is a **PABA antagonist** and blocks dihydropteroate synthase, so it is **bacteriostatic** against *Mycobacterium leprae*.

SAR: **both para-amino groups are needed** for full activity; blocking them gives prodrugs that must be converted back in the body (for example acedapsone, a long-acting repository form). The sulphone bridge is essential — replacing it lowers activity.

**Uses:** the backbone of **multidrug therapy for leprosy** (with rifampicin and clofazimine, as in India's national programme — multidrug therapy is used to prevent the dapsone resistance that appeared with monotherapy); also dermatitis herpetiformis and, with pyrimethamine, in *Pneumocystis* prophylaxis.

**Adverse effects:** dose-related **haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia** (much worse in G6PD deficiency), agranulocytosis, and the **"sulfone syndrome"** (fever, rash, hepatitis and lymphadenopathy appearing some weeks after starting). Related drugs: **clofazimine**, a riminophenazine dye that also has an anti-inflammatory action and colours the skin red-brown.

## Remember

- Polyenes bind ergosterol and make pores; azoles block 14α-demethylase; allylamines and tolnaftate block squalene epoxidase; griseofulvin binds tubulin and deposits in keratin.
- Azole SAR: a free basic azole nitrogen to bind haem iron, 2,4-dihalophenyl ring, triazole for fewer human P450 effects.
- Metronidazole and its relatives are prodrugs needing anaerobic reduction of the **5-nitro** group; disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.
- Diloxanide furoate and iodoquinol clear cysts in the lumen; pentamidine and eflornithine for trypanosomes.
- Mebendazole and albendazole bind worm β-tubulin and block glucose uptake; niclosamide uncouples oxidative phosphorylation; praziquantel opens calcium channels; ivermectin opens glutamate-gated chloride channels; diethylcarbamazine is the filaricide.
- Prontosil → sulphanilamide was the first prodrug; sulphonamides are PABA antagonists blocking dihydropteroate synthase.
- Sulphonamide SAR: free para-NH2, para arrangement, unsubstituted ring, one N1 substituent (best heterocyclic), acidic N1-H.
- Co-trimoxazole = sulfamethoxazole + trimethoprim = sequential blockade = bactericidal; dapsone is 4,4'-diaminodiphenylsulfone, the backbone of leprosy multidrug therapy.
