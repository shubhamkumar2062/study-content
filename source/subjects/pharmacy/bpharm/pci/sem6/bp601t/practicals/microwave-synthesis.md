# Preparation of a medicinally important compound by microwave irradiation

**Aim:** To prepare a medicinally important compound or intermediate by microwave-assisted synthesis, to compare the reaction time and yield with the conventional method, and to report the result.

> Use only a laboratory microwave reactor, or the domestic-type oven provided and modified by the department, under the direct supervision of the teacher. Never seal a vessel, never use a metal container, and never heat a flammable solvent in an unmodified domestic oven. Goggles, a lab coat and gloves are compulsory.

## Principle

In a conventional reaction the heat travels from a flame or mantle through the vessel wall and then into the mixture, so heating is slow and uneven. **Microwave heating is direct and volumetric:** microwaves at 2450 MHz interact with the reaction mixture itself through two mechanisms.

1. **Dipolar polarisation** — polar molecules (water, ethanol, dimethylformamide, the reactants themselves) try to align with the rapidly oscillating electric field; the molecular friction that results produces heat throughout the bulk.
2. **Ionic conduction** — ions in the mixture migrate back and forth with the field and generate heat by collision.

```text
Conventional:   mantle → glass wall → outer layer → bulk    (slow, uneven, wall hottest)
Microwave:      field  → whole bulk at once                  (fast, uniform, superheating)
```

Because the solvent can be superheated above its normal boiling point in a closed-vessel reactor, reactions that take hours under reflux are often complete in **minutes**, usually with a **cleaner product and a better yield** because there is less time for side reactions. Reactions can also be run with very little solvent or with none at all, on a solid support, which is why microwave chemistry is described as a **green chemistry** technique.

**Suitable exercises** (your department will set one): the Pechmann condensation to 7-hydroxy-4-methylcoumarin, the Radziszewski synthesis of 2,4,5-triphenylimidazole, a Knoevenagel or Claisen-Schmidt condensation to a chalcone, the preparation of aspirin or of paracetamol by acetylation, or the formation of a benzimidazole from o-phenylenediamine and a carboxylic acid.

## Requirements

Laboratory microwave reactor (or departmental modified oven with a vented reaction vessel), the reactants and catalyst for the chosen preparation, a polar solvent or solid support as directed, open or vented borosilicate vessel, thermometer or the reactor's temperature probe, melting point apparatus, thin-layer chromatography plates and chamber, Buchner funnel and flask.

## Procedure (outline, as directed by the teacher)

1. Weigh the reactants accurately and mix them with the minimum quantity of the chosen polar solvent, or grind them with the solid support if a solvent-free method is used.
2. Transfer the mixture to the reaction vessel, **leaving it open or properly vented**, and place it in the microwave cavity.
3. Irradiate at the power and for the time set by the teacher, in **short bursts** (for example 30 to 60 seconds), removing the vessel and swirling it between bursts to even out the temperature and to avoid bumping.
4. Follow the progress of the reaction by thin-layer chromatography against the starting material; stop when the spot of the starting material has disappeared.
5. Cool the vessel to room temperature before opening or handling it further.
6. Work up the mixture as in the conventional method — usually by pouring into cold water or ice, filtering the solid and washing it.
7. Recrystallise from a suitable solvent, dry, weigh and determine the melting point.
8. Repeat the same preparation by the conventional method, or use the class results, so that the two can be compared.

```text
Percentage yield = (practical yield ÷ theoretical yield) × 100
Theoretical yield (g) = moles of limiting reactant × molecular weight of product
```

**Worked example.** A preparation with a theoretical yield of 2.96 g gives 2.35 g by the microwave method in 6 minutes, that is 79.4 per cent, against 2.10 g in 90 minutes by refluxing, that is 70.9 per cent. The microwave method is therefore both faster and slightly better yielding, and both products melt at the same temperature.

## Precautions

1. **Never heat a sealed vessel** in a domestic-type oven; pressure builds up and the vessel can burst. Use an open or vented vessel, or a purpose-built pressure-rated reactor with its own controls.
2. Never place metal, metal foil or a metal spatula in the cavity; arcing and fire can result.
3. Irradiate in short bursts and swirl between them; continuous irradiation causes local overheating, bumping and loss of material.
4. Avoid low-boiling flammable solvents such as diethyl ether and petroleum ether.
5. Allow the vessel to cool before handling, as superheated liquid can boil over violently when disturbed.
6. Use a polar solvent or a polar reactant; non-polar media absorb microwaves poorly and are not heated.
7. Keep the quantity small and never leave the oven unattended while it is running.

## Viva questions

1. **How does microwave heating differ from conventional heating?** Microwaves heat the mixture itself throughout its volume by dipolar polarisation and ionic conduction, whereas conventional heating passes through the vessel wall by conduction and convection.
2. **What frequency is used?** 2450 MHz, which corresponds to a wavelength of about 12.2 cm.
3. **Why are polar solvents preferred?** They couple efficiently with the microwave field; non-polar solvents such as toluene or hexane absorb very little energy and heat poorly.
4. **State three advantages of microwave synthesis.** Reaction times reduced from hours to minutes, better yields with fewer side products, and the possibility of solvent-free reactions, which makes it a green chemistry technique.
5. **State two limitations.** Not every reaction benefits, scaling up is difficult, there is a risk of pressure build-up with volatile solvents, and the temperature inside the vessel is harder to measure accurately.
6. **Why must the vessel be vented?** To release the vapour formed by rapid heating and to prevent a dangerous pressure rise.
7. **How is the progress of the reaction monitored?** By thin-layer chromatography against the starting material, and finally by the melting point and the yield of the purified product.
