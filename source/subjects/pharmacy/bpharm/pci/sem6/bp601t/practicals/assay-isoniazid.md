# Assay of isoniazid (isonicotinic acid hydrazide) by bromatometric titration

**Aim:** To determine the percentage purity of a given sample of isoniazid by titration with standard potassium bromate.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your own lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles, a lab coat and gloves; bromine vapour is irritant, so work in a fume cupboard.

## Principle

Isoniazid is the hydrazide of pyridine-4-carboxylic acid and is a **reducing agent** because of its hydrazide group. In acid solution, potassium bromate and potassium bromide generate bromine, which oxidises the hydrazide to isonicotinic acid with loss of nitrogen:

```text
BrO3− + 5 Br− + 6 H+ → 3 Br2 + 3 H2O

Pyridine-4-CO-NH-NH2 + 2 Br2 + H2O → pyridine-4-COOH + N2 + 4 HBr
```

Each molecule of isoniazid therefore consumes **two molecules of bromine**, that is four equivalents of oxidising power. The end point is shown by methyl red or methyl orange, which the first excess of free bromine bleaches irreversibly, so the colour change is from red (or pink) to colourless.

**Equivalent factor:** 1 ml of 0.0167 M (0.1 N) potassium bromate ≡ **0.003429 g of isoniazid** (molecular weight 137.14; each mole reacts with 4 equivalents, so the equivalent weight is 137.14 ÷ 4 = 34.29).

## Requirements

Isoniazid sample, 0.0167 M (0.1 N) potassium bromate (standardised), potassium bromide, concentrated hydrochloric acid, methyl red (or methyl orange) indicator, distilled water, burette, pipette, conical flask, volumetric flask, analytical balance.

## Procedure

1. Weigh accurately about 0.25 g of the isoniazid sample, dissolve it in distilled water and make up to 100 ml in a volumetric flask. Mix well.
2. Pipette 25 ml of this solution into a conical flask.
3. Add about 2 g of potassium bromide and 20 ml of dilute hydrochloric acid, and dilute to about 100 ml with distilled water.
4. Add 2 to 3 drops of methyl red solution.
5. Titrate slowly with 0.0167 M (0.1 N) potassium bromate from the burette, swirling continuously, until the red colour is discharged and the solution becomes colourless. Add the last portions dropwise.
6. Note the burette reading and repeat until two concordant readings are obtained.
7. Perform a blank titration in the same way, omitting the sample, and subtract the blank from each reading.

Mean corrected volume of 0.0167 M KBrO3 = V ml.

```text
Isoniazid in 25 ml aliquot (g) = V × 0.003429 × (molarity factor)
Isoniazid in 100 ml           = that value × 4
% purity = (isoniazid found × 100) / weight of sample taken
```

**Worked example.** Sample weight 0.2500 g, made up to 100 ml; 25 ml aliquot consumed 18.2 ml of 0.0167 M (0.1 N) potassium bromate.

- Isoniazid in the aliquot = 18.2 × 0.003429 = 0.0624 g
- Isoniazid in 100 ml = 0.0624 × 4 = 0.2496 g
- Percentage purity = (0.2496 ÷ 0.2500) × 100 = **99.8 % w/w**

## Result

The given sample of isoniazid contains ______ % w/w of isonicotinic acid hydrazide. (The Indian Pharmacopoeia limit for isoniazid is 98.0 to 101.0 % of C6H7N3O, calculated on the dried basis; compare your value with the monograph limit.)

## Precautions

1. Titrate slowly near the end point, because the oxidation of the hydrazide is not instantaneous.
2. Keep the solution cold and swirl well; bromine is volatile and loss gives a low result.
3. The indicator is destroyed irreversibly, so the end point cannot be overshot and re-checked — approach it dropwise.
4. Always run and subtract a blank, since the acid and bromide may carry traces of oxidisable matter.
5. Work in a fume cupboard; bromine vapour irritates the eyes and the respiratory tract.
6. Standardise the potassium bromate solution before use, or use a freshly prepared standard solution.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is potassium bromide added?** Bromate alone reacts slowly; with bromide in acid it liberates bromine, which is the actual oxidising species formed in situ.
2. **Why is the indicator called irreversible?** Methyl red is oxidised and bleached by the first excess of bromine, so the colour cannot be restored by adding more analyte.
3. **What is the chemical name of isoniazid?** Pyridine-4-carboxylic acid hydrazide, that is isonicotinic acid hydrazide.
4. **What is the oxidation product of isoniazid in this titration?** Isonicotinic acid, with liberation of nitrogen gas.
5. **Name two other drugs assayed bromatometrically.** Phenol, sodium salicylate, isoniazid and metronidazole-type nitro compounds after reduction are classical examples; ascorbic acid is assayed by a similar oxidation.
6. **Why is a blank titration necessary?** To correct for the small volume of bromate consumed by the reagents themselves and for the amount needed to produce the visible excess.
7. **What type of titration is this?** A redox titration, more precisely a bromatometric assay with internal generation of bromine.
