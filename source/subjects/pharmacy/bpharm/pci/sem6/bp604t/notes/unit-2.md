# Unit 2: Elimination; bioavailability and bioequivalence

Once a drug is in the body it is removed by **elimination** — chemical change (metabolism) and removal of the drug or its products (excretion). The second half of this unit explains **bioavailability**, how it is measured, how dissolution testing predicts it, and how two products of the same drug are shown to be equivalent.

## 2.1 Elimination — an overview

**Elimination** = metabolism (biotransformation) + excretion. It is the irreversible loss of drug from the body.

**Elimination rate constant (KE or k)** — fraction of drug removed per unit time (unit h⁻¹). For a first-order process, KE = Km + Ke (metabolism + excretion), and t½ = 0.693/KE.

**Clearance (CL)** — the volume of plasma (or blood) completely cleared of drug per unit time (L/h or mL/min). Total body clearance is the sum of organ clearances: CLT = CLR + CLH + CLother.

## 2.2 Drug metabolism and basic metabolic pathways

**Drug metabolism (biotransformation)** is the chemical conversion of a drug into another compound (metabolite), mainly to make lipophilic drugs more polar so they can be excreted in urine or bile.

**Possible outcomes of metabolism**

| Outcome | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Active drug → inactive metabolite (most common) | Phenobarbital → hydroxyphenobarbital |
| Active drug → active metabolite | Diazepam → desmethyldiazepam (oxazepam); codeine → morphine |
| Inactive prodrug → active drug | Levodopa → dopamine; enalapril → enalaprilat |
| Drug → toxic metabolite | Paracetamol → N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (in overdose) |

**Sites:** liver (main site, smooth endoplasmic reticulum — "microsomal" enzymes), gut wall, kidney, lungs, plasma, skin, placenta.

### Phase I (functionalisation) reactions

Introduce or expose a polar group (–OH, –NH2, –COOH, –SH). Products may stay active.

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Oxidation (most common; cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, CYP3A4 handles about half of drugs) | Aromatic and aliphatic hydroxylation, N- and O-dealkylation, N-oxidation, S-oxidation, deamination |
| Reduction | Nitro reduction (chloramphenicol), azo reduction (prontosil → sulfanilamide), carbonyl reduction |
| Hydrolysis | Esters (procaine, aspirin by esterases), amides (lidocaine, procainamide) |
| Others | Cyclisation, decyclisation |

### Phase II (conjugation) reactions

Join the drug or its phase I metabolite to an endogenous molecule, giving large, polar, usually inactive products.

| Conjugation | Enzyme | Example substrates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Glucuronidation (most common) | UDP-glucuronosyltransferase | Morphine, paracetamol, chloramphenicol |
| Sulfation | Sulfotransferase | Paracetamol, steroids |
| Acetylation | N-acetyltransferase (genetic fast and slow acetylators) | Isoniazid, sulfonamides, hydralazine |
| Methylation | Methyltransferases | Catecholamines, histamine |
| Glycine conjugation | — | Salicylic acid, benzoic acid |
| Glutathione conjugation | Glutathione-S-transferase | Reactive paracetamol metabolite (detoxified) |

**Factors affecting metabolism:** age (neonates and elderly metabolise slowly), sex, genetics (acetylator status, CYP2D6 poor metabolisers), diet, disease (liver cirrhosis, heart failure), **enzyme induction** (rifampicin, phenobarbital, phenytoin, smoking — faster metabolism, possible therapy failure) and **enzyme inhibition** (cimetidine, ketoconazole, erythromycin, grapefruit juice — slower metabolism, possible toxicity).

**First-pass (presystemic) metabolism** removes drug in the gut wall and liver before it reaches the systemic circulation after oral dosing. Hepatic extraction ratio E = (Cin − Cout)/Cin; oral F ≤ 1 − E. High-extraction drugs (propranolol, lidocaine, glyceryl trinitrate) have low oral bioavailability.

## 2.3 Renal excretion of drugs

The kidney handles drugs by three processes. Net renal excretion:

**Rate of excretion = Rate of filtration + Rate of secretion − Rate of reabsorption**

```text
Blood in (afferent arteriole)
   │
 [Glomerulus] ── filtration of FREE drug (≈125 mL/min) ──►
   │                                                     Tubule
 Peritubular capillary ── active secretion (proximal) ──►  │
   │  ◄────────────── passive reabsorption (distal) ──────┘
   ▼                                                       ▼
Renal vein                                               Urine
```

**1. Glomerular filtration**

- Passive, non-selective process through the glomerular capillary pores.
- Only **unbound** drug of molecular weight below about 20,000 (proteins are not filtered).
- Rate depends on GFR (about 125 mL/min or 180 L/day in a healthy adult) and the free fraction: filtration clearance = fu × GFR.
- GFR is measured with **inulin** (filtered only) or estimated from **creatinine clearance**.

**2. Active tubular secretion**

- In the proximal tubule; carrier-mediated, needs energy, saturable, shows competition.
- Separate systems for **anions** (penicillins, probenecid, salicylates, furosemide) and **cations** (morphine, quinine, procainamide).
- Protein binding does not stop it (the carrier strips drug from protein).
- **Probenecid** competes with penicillin for secretion and prolongs its action.
- PAH (para-aminohippuric acid) is filtered and almost completely secreted; its clearance measures renal plasma flow (about 650 mL/min).

**3. Tubular reabsorption**

- Mainly passive diffusion of the un-ionised, lipophilic form in the distal tubule (water is reabsorbed, so drug concentration in the tubule rises).
- Depends on **urine pH** and drug pKa: acidic urine favours reabsorption of weak acids; alkaline urine favours reabsorption of weak bases.
- Used in poisoning: alkalinising urine with sodium bicarbonate speeds excretion of weak acids (salicylates, phenobarbital); acidifying urine speeds excretion of weak bases (amphetamine).
- Active reabsorption occurs for endogenous substances (glucose, vitamins, electrolytes) and a few drugs (lithium with sodium).

## 2.4 Factors affecting renal excretion of drugs

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Physicochemical properties | Polar, ionised, small, water-soluble drugs are excreted well; lipophilic drugs are reabsorbed |
| Plasma protein binding | Only free drug is filtered; high binding reduces filtration |
| Urine pH | Changes ionisation and so reabsorption (see above) |
| Urine flow rate | Diuretics or high fluid intake reduce reabsorption of drugs reabsorbed passively |
| Renal blood flow | Affects drugs cleared by secretion (high extraction) |
| Biological factors | Age (GFR low at birth, falls with age), sex, body size |
| Drug interactions | Competition for secretion (probenecid–penicillin), change of urine pH, diuretics |
| Disease states | Renal failure, uraemia, hypertension and heart failure lower GFR; dose of renally cleared drugs (aminoglycosides, digoxin) must be reduced |

## 2.5 Renal clearance

**Renal clearance (CLR)** is the volume of plasma from which drug is completely removed by the kidney per unit time:

CLR = Rate of urinary excretion / Plasma drug concentration = (dXu/dt) / C

Also CLR = fe × CLT (fe = fraction excreted unchanged) and CLR = ke × Vd (ke = urinary excretion rate constant).

**Clearance ratio** (CLR of drug ÷ GFR, for unbound drug):

| Ratio | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| = 1 | Only filtration (inulin) |
| Less than 1 | Filtration with some reabsorption |
| More than 1 | Filtration plus active secretion (penicillin, PAH) |

**Worked example (example numbers).** A urine sample collected from 2 h to 4 h after a dose contains 36 mg of unchanged drug. The plasma concentration at the midpoint (3 h) is 3 mg/L.

- Excretion rate = 36 mg / 2 h = 18 mg/h
- CLR = 18 mg/h ÷ 3 mg/L = **6 L/h = 100 mL/min**
- If the drug is not protein bound, 100 mL/min is below a GFR of 125 mL/min, so some drug is reabsorbed.

**Creatinine clearance (estimate of GFR) — Cockcroft–Gault equation**

CLcr (mL/min) = [(140 − age) × body weight (kg)] / [72 × serum creatinine (mg/dL)], × 0.85 for women.

Example (example numbers): man, 60 years, 70 kg, serum creatinine 1.2 mg/dL → CLcr = (80 × 70)/(72 × 1.2) = **64.8 mL/min**; a woman with the same values → 55.1 mL/min.

**Dose adjustment in renal failure.** For a drug cleared mostly by the kidney, the dose or dosing interval is adjusted in proportion to the patient's clearance compared with normal (for example, if CLcr is half normal and the drug is almost entirely renally cleared, give about half the dose at the same interval, or the same dose at twice the interval).

## 2.6 Non-renal routes of drug excretion

| Route | Key points | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Biliary excretion | Polar, high molecular weight (above about 500) compounds and glucuronide conjugates are secreted actively into bile | Erythromycin, rifampicin, conjugates of morphine and chloramphenicol |
| Enterohepatic circulation | Conjugate in bile is hydrolysed by gut bacteria; free drug is reabsorbed; prolongs action and gives a second peak | Oral contraceptives, digitoxin, indomethacin; broad-spectrum antibiotics that kill gut flora can reduce it |
| Pulmonary | Gases and volatile substances leave in expired air | General anaesthetics, alcohol (breath test) |
| Salivary | Passive diffusion of un-ionised drug; saliva level may reflect free plasma level (used for monitoring) | Phenytoin, lithium, caffeine |
| Mammary (milk) | Milk is slightly acidic (about pH 6.6–7), so weak bases concentrate in milk; important for the nursing infant | Morphine, nicotine, lithium, tetracycline |
| Skin (sweat) | Passive diffusion | Benzoic acid, salicylic acid, iodides, heavy metals |
| Gastrointestinal | Drugs pass from blood into gut (and are trapped) | Nicotine, quinine; oral charcoal adsorbs them |
| Genital secretions, hair, tears | Minor | Arsenic in hair (forensic) |

## 2.7 Bioavailability — definition and objectives

**Bioavailability (F)** is the **rate and extent** to which the active drug is absorbed from a dosage form and becomes available in the systemic circulation (or at the site of action).

**Objectives of bioavailability studies**

1. Development of new formulations and choice of the best one.
2. Finding the influence of excipients, manufacturing and storage on absorption.
3. Quality control and checking batch-to-batch consistency.
4. Comparing a generic product with the innovator product (bioequivalence).
5. Deciding the dose and dosage regimen; checking food and drug interactions.
6. Regulatory approval of new and generic products.

**Absolute bioavailability** compares an extravascular product with an **IV** dose (taken as 100% available):

F = (AUCoral × DIV) / (AUCIV × Doral)

**Relative (comparative) bioavailability** compares two extravascular products, one of which is a standard (often an oral solution or the innovator product):

Frel = (AUCtest × Dstd) / (AUCstd × Dtest)

**Worked examples (example numbers)**

- IV 100 mg gives AUC 40 mg·h/L; an oral tablet of 250 mg gives AUC 70 mg·h/L. F = (70 × 100)/(40 × 250) = **0.70 (70%)**.
- A test tablet gives AUC 85 mg·h/L and an oral solution of the same dose gives 100 mg·h/L. Frel = 85/100 = **0.85 (85%)**.

## 2.8 Measurement of bioavailability

### A. Pharmacokinetic methods (indirect but most used)

**1. Plasma level–time studies (best method).** Blood samples after a single dose give three key parameters:

| Parameter | Tells about | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cmax (peak plasma concentration) | Rate and extent | Highest concentration; relates to intensity and toxicity |
| tmax (time to peak) | Rate of absorption | Shorter tmax = faster absorption |
| AUC (area under the curve) | Extent of absorption | Total amount reaching the blood (AUC = F × Dose / CL) |

```text
 C│        Cmax
  │       .-'''-.
  │     .'       '.            MSC (minimum safe / toxic)
  │----/-----------\------------------------------
  │   /             '.         MEC (minimum effective)
  │--/----------------'.-------------------------
  │ /    onset   duration '.
  │/________________________'--------── time
     tmax
```

AUC is found by the trapezoidal rule (worked example in Unit 3).

**2. Urinary excretion studies.** Useful when the drug is excreted largely unchanged and plasma assay is difficult.

| Parameter | Equivalent in plasma data |
| --- | --- |
| (dXu/dt)max — maximum urinary excretion rate | Cmax |
| tu — time for maximum excretion rate | tmax |
| Xu∞ — cumulative amount excreted unchanged | AUC |

Urine must be collected for about 7 half-lives; complete emptying of the bladder at each collection is essential.

### B. Pharmacodynamic methods (direct)

1. **Acute pharmacological response** — measure an effect such as ECG, pupil size, blood pressure or blood sugar against time. Used when no good assay exists. Less precise, more variable.
2. **Therapeutic response (clinical trials)** — measure clinical improvement in patients. Poorly quantitative; used only when the other methods cannot be applied.

### Design of a single-dose bioavailability study (summary)

- Healthy adult volunteers, overnight fasting, standard volume of water.
- Crossover design (each subject receives every product) with a **washout period** of at least 5–10 elimination half-lives.
- Sampling long enough (3 or more half-lives, ideally until AUC to the last point is at least 80% of AUC0–∞).
- Validated analytical method.

## 2.9 In-vitro drug dissolution models

Dissolution testing measures the rate and extent of drug release in a laboratory. It is used for quality control, formulation development and, when correlated, as a substitute for in-vivo studies.

**Classification of dissolution apparatus**

| Type | Principle | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed-compartment (fixed volume, non-sink may develop) | Limited volume of medium (usually 500–1000 ml) | Rotating basket (USP/IP Apparatus 1), paddle (Apparatus 2), reciprocating cylinder (Apparatus 3), rotating bottle, beaker method |
| Open-compartment (continuous flow) | Fresh medium flows through the cell, keeping sink conditions | Flow-through cell (USP Apparatus 4) |
| Dialysis systems | Membrane separates dosage form and receptor medium | Useful for suspensions and ointments |
| Others | — | Paddle over disc (Apparatus 5, transdermal), rotating cylinder (Apparatus 6), reciprocating holder (Apparatus 7) |

**Conditions:** medium at 37 ± 0.5 °C, deaerated; pH chosen to imitate the GIT (0.1 N HCl, acetate or phosphate buffers pH 4.5–6.8); stirring usually 50–100 rpm; sink condition (volume at least 3 times that needed to saturate).

**Comparing dissolution profiles**

- Single points: t50%, t90%, percent dissolved at a set time (Q value).
- **Dissolution efficiency** — area under the dissolution curve up to time t as a percentage of the rectangle for 100% dissolution.
- **Similarity factor f2** = 50 × log10 {[1 + (1/n) Σ (Rt − Tt)²]^−0.5 × 100}. f2 between 50 and 100 means the two profiles are similar (average difference not more than about 10%).
- Model fitting: zero order, first order, Higuchi (√t), Korsmeyer–Peppas, Hixson–Crowell (cube root).

## 2.10 In-vitro – in-vivo correlation (IVIVC)

**IVIVC** is a predictive mathematical relationship between an in-vitro property (usually rate or extent of dissolution) and an in-vivo response (plasma concentration or amount absorbed).

| Level | What is correlated | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Level A | Point-to-point: fraction dissolved in vitro vs fraction absorbed in vivo (from Wagner–Nelson or deconvolution) at each time | Highest; can replace in-vivo studies for some changes |
| Level B | Statistical moments: mean dissolution time (in vitro) vs mean residence or absorption time (in vivo) | Uses all data but different curves can give the same mean |
| Level C | Single point: one dissolution value (t50, % at 4 h) vs one PK parameter (AUC, Cmax) | Weakest; useful in early development |
| Multiple Level C | Several dissolution time points vs one or more PK parameters | Better than single level C |

**Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS)** groups drugs by solubility and intestinal permeability:

| Class | Solubility | Permeability | Absorption limited by | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | High | High | Gastric emptying | Metoprolol, propranolol |
| II | Low | High | Dissolution (IVIVC likely) | Ketoconazole, carbamazepine, glibenclamide |
| III | High | Low | Permeability | Atenolol, cimetidine |
| IV | Low | Low | Both | Furosemide (placed in Class IV in most texts) |

IVIVC is most likely for Class II drugs, where dissolution controls absorption.

## 2.11 Bioequivalence studies

**Pharmaceutical equivalents** — same drug, same amount, same dosage form and route, meeting the same standards.

**Bioequivalence** — two pharmaceutically equivalent (or alternative) products show no significant difference in the **rate and extent** of absorption when given at the same molar dose under similar conditions.

**Therapeutic equivalence** — bioequivalent products expected to have the same clinical effect and safety.

**Study design (typical generic study)**

1. **Subjects:** healthy adult volunteers (often 12–24 or more, chosen by power calculation), screened, informed consent, ethics committee approval.
2. **Design:** randomised, two-period, two-sequence, **crossover** (Latin square) design so each subject acts as his own control. Parallel design for drugs with very long half-lives.
3. **Washout** between periods: at least 5 half-lives.
4. **Dosing:** single dose under fasting (and fed when required); standard water volume.
5. **Sampling:** frequent around tmax; continued for 3 or more half-lives.
6. **Analysis:** validated method (HPLC, LC–MS/MS).
7. **Parameters:** AUC0–t, AUC0–∞, Cmax (and tmax).
8. **Statistics:** log-transform AUC and Cmax; ANOVA; **two one-sided tests**; the **90% confidence interval** of the test/reference geometric mean ratio must lie within **80.00–125.00%**.

**Worked example (example numbers; simplified paired analysis on logs, ignoring period effects).** Eight subjects take a test (T) and reference (R) tablet in crossover.

| Subject | AUC T | AUC R | ln(T/R) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 88 | 92 | −0.0445 |
| 2 | 95 | 90 | 0.0541 |
| 3 | 102 | 108 | −0.0572 |
| 4 | 76 | 80 | −0.0513 |
| 5 | 110 | 105 | 0.0465 |
| 6 | 92 | 97 | −0.0529 |
| 7 | 84 | 90 | −0.0690 |
| 8 | 99 | 95 | 0.0412 |

- Mean of ln(T/R) = −0.0166; SD = 0.0535; SE = SD/√8 = 0.0189.
- Geometric mean ratio = e^(−0.0166) = 0.984 (98.4%).
- t for 90% CI with 7 degrees of freedom = 1.895. CI = e^(−0.0166 ± 1.895 × 0.0189) = **94.9% to 101.9%**.
- The whole interval lies inside 80–125%, so for AUC the test product is bioequivalent. The same test is repeated for Cmax.

## 2.12 Methods to enhance dissolution rate and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs

| Approach | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Micronisation / nanosizing | Larger surface area (Noyes–Whitney) | Griseofulvin, fenofibrate nanocrystals |
| Salt formation | More soluble ionised form at the dissolving surface | Sodium salts of acids, hydrochloride of bases |
| Polymorphs and amorphous forms | Higher free energy, faster dissolution | Amorphous novobiocin |
| Solid dispersions / eutectic mixtures | Drug dispersed in a hydrophilic carrier (PEG, PVP); fine particles, better wetting | Griseofulvin–PEG |
| Complexation | Inclusion complex with cyclodextrins | Piroxicam–β-cyclodextrin |
| Surfactants and wetting agents | Lower interfacial tension, micellar solubilisation | Polysorbate 80 |
| Cosolvency | Water-miscible solvents raise solubility | Propylene glycol, ethanol, PEG 400 |
| pH adjustment / buffering | Keeps drug ionised near the dissolving particle | Buffered aspirin |
| Prodrugs | More soluble ester or salt converted back in the body | Phosphate esters |
| Lipid-based systems | SEDDS/SMEDDS form fine emulsions in the gut | Cyclosporine microemulsion |
| Hydrotropy, supercritical fluid processing, spray drying, liquisolid compacts | Various | — |

## Remember

- Elimination = metabolism + excretion; CLT = CLR + CLH + others; t½ = 0.693/KE.
- Phase I adds or exposes a polar group (CYP oxidation most common); phase II conjugates (glucuronidation most common).
- Renal excretion = filtration + secretion − reabsorption; only free drug is filtered; urine pH controls reabsorption of weak acids and bases.
- CLR = excretion rate / plasma concentration; ratio to GFR > 1 means secretion, < 1 means reabsorption.
- Bioavailability = rate (Cmax, tmax) and extent (AUC); absolute F uses an IV reference.
- IVIVC: Level A point-to-point is the best; BCS Class II drugs are most likely to correlate.
- Bioequivalence: crossover, log-transformed AUC and Cmax, 90% CI of the ratio within 80–125%.
- Enhance dissolution by size reduction, salts, amorphous forms, solid dispersions, cyclodextrins, surfactants, cosolvents, lipid systems.
