# How to use this guide

Biopharmaceutics and Pharmacokinetics is half theory and half numericals. Units 1 and 2 are mostly descriptive (absorption, distribution, protein binding, elimination, bioavailability, bioequivalence). Units 3, 4 and 5 are built on equations and graphs. Study one unit a day: read the notes, redo every worked example with a calculator without looking, draw each graph once from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

**Units.** The notes follow the five PCI syllabus units exactly. Unit 1 (absorption, distribution, protein binding) and Unit 2 (elimination plus bioavailability and bioequivalence) are long because the syllabus packs two big topics into each; they are kept as single units so the numbering matches your syllabus.

**Worked examples.** Every data table in the notes uses example numbers for a hypothetical drug, chosen to make the arithmetic clear. They are not values for any real medicine. The same drug (KE 0.1733 h⁻¹, t½ 4 h, Vd 25 L) runs through Units 3 and 4, so you can see how IV bolus, infusion, oral and multiple-dose answers fit together.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with equations, a graph and, where it fits, a worked numerical.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start each answer with a one-line definition, then the equation, then explain every symbol with its unit (h⁻¹, L, mg·h/L, L/h).
2. Draw the graph: log C vs t (straight line for one compartment, biexponential for two), infusion curve rising to Css, oral curve with tmax and Cmax, residual line, multiple-dosing saw-tooth, Michaelis–Menten curve, Scatchard plot.
3. Numericals: write the formula first, substitute with units, box the answer. Method marks are given even if the arithmetic slips.
4. Memorise the core set: t½ = 0.693/KE; Vd = X0/C0; AUC = C0/KE; CL = KE × Vd = Dose/AUC; Css = R0/CL; tmax = ln(Ka/KE)/(Ka − KE); R = 1/(1 − e^(−KE τ)); DL = Css × Vd/F; DM = Css,av × CL × τ/F; Css = Km R/(Vmax − R).
5. Remember the "rules of thumb": 90% of steady state in 3.3 t½, 97% in 5 t½; loading dose = 2 × maintenance dose when τ = t½; BE limits 80–125% for the 90% CI.
6. For descriptive answers (mechanisms, factors, routes, dissolution apparatus, IVIVC levels, causes of nonlinearity) use a table with one example drug per row.
7. Always end essays with 3–4 clinical or formulation applications — dose adjustment in renal failure, displacement interactions, choosing a loading dose, phenytoin monitoring.
