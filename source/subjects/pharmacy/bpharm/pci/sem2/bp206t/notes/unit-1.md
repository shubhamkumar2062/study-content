# Unit 1: Multidisciplinary nature of environmental studies and natural resources

Everything a pharmacist uses comes from the environment: water for manufacturing, plants for crude drugs, minerals for inorganic compounds, energy to run the plant. This unit explains what environmental studies covers, why it draws on many subjects, and how each natural resource is used, overused and protected.

## 1.1 The environment and environmental studies

**Environment** is the sum of all living and non-living surroundings that affect an organism: air, water, soil, other plants and animals, and the social and cultural setting created by people. The word comes from the French *environner*, "to surround".

The environment has four connected parts (spheres):

| Sphere | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lithosphere | Solid crust of the earth: rocks, soil, minerals | Mountains, farmland, ores |
| Hydrosphere | All water on, under and above the earth | Oceans, rivers, groundwater, glaciers, clouds |
| Atmosphere | Layer of gases held around the earth by gravity | Nitrogen, oxygen, carbon dioxide, water vapour |
| Biosphere | The thin zone where life exists, overlapping the other three | Forests, oceans, soil organisms, people |

```text
            Atmosphere
           /          \
  Lithosphere ---- Hydrosphere
           \          /
            Biosphere  (life exists where the three meet)
```

**Environmental studies** (environmental science) is the scientific study of the environmental system and of the changes, natural or caused by people, that affect organisms. It covers the physical and biological features of the environment and also the social and cultural factors and the impact of humans on it.

## 1.2 Multidisciplinary nature

No single subject can explain an environmental problem. Take a river polluted by a drug factory: chemistry identifies the effluent, biology shows the harm to fish, engineering designs the treatment plant, economics weighs the cost, law sets the discharge limits, and sociology explains why villagers downstream are affected. That is why environmental studies is called **multidisciplinary** (or interdisciplinary).

| Discipline | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| Biology, botany, zoology, microbiology | Living organisms, food chains, biodiversity, biodegradation |
| Chemistry and biochemistry | Composition of air, water and soil; reactions of pollutants; toxicity |
| Physics | Energy flow, radiation, heat balance, movement of pollutants |
| Geology and geography | Rocks, soil formation, minerals, landforms, climate zones, mapping |
| Mathematics and statistics | Models, sampling, data analysis, forecasting |
| Engineering and technology | Pollution control devices, waste treatment, renewable energy |
| Economics | Cost of damage and control, valuing resources, sustainable development |
| Law and political science | Environmental acts, rules, international treaties |
| Sociology, ethics, education | Public attitudes, people's movements, awareness |
| Medicine and pharmacy | Health effects of pollution, safe disposal of medicines, drug residues in water |

### Scope of environmental studies

- Conservation and wise use of natural resources.
- Understanding ecosystems and biodiversity.
- Control of pollution and management of wastes.
- Environmental impact assessment of new projects (dams, factories, roads).
- Environmental law, policy and management.
- Research and jobs: pollution control boards, effluent treatment, environmental consultancy, green chemistry in industry.

### Importance and need for public awareness

1. Resources are limited while population and demand keep growing.
2. Many problems (global warming, ozone depletion, plastic waste) are global and need everyone's action.
3. Pollution harms health directly: respiratory disease, water-borne infection, poisoning by heavy metals.
4. Laws work only when citizens understand and support them.
5. Development must be **sustainable**: meet today's needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs.
6. Awareness changes daily habits: saving water and power, segregating waste, returning unused medicines rather than flushing them.

## 1.3 Natural resources

A **natural resource** is anything obtained from nature that people use to meet their needs: air, water, soil, minerals, forests, wildlife, fossil fuels and sunlight.

### Classification

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Renewable** | Replaced by natural processes within a human time scale, if not overused | Forests, water, fish, crops, soil (slowly), solar and wind energy |
| **Non-renewable** | Formed over millions of years; once used up they are gone for practical purposes | Coal, petroleum, natural gas, metal ores |
| Inexhaustible | Will not run out whatever we use | Sunlight, wind, tides |
| Exhaustible | Can be used up | Fossil fuels, minerals; also renewables when overused |

A renewable resource becomes effectively non-renewable when it is used faster than it is replaced: cutting a forest faster than it regrows, or pumping groundwater faster than rain refills it.

```text
Natural resources
├── Renewable ........ forests, water, food, soil, wildlife, solar/wind energy
└── Non-renewable .... fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas), minerals, nuclear fuels
```

## 1.4 Forest resources

About a third of India's land should be under forest according to the National Forest Policy of 1988, but actual cover is well below this.

### Uses of forests

| Productive (direct) uses | Protective (indirect) uses |
| --- | --- |
| Timber and fuelwood | Produce oxygen and absorb carbon dioxide |
| Paper pulp, bamboo, cane | Hold soil and prevent erosion and landslides |
| Food: fruits, honey, nuts | Regulate rainfall and the water cycle; recharge groundwater |
| **Medicinal plants** and crude drugs (for example rauwolfia, cinchona, neem) | Home to wildlife; protect biodiversity |
| Gums, resins, tannins, dyes, essential oils | Moderate local climate; reduce floods |
| Fodder for cattle; livelihood of tribal people | Recreation, tourism, research |

### Over-exploitation and deforestation

**Deforestation** is the permanent removal of forest for other uses.

**Causes**

- Clearing for agriculture, including shifting (jhum) cultivation in the north-east.
- Fuelwood collection and commercial timber logging.
- Expansion of cities, roads, railways and industries.
- Mining and quarrying.
- Large dams that submerge forest land.
- Overgrazing, forest fires, and pests and diseases.

**Effects**

- Soil erosion, landslides and silting of rivers and reservoirs.
- More floods and droughts; falling groundwater.
- Loss of habitat and extinction of plants and animals, including medicinal species.
- Less carbon absorbed, which adds to global warming.
- Change in local rainfall and climate; spread of deserts.
- Loss of livelihood for forest dwellers.

### Timber extraction, mining and dams

- **Timber extraction:** large-scale logging removes the best trees, damages young ones, opens the canopy and builds roads that bring further clearing.
- **Mining:** open-cast mining removes all vegetation and topsoil, leaves pits and waste dumps, and pollutes nearby streams.
- **Dams:** reservoirs submerge forests and villages. Tribal and rural people lose their land and are displaced, often with poor rehabilitation. The protests over the Narmada (Sardar Sarovar) project are a well-known Indian example.

### Conservation of forests

- Afforestation and reforestation; social forestry and agroforestry.
- Joint Forest Management, where villagers protect forests and share benefits.
- Protected areas: national parks, sanctuaries, biosphere reserves.
- Use of alternative fuels (biogas, LPG) to reduce fuelwood demand.
- Controlled grazing and fire control.
- People's movements: the **Chipko movement** of the 1970s in the Garhwal Himalaya, where villagers, many of them women, hugged trees to stop felling.

## 1.5 Water resources

Water covers most of the earth's surface, but about 97% of it is salt water in the oceans. Only about 3% is fresh, and most of that is locked in ice caps and glaciers, so the water readily available in lakes, rivers and shallow groundwater is a very small fraction.

### Uses

Drinking and domestic use, irrigation (the largest use in India), industry (cooling, processing, pharmaceutical manufacture as purified water and water for injection), hydroelectric power, transport, fisheries and recreation.

### Over-utilisation of surface and groundwater

| Problem | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Falling water table | Pumping from tube wells faster than recharge; wells run dry |
| Ground subsidence | Land sinks when water is removed from below |
| Salt-water intrusion | In coastal areas, sea water moves into emptied aquifers |
| Drying of rivers and lakes | Excess withdrawal for irrigation and cities |
| Water logging and salinity | Over-irrigation raises the water table and salts collect at the surface |
| Pollution | Less water to dilute the waste that enters it |

### Floods and drought

- **Floods:** heavy rain, melting snow, cloud bursts or dam bursts make water overflow its banks. Deforestation and loss of wetlands make floods worse because water runs off faster. Effects: loss of life and crops, damage to homes, water-borne diseases.
- **Drought:** a long period of rainfall well below normal. Causes include failure of the monsoon, deforestation and overuse of groundwater. Effects: crop failure, shortage of drinking water and fodder, migration, famine.

### Conflicts over water

When rivers cross state or national borders, sharing their water causes disputes. Examples in India are disputes between states over the Cauvery, Krishna and other rivers; internationally, sharing of the Indus and Ganga waters is governed by treaties. Within a region, farmers, industries and cities compete for the same water.

### Dams: benefits and problems

| Benefits | Problems |
| --- | --- |
| Irrigation water for dry areas | Submergence of forests, farmland and villages |
| Hydroelectric power | Displacement of people, mostly poor and tribal |
| Drinking water supply | Silting of the reservoir, shortening its life |
| Flood control | Water logging and salinity in command areas |
| Fisheries, navigation, recreation | Spread of water-borne diseases; loss of downstream flow |
| | Possible earthquakes and the risk of dam failure |

### Conservation of water

Rainwater harvesting, check dams and watershed management, drip and sprinkler irrigation, repair of leaks, reuse of treated wastewater, protecting wetlands, and treating effluent before discharge.

## 1.6 Mineral resources

**Minerals** are naturally occurring inorganic substances with a definite composition, found in rocks. **Ores** are minerals from which a metal can be extracted profitably.

### Uses

| Mineral | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Iron, aluminium, copper | Construction, machines, utensils, wires |
| Coal and petroleum | Fuel, power generation, petrochemicals |
| Limestone, gypsum | Cement, building |
| Uranium | Nuclear power |
| Gold, silver, diamonds | Jewellery, electronics |
| Sulphur, phosphate, potash | Fertilisers, chemicals |
| Talc, kaolin, bentonite, calcium carbonate | **Pharmaceutical excipients**, cosmetics |

### Environmental effects of extracting and using minerals

- **Mining** destroys vegetation and topsoil; creates pits, waste dumps and scarred land.
- Dust and gases pollute air; miners suffer lung diseases (for example silicosis, black lung from coal dust).
- Acid mine drainage: water passing through sulphide ores becomes acidic and carries heavy metals into rivers.
- Groundwater lowered or contaminated.
- Subsidence of land over underground mines; landslides on waste heaps.
- **Processing (smelting)** releases sulphur dioxide, particulate matter and toxic metals.
- Displacement of people and loss of farmland.

**Remedies:** environment-friendly mining methods, refilling and re-planting mined land (reclamation), treating mine water, recycling metals, using substitutes.

## 1.7 Food resources

Food comes from crops, livestock and fisheries. The main food crops in India are rice, wheat, maize, millets and pulses.

### World food problems

- Rising population needs more food each year.
- Malnutrition and under-nutrition in poor regions, while food is wasted elsewhere.
- Loss of farmland to cities and land degradation.
- Climate change, drought and floods reduce yields.
- Poor storage and distribution; poverty limits access to food.

### Changes caused by agriculture and overgrazing

- **Overgrazing:** too many animals on a pasture remove plant cover, compact soil, expose it to erosion and can lead to desertification.
- **Agriculture:** clearing of natural vegetation, loss of wild species, soil erosion and nutrient loss.

### Effects of modern agriculture

The **Green Revolution** (high-yielding varieties, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation) raised food production but brought problems:

| Problem | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Fertiliser problems | Excess nitrate leaches into groundwater; runoff of nitrate and phosphate causes **eutrophication** of lakes; soil micro-life declines |
| Pesticide problems | Kill useful insects; pests develop resistance; residues persist and **biomagnify** in food chains (DDT is the classic example) |
| Water logging | Over-irrigation raises the water table so roots cannot get air |
| Salinity | Salts brought up by irrigation water collect at the surface as water evaporates |
| Loss of genetic diversity | A few high-yielding varieties replace many traditional ones |

**Remedies:** integrated pest management, biofertilisers and organic manure, crop rotation, balanced fertiliser use, drip irrigation, good drainage.

## 1.8 Energy resources

Energy demand grows with population, industry, transport and living standards. Most of the world's commercial energy still comes from fossil fuels.

| Non-renewable sources | Renewable (alternative) sources |
| --- | --- |
| Coal | Solar energy (solar cells, solar heaters) |
| Petroleum (crude oil) | Wind energy |
| Natural gas | Hydropower |
| Nuclear fuels (uranium) | Biomass and **biogas** (from cow dung and organic waste) |
| | Geothermal energy |
| | Tidal and wave energy |
| | Hydrogen (fuel cells) |

**Problems with fossil fuels:** limited reserves, air pollution (SO2, NOx, particulates), carbon dioxide emissions causing global warming, oil spills. **Nuclear energy** produces little CO2 but raises concerns about radioactive waste and accidents.

**Advantages of renewable sources:** do not run out, cause little pollution, can be used locally in villages. Their limits are high initial cost, dependence on weather and the need for storage.

**Energy conservation:** use efficient lights and appliances, switch off when not needed, use public transport, insulate buildings, and design processes (including in pharmaceutical plants) to recover heat.

## 1.9 Land resources

Land supports farming, forests, homes, industry and wildlife, but its area is fixed.

### Land degradation

**Land degradation** is the decline in the quality and productivity of land. Causes: soil erosion, salinity and water logging, over-use of chemicals, mining, dumping of waste, deforestation and overgrazing.

### Man-induced landslides

Building roads and dams on steep hills, blasting, mining and removing trees weaken slopes, so rock and soil slide down, especially in the rainy season. The Himalayan and Western Ghats regions are prone to them.

### Soil erosion

**Soil erosion** is the removal of fertile topsoil by water or wind. Forms of water erosion include sheet, rill and gully erosion.

- Causes: deforestation, overgrazing, poor farming (ploughing down the slope), heavy rain on bare soil.
- Control: planting trees and grass, contour ploughing, terracing on slopes, strip cropping, windbreaks (shelter belts), check dams, keeping a plant cover.

### Desertification

**Desertification** is the process by which fertile land in dry regions turns into desert-like land because of climate variation and human activity (overgrazing, deforestation, poor irrigation). The Thar region of Rajasthan is the main area at risk in India. Control: afforestation, controlled grazing, water conservation and sand-dune stabilisation.

## 1.10 Role of an individual in conserving natural resources

| Resource | What one person can do |
| --- | --- |
| Water | Turn off taps, fix leaks, reuse water for plants, harvest rainwater |
| Energy | Switch off lights and fans, use LED bulbs, use public transport or cycle |
| Forests and paper | Use both sides of paper, recycle, plant and care for trees |
| Food | Avoid waste, buy local and seasonal food, prefer organic manure in gardens |
| Land and soil | Avoid plastics, compost kitchen waste, segregate waste at home |
| Medicines | Return expired or unused medicines to a take-back point instead of throwing them in drains or open dumps |
| General | Spread awareness, support conservation laws, practise the 3 Rs: reduce, reuse, recycle |

**Equitable use of resources:** rich countries and people use a far larger share of resources than poor ones. Sustainable development needs the rich to cut waste and the poor to have fair access.

## Remember

- Environmental studies is multidisciplinary: science, engineering, economics, law and sociology all contribute.
- Renewable resources can be replaced; non-renewable ones cannot. Overuse makes a renewable resource behave like a non-renewable one.
- Deforestation → erosion, floods, loss of biodiversity and medicinal plants, global warming.
- About 97% of earth's water is saline; usable fresh water is a tiny fraction.
- Dams give water and power but displace people and submerge forests.
- Mining causes land damage, dust, acid mine drainage and lung disease.
- Modern agriculture: fertilisers → eutrophication; pesticides → resistance and biomagnification; over-irrigation → water logging and salinity.
- Alternative energy: solar, wind, hydro, biogas, geothermal, tidal.
- Land degradation includes landslides, soil erosion and desertification.
- Every individual can conserve resources through the 3 Rs and safe disposal of medicines.
