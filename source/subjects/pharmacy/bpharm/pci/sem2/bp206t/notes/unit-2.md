# Unit 2: Ecosystems

Living things never live alone. A pond, a forest or a field is a working unit in which plants, animals and microbes depend on one another and on light, water, air and soil. This unit explains what an ecosystem is, how it is built, how energy and nutrients move through it, and the main kinds of ecosystem found on land and in water.

## 2.1 Concept of an ecosystem

An **ecosystem** is a community of living organisms interacting with one another and with their non-living surroundings in a given area, forming a self-supporting unit through which energy flows and materials cycle. The term was introduced by the British ecologist A. G. Tansley in 1935.

Key ideas:

- It has living (**biotic**) and non-living (**abiotic**) parts that act together.
- Energy enters as sunlight and flows in one direction; nutrients go round in cycles.
- It can be any size: a drop of pond water, a pond, a forest, an ocean. The whole earth is the largest ecosystem (the **ecosphere** or biosphere).
- It can be **natural** (forest, grassland, lake) or **artificial / man-made** (crop field, aquarium, garden, reservoir).

Related terms:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Species | Group of similar organisms that can interbreed |
| Population | All members of one species in an area |
| Community | All populations of different species living together in an area |
| Habitat | The place where an organism lives |
| Niche | The role of an organism in its ecosystem: what it eats, where it lives, how it affects others |
| Biome | A large region with a typical climate and vegetation, such as a tropical rain forest or a desert |

## 2.2 Structure of an ecosystem

Structure means the parts of the ecosystem and how they are arranged.

```text
                       ECOSYSTEM
          ┌────────────────┴────────────────┐
     Abiotic parts                     Biotic parts
  ├ Inorganic: C, N, P, water,      ├ Producers (autotrophs): green plants, algae
  │  CO2, O2, minerals              ├ Consumers (heterotrophs)
  ├ Organic: proteins, fats,        │    ├ Primary: herbivores
  │  carbohydrates, humus           │    ├ Secondary: carnivores eating herbivores
  └ Climatic: light, temperature,   │    └ Tertiary: top carnivores
     rainfall, humidity, wind, pH   └ Decomposers: bacteria, fungi
```

### Abiotic components

- **Inorganic substances:** water, carbon dioxide, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, calcium and other minerals.
- **Organic substances:** carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and humus from dead matter; they link the living and non-living parts.
- **Climatic (physical) factors:** sunlight, temperature, rainfall, humidity, wind, soil type and pH. They decide which organisms can live in a place.

### Biotic components

1. **Producers (autotrophs):** green plants, algae and some bacteria that make food from CO2 and water using sunlight (photosynthesis). A few bacteria make food from chemical energy (chemosynthesis).
2. **Consumers (heterotrophs, macro-consumers):**
   - Primary consumers — herbivores (deer, grasshopper, cow, zooplankton).
   - Secondary consumers — carnivores that eat herbivores (frog, small fish, fox).
   - Tertiary consumers — top carnivores (tiger, eagle, large fish).
   - Omnivores eat both plants and animals (humans, bears).
3. **Decomposers (micro-consumers, saprotrophs):** bacteria and fungi that break down dead plants and animals into simple inorganic substances, returning nutrients to the soil and water. Without them, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter.

## 2.3 Function of an ecosystem

The main functions are:

1. Energy flow through food chains and food webs.
2. Cycling of nutrients (biogeochemical cycles).
3. Production of organic matter (productivity).
4. Regulation and development of the community (ecological succession, homeostasis).

### Productivity

- **Primary productivity:** the rate at which producers make organic matter.
  - **Gross primary productivity (GPP):** total organic matter made by photosynthesis.
  - **Net primary productivity (NPP):** GPP minus what the plants use in their own respiration; this is what is available to consumers.
- **Secondary productivity:** the rate at which consumers form new tissue.

Tropical rain forests, coral reefs and estuaries are among the most productive ecosystems; deserts and the open ocean are among the least productive per unit area.

## 2.4 Energy flow

The sun is the source of energy for almost all ecosystems. Producers capture only a small part of the sunlight falling on them. At each step (trophic level), most of the energy is used for living (respiration) and lost as heat, so only a small part passes on.

**Ten per cent law** (proposed by Lindeman): on average only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed on to the next.

```text
Sun → Producers → Herbivores → Carnivores → Top carnivores
       10000 J      1000 J        100 J         10 J      (example only)
         ↓            ↓             ↓             ↓
       heat         heat          heat          heat
```

Features of energy flow:

- It is **unidirectional**: energy moves from producers to consumers and never returns to the sun.
- It decreases at each level, which is why food chains are usually only 4–5 steps long.
- Nutrients, unlike energy, are recycled.

## 2.5 Food chains and food webs

A **food chain** is a sequence of organisms in which each one eats the one before it and is eaten by the next. Each step is a **trophic level**.

| Type | Starts with | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Grazing food chain** | Living green plants | Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle |
| **Detritus food chain** | Dead organic matter | Fallen leaves → earthworm → bird; dead matter → bacteria and fungi → protozoa |

Examples by ecosystem:

- Pond: Phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → large fish → kingfisher.
- Grassland: Grass → rabbit → fox.
- Forest: Leaves → deer → tiger.

A **food web** is a network of interconnected food chains. In nature, most animals eat more than one kind of food and are eaten by more than one predator, so food chains cross-link.

```text
A simple grassland food web (each arrow means "is eaten by"):

Grass ──► Grasshopper ──► Frog ──► Snake ──► Eagle
Grass ──► Rabbit ───────────────► Fox
Grass ──► Rabbit ─────────────────────────► Eagle
Seeds ──► Mouse ──► Snake
Seeds ──► Mouse ──► Owl
Grass ──► Grasshopper ──► Bird ──► Eagle
```

**Importance of food webs:** they make the ecosystem stable. If one prey species declines, the predator can switch to another food, so the system does not collapse.

### Biomagnification

Some pollutants that do not break down and dissolve in fat (DDT, mercury, some other pesticides) are not excreted. Their concentration increases at each step up the food chain, so top carnivores (fish-eating birds, humans) carry the highest levels. This is **biomagnification**. DDT caused thin egg shells and population decline in birds of prey.

## 2.6 Ecological pyramids

An **ecological pyramid** shows the relationship between trophic levels as a graph, with producers at the base and top carnivores at the apex.

| Pyramid | What it shows | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pyramid of numbers** | Number of organisms at each level | Upright in grassland and pond; **spindle-shaped** in a tree food chain (one tree, many insects, fewer birds); fully **inverted** in a parasite food chain (one tree → many birds → still more parasites on them) |
| **Pyramid of biomass** | Total dry weight of living matter at each level | Upright on land; **inverted** in a pond or ocean, where a small standing mass of fast-growing phytoplankton supports a larger mass of zooplankton and fish |
| **Pyramid of energy** | Energy flowing through each level per unit time | **Always upright**, because energy is lost at every step |

```text
Upright (grassland numbers)         Spindle (tree numbers)
           ▲   hawks                        ███   birds
          ███  snakes                   ███████████ insects (many)
         █████ frogs                         █     one tree
      █████████ grasshoppers
    █████████████ grass
```

## 2.7 Biogeochemical (nutrient) cycles

Nutrients move from the environment into organisms and back again. These movements are **biogeochemical cycles**.

### Water (hydrological) cycle

Evaporation from oceans, lakes and soil + transpiration from plants → water vapour → condensation into clouds → precipitation (rain, snow) → runoff into rivers and seepage into groundwater → back to the oceans.

### Carbon cycle

```text
CO2 in air ──photosynthesis──► plants ──eaten──► animals
   ▲                              │                 │
   │ respiration, decay,          └──── death ──────┤
   │ burning of fuels                               ▼
   └──────────────── decomposers, fossil fuels ◄── dead matter
```

Burning of fossil fuels and deforestation add CO2 faster than plants and oceans remove it, which drives global warming.

### Nitrogen cycle

Air is about 78% nitrogen, but most organisms cannot use N2 directly.

| Step | What happens | Done by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nitrogen fixation | N2 → ammonia/nitrogen compounds | *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules, free-living bacteria (*Azotobacter*), blue-green algae, lightning, industrial fertiliser manufacture |
| Assimilation | Plants absorb nitrate and ammonium and make proteins; animals eat plants | Plants, animals |
| Ammonification | Dead matter and wastes → ammonia | Decomposer bacteria and fungi |
| Nitrification | Ammonia → nitrite → nitrate | *Nitrosomonas*, then *Nitrobacter* |
| Denitrification | Nitrate → N2 gas returned to air | Denitrifying bacteria (for example *Pseudomonas*) |

### Phosphorus cycle

A sedimentary cycle with no gaseous phase: phosphate is weathered from rocks into soil, taken up by plants, passed to animals, returned by decomposers, and partly lost to ocean sediments. Excess phosphate from fertilisers and detergents causes eutrophication.

## 2.8 Ecological succession

**Ecological succession** is the gradual, orderly replacement of one community by another in an area until a stable **climax community** is reached.

- **Primary succession** starts on bare ground where no life existed (bare rock, new sand, a new pond).
- **Secondary succession** starts where a community was removed but soil remains (abandoned farmland, burnt forest). It is faster.
- The first organisms are **pioneer species** (lichens on rock; phytoplankton in water).
- The stages in between are **seral stages**; the whole series is a **sere**.
- **Hydrosere** begins in water, **xerosere** on a dry area such as rock.

```text
Bare rock → lichens → mosses → grasses and herbs → shrubs → trees (climax forest)
```

## 2.9 Forest ecosystem

**Introduction:** land areas dominated by trees, covering a large part of the earth's land.

**Types:**

| Type | Main features | Indian examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tropical rain (evergreen) forest | Heavy rainfall, warm all year, dense multi-layered canopy, very high biodiversity | Western Ghats, north-east India, Andaman and Nicobar |
| Tropical deciduous forest | Seasonal rain; trees shed leaves in the dry season | Teak and sal forests of central India |
| Tropical thorn / scrub forest | Low rainfall; thorny trees and shrubs | Parts of Rajasthan, Gujarat, Deccan |
| Temperate (coniferous) forest | Cold, snow; cone-bearing evergreen trees | Pine, fir and deodar forests of the Himalaya |
| Mangrove forest | Salt-tolerant trees on tidal coasts | Sundarbans |

**Structure:**

- Producers: trees (teak, sal, pine), shrubs, herbs, climbers.
- Primary consumers: insects, birds, deer, rabbits, elephants, squirrels.
- Secondary and tertiary consumers: snakes, foxes, wolves, tigers, leopards, hawks.
- Decomposers: fungi and bacteria in the thick leaf litter.
- Abiotic: soil, organic matter, rainfall, light, temperature.

**Function and importance:** high productivity; store carbon; regulate climate and water; protect soil; supply timber, food and many medicinal plants; home to tribal communities.

## 2.10 Grassland ecosystem

**Introduction:** areas dominated by grasses with few or no trees, where rainfall is too low for forests but more than in deserts.

**Types:** tropical grasslands (savannas, with scattered trees, as in Africa and parts of India), temperate grasslands (prairies of North America, steppes of Asia, pampas of South America), and Himalayan alpine meadows.

**Characteristic features:** moderate seasonal rainfall, grazing animals, periodic fires that keep trees out, deep fertile soil (many are now farmland).

**Structure:**

- Producers: grasses, herbs, some shrubs.
- Primary consumers: cows, buffaloes, deer, rabbits, grasshoppers, termites.
- Secondary consumers: frogs, snakes, lizards, foxes.
- Tertiary consumers: hawks, eagles, wolves.
- Decomposers: bacteria and fungi.

**Threats:** overgrazing, conversion to farmland and land degradation, which can lead to desertification.

## 2.11 Desert ecosystem

**Introduction:** regions with very low and irregular rainfall (usually under about 25 cm a year), high evaporation and sparse life.

**Types:** hot deserts (Thar in Rajasthan, Sahara) and cold deserts (Ladakh).

**Characteristic features:** very hot days and cold nights in hot deserts, sandy or rocky soil poor in organic matter, strong winds, little vegetation.

**Adaptations:**

| Plants (xerophytes) | Animals |
| --- | --- |
| Thick waxy cuticle, few or spiny leaves (cactus, opuntia, acacia) | Burrow and stay active at night |
| Fleshy stems store water | Concentrated urine, little sweating |
| Deep or widely spread roots | Camel stores fat in its hump and tolerates water loss |
| Short life cycle after rain | Thick skin, scales (reptiles) |

**Structure:** producers — cacti, shrubs, some grasses; consumers — insects, reptiles, rodents, camels, birds, foxes; decomposers — few, as heat and dryness slow decay.

## 2.12 Aquatic ecosystems

Aquatic ecosystems are **freshwater** (ponds, lakes, streams, rivers) or **marine** (oceans), with **estuaries** where the two meet.

### Pond ecosystem

A small, shallow body of still water; often seasonal.

| Component | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Abiotic | Water, dissolved O2 and CO2, minerals, light, temperature, bottom mud |
| Producers | Phytoplankton (algae), rooted plants (*Hydrilla*), floating plants (water lettuce, duckweed) |
| Primary consumers | Zooplankton, snails, insect larvae, tadpoles |
| Secondary consumers | Small fish, frogs, water beetles |
| Tertiary consumers | Large fish, water snakes, kingfishers, herons |
| Decomposers | Bacteria and fungi in the bottom mud |

### Lake ecosystem

Larger and deeper than a pond. A lake has zones:

```text
Shore  ───────────── water surface ─────────────
  │ LITTORAL zone  │        LIMNETIC zone          (light reaches; phytoplankton, fish)
  │ (shallow,      │ ─ ─ ─ ─ light limit ─ ─ ─ ─ ─
  │ rooted plants) │        PROFUNDAL zone         (dark, cold; few organisms)
  └────────────────┴──── BENTHIC zone (bottom mud: decomposers, worms) ─────
```

Lakes are classed as **oligotrophic** (clear, nutrient-poor, rich in oxygen) or **eutrophic** (nutrient-rich, algal blooms, low oxygen in deep water). Thermal stratification develops in summer in deep lakes in temperate regions.

### Stream and river ecosystems

Flowing (lotic) water. Features: continuous flow in one direction, well-oxygenated water, organisms adapted to current (attached algae, insects with hooks, streamlined fish). A **stream** is small, fast and cool near its source; a **river** is larger, slower and warmer in the plains, carrying silt. Rivers carry nutrients and also pollutants downstream, supply water for drinking and irrigation, and form fertile plains.

### Ocean ecosystem

Oceans cover about 71% of the earth's surface. Features: salt water, great depth, tides and currents.

| Zone | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Coastal (intertidal) zone | Covered and uncovered by tides; rich in life |
| Open sea, upper lit (photic) zone | Phytoplankton make most of the ocean's food |
| Deep sea (aphotic) zone | Dark, cold, high pressure; animals feed on matter falling from above |
| Special areas | Coral reefs (very high biodiversity), mangroves |

Importance: food (fish), oxygen from phytoplankton, regulation of climate, minerals, oil and gas, and marine sources of drugs (some anticancer and antibacterial compounds come from marine organisms).

### Estuary ecosystem

An **estuary** is a partly enclosed coastal area where a river meets the sea, so fresh and salt water mix (brackish water). Examples: mouths of the Ganga (Sundarbans), Mahanadi, Godavari.

Features: changing salinity with tides, high nutrients brought by rivers, high productivity, mangroves and salt marshes, nurseries for fish and prawns. Organisms are adapted to changing salinity. Estuaries filter pollutants and protect the coast from storms, but are threatened by pollution, reclamation and over-fishing.

## Remember

- Ecosystem = biotic community + abiotic environment working as one unit (term by Tansley, 1935).
- Structure: abiotic (inorganic, organic, climatic) + biotic (producers, consumers, decomposers).
- Functions: energy flow, nutrient cycling, productivity, succession.
- Energy flow is one-way; about 10% passes to the next level (Lindeman).
- Food chains: grazing and detritus; a food web makes the system stable.
- Pyramid of energy is always upright; numbers can be spindle-shaped (tree) or inverted (parasites), biomass can be inverted (pond, ocean).
- Nitrogen cycle: fixation, assimilation, ammonification, nitrification, denitrification.
- Succession: pioneer → seral stages → climax; primary and secondary.
- Ecosystem types: forest, grassland, desert, and aquatic (pond, lake, stream, river, ocean, estuary).
- Lake zones: littoral, limnetic, profundal, benthic. Estuary = brackish, very productive.
