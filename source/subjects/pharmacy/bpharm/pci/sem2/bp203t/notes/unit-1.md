# Unit 1: Biomolecules and bioenergetics

Every living cell is built from a small set of molecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins (made of amino acids) and nucleic acids. This unit describes what each group is, how it is classified and what it does in the body. The second half explains bioenergetics: how cells decide whether a reaction can happen, and how ATP and other energy-rich compounds carry energy from fuel breakdown to work.

## 1.1 Biomolecules: an overview

| Biomolecule | Building unit | Main bond joining units | Main roles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Monosaccharides (glucose) | Glycosidic bond | Fuel, energy store (glycogen), structure (cellulose), cell recognition |
| Lipids | Fatty acids + glycerol (and others) | Ester bond | Energy store, membranes, hormones, insulation, fat-soluble vitamins |
| Proteins | Amino acids | Peptide bond | Enzymes, transport, structure, hormones, antibodies, contraction |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotides | 3′,5′-phosphodiester bond | Store and express genetic information |

## 1.2 Carbohydrates

**Definition.** Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones, or compounds that give these on hydrolysis. Many have the formula (CH2O)n, which is why the name means "hydrates of carbon".

### Classification

```text
Carbohydrates
├── Monosaccharides (cannot be hydrolysed further)
│     ├── by carbon number: triose (C3, glyceraldehyde), tetrose (C4, erythrose),
│     │     pentose (C5, ribose, xylulose), hexose (C6, glucose, fructose, galactose)
│     └── by functional group: aldoses (aldehyde: glucose) and ketoses (keto: fructose)
├── Oligosaccharides (2–10 monosaccharide units)
│     └── Disaccharides: sucrose, lactose, maltose
└── Polysaccharides (more than 10 units)
      ├── Homopolysaccharides (one kind of unit): starch, glycogen, cellulose, inulin, dextrin, chitin
      └── Heteropolysaccharides (more than one kind): glycosaminoglycans such as
            hyaluronic acid, heparin, chondroitin sulphate, dermatan and keratan sulphate
```

### Important disaccharides

| Disaccharide | Units | Linkage | Reducing? | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sucrose | Glucose + fructose | α1→β2 | No (both anomeric carbons are used) | Cane and beet sugar |
| Lactose | Galactose + glucose | β1→4 | Yes | Milk |
| Maltose | Glucose + glucose | α1→4 | Yes | Starch digestion, malt |

### Important polysaccharides

- **Starch** (plant store): amylose (unbranched, α1→4) and amylopectin (α1→4 chains with α1→6 branches). Gives a blue colour with iodine.
- **Glycogen** (animal store, liver and muscle): like amylopectin but more highly branched; red-brown colour with iodine.
- **Cellulose**: β1→4 glucose chains; humans lack the enzyme to digest it, so it acts as dietary fibre.
- **Inulin**: a fructose polymer; used to measure the glomerular filtration rate.
- **Heparin**: a sulphated glycosaminoglycan; natural anticoagulant.
- **Hyaluronic acid**: lubricant in joints and part of the ground substance of connective tissue.

### Isomerism (common short-note topic)

- **D and L forms** — decided by the –OH on the carbon farthest from the carbonyl group; body sugars are mostly D.
- **Epimers** — differ at one carbon only: glucose and galactose (C4), glucose and mannose (C2).
- **Anomers** — the α and β ring forms that differ at the new carbon (C1 in glucose) made when the ring closes.
- **Mutarotation** — a freshly made solution of α-D-glucose slowly changes its optical rotation (from about +112° towards about +52.7°) as α and β forms reach equilibrium.

### Biological roles of carbohydrates

1. Main fuel: glucose is the preferred fuel of the brain and the only fuel of red cells.
2. Storage as glycogen in liver (keeps blood glucose up) and muscle (fuel for contraction).
3. Structure: cellulose in plants, chitin in insects, glycosaminoglycans in cartilage.
4. Parts of other molecules: ribose and deoxyribose in nucleic acids; glycoproteins and glycolipids on cell surfaces act in cell recognition and blood groups.
5. Dietary fibre aids bowel movement.

## 1.3 Lipids

**Definition.** Lipids are a varied group of compounds that are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents such as chloroform, ether and benzene, and that are related to fatty acids.

### Classification (Bloor)

| Class | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple lipids | Esters of fatty acids with alcohols | Fats and oils (triacylglycerols), waxes |
| Compound (complex) lipids | Esters with extra groups | Phospholipids (lecithin = phosphatidylcholine, cephalin = phosphatidylethanolamine, phosphatidylinositol, cardiolipin, sphingomyelin); glycolipids (cerebrosides, gangliosides); lipoproteins |
| Derived lipids | Obtained by hydrolysis of the above | Fatty acids, glycerol, cholesterol and other steroids, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), ketone bodies |
| Miscellaneous | Lipid-like compounds | Carotenoids, squalene, terpenes |

### Fatty acids

- **Saturated**: palmitic acid (C16), stearic acid (C18).
- **Monounsaturated**: oleic acid (C18, one double bond, ω-9).
- **Polyunsaturated**: linoleic acid (C18:2, ω-6), α-linolenic acid (C18:3, ω-3), arachidonic acid (C20:4, ω-6).
- **Essential fatty acids**: linoleic and α-linolenic acid must come from the diet because humans cannot put double bonds beyond carbon 9. Arachidonic acid can be made from linoleic acid and is the parent of prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes (eicosanoids).

### Biological roles of lipids

1. Concentrated fuel store: fat gives about 9 kcal/g compared with about 4 kcal/g for carbohydrate and protein.
2. Structural parts of membranes (phospholipids, cholesterol).
3. Insulation and protection of organs (subcutaneous and visceral fat).
4. Precursors of steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D (from cholesterol) and of eicosanoids (from arachidonic acid).
5. Carry fat-soluble vitamins; lung surfactant (dipalmitoyl lecithin) prevents collapse of alveoli.

## 1.4 Amino acids

**Definition.** Amino acids are organic compounds with an amino group and a carboxyl group attached to the same (α) carbon, plus a side chain (R). Twenty standard amino acids make proteins. All except glycine are optically active, and those in proteins are of the L-form.

At physiological pH an amino acid exists as a **zwitterion** (–NH3+ and –COO−). The pH at which its net charge is zero is its **isoelectric pH (pI)**; solubility is lowest at this pH.

### Classification

**1. By side chain**

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Non-polar (hydrophobic) | Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, proline, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan |
| Polar, uncharged | Serine, threonine, cysteine, tyrosine, asparagine, glutamine |
| Acidic (negative) | Aspartic acid, glutamic acid |
| Basic (positive) | Lysine, arginine, histidine |

**2. By nutritional need**

- **Essential** (must be in the diet): phenylalanine, valine, threonine, tryptophan, isoleucine, methionine, histidine, leucine, lysine. Arginine is semi-essential (needed in the diet during growth). Memory aid: **PVT TIM HALL** (P-V-T, T-I-M, H-A-L-L, with A for arginine).
- **Non-essential**: made in the body (alanine, glycine, serine, aspartate, glutamate and others).

**3. By metabolic fate**

- **Purely ketogenic**: leucine and lysine (give acetyl-CoA or acetoacetate).
- **Both ketogenic and glucogenic**: phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, isoleucine.
- **Glucogenic**: all the rest (can form glucose).

**Special amino acids**: selenocysteine (the 21st amino acid), and non-protein amino acids such as ornithine and citrulline (urea cycle) and GABA (neurotransmitter).

### Biological roles

Building blocks of proteins; precursors of hormones and amines (tyrosine → catecholamines and thyroxine; tryptophan → serotonin, melatonin and niacin; histidine → histamine; glycine → heme, creatine and purines); neurotransmitters (glutamate, glycine, GABA); glucose source during fasting.

## 1.5 Proteins

**Definition.** Proteins are large polymers of L-amino acids joined by peptide bonds (–CO–NH–).

### Levels of structure

| Level | What it means | Bonds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | Sequence of amino acids | Peptide bonds (covalent) |
| Secondary | Local folding: α-helix and β-pleated sheet | Hydrogen bonds between backbone groups |
| Tertiary | Overall 3-D folding of one chain | Disulphide, hydrogen, ionic and hydrophobic bonds |
| Quaternary | Arrangement of two or more chains | Non-covalent bonds (e.g. haemoglobin, four chains) |

**Denaturation** is the loss of secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure (and of function) by heat, extremes of pH, heavy metals, urea or organic solvents, with the primary structure left intact.

### Classification

**By composition**

- **Simple proteins** (amino acids only): albumins (soluble in water, coagulated by heat — egg and serum albumin), globulins, glutelins, prolamines, histones, protamines, scleroproteins (keratin, collagen, elastin).
- **Conjugated proteins** (protein + non-protein prosthetic group): glycoproteins (mucin), lipoproteins (LDL, HDL), nucleoproteins (chromatin), phosphoproteins (casein), chromoproteins (haemoglobin, cytochromes), metalloproteins (ceruloplasmin with copper, ferritin with iron).
- **Derived proteins**: primary (denatured or coagulated proteins) and secondary (proteoses, peptones, peptides formed by partial hydrolysis).

**By shape**: fibrous (collagen, keratin — insoluble, structural) and globular (albumin, enzymes, haemoglobin — soluble, functional).

### Biological roles

| Function | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Catalysis | Enzymes (amylase, trypsin) |
| Transport | Haemoglobin (O2), albumin (fatty acids, drugs, bilirubin), transferrin (iron) |
| Structure | Collagen, keratin, elastin |
| Defence | Immunoglobulins, fibrinogen in clotting |
| Regulation | Insulin, growth hormone, receptors |
| Movement | Actin and myosin |
| Storage | Ferritin (iron), casein |

## 1.6 Nucleic acids

**Definition.** Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides that store and pass on genetic information.

- **Nitrogen bases**: purines — adenine (A) and guanine (G); pyrimidines — cytosine (C), thymine (T, DNA only) and uracil (U, RNA only).
- **Nucleoside** = base + pentose sugar (adenosine, guanosine).
- **Nucleotide** = nucleoside + phosphate (AMP, ATP).
- DNA has deoxyribose; RNA has ribose.
- **Chargaff's rules (DNA)**: A = T and G = C, so purines equal pyrimidines.

**Biological roles**: DNA stores genetic information; RNA (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) expresses it as proteins; free nucleotides act as energy carriers (ATP, GTP), second messengers (cAMP, cGMP) and parts of coenzymes (NAD+, FAD, coenzyme A). Structure in detail is in Unit 4.

## 1.7 Bioenergetics: free energy

**Bioenergetics** is the study of energy changes in biochemical reactions.

**Free energy (G)** is the part of a system's energy that can do useful work at constant temperature and pressure. The change in free energy (ΔG) decides whether a reaction can occur on its own.

**Relationship between free energy, enthalpy and entropy**

**ΔG = ΔH − TΔS**

- ΔH = change in enthalpy (heat content).
- ΔS = change in entropy (randomness or disorder).
- T = absolute temperature (K).

| Sign of ΔG | Type of reaction | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negative (ΔG < 0) | **Exergonic** | Releases free energy; can occur spontaneously (example: ATP hydrolysis, glucose oxidation) |
| Positive (ΔG > 0) | **Endergonic** | Needs input of energy; does not occur on its own (example: glucose + Pi → glucose-6-phosphate) |
| Zero | Equilibrium | No net change |

**Points to note**

- ΔG tells whether a reaction **can** occur, not how fast. Speed depends on the activation energy, which enzymes lower.
- **Standard free energy change (ΔG°′)** is ΔG at pH 7 with all reactants at 1 M. It is linked to the equilibrium constant: **ΔG°′ = −2.303 RT log K′eq**. A large K′eq means a large negative ΔG°′.
- ΔG values of steps in a pathway are additive. An endergonic step can be driven by **coupling** it to an exergonic one, usually ATP hydrolysis.

```text
Coupling:  Glucose + Pi → Glucose-6-P           ΔG°′ = +13.8 kJ/mol (cannot go alone)
           ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi                 ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ/mol
           ---------------------------------------------------------------
           Glucose + ATP → Glucose-6-P + ADP    ΔG°′ = −16.7 kJ/mol (proceeds; hexokinase)
```

## 1.8 Redox potential

Oxidation is loss of electrons (or hydrogen); reduction is gain. They always occur together as a **redox couple** (e.g. NAD+/NADH).

**Redox potential (E°′)** measures how readily a couple accepts electrons, compared with the hydrogen electrode, at pH 7.

- A more **negative** E°′ means a stronger tendency to **give** electrons (NAD+/NADH about −0.32 V).
- A more **positive** E°′ means a stronger tendency to **accept** electrons (½O2/H2O about +0.82 V).
- Electrons flow from negative to positive potential. This is the order of carriers in the electron transport chain.

Relation to free energy: **ΔG°′ = −nFΔE°′** (n = electrons transferred, F = Faraday constant, 96.5 kJ/V/mol).

Worked example: NADH → O2, ΔE°′ = +0.82 − (−0.32) = 1.14 V; ΔG°′ = −2 × 96.5 × 1.14 ≈ **−220 kJ/mol** (about −52.6 kcal/mol). This large energy release is captured as ATP in oxidative phosphorylation (Unit 2).

## 1.9 Energy-rich compounds

**High-energy compounds** release a large amount of free energy (usually more than about 25–30 kJ/mol) when a particular bond is hydrolysed. The bond is often written with a squiggle (~P). The energy is not stored "in the bond" itself; it comes from the difference in stability between reactants and products (resonance stabilisation of products, relief of charge repulsion, ionisation).

**Classification**

| Class | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Phosphoanhydrides (pyrophosphates) | ATP, GTP, UTP, CTP; ADP; pyrophosphate (PPi) |
| Enol phosphates | Phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) |
| Acyl phosphates | 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate, carbamoyl phosphate, acetyl phosphate |
| Guanidino phosphates (phosphagens) | Creatine phosphate (vertebrate muscle), arginine phosphate (invertebrates) |
| Thioesters | Acetyl-CoA, succinyl-CoA |
| Sulphonium compounds | S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) |

**Standard free energy of hydrolysis (ΔG°′)**

| Compound | kJ/mol | kcal/mol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phosphoenolpyruvate | −61.9 | −14.8 |
| 1,3-Bisphosphoglycerate | −49.3 | −11.8 |
| Creatine phosphate | −43.1 | −10.3 |
| ATP → ADP + Pi | −30.5 | −7.3 |
| Glucose-1-phosphate (low energy) | −20.9 | −5.0 |
| Glucose-6-phosphate (low energy) | −13.8 | −3.3 |

ATP sits in the **middle** of the scale. This lets it accept a phosphate group from higher compounds (PEP, 1,3-BPG) and give one to lower compounds (glucose → glucose-6-phosphate). It is the energy currency, not an energy store.

## 1.10 ATP and its biological significance

**Structure**: adenine + ribose + three phosphate groups (α, β, γ). The two terminal (β–γ and α–β) bonds are phosphoanhydride (high-energy) bonds.

**How ATP is made**

1. **Oxidative phosphorylation** in mitochondria — most ATP.
2. **Substrate-level phosphorylation** — directly from high-energy intermediates (phosphoglycerate kinase, pyruvate kinase, succinyl-CoA synthetase).
3. From creatine phosphate in muscle (creatine kinase) during sudden effort.

**Uses of ATP**

1. **Chemical work**: biosynthesis of glycogen, proteins, fats and nucleic acids; activation of glucose, fatty acids and amino acids.
2. **Mechanical work**: muscle contraction, movement of cilia and flagella.
3. **Transport work**: active transport by Na+/K+-ATPase and other pumps.
4. **Signalling**: substrate of protein kinases (phosphorylation of enzymes) and of adenylyl cyclase (makes cAMP).
5. Source of other nucleotides and part of coenzymes (NAD+, FAD, CoA all contain an adenosine portion).
6. **ATP–ADP cycle**: a resting adult turns over a quantity of ATP close to body weight each day, because ATP is used and remade within minutes.

## 1.11 Cyclic AMP and its biological significance

**Cyclic AMP (cAMP, 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate)** is made from ATP by **adenylyl (adenylate) cyclase** in the cell membrane and broken down to 5′-AMP by **phosphodiesterase**.

```text
Hormone (first messenger) → receptor → Gs protein → adenylyl cyclase (ON)
        ATP → cAMP (second messenger) + PPi
        cAMP → activates protein kinase A (PKA)
        PKA phosphorylates enzymes → cell response
        cAMP → 5'-AMP by phosphodiesterase (signal off)
```

**Significance**

1. **Second messenger** for many hormones: glucagon, adrenaline (β receptors), ACTH, TSH, LH, FSH, PTH, calcitonin and vasopressin (V2).
2. **Glycogen breakdown**: PKA activates phosphorylase and inactivates glycogen synthase → blood glucose rises.
3. **Lipolysis**: PKA activates hormone-sensitive lipase in adipose tissue.
4. Gene expression through the CREB protein; in bacteria cAMP with CAP protein controls the lac operon.
5. Water reabsorption in the kidney (vasopressin), steroid hormone synthesis (ACTH), heart rate and force (β1).
6. **Drug relevance**:
   - Methylxanthines (caffeine, theophylline) inhibit phosphodiesterase → cAMP rises (bronchodilation).
   - Cholera toxin keeps Gs active → cAMP rises in gut cells → severe watery diarrhoea.
   - Pertussis toxin blocks Gi (the inhibitory G protein).
   - Gi-linked receptors (α2, M2, opioid) lower cAMP.

## Remember

- Carbohydrates: polyhydroxy aldehydes/ketones; sucrose is non-reducing; glucose and galactose are C4 epimers; α and β forms are anomers.
- Lipid classes: simple, compound, derived; essential fatty acids are linoleic and α-linolenic acid.
- Purely ketogenic amino acids: leucine and lysine. Essential: PVT TIM HALL.
- Protein structure: primary (peptide bonds) → secondary (H bonds) → tertiary → quaternary; denaturation spares the primary structure.
- ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; negative ΔG = exergonic; ΔG°′ = −2.303 RT log K′eq = −nFΔE°′.
- NAD+/NADH −0.32 V and ½O2/H2O +0.82 V; NADH → O2 gives about −220 kJ/mol.
- ATP hydrolysis ΔG°′ = −30.5 kJ/mol (−7.3 kcal/mol); PEP (−61.9) and creatine phosphate (−43.1) are higher.
- cAMP: adenylyl cyclase makes it, phosphodiesterase destroys it, PKA carries out its effects; caffeine and theophylline block phosphodiesterase.
