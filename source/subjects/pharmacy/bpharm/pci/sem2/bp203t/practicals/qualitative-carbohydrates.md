# Qualitative analysis of carbohydrates

**Aim:** To identify the given carbohydrate (glucose, fructose, lactose, maltose, sucrose or starch) by a systematic series of chemical tests.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Concentrated sulphuric and hydrochloric acids are corrosive: add them slowly down the side of the tube, wear goggles, and never point a heated tube at anyone.

## Principle

Carbohydrates are identified by their general reactions and by the special groups they carry:

- **Molisch test (all carbohydrates)**: concentrated H2SO4 dehydrates sugars to furfural (pentoses) or hydroxymethylfurfural (hexoses), which condense with α-naphthol to give a violet ring.
- **Iodine test (polysaccharides)**: iodine fits inside the helix of amylose, giving a blue colour; glycogen and dextrins give red-brown.
- **Benedict's and Fehling's tests (reducing sugars)**: a free aldehyde or keto group reduces Cu2+ (blue) to Cu+, forming red cuprous oxide (Cu2O) on boiling in alkaline medium.
- **Barfoed's test (monosaccharide vs disaccharide)**: copper acetate in dilute acetic acid is a weak oxidising reagent; monosaccharides reduce it within about 2 minutes, reducing disaccharides only after longer boiling.
- **Seliwanoff's test (ketoses)**: HCl dehydrates ketohexoses quickly to hydroxymethylfurfural, which gives a cherry-red colour with resorcinol; aldoses react much more slowly.
- **Osazone test**: reducing sugars react with excess phenylhydrazine on heating to give yellow crystals (osazones) of characteristic shape and time of formation.
- **Hydrolysis of sucrose**: dilute HCl splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, which then reduce Benedict's reagent.

## Requirements

Test solutions (1–2% of each sugar, 1% starch), Molisch reagent (5% α-naphthol in ethanol), concentrated H2SO4, Benedict's qualitative reagent, Fehling's A and B, Barfoed's reagent, Seliwanoff's reagent (resorcinol in dilute HCl), phenylhydrazine hydrochloride, sodium acetate, iodine solution, dilute HCl, sodium carbonate, test tubes, water bath, microscope and slides.

## Procedure

1. **Molisch test**: to 2 ml of the solution add 2 drops of Molisch reagent, mix, then pour 2 ml of concentrated H2SO4 gently down the side of the tilted tube. A violet ring at the junction shows a carbohydrate.
2. **Iodine test**: to 2 ml of solution add 2 drops of iodine solution. Blue colour (disappearing on heating, returning on cooling) shows starch.
3. **Benedict's test**: to 5 ml of Benedict's reagent add 8 drops of solution, boil in a water bath for 3–5 minutes. Green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate shows a reducing sugar.
4. **Fehling's test**: mix 1 ml each of Fehling's A and B, add 2 ml of solution and boil. Red precipitate shows a reducing sugar.
5. **Barfoed's test**: to 2 ml of Barfoed's reagent add 1 ml of solution and keep in a boiling water bath. Note the time a red precipitate appears: within about 2 minutes = monosaccharide; after about 7–10 minutes = reducing disaccharide.
6. **Seliwanoff's test**: to 3 ml of reagent add 1 ml of solution and heat in a boiling water bath. Cherry-red colour within about 1 minute shows a ketose (fructose, or sucrose after it is hydrolysed by the acid).
7. **Osazone test**: to 5 ml of solution add about 0.3 g phenylhydrazine hydrochloride and 0.5 g sodium acetate, warm to dissolve, and keep in a boiling water bath. Note the time of formation; cool and observe the crystals under the microscope.
8. **Sucrose hydrolysis**: boil 5 ml of sucrose solution with 2 drops of dilute HCl for 5 minutes, cool, neutralise with sodium carbonate, and repeat Benedict's test.

## Precautions

1. Use clean tubes; traces of reducing substances give false results.
2. Add concentrated H2SO4 slowly and do not shake in the Molisch test.
3. Note the exact time in Barfoed's and Seliwanoff's tests; prolonged heating gives false positives.
4. Neutralise acid before doing Benedict's test on hydrolysed sucrose.
5. Phenylhydrazine is toxic; avoid skin contact.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is sucrose non-reducing?** Both anomeric carbons (C1 of glucose and C2 of fructose) are used in the glycosidic bond.
2. **What is the principle of Benedict's test?** Reducing sugars reduce Cu2+ to red Cu2O in alkaline solution on heating.
3. **Why is Benedict's preferred to Fehling's?** Its reagent is stable as a single solution and it is less affected by uric acid and creatinine.
4. **Why do glucose and fructose give the same osazone?** Osazone formation involves C1 and C2; the two sugars differ only there.
5. **What does Barfoed's test distinguish?** Monosaccharides from reducing disaccharides.
6. **Which sugar gives Seliwanoff's test quickly?** Fructose (a ketohexose).
7. **Why does the blue starch–iodine colour vanish on heating?** Heat opens the amylose helix and releases iodine; the colour returns on cooling.
