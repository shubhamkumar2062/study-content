# Effect of temperature on salivary amylase activity

**Aim:** To study the effect of temperature on the activity of salivary amylase and to find its optimum temperature.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Use only your own saliva. Take care with boiling water baths.

## Principle

Enzyme-catalysed reactions speed up as temperature rises because molecules collide more often and with more energy (roughly doubling for each 10 °C rise over a moderate range). Above a certain temperature, however, the enzyme protein is **denatured** and activity falls rapidly. The temperature giving maximum activity under the test conditions is the **optimum temperature** — for human salivary amylase about **37 °C** (activity is often similar up to about 40 °C).

```text
Activity │          ●
         │       ●     ●
         │    ●           ●
         │ ●                 ●
         │●                    ●
         └─────────────────────────── Temperature
          0   20   37   50   70   100 °C
              (bell-shaped curve, optimum near 37 °C)
```

At low temperature (0–4 °C) the enzyme is inactive but **not destroyed**; it recovers when warmed. After boiling it is permanently inactivated.

Activity is measured either by the **achromic point** (time to lose colour with iodine — shorter time = more activity) or by **maltose released** (DNSA method).

## Requirements

1% starch in phosphate buffer pH 6.8 with NaCl, diluted saliva, iodine solution, spotting tile, test tubes, water baths or beakers at 0 °C (ice), room temperature, 37 °C, 50 °C, 70 °C and 100 °C, thermometer, stopwatch.

## Procedure (achromic point method)

1. Take six tubes each with 5 ml buffered starch; place one in each bath (0, room temperature, 37, 50, 70 and 100 °C) for 5 minutes.
2. Place six tubes each with 1 ml diluted saliva in the same baths for 5 minutes (so the enzyme is at the test temperature).
3. Add the saliva to the starch at the same temperature, mix and start the stopwatch.
4. Every 30 seconds transfer a drop from each tube to iodine on the tile.
5. Record the achromic time for each temperature (stop recording at about 30 minutes; write "> 30 min" if colour persists).
6. Take the 0 °C tube out, warm it to 37 °C and observe that hydrolysis now proceeds.
7. Calculate relative activity as 1/achromic time (min⁻¹) and plot against temperature.

## Observations and calculation

| Temperature (°C) | Achromic time (min) | Relative activity (1/time, min⁻¹) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | > 30 | ≈ 0 |
| Room (about 25) | | |
| 37 | | |
| 50 | | |
| 70 | | |
| 100 | No hydrolysis | 0 |

**Worked example.** If achromic times are 12 min (25 °C), 5 min (37 °C) and 9 min (50 °C), relative activities are 0.083, 0.20 and 0.11 min⁻¹. The highest value, at 37 °C, shows the optimum.

## Result

The activity of salivary amylase was maximum at ______ °C (optimum temperature). Activity was low at 0 °C (reversible) and absent after boiling (irreversible denaturation).

## Precautions

1. Bring both enzyme and substrate to the test temperature before mixing.
2. Keep each bath at a constant temperature throughout.
3. Use the same saliva dilution for all tubes.
4. Remove drops quickly so the tube temperature does not change.

## Viva questions

1. **Why does activity fall at high temperature?** The enzyme protein is denatured and loses its active-site shape.
2. **Is cold inactivation permanent?** No; the enzyme regains activity on warming.
3. **What is Q10?** The factor by which the rate increases for a 10 °C rise; about 2 for many enzymes.
4. **Why is optimum temperature not a constant?** It depends on time of incubation, pH and substrate concentration.
5. **Why are vaccines and insulin stored cold?** Low temperature slows denaturation and degradation of proteins.
