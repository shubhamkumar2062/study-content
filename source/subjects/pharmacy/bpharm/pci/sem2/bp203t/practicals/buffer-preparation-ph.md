# Preparation of buffer solutions and measurement of pH

**Aim:** To prepare acetate and phosphate buffers of required pH using the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and to measure their pH with a pH meter.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Handle the glass electrode gently and keep it wet.

## Principle

A **buffer** is a solution that resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added. It contains a weak acid and its salt (conjugate base), or a weak base and its salt.

**Henderson–Hasselbalch equation**: **pH = pKa + log ([salt] / [acid])**

- When [salt] = [acid], pH = pKa.
- A buffer works best within about pKa ± 1, and its capacity is greatest at pH = pKa.

| Buffer | Components | pKa (about, 25 °C) | Useful pH range |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acetate | Acetic acid / sodium acetate | 4.76 | 3.8–5.8 |
| Phosphate | NaH2PO4 / Na2HPO4 | about 6.8–7.2 (depends on ionic strength) | 6.0–8.0 |

**pH meter**: measures the potential of a glass electrode against a reference electrode; the potential changes by about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C. It is calibrated with standard buffers (pH 4.0, 7.0 and 9.2) before use.

Body buffers: bicarbonate (main buffer of plasma), phosphate (intracellular, urine) and proteins (haemoglobin).

## Requirements

0.2 M acetic acid, 0.2 M sodium acetate, 0.2 M NaH2PO4, 0.2 M Na2HPO4, standard buffer tablets or solutions (pH 4.0, 7.0, 9.2), 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH, pH meter with combined glass electrode, beakers, pipettes, burettes, wash bottle.

## Procedure

1. **Calibrate the pH meter**: switch on and allow warming; rinse electrode with distilled water and blot; immerse in pH 7.0 buffer and adjust; rinse and check with pH 4.0 (or 9.2) and adjust the slope.
2. **Calculate volumes** for the required acetate buffer using the equation (see worked example).
3. Mix the calculated volumes of 0.2 M acetic acid and 0.2 M sodium acetate in a beaker; make up to the required volume.
4. Measure the pH; note the difference from the calculated value. Adjust with a few drops of acid or salt solution if needed.
5. Prepare a phosphate buffer the same way from NaH2PO4 and Na2HPO4.
6. **Buffer action**: to 20 ml of the buffer and to 20 ml of distilled water add 1 ml of 0.1 M HCl; measure pH of each. Repeat with 0.1 M NaOH.
7. Rinse the electrode and store it in its storage solution.

## Observations and calculations

**Worked example (acetate buffer, pH 5.0, 20 ml).**

log ([salt]/[acid]) = 5.0 − 4.76 = 0.24 → [salt]/[acid] = 10^0.24 ≈ 1.74

Since both stock solutions are 0.2 M, volumes are in the same ratio: salt = 20 × 1.74 / 2.74 ≈ **12.7 ml** sodium acetate; acid ≈ **7.3 ml** acetic acid.

| Buffer | Calculated pH | Measured pH | pH after 1 ml 0.1 M HCl | pH after 1 ml 0.1 M NaOH |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Acetate | 5.0 | | | |
| Phosphate | | | | |
| Distilled water | — | | | |

Expected: the buffer pH changes by only a small fraction of a unit, while water changes by several units.

## Precautions

1. Calibrate the pH meter with fresh standard buffers at the same temperature as the samples.
2. Rinse and blot (do not rub) the electrode between solutions.
3. Never let the glass bulb dry out.
4. Use CO2-free distilled water where accuracy matters.
5. Stir gently while reading and allow the reading to become steady.

## Viva questions

1. **Define buffer capacity.** The amount of strong acid or base needed to change the pH of 1 litre of buffer by one unit; maximum when pH = pKa.
2. **What is the main buffer of blood?** The bicarbonate–carbonic acid system (normal blood pH about 7.35–7.45).
3. **Why do calculated and measured pH differ?** Activity coefficients, temperature and ionic strength alter the effective pKa.
4. **Why is the glass electrode stored wet?** The hydrated gel layer of the glass membrane is needed for a correct response.
5. **Name a buffer used in pharmaceutical products.** Phosphate, citrate, acetate and borate buffers in injections and eye drops.
