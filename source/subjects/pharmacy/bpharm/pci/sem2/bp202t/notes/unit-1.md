# Unit 1: Classification, nomenclature and isomerism

Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Carbon forms four bonds, joins to itself in long chains and rings (catenation), and bonds strongly to H, O, N, S and the halogens. This is why there are millions of organic compounds, and why almost every drug you will study is organic. This unit teaches three basic skills: sorting compounds into families, giving them correct names, and recognising compounds that share a formula but differ in structure (isomers). A short toolkit on electronic effects and reaction intermediates closes the unit, because Units 2–5 use it in every mechanism.

## 1.1 Classification of organic compounds

### By the carbon skeleton

```text
                       ORGANIC COMPOUNDS
                 ┌─────────────┴──────────────┐
          ACYCLIC (open chain)          CYCLIC (closed chain)
          e.g. butane, ethanol       ┌──────────┴───────────┐
          straight or branched   HOMOCYCLIC              HETEROCYCLIC
                                 (ring of C only)        (ring has N, O or S)
                              ┌──────┴───────┐       e.g. pyridine, furan,
                          ALICYCLIC      AROMATIC         thiophene
                       cyclohexane,     benzene,
                       cyclopentane     naphthalene
```

- **Acyclic (aliphatic, open-chain)** — carbons in a straight or branched chain. Examples: propane, acetic acid, glycerol.
- **Alicyclic** — carbon rings that behave like aliphatic compounds. Examples: cyclopropane, cyclohexane, menthol.
- **Aromatic (benzenoid)** — contain one or more benzene rings with 6 delocalised π electrons. Examples: benzene, toluene, aspirin.
- **Heterocyclic** — ring contains at least one atom other than carbon. Examples: pyridine (N), furan (O), thiophene (S). Many drugs are heterocycles (nicotine, diazepam, penicillin).

### By saturation

- **Saturated** — only C–C single bonds (alkanes, cycloalkanes).
- **Unsaturated** — at least one C=C or C≡C (alkenes, alkynes, dienes).

### By functional group

A **functional group** is the atom or group of atoms that decides the characteristic reactions of a molecule. Compounds with the same functional group form a **homologous series**.

**Features of a homologous series**

1. Same general formula (alkanes CnH2n+2, alkenes CnH2n, alkynes CnH2n−2, alcohols CnH2n+1OH).
2. Neighbouring members differ by –CH2– (14 mass units).
3. Similar chemical properties; physical properties (boiling point, density) change gradually.
4. Prepared by similar general methods.

**Main functional groups**

| Family | Group | General formula | Example (IUPAC / common) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alkane | C–C | R–H | Ethane |
| Alkene | C=C | R–CH=CH2 | Ethene / ethylene |
| Alkyne | C≡C | R–C≡CH | Ethyne / acetylene |
| Alkyl halide | –X | R–X | Chloroethane / ethyl chloride |
| Alcohol | –OH | R–OH | Ethanol / ethyl alcohol |
| Ether | –O– | R–O–R' | Ethoxyethane / diethyl ether |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | R–CHO | Ethanal / acetaldehyde |
| Ketone | >C=O | R–CO–R' | Propanone / acetone |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | R–COOH | Ethanoic acid / acetic acid |
| Ester | –COOR' | R–COOR' | Ethyl ethanoate / ethyl acetate |
| Acid chloride | –COCl | R–COCl | Ethanoyl chloride / acetyl chloride |
| Amide | –CONH2 | R–CONH2 | Ethanamide / acetamide |
| Amine | –NH2 | R–NH2 | Ethanamine / ethylamine |
| Nitrile | –C≡N | R–CN | Ethanenitrile / acetonitrile |
| Nitro | –NO2 | R–NO2 | Nitromethane |

## 1.2 Common (trivial) names

Before IUPAC rules, compounds were named from their source or a property: **formic acid** (from ants, Latin *formica*), **acetic acid** (vinegar, *acetum*), **urea** (urine), **wood spirit** (methanol, from wood distillation). Common names are still widely used in pharmacy and in the Indian Pharmacopoeia, so learn both.

**Alkyl group names you must know**

| Group | Structure | Common name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CH3– | methyl | methyl |
| CH3CH2– | ethyl | ethyl |
| CH3CH2CH2– | propyl | n-propyl |
| (CH3)2CH– | 1-methylethyl | isopropyl |
| CH3CH2CH2CH2– | butyl | n-butyl |
| (CH3)2CHCH2– | 2-methylpropyl | isobutyl |
| CH3CH2CH(CH3)– | 1-methylpropyl | sec-butyl |
| (CH3)3C– | 1,1-dimethylethyl | tert-butyl |
| (CH3)3CCH2– | 2,2-dimethylpropyl | neopentyl |
| CH2=CH– | ethenyl | vinyl |
| CH2=CH–CH2– | prop-2-en-1-yl | allyl |
| C6H5– | phenyl | phenyl |
| C6H5CH2– | phenylmethyl | benzyl |

**Prefix meanings in common names**

- **n-** (normal): straight chain, e.g. n-butane.
- **iso-**: a –CH(CH3)2 group at the end of a straight chain, e.g. isobutane (CH3)2CHCH3, isopentane.
- **neo-**: a –C(CH3)3 group at the end, e.g. neopentane C(CH3)4.
- **sec-** and **tert-**: the functional group is on a secondary or tertiary carbon, e.g. sec-butyl alcohol, tert-butyl alcohol.

**Classes of carbon and hydrogen.** A **primary (1°)** carbon is bonded to one other carbon, **secondary (2°)** to two, **tertiary (3°)** to three, **quaternary (4°)** to four. Hydrogens take the class of the carbon they sit on. This idea returns in halogenation (Unit 2) and in SN1/SN2 (Unit 3).

```text
            CH3            1° = carbons a (bonded to 1 C)
            |              2° = carbon c
  a CH3 – C – CH2 – CH3    4° = carbon b
            |
            CH3
   (b is quaternary, c is the CH2, the last CH3 is primary)
```

## 1.3 IUPAC system of nomenclature

The IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry) system gives one clear name for every structure. A name has three parts:

```text
     PREFIX(ES)   +   WORD ROOT   +   PRIMARY SUFFIX   +   SECONDARY SUFFIX
   (substituents,     (number of     (saturation:           (main functional
    alphabetical)      C in chain)    -ane, -ene, -yne)      group: -ol, -al …)

   e.g.  2-methyl       but            -an-                   -1-ol    →  2-methylbutan-1-ol
```

**Word roots (1 to 10 carbons)**

| C atoms | Root | C atoms | Root |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | meth- | 6 | hex- |
| 2 | eth- | 7 | hept- |
| 3 | prop- | 8 | oct- |
| 4 | but- | 9 | non- |
| 5 | pent- | 10 | dec- |

### Rules for open-chain compounds

1. **Find the parent chain** — the longest continuous carbon chain that contains the main functional group and as many multiple bonds as possible. If two chains are equally long, choose the one with more substituents.
2. **Number the chain** so that the main functional group gets the lowest locant; then multiple bonds; then substituents (**lowest locant rule**). If there is still a tie, the substituent cited first in alphabetical order gets the lower number.
3. **Name substituents** as prefixes with their position numbers: methyl, ethyl, chloro, bromo, nitro, methoxy.
4. **Use multiplying prefixes** di-, tri-, tetra- for repeated groups (2,2-dimethyl). Every repeat needs its own locant.
5. **Arrange prefixes alphabetically**, ignoring di-, tri-, sec-, tert- (but "iso" and "neo" are counted).
6. **Punctuation**: commas between numbers, hyphens between numbers and words, no space before the root.

### Priority of functional groups (decides the suffix)

When a molecule has more than one group, the highest-priority one becomes the suffix and the rest become prefixes.

| Priority | Group | As suffix | As prefix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Carboxylic acid | -oic acid | carboxy |
| 2 | Sulfonic acid | -sulfonic acid | sulfo |
| 3 | Ester | alkyl …-oate | alkoxycarbonyl |
| 4 | Acid halide | -oyl halide | halocarbonyl |
| 5 | Amide | -amide | carbamoyl |
| 6 | Nitrile | -nitrile | cyano |
| 7 | Aldehyde | -al | formyl / oxo |
| 8 | Ketone | -one | oxo |
| 9 | Alcohol | -ol | hydroxy |
| 10 | Amine | -amine | amino |
| — | Halide, nitro, ether | (prefix only) | halo, nitro, alkoxy |

Learn the short form: **acid > ester > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine**.

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name / note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CH3–CH(CH3)–CH2–CH3 | 2-methylbutane | isopentane |
| (CH3)3C–CH2–CH(CH3)2 | 2,2,4-trimethylpentane | isooctane (fuel standard) |
| CH3CH2–CH(C2H5)–CH2CH2CH3 | 3-ethylhexane | longest chain is 6 C |
| CH3–CH(C2H5)–CH2–CH3 | 3-methylpentane | not "2-ethylbutane" — the ethyl extends the chain |
| CH3–CH=CH–CH3 | but-2-ene | 2-butene |

| CH3–C≡C–CH2–CH3 | pent-2-yne | — |
| CH3–CH(OH)–CH3 | propan-2-ol | isopropyl alcohol |

| CH3–CO–CH2–CHO | 3-oxobutanal | aldehyde outranks ketone |

| CH2(OH)–CH(OH)–CH2(OH) | propane-1,2,3-triol | glycerol |

**How to work an example (3-chlorobutanoic acid):**

```text
   4     3     2     1
   CH3 – CH – CH2 – COOH        1. Main group = –COOH → suffix "-oic acid"
         |                      2. Chain has 4 C (C of COOH counted) → "butan"
         Cl                     3. Number from COOH carbon = C1
                                4. Cl on C3 → prefix "3-chloro"
   Name: 3-chlorobutanoic acid
```

**Common mistakes**

- Choosing a chain that is not the longest (an ethyl group at C2 means the chain is really longer).
- Numbering from the wrong end (always give the functional group the lower number).
- Forgetting to count the carbon of –CHO, –COOH or –CN in the parent chain.
- Writing prefixes in the order of their numbers instead of alphabetically (4-ethyl-2-methyl, not 2-methyl-4-ethyl).

### Carbocyclic compounds (rings up to 10 carbons)

Rules:

1. Add **cyclo-** before the root: cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclohexane.
2. A ring with one substituent needs no number: methylcyclohexane, chlorocyclopentane.
3. With two or more substituents, number the ring to give the lowest set of locants; on a tie, follow alphabetical order.
4. If the functional group is on the ring, its carbon is C1: cyclohexanol, cyclohexanone.
5. If a chain has more carbons than the ring, the ring becomes a substituent (cyclohexyl); otherwise the ring is the parent.
6. When –COOH, –CHO or –CN is attached to a ring, use the suffixes -carboxylic acid, -carbaldehyde, -carbonitrile (the carbon of the group is not counted in the ring).

| Structure | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- |
| Cyclohexane ring with –CH3 | methylcyclohexane |
| Cyclopentane with CH3 on adjacent carbons | 1,2-dimethylcyclopentane |
| Cyclohexane with –OH | cyclohexanol |
| Cyclohexane ring with C=O | cyclohexanone |
| Cyclohexanone with CH3 two carbons away from C=O | 3-methylcyclohexan-1-one |
| Cyclohexene with –OH next to the double bond | cyclohex-2-en-1-ol |
| Cyclohexane bearing –COOH | cyclohexanecarboxylic acid |
| Cyclopentane bearing –CHO | cyclopentanecarbaldehyde |
| Cyclopropane on a heptane chain | 1-cyclopropylheptane |

**Benzene derivatives (common names accepted by IUPAC):** toluene (methylbenzene), phenol (hydroxybenzene), aniline (benzenamine), benzaldehyde, benzoic acid, anisole (methoxybenzene). For two substituents, positions are **ortho (1,2)**, **meta (1,3)** and **para (1,4)**: p-nitrophenol = 4-nitrophenol. Ring chemistry itself is covered in Pharmaceutical Organic Chemistry II.

## 1.4 Isomerism

**Isomers** are compounds with the **same molecular formula** but different structures or arrangements, so they have different properties. The word comes from Greek *isos* (equal) and *meros* (parts).

```text
                          ISOMERISM
              ┌───────────────┴────────────────┐
     STRUCTURAL (constitutional)          STEREOISOMERISM
     atoms joined differently             same joining, different
   ┌──────┬────────┬───────┬──────┐       arrangement in space
 Chain Position Functional Metamerism  Tautomerism    (geometrical, optical,
                 group                (+ ring-chain)    conformational —
                                                        studied later)
```

### Structural isomerism

**1. Chain (skeletal) isomerism** — different carbon skeletons (straight vs branched).

```text
C5H12:   CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3          n-pentane       (bp 36 °C)
         CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3         isopentane      (2-methylbutane)
         C(CH3)4                  neopentane      (2,2-dimethylpropane, bp 9.5 °C)
```

More branching → smaller surface area → weaker van der Waals forces → lower boiling point.

Number of chain isomers of alkanes: C4H10 = 2, C5H12 = 3, C6H14 = 5, C7H16 = 9.

**2. Position isomerism** — same chain and same group, but the group or multiple bond is at a different position.

- C3H7OH: propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol.
- C4H8: but-1-ene and but-2-ene.
- C3H7Cl: 1-chloropropane and 2-chloropropane.

**3. Functional group isomerism** — same formula, different functional groups.

| Formula | Isomers |
| --- | --- |
| C2H6O | ethanol (alcohol) and methoxymethane (ether) |
| C3H6O | propanal (aldehyde) and propanone (ketone) |
| C3H6O2 | propanoic acid (acid) and methyl ethanoate (ester) |
| C3H9N | propan-1-amine (1°), N-methylethanamine (2°), N,N-dimethylmethanamine (3°) |
| C4H6 | buta-1,3-diene (diene) and but-1-yne (alkyne) |

**4. Metamerism** — same functional group, but different alkyl groups on either side of a polyvalent group (–O–, –CO–, –NH–, –S–).

- C4H10O ethers: ethoxyethane (C2H5–O–C2H5) and 1-methoxypropane (CH3–O–C3H7).
- C5H10O ketones: pentan-3-one (C2H5–CO–C2H5) and pentan-2-one (CH3–CO–C3H7).

**5. Tautomerism** — two isomers that change into each other rapidly and exist in **dynamic equilibrium**, usually by the shift of a proton and a double bond. The commonest is **keto–enol tautomerism**.

```text
      O                       OH
      ||                      |
  CH3–C–CH3      ⇌      CH2=C–CH3
   keto form               enol form
  (acetone, almost         (tiny amount)
   entirely keto)
```

Tautomers are true compounds (each can sometimes be isolated); resonance structures are not. The enol content rises when the enol is stabilised by conjugation or hydrogen bonding, as in ethyl acetoacetate and acetylacetone. Other types: nitro–aci-nitro and imine–enamine tautomerism.

**6. Ring–chain isomerism** — one isomer is open-chain, the other cyclic. C3H6: propene and cyclopropane. C4H8: but-1-ene and cyclobutane.

### Counting isomers: worked example C4H10O

```text
Alcohols (4):  butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol
Ethers   (3):  1-methoxypropane, 2-methoxypropane, ethoxyethane
Total structural isomers = 7
   chain isomers:     butan-1-ol / 2-methylpropan-1-ol
   position isomers:  butan-1-ol / butan-2-ol
   functional:        any alcohol / any ether
   metamers:          ethoxyethane / methoxypropane
```

C4H9Cl has **4** structural isomers: 1-chlorobutane, 2-chlorobutane, 1-chloro-2-methylpropane and 2-chloro-2-methylpropane.

**Why isomerism matters in pharmacy:** isomers can differ in activity, toxicity and stability. Propan-2-ol is used as a skin antiseptic while its isomer propan-1-ol is not; ethanol is a beverage solvent while its isomer dimethyl ether is a gas.

## 1.5 Toolkit for Units 2–5: electronic effects and intermediates

The syllabus scope mentions "intermediates forming in reactions". You will meet these in every mechanism, so learn them now.

### Electronic effects

| Effect | What happens | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inductive (I) | Permanent shift of σ electrons along a chain; fades after 2–3 bonds | –I: –NO2, –CN, –COOH, –F, –Cl, –Br, –OH; +I: –CH3, –C2H5, (CH3)3C– |
| Resonance / mesomeric (R or M) | Delocalisation of π or lone-pair electrons through conjugation | +R: –OH, –NH2, –OCH3; −R: –NO2, –CHO, –COOH |
| Electromeric (E) | Temporary, complete shift of a π pair when a reagent attacks; disappears when the reagent leaves | C=O, C=C under attack (Unit 4) |
| Hyperconjugation | Overlap of a C–H σ bond with an adjacent empty p orbital or π bond ("no-bond resonance") | Explains carbocation and alkene stability (Unit 2) |

Order of +I: (CH3)3C– > (CH3)2CH– > CH3CH2– > CH3–. Order of −I among halogens: F > Cl > Br > I.

### Bond fission and reaction intermediates

- **Homolytic fission** — the bond splits evenly, each atom keeps one electron → **free radicals**. Favoured by light, heat, peroxides and non-polar conditions.
- **Heterolytic fission** — one atom takes both electrons → **ions** (carbocation + anion, or carbanion + cation). Favoured by polar solvents.

| Intermediate | Charge / electrons | Hybridisation and shape | Stability order |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carbocation (R3C+) | Positive, 6 valence electrons | sp2, planar | 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3+ (allyl and benzyl are also very stable) |
| Carbanion (R3C:−) | Negative, 8 electrons | sp3, pyramidal | CH3− > 1° > 2° > 3° |
| Free radical (R3C·) | Neutral, 7 electrons | Nearly planar | 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3· |
| Carbene (:CR2) | Neutral, 6 electrons, two non-bonded | Singlet or triplet | Very reactive; :CCl2 forms from CHCl3 + base |

**Reagents.** An **electrophile** ("electron lover") is electron-poor and attacks electron-rich sites: H+, NO2+, Br+, carbocations, BF3, AlCl3. A **nucleophile** ("nucleus lover") has a lone pair or negative charge and attacks electron-poor carbon: OH−, CN−, Cl−, NH3, H2O, ROH.

**Types of reaction** used in the following units: substitution (Unit 3), addition (Units 2 and 4), elimination (Unit 2), rearrangement (Units 2 and 3) and condensation (Unit 4).

## Remember

- Classify by skeleton (acyclic, alicyclic, aromatic, heterocyclic) and by functional group; a homologous series differs by –CH2–.
- IUPAC name = prefixes (alphabetical) + root + saturation suffix + functional suffix; the main group gets the lowest locant.
- Priority: acid > ester > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine; halo, nitro and alkoxy are prefixes only.
- Count the C of –CHO, –COOH and –CN in the chain; on rings use -carbaldehyde, -carboxylic acid.
- Structural isomers: chain, position, functional group, metamerism, tautomerism, ring–chain. C4H10O has 7 structural isomers; C5H12 has 3.
- Carbocations and radicals: 3° most stable; carbanions: 3° least stable.
