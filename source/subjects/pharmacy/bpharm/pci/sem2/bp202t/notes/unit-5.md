# Unit 5: Carboxylic acids and aliphatic amines

This unit contrasts the two halves of acid–base chemistry in organic molecules. Carboxylic acids give up a proton because their anion is stabilised by resonance; amines accept a proton because nitrogen has a free lone pair. In both cases, substituents that push or pull electrons change the strength, and that is what examiners ask. The unit ends with the structures and uses of twelve acid-derived compounds and three amines, many of them familiar drugs.

## 5.1 Carboxylic acids

Carboxylic acids contain the **carboxyl group, –COOH** (carbonyl + hydroxyl). Saturated monocarboxylic acids: CnH2n+1COOH.

**Nomenclature**

| Formula | Common name | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| HCOOH | Formic acid | Methanoic acid |
| CH3COOH | Acetic acid | Ethanoic acid |
| CH3CH2COOH | Propionic acid | Propanoic acid |
| CH3(CH2)2COOH | Butyric acid | Butanoic acid |
| HOOC–COOH | Oxalic acid | Ethanedioic acid |
| HOOC–CH2–CH2–COOH | Succinic acid | Butanedioic acid |
| CH3CH(OH)COOH | Lactic acid | 2-Hydroxypropanoic acid |

In common names, positions are marked with Greek letters starting from the carbon next to –COOH: α (C2), β (C3), γ (C4). So 2-chloropropanoic acid = α-chloropropionic acid.

**Physical properties:** carboxylic acids form **hydrogen-bonded dimers**, so their boiling points are higher than those of alcohols of similar mass (acetic acid 118 °C vs propan-1-ol 97 °C). Lower acids mix with water; solubility falls as the chain grows.

```text
             O ······ H – O
           //              \
   R  –  C                    C  –  R        cyclic dimer held by two H-bonds
           \              //
             O – H ······ O
```

### Preparation of carboxylic acids (two methods + others)

| Method | Reaction |
| --- | --- |
| Oxidation of 1° alcohols and aldehydes | R–CH2OH ──KMnO4 or K2Cr2O7 / H2SO4──▶ R–CHO ──▶ R–COOH |
| Hydrolysis of nitriles | R–C≡N + 2 H2O ──H⁺, heat──▶ R–COOH + NH4⁺ (adds one carbon to R–X via R–CN) |
| Grignard reagent + CO2 | R–MgX + CO2 → R–COOMgX ──H3O⁺──▶ R–COOH |
| Hydrolysis of esters, amides, anhydrides | R–COOR' + H2O ──H⁺ or OH⁻──▶ R–COOH + R'OH |
| Haloform reaction of methyl ketones | R–COCH3 + 3 I2 + 4 NaOH → R–COONa + CHI3 + 3 NaI + 3 H2O |
| Industrial acetic acid | CH3OH + CO ──rhodium/iodide catalyst──▶ CH3COOH (methanol carbonylation) |

### Reactions of carboxylic acids

| Reaction | Equation | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Salt formation | R–COOH + NaHCO3 → R–COONa + H2O + CO2↑ | Effervescence: test for –COOH (phenols do not react) |
| Esterification (Fischer) | R–COOH + R'OH ⇌ R–COOR' + H2O (conc. H2SO4) | Reversible; excess alcohol or removal of water pushes it forward |
| Acid chloride | R–COOH + SOCl2 → R–COCl + SO2 + HCl (or PCl5, PCl3) | Acid chlorides make esters and amides easily |
| Amide | R–COONH4 ──heat──▶ R–CONH2 + H2O | Via the ammonium salt |
| Reduction | R–COOH ──LiAlH4──▶ R–CH2OH | NaBH4 does not reduce acids |
| Decarboxylation | R–COONa + NaOH ──CaO, heat──▶ R–H + Na2CO3 | One carbon fewer |
| Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky | R–CH2–COOH + Br2 ──red P──▶ R–CHBr–COOH + HBr | α-Halogenation; α-halo acids lead to amino and hydroxy acids |

## 5.2 Acidity of carboxylic acids

```text
   R–COOH  +  H2O   ⇌   R–COO⁻  +  H3O⁺          Ka = [RCOO⁻][H3O⁺] / [RCOOH]
                                                  pKa = −log Ka  (smaller pKa = stronger acid)

   Carboxylate resonance (two identical structures):

        O                 O⁻
       //                 /
   R–C          ⟷     R–C          =    R–C(···O)2⁻½ each
       \                 \\
        O⁻                O
```

**Why carboxylic acids are stronger than alcohols and phenols:**

1. In the **carboxylate ion** the negative charge is shared **equally by two oxygen atoms** (two identical resonance forms). Both C–O bonds become equal in length (about 1.27 Å in sodium formate, between C=O 1.23 Å and C–O 1.36 Å).
2. In the **phenoxide ion** the charge is spread partly onto ring carbons, which are less electronegative, so it is less stabilised.
3. In the **alkoxide ion** there is no resonance at all; alkyl groups even push electrons onto the oxygen.

| Compound | Approximate pKa | Reacts with NaHCO3? | Reacts with NaOH? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ethanol | 16 | No | No |
| Water | 15.7 | — | — |
| Phenol | 10 | No | Yes |
| Carbonic acid (first) | 6.4 | — | Yes |
| Acetic acid | 4.76 | Yes (CO2 evolves) | Yes |
| Formic acid | 3.75 | Yes | Yes |

A carboxylic acid is stronger than carbonic acid, so it releases CO2 from bicarbonate; phenol is weaker, so it cannot.

## 5.3 Effect of substituents on acidity (inductive effect)

**Rule:** anything that **stabilises the carboxylate anion** (spreads its negative charge) **increases acidity**; anything that **destabilises** it decreases acidity.

- **Electron-withdrawing groups (–I):** –NO2, –CN, –F, –Cl, –Br, –I, –OH, –COOH, –C6H5 pull electron density away from –COO⁻ → **stronger acid**.
- **Electron-releasing groups (+I):** –CH3, –C2H5, –C(CH3)3 push electrons into –COO⁻ → **weaker acid**.

| Acid | pKa | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (CH3)3C–COOH | 5.0 | More +I groups → weaker |
| CH3CH2COOH | 4.87 | +I of ethyl |
| CH3COOH | 4.76 | Reference |
| HCOOH | 3.75 | No +I group → stronger than acetic |
| ICH2COOH | 3.18 | –I of I (weakest halogen) |
| BrCH2COOH | 2.90 | |
| ClCH2COOH | 2.86 | |
| FCH2COOH | 2.59 | Most electronegative halogen → strongest −I |
| NCCH2COOH | 2.5 | –CN strongly −I |
| Cl2CHCOOH | 1.29 | Two Cl add up |
| Cl3CCOOH | 0.65 | Three Cl: almost a strong acid |

**Four rules from the table**

1. **Electronegativity of the substituent:** F > Cl > Br > I in acid strength of the haloacetic acids.
2. **Number of substituents:** each extra –I group increases acidity (mono < di < trichloroacetic acid).
3. **Distance from –COOH:** the inductive effect fades quickly along the chain.

```text
   CH3CH2CHClCOOH   2-chlorobutanoic acid   pKa ≈ 2.8   (Cl at α: strong effect)
   CH3CHClCH2COOH   3-chlorobutanoic acid   pKa ≈ 4.1
   ClCH2CH2CH2COOH  4-chlorobutanoic acid   pKa ≈ 4.5
   CH3CH2CH2COOH    butanoic acid           pKa ≈ 4.8   (no Cl)
```

4. **Electron donors weaken:** formic > acetic > propanoic > 2,2-dimethylpropanoic acid.

**Dicarboxylic acids:** the first ionisation of oxalic acid is strong (pKa1 about 1.3) because the second –COOH group is strongly −I; the second ionisation is weaker because removing H⁺ from an anion is harder. Aromatic acids (benzoic acid, pKa 4.2) and the ortho effect are covered in Pharmaceutical Organic Chemistry II.

## 5.4 Qualitative tests for carboxylic acids, amides and esters

**Carboxylic acids**

| Test | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| Litmus | Blue litmus turns red |
| Sodium bicarbonate | Brisk effervescence of CO2 (turns lime water milky) |
| Ester test | Warm with ethanol + a few drops of conc. H2SO4; pour into water → fruity smell |
| Neutral FeCl3 (on the neutralised acid) | Acetate/formate: deep red colour, brown-red precipitate on boiling; benzoate: buff precipitate; salicylate: violet colour; succinate: brownish precipitate |
| Oxalic acid | White precipitate of calcium oxalate with CaCl2 (insoluble in acetic acid); decolourises warm acidified KMnO4 |
| Formic acid | Reduces Tollens' reagent (silver mirror) — the only common acid that does, because it contains an aldehyde-like H–C=O |

**Amides (R–CONH2)**

| Test | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| Boil with NaOH | Smell of NH3; moist red litmus turns blue (R–CONH2 + NaOH → R–COONa + NH3↑) |
| Biuret test (urea) | Heat urea gently → biuret; cool, add NaOH and a drop of dilute CuSO4 → violet colour |
| Nitrous acid (urea) | Effervescence of N2 with NaNO2 + dilute HCl |

**Esters (R–COOR')**

| Test | Observation |
| --- | --- |
| Odour | Pleasant fruity smell |
| Hydroxamic acid test | Warm ester with hydroxylamine hydrochloride + NaOH; acidify; add FeCl3 → red to violet (ferric hydroxamate) |
| Saponification | Pink NaOH–phenolphthalein solution loses its colour on warming with the ester (alkali is used up) |

## 5.6 Aliphatic amines

Amines are derivatives of ammonia in which one, two or three H atoms are replaced by alkyl groups.

| Class | General formula | Example | IUPAC name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Primary (1°) | R–NH2 | CH3CH2NH2 (ethylamine) | Ethanamine |
| Secondary (2°) | R2NH | (CH3)2NH (dimethylamine) | N-Methylmethanamine |
| Tertiary (3°) | R3N | (CH3)3N (trimethylamine) | N,N-Dimethylmethanamine |
| Quaternary ammonium salt | R4N⁺ X⁻ | (CH3)4N⁺ I⁻ | Tetramethylammonium iodide |

Note: 1°, 2°, 3° for amines counts the **groups on nitrogen**, not the type of carbon. Amphetamine has its NH2 on a secondary carbon, but it is a **primary amine**.

### Preparation of amines (two methods + others)

| Method | Reaction | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hofmann bromamide degradation | R–CONH2 + Br2 + 4 NaOH → R–NH2 + Na2CO3 + 2 NaBr + 2 H2O | Gives a pure 1° amine with **one carbon fewer** |
| Reduction of nitriles | R–C≡N + 4[H] ──LiAlH4 or Na / ethanol──▶ R–CH2–NH2 | One carbon more than R–X used to make the nitrile |
| Reduction of nitro compounds | R–NO2 + 6[H] ──Sn / HCl or H2 / Ni──▶ R–NH2 + 2 H2O | |
| Ammonolysis of alkyl halides (Hofmann) | R–X + NH3 → R–NH2 → R2NH → R3N → R4N⁺X⁻ | Gives a mixture; excess NH3 favours 1° |
| Gabriel phthalimide synthesis | Potassium phthalimide + R–X → N-alkylphthalimide ──hydrolysis or hydrazine──▶ R–NH2 | Pure 1° amines only |
| Reductive amination | R2C=O + NH3 + H2 ──Ni──▶ R2CH–NH2 | Used for amphetamine |

### Reactions of amines

| Reaction | Result |
| --- | --- |
| With acids | Salt formation: R–NH2 + HCl → R–NH3⁺ Cl⁻ (water-soluble; most amine drugs are given as salts, e.g. hydrochlorides) |
| Acylation | R–NH2 + CH3COCl → R–NHCOCH3 + HCl (1° and 2° only) |
| Carbylamine reaction (1° only) | R–NH2 + CHCl3 + 3 KOH (alc.) ──heat──▶ R–NC + 3 KCl + 3 H2O (foul-smelling isocyanide) |
| Nitrous acid | 1°: R–NH2 + HNO2 → R–OH + N2↑ + H2O; 2°: R2N–N=O (yellow oily nitrosamine); 3°: forms a soluble salt |
| Hinsberg reagent (C6H5SO2Cl + KOH) | 1°: sulfonamide soluble in alkali; 2°: sulfonamide insoluble in alkali; 3°: no reaction |
| Alkylation | Further alkyl groups add, finally a quaternary ammonium salt |

## 5.7 Basicity of amines

```text
   R–NH2  +  H2O   ⇌   R–NH3⁺  +  OH⁻          Kb = [RNH3⁺][OH⁻] / [RNH2]
                                                pKb = −log Kb (smaller pKb = stronger base)
   The lone pair on N accepts a proton (Lewis base, Brønsted base).
```

**Aliphatic amines are stronger bases than ammonia** because alkyl groups push electrons (+I) onto nitrogen, making the lone pair more available and stabilising the ammonium ion.

| Amine | Approximate pKb in water |
| --- | --- |
| NH3 | 4.75 |
| CH3NH2 | 3.4 |
| (CH3)2NH | 3.3 |
| (CH3)3N | 4.2 |
| C2H5NH2 | 3.3 |
| (C2H5)2NH | 3.0 |
| (C2H5)3N | 3.2 |

**Order in water**

- Methyl series: (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3.
- Ethyl series: (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3.
- In the gas phase (no solvent), the order is simply 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3, following the +I effect.

**Why 3° amines are not the strongest in water — three factors act together:**

1. **Inductive effect (+I):** more alkyl groups → more electron density on N → 3° > 2° > 1°.
2. **Solvation (hydration) of the ammonium ion:** R–NH3⁺ has three N–H hydrogens to hydrogen-bond with water, R2NH2⁺ two, R3NH⁺ only one. Better solvation stabilises the ion → 1° > 2° > 3°.
3. **Steric hindrance:** bulky groups crowd the lone pair and hinder both protonation and solvation, which lowers the basicity of 3° amines.

The balance of the three makes 2° amines the strongest in water; with ethyl groups, crowding is less severe relative to the +I effect, so triethylamine moves ahead of ethylamine.

### Effect of substituents on basicity

- **Electron-releasing groups (+I)** increase basicity (as above).
- **Electron-withdrawing groups (−I)** near nitrogen reduce basicity: 2-chloroethylamine and 2,2,2-trifluoroethylamine are much weaker bases than ethylamine; **ethanolamine** (–OH, −I) is weaker than ethylamine.
- **Acyl groups:** in **amides** the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the C=O (R–CO–NH2 ⟷ R–C(O⁻)=NH2⁺), so amides are practically neutral.
- **Aryl groups:** aniline is far weaker than ethylamine because its lone pair is delocalised into the ring (details in POC II).
- **Quaternary ammonium hydroxides** (R4N⁺ OH⁻) are as strong as NaOH because they are fully ionised.

### Qualitative tests for aliphatic amines

| Test | 1° | 2° | 3° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Odour, litmus | Fishy/ammoniacal smell; red litmus blue | Same | Same |
| Carbylamine (CHCl3 + alc. KOH, heat) | Offensive isocyanide smell | Negative | Negative |
| Nitrous acid (NaNO2 + HCl, cold) | Brisk effervescence of N2 | Yellow oily nitrosamine | No gas; dissolves as salt |
| Hinsberg | Precipitate soluble in KOH | Precipitate insoluble in KOH | No reaction |

## Remember

- Carboxylate = two equal resonance forms → acids (pKa about 4–5) are stronger than phenol (10) and alcohols (16); they release CO2 from NaHCO3.
- −I groups increase acidity: F > Cl > Br > I; more halogens → stronger; closer to –COOH → stronger; +I groups weaken.
- Esters: hydroxamic acid test (red-violet with FeCl3); amides: NH3 on boiling with NaOH; urea: biuret violet.
- Aspirin = salicylic acid + acetic anhydride; methyl salicylate = salicylic acid + methanol; both via esterification.
- Amines: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH3 (methyl, in water) because of +I, solvation and steric effects together; amides are neutral.
- Carbylamine = 1° only; nitrous acid: 1° N2 gas, 2° yellow oil, 3° salt; Hinsberg separates 1°, 2°, 3°.
- Ethanolamine (emulsifiers, sclerosant), ethylenediamine (aminophylline, EDTA), amphetamine (CNS stimulant, controlled).
