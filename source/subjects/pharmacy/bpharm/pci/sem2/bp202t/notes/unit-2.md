# Unit 2: Alkanes, alkenes and conjugated dienes

This is the heaviest unit of the paper. Alkanes teach you free radical substitution, alkenes teach you electrophilic addition and elimination (E1 and E2), and dienes show what happens when two double bonds talk to each other. Every compound marked with * in the syllabus needs **nomenclature, two methods of preparation, two reactions and uses**; these are given in tables you can copy into an answer.

## 2.1 Alkanes (paraffins)

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons, CnH2n+2. The old name **paraffin** means "little affinity": they are unreactive towards acids, alkalis and oxidising agents at room temperature.

### sp3 hybridisation in alkanes

1. Ground state carbon is 1s2 2s2 2p2 (only two unpaired electrons).
2. One 2s electron is promoted to the empty 2p orbital: 2s1 2px1 2py1 2pz1.
3. The 2s and three 2p orbitals mix to give **four equal sp3 hybrid orbitals** (25% s, 75% p character).
4. The four orbitals point to the corners of a regular **tetrahedron**, bond angle **109°28′**.
5. Each sp3 orbital overlaps head-on with a hydrogen 1s orbital (or another sp3 orbital) to form strong **σ bonds**.

```text
                H                     Methane, CH4
                |                     • 4 σ (sp3–1s) bonds
                C                     • H–C–H = 109°28′
              / | \                   • C–H about 1.10 Å
             H  H  H                  Ethane: C–C σ (sp3–sp3) = 1.54 Å
      (tetrahedral: one H points      Free rotation about C–C gives
       towards you, two away)          conformations (staggered, eclipsed)
```

### Preparation of alkanes (two methods)

| Method | Reaction | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wurtz reaction | 2 R–X + 2 Na → R–R + 2 NaX (dry ether) | 2 CH3Br + 2 Na → CH3–CH3; gives even-numbered symmetric alkanes; methane cannot be made |
| Hydrogenation of alkenes (Sabatier–Senderens) | R–CH=CH2 + H2 → R–CH2–CH3 (Ni, about 200–300 °C; or Pt/Pd at room temperature) | Same carbon number as the alkene |
| Decarboxylation | R–COONa + NaOH (soda lime, CaO) → R–H + Na2CO3, heat | CH3COONa → CH4; product has one carbon less |
| Kolbe electrolysis | 2 RCOONa + 2 H2O → R–R + 2 CO2 + 2 NaOH + H2 | Electrolysis of concentrated sodium acetate gives ethane at the anode |
| Reduction of alkyl halides | R–X + 2[H] → R–H + HX (Zn/HCl) | Keeps the carbon number |

### Reactions of alkanes (two important ones + others)

1. **Halogenation** (free radical substitution, see below): CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl (UV light).
2. **Combustion**: CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O + heat (fuel use).
3. **Pyrolysis (cracking)**: large alkanes break into smaller alkanes and alkenes on heating (about 500 °C or with catalysts).
4. **Isomerisation**: n-butane → isobutane (AlCl3 + HCl).

### Halogenation of alkanes — free radical mechanism

```text
Initiation    Cl–Cl  ──(hν or heat)──▶  2 Cl·                 (homolysis)

Propagation   Cl·  +  H–CH3   ──▶  H–Cl  +  ·CH3
              ·CH3 +  Cl–Cl   ──▶  CH3–Cl + Cl·              (Cl· is regenerated:
                                                              chain reaction)
Termination   Cl· + Cl·  ──▶ Cl2
              ·CH3 + ·CH3 ──▶ CH3–CH3   (explains traces of ethane)
              ·CH3 + Cl·  ──▶ CH3Cl
```

With excess chlorine the reaction continues: CH3Cl → CH2Cl2 → CHCl3 → CCl4 (all used in pharmacy, Unit 3).

**Reactivity of halogens:** F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2. Fluorination is explosive; iodination is reversible (HI reduces CH3I back), so an oxidant such as HIO3 is needed to remove HI.

**Reactivity of hydrogens:** 3° > 2° > 1°, because the radical formed is more stable (3° radical most stable).

```text
Chlorination of propane (room temperature):
  CH3CH2CH3 + Cl2 ──hν──▶ CH3CH2CH2Cl (about 45%)  +  CH3CHClCH3 (about 55%)
  6 primary H × 1.0 = 6.0   :   2 secondary H × 3.8 = 7.6   → 44 : 56 (matches)
Relative rate per H for chlorination: 3° : 2° : 1° ≈ 5.0 : 3.8 : 1.0
Bromination is far more selective (3° H is attacked many hundreds of times faster),
so bromination gives mainly one product.
```

### Uses of paraffins

| Product | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Liquid paraffin (mineral oil) | Lubricant laxative; base of emulsions, cold creams, baby oil |
| Soft paraffin (yellow / white petrolatum) | Ointment base (e.g. simple ointment), emollient |
| Hard paraffin (paraffin wax) | Stiffening agent in ointments; wax baths for joint pain; candles |
| Light petroleum (petroleum ether) | Extraction solvent for fixed oils and alkaloid bases |
| Methane, propane, butane | Fuels (CNG, LPG); butane and propane as aerosol propellants |

## 2.2 Alkenes (olefins)

Alkenes are unsaturated hydrocarbons with one C=C, CnH2n. "Olefin" means "oil forming", because ethylene adds chlorine to give an oily liquid (1,2-dichloroethane).

### sp2 hybridisation in alkenes

1. Each double-bonded carbon mixes 2s with two 2p orbitals → **three sp2 orbitals** in one plane at **120°**; one 2p orbital stays unhybridised, perpendicular to the plane.
2. sp2–sp2 overlap gives the C–C **σ bond**; sp2–1s overlaps give the C–H bonds.
3. The two unhybridised p orbitals overlap **sideways** → **π bond**, with electron density above and below the plane.

```text
          p                 p
          ●                 ●          π cloud above
     H    |                 |    H
       \  |                 |  /
         C ═════════════════ C          σ bond along the axis (C=C 1.34 Å)
       /  |                 |  \
     H    |                 |    H
          ●                 ●          π cloud below
   All six atoms lie in one plane, angles about 120°.
```

**Consequences of the π bond:**

- The π bond is weaker than the σ bond (C=C ≈ 146 kcal/mol, which is less than twice the C–C single bond ≈ 83 kcal/mol), so alkenes undergo **addition** easily.
- The exposed π electrons attract **electrophiles**.
- Rotation about C=C would break the π overlap, so rotation is restricted → **geometrical (cis–trans) isomerism** (e.g. cis- and trans-but-2-ene).

### Stability of alkenes

Stability is measured by the **heat of hydrogenation** (heat released when one mole adds H2). Less heat released = more stable alkene.

| Alkene | Substitution | Heat of hydrogenation (kcal/mol) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ethene | none | 32.8 |
| Propene | mono | 30.1 |
| But-1-ene | mono | 30.3 |
| cis-But-2-ene | di | 28.6 |
| 2-Methylpropene | di | 28.4 |
| trans-But-2-ene | di | 27.6 |
| 2-Methylbut-2-ene | tri | 26.9 |
| 2,3-Dimethylbut-2-ene | tetra | 26.6 |

**Order of stability:** R2C=CR2 > R2C=CHR > R2C=CH2 ≈ RCH=CHR (trans > cis) > RCH=CH2 > CH2=CH2.

Why: (1) **hyperconjugation** — more alkyl groups give more α C–H bonds that share electrons with the π bond; (2) alkyl groups push electrons (+I) and the sp2 carbon is a better partner for them; (3) **trans** is more stable than **cis** because the two groups are far apart (less crowding).

### Preparation of alkenes (two methods)

| Method | Reaction | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dehydration of alcohols | CH3CH2OH ──conc. H2SO4, 170 °C──▶ CH2=CH2 + H2O (or Al2O3, about 350 °C) | Ease: 3° > 2° > 1°; follows Saytzeff; carbocation may rearrange (E1) |
| Dehydrohalogenation | CH3CH2CH2Br + KOH (alcoholic) ──heat──▶ CH3CH=CH2 + KBr + H2O | β-elimination by E2; Saytzeff product major |
| Dehalogenation of vicinal dihalides | BrCH2–CH2Br + Zn ──▶ CH2=CH2 + ZnBr2 | Used to protect/regenerate C=C |
| Partial reduction of alkynes | R–C≡C–R + H2 (Lindlar's catalyst) → cis-alkene; Na in liquid NH3 → trans-alkene | Stereoselective |

### Reactions of alkenes (summary)

| Reagent | Product | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| H2 / Ni or Pt | Alkane | Catalytic addition |
| Br2 in CCl4 | Vicinal dibromide (red-brown colour disappears: test for unsaturation) | Electrophilic addition (anti) |
| HX | Alkyl halide (Markownikoff) | Electrophilic addition |
| HBr + peroxide | Alkyl bromide (anti-Markownikoff) | Free radical addition |
| H2O / H2SO4 | Alcohol (Markownikoff) | Acid-catalysed hydration |
| Cold dilute alkaline KMnO4 (Baeyer's reagent) | 1,2-Diol (glycol); purple colour lost, brown MnO2 forms | Oxidation (test for unsaturation) |
| O3, then Zn / H2O | Aldehydes and/or ketones | Ozonolysis |
| Heat, pressure, catalyst | Polymer (polythene, PVC, PTFE) | Polymerisation |

## 2.3 Electrophilic addition and Markownikoff's orientation

**Markownikoff's rule:** when an unsymmetrical reagent (HX, H2O, HOCl) adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the **negative part** of the reagent goes to the **carbon that carries fewer hydrogens** (the more substituted carbon).

**Mechanism (propene + HBr):**

```text
Step 1 (slow) — the π electrons attack H+; the more stable carbocation forms

   CH3–CH=CH2  +  H–Br  ──▶  CH3–C⁺H–CH3   (2°, more stable)   +  Br⁻
                       (not CH3–CH2–C⁺H2, a 1° cation)

Step 2 (fast) — bromide attacks the carbocation

   CH3–C⁺H–CH3  +  Br⁻  ──▶  CH3–CHBr–CH3      2-bromopropane (major)
```

**Modern statement:** electrophilic addition goes through the **more stable carbocation**. Because 3° > 2° > 1° carbocations, the H adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens. The modern statement also explains additions where rearrangement occurs.

**Addition of bromine (anti addition):**

```text
   C=C  +  Br–Br  ──▶  cyclic bromonium ion  ──Br⁻ attacks from the back──▶  trans-1,2-dibromide
   (Br2 is polarised by the π cloud; Br⁺ bridges both carbons; Br⁻ opens the bridge from the opposite face)
   Cyclopentene + Br2 → trans-1,2-dibromocyclopentane only.
```

## 2.4 Free radical addition: anti-Markownikoff's orientation (peroxide effect)

In the presence of organic peroxides (ROOR) or light, **HBr** adds to unsymmetrical alkenes in the opposite way: Br goes to the carbon with **more** hydrogens. This is the **Kharasch (peroxide) effect**.

```text
Initiation:    RO–OR ──heat──▶ 2 RO·
               RO· + H–Br ──▶ RO–H + Br·

Propagation:   Br· + CH2=CH–CH3 ──▶ Br–CH2–C·H–CH3      (2° radical, more stable
                                                          than ·CH2–CHBr–CH3, 1°)
               Br–CH2–C·H–CH3 + H–Br ──▶ Br–CH2–CH2–CH3 + Br·

Product:       1-bromopropane (anti-Markownikoff)

Termination:   any two radicals combine (Br· + Br· → Br2, etc.)
```

**Why only HBr shows the peroxide effect:**

- **HCl:** the H–Cl bond is strong, so the step R· + H–Cl → R–H + Cl· is energetically unfavourable.
- **HI:** the I· radical is too weak (unreactive) to add to the C=C, and I· atoms combine to I2.
- **HBr:** both propagation steps are exothermic, so the chain runs.

## 2.5 Ozonolysis

Ozone adds across the C=C to give an unstable **molozonide**, which rearranges to an **ozonide**. Hydrolysis with **zinc dust and water** (zinc removes H2O2 so the aldehydes are not oxidised) gives carbonyl compounds.

```text
   R2C=CHR'  ──O3──▶  ozonide  ──Zn / H2O──▶  R2C=O  +  O=CHR'  (+ ZnO)

Examples:
   CH3–CH=CH–CH3 (but-2-ene)     ──▶  2 CH3CHO           (2 acetaldehyde)
   (CH3)2C=CH2 (2-methylpropene) ──▶  (CH3)2C=O + HCHO   (acetone + formaldehyde)
   CH3CH2CH=CH2 (but-1-ene)      ──▶  CH3CH2CHO + HCHO
```

**Use:** locating the double bond. Join the two carbonyl carbons back together with a C=C to rebuild the alkene. This was a classic way of proving structures of natural products.

## 2.6 Elimination reactions: E1 and E2

In a **β-elimination**, a leaving group (X) leaves from the α-carbon and an H leaves from the neighbouring β-carbon, forming a C=C.

### E2 (bimolecular elimination)

- **One step, concerted**: the base removes the β-H while the C–X bond breaks and the π bond forms.
- **Kinetics:** rate = k[RX][Base] — **second order**.
- Needs a **strong base** in high concentration (alcoholic KOH, NaOEt).
- **Anti-periplanar** geometry: H and X on opposite sides, in one plane.

```text
          B:⁻  ↘
                H
                |                                     R        R
          R  –  Cβ  –  Cα  –  R     ──one step──▶      C ═══ C      + B–H + X⁻
                       |                              R        R
                       X  ↘ (leaves at the same time)
   Transition state: B····H····C····C····X  (partial bonds)
```

### E1 (unimolecular elimination)

- **Two steps** through a **carbocation**.
- **Kinetics:** rate = k[RX] — **first order** (only the slow ionisation counts).
- Favoured by 3° substrates, weak bases, polar protic solvents (water, alcohol) and heat. Competes with SN1.

```text
Step 1 (slow):   (CH3)3C–Br  ──▶  (CH3)3C⁺  +  Br⁻
Step 2 (fast):   (CH3)3C⁺  ──base removes β-H──▶  (CH3)2C=CH2  +  H–base⁺
```

### Order of reactivity of alkyl halides

For **both** E1 and E2: **3° > 2° > 1°**.

- E1: 3° carbocation is most stable.
- E2: the more substituted alkene forms, so the transition state is lower in energy.
- Leaving group: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F (weaker C–X bond, better leaving group).

### Rearrangement of carbocations

A carbocation may rearrange to a more stable one by a **1,2-hydride shift** or a **1,2-methyl (alkyl) shift**. This only happens in reactions that go through carbocations (E1, SN1, acid-catalysed dehydration and HX addition), never in E2 or SN2.

```text
1,2-Methyl shift (dehydration of 3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol, conc. H2SO4):

  (CH3)3C–CH(OH)–CH3  ──H⁺, –H2O──▶  (CH3)3C–C⁺H–CH3          2° carbocation
                                          │
                     a CH3 moves with its │ bond pair to the C⁺ (1,2-methyl shift)
                                          ▼
                                    (CH3)2C⁺–CH(CH3)–CH3       3° carbocation (more stable)

  ──loss of H⁺──▶  (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2   2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene (major product)

1,2-Hydride shift: CH3–CH(CH3)–C⁺H–CH3 (2°)  ──H shift──▶  CH3–C⁺(CH3)–CH2–CH3 (3°)
```

### Saytzeff's (Zaitsev's) orientation

**Saytzeff's rule:** when more than one alkene can form, the **more highly substituted alkene** (fewer H on the double-bonded carbons) is the major product.

```text
   CH3–CH2–CHBr–CH3  ──alc. KOH, heat──▶  CH3–CH=CH–CH3  (about 80%, but-2-ene, Saytzeff)
                                       +  CH3–CH2–CH=CH2 (about 20%, but-1-ene)
```

**Evidences / explanation of the rule**

1. **Stability data:** heats of hydrogenation show the more substituted alkene is more stable (but-2-ene 27.6–28.6 vs but-1-ene 30.3 kcal/mol).
2. **Hyperconjugation:** but-2-ene has 6 α-hydrogens against 2 for but-1-ene, so more electron sharing.
3. **Transition state:** in E2 the C=C is partly formed in the transition state, so the factors that stabilise the alkene also lower the energy of the path that leads to it — the more stable alkene forms faster.
4. **Product analysis** of many dehydrohalogenations and dehydrations agrees with the rule.

**Exceptions — Hofmann product (less substituted alkene) forms when:** the base is bulky (potassium tert-butoxide), the leaving group is poor and bulky (quaternary ammonium, –NR3+, Hofmann elimination), or the substrate is crowded.

### Evidences that distinguish E2 and E1

| Evidence | E2 | E1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kinetics | Second order: depends on base concentration | First order: independent of base |
| Isotope effect | Large deuterium isotope effect (C–D breaks slower), so C–H breaks in the slow step | No primary isotope effect |
| Rearrangement | Never | Common |
| Stereochemistry | Anti-periplanar H and X needed (stereospecific) | No special geometry |
| Competition | With SN2 | With SN1 (same carbocation; ratio of products independent of leaving group) |

### E1 versus E2 and factors affecting them

| Factor | Favours E2 | Favours E1 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substrate | 1°, 2°, 3° (3° fastest) | 3° (and 2°, allylic, benzylic) |
| Base | Strong, high concentration (OH⁻, RO⁻) | Weak base or none (H2O, ROH) |
| Solvent | Less polar; alcoholic KOH | Polar protic (stabilises carbocation) |
| Leaving group | Good LG speeds both | Good LG speeds both |
| Temperature | High temperature favours elimination over substitution in both | |
| Rearranged product | Absent | May appear |

**Elimination vs substitution:** alcoholic KOH and heat → elimination (alkene); aqueous KOH at lower temperature → substitution (alcohol). Bulky bases favour elimination.

## 2.7 Conjugated dienes

**Dienes** have two C=C bonds. They are classified by how the double bonds sit:

| Type | Structure | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cumulated (allenes) | C=C=C | Propa-1,2-diene (allene) |
| Conjugated | C=C–C=C (alternate single and double) | Buta-1,3-diene, isoprene |
| Isolated | C=C–(CH2)n–C=C, n ≥ 1 | Penta-1,4-diene |

### Stability of conjugated dienes

1. **Heat of hydrogenation:** buta-1,3-diene releases 57.1 kcal/mol, while two isolated monosubstituted C=C would release about 2 × 30.3 = 60.6 kcal/mol. The **delocalisation energy is about 3.5 kcal/mol**.
2. **Bond length:** the central C2–C3 bond is **1.48 Å**, shorter than a normal C–C (1.54 Å), showing partial double-bond character.
3. **Orbital picture:** all four carbons are sp2; four p orbitals overlap continuously, so the four π electrons are delocalised over C1–C4.
4. **Resonance:** CH2=CH–CH=CH2 ↔ ⁺CH2–CH=CH–CH2⁻ (minor contributor).

```text
   p   p   p   p          continuous sideways overlap over 4 carbons
   ●   ●   ●   ●
   C1==C2––C3==C4         C2–C3 = 1.48 Å (shorter than 1.54 Å)
   ●   ●   ●   ●
   Conformations: s-trans (more stable)  ⇌  s-cis (needed for Diels–Alder)
```

### Preparation of buta-1,3-diene (two methods)

1. **Catalytic dehydrogenation** of n-butane or butenes over a chromium oxide–alumina catalyst at high temperature.
2. **Dehydration of diols**, e.g. butane-1,3-diol or butane-1,4-diol heated with an acid catalyst: HO–CH2CH2CH2CH2–OH → CH2=CH–CH=CH2 + 2 H2O.

**Uses:** monomer for synthetic rubbers (Buna-S, Buna-N, polybutadiene); isoprene units build terpenes, natural rubber and vitamin A.

### Electrophilic addition to conjugated dienes: 1,2- and 1,4-addition

```text
   CH2=CH–CH=CH2  +  HBr

Step 1: H⁺ adds to C1 → allylic carbocation (resonance-stabilised)
   CH3–C⁺H–CH=CH2   ⟷   CH3–CH=CH–C⁺H2

Step 2: Br⁻ attacks either positive end
   attack at C2 → CH3–CHBr–CH=CH2       3-bromobut-1-ene   (1,2-adduct)
   attack at C4 → CH3–CH=CH–CH2Br       1-bromobut-2-ene   (1,4-adduct)
```

| Condition | 1,2-product | 1,4-product | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| About −80 °C | About 80% | About 20% | **Kinetic** — 1,2 forms faster (Br⁻ is nearest C2, and the C2 cation is 2°) |
| About 40 °C | About 20% | About 80% | **Thermodynamic** — 1,4 product is more stable (disubstituted C=C) and the reaction becomes reversible |

Bromine adds the same way: buta-1,3-diene + Br2 → 3,4-dibromobut-1-ene (1,2) and 1,4-dibromobut-2-ene (1,4).

### Free radical addition to conjugated dienes

With HBr and peroxide, Br· adds to the terminal carbon to give a resonance-stabilised **allylic radical**, which picks up H at C2 or C4:

```text
   Br· + CH2=CH–CH=CH2 ──▶ BrCH2–C·H–CH=CH2  ⟷  BrCH2–CH=CH–C·H2
   + HBr ──▶ BrCH2–CH2–CH=CH2  (1,2)   and   BrCH2–CH=CH–CH3  (1,4, usually major)
```

Free radical **polymerisation** of dienes in the same way gives synthetic rubbers.

### Diels–Alder reaction

A **[4 + 2] cycloaddition** between a conjugated diene (4 π electrons) and a **dienophile** (an alkene, 2 π electrons), forming a **cyclohexene ring**. Otto Diels and Kurt Alder received the 1950 Nobel Prize for it.

```text
         CH2                         CH2
       //                          /    \
     HC        CH2               HC      CH2
     |    +    ||     ──heat──▶  ||      |
     HC        CH2               HC      CH2
       \\                          \    /
         CH2                         CH2
   buta-1,3-diene + ethene          cyclohexene
   (two new σ bonds at C1 and C4; new π bond at C2–C3)

   Buta-1,3-diene + maleic anhydride (benzene, warm) → cyclohex-4-ene-1,2-dicarboxylic
   anhydride (a fast, high-yield reaction because the anhydride is an electron-poor dienophile)
```

**Features**

1. **Concerted, one step**, no intermediate; needs only heat.
2. The diene must take the **s-cis** conformation.
3. **Electron-withdrawing groups** (–CHO, –COOR, –CN, anhydride) on the dienophile speed it up.
4. **Stereospecific (syn addition):** a cis-dienophile gives a cis-substituted ring; a trans one gives trans.
5. **Endo rule:** with cyclic dienes the endo adduct is usually the main product.

**Uses:** builds six-membered rings in one step; used in syntheses of steroids, terpenes and many natural products; the reaction with maleic anhydride is a test for conjugated dienes.

### Allylic rearrangement

When a reaction goes through an **allylic cation** (or allylic radical), the positive charge (or odd electron) is shared by the two end carbons. The incoming group can attach at either end, so the **double bond can shift** in the product.

```text
   CH3–CHCl–CH=CH2  ──H2O (SN1)──▶  [CH3–C⁺H–CH=CH2  ⟷  CH3–CH=CH–C⁺H2]
   3-chlorobut-1-ene                         allylic cation

   + H2O ──▶  CH3–CH(OH)–CH=CH2  (but-3-en-2-ol)
          and CH3–CH=CH–CH2OH    (but-2-en-1-ol, the "rearranged" allylic product)
```

The 1,4-addition to buta-1,3-diene is itself an allylic rearrangement. It also occurs in allylic bromination with N-bromosuccinimide (NBS), which goes through an allylic radical.

## Remember

- Alkanes: sp3, 109°28′; free radical halogenation (initiation, propagation, termination); 3° H > 2° > 1°; F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2.
- Alkenes: sp2, 120°, σ + π; stability rises with substitution (hyperconjugation), trans > cis.
- Markownikoff = more stable carbocation; peroxide effect = HBr only, free radical, anti-Markownikoff.
- Ozonolysis + Zn/H2O → carbonyl compounds; locates the C=C.
- E2: one step, rate = k[RX][B], anti-periplanar, no rearrangement. E1: two steps, rate = k[RX], carbocation, rearrangement. Both: 3° > 2° > 1°.
- Saytzeff: more substituted alkene; bulky base gives Hofmann product.
- Dienes: conjugation energy about 3.5 kcal/mol; 1,2 (kinetic, low temperature) vs 1,4 (thermodynamic, higher temperature); Diels–Alder = diene (s-cis) + dienophile → cyclohexene.
