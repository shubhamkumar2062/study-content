# Unit 3: Alkyl halides and alcohols

Alkyl halides are the classic substrates for **nucleophilic substitution**: SN1 and SN2 are among the most asked 10-mark questions of this paper. Alcohols follow, with their tests and seven pharmaceutically important members. Both families are marked * in the syllabus, so learn nomenclature, two preparations, two reactions and uses for each.

## 3.1 Alkyl halides (haloalkanes)

Alkyl halides have a halogen bonded to an sp3 carbon: R–X (X = F, Cl, Br, I). General formula of monohalides CnH2n+1X.

**Classification**

| Basis | Classes | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Number of halogens | Mono-, di-, tri-, polyhalides | CH3Cl, CH2Cl2, CHCl3, CCl4 |
| Carbon bearing X | Primary, secondary, tertiary | CH3CH2Br (1°), (CH3)2CHBr (2°), (CH3)3CBr (3°) |
| Special positions | Allylic, benzylic (very reactive); vinylic, aryl (very unreactive) | CH2=CH–CH2Cl (allyl chloride), C6H5CH2Cl (benzyl chloride), CH2=CHCl (vinyl chloride) |
| Dihalides | Geminal (same C), vicinal (adjacent C) | CH3CHCl2 (1,1-dichloroethane), ClCH2CH2Cl (1,2-dichloroethane) |

**Nomenclature:** common names use "alkyl halide" (ethyl bromide, isopropyl chloride); IUPAC uses the prefix "halo" on the alkane (bromoethane, 2-chloropropane, 2-bromo-2-methylpropane).

**The C–X bond** is polar: carbon carries δ+ and halogen δ−, so the carbon is attacked by nucleophiles. Bond strength falls from C–F to C–I, so **R–I reacts fastest** and R–F hardly reacts.

### Preparation of alkyl halides (two methods + others)

| Method | Reaction | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| From alcohols with thionyl chloride (Darzens) | R–OH + SOCl2 ──pyridine──▶ R–Cl + SO2↑ + HCl↑ | Best method: both by-products are gases, so the product is pure |
| From alcohols with HX | R–OH + HCl ──anhydrous ZnCl2──▶ R–Cl + H2O | Groove's process; ease 3° > 2° > 1° |
| From alcohols with phosphorus halides | 3 R–OH + PCl3 → 3 R–Cl + H3PO3; R–OH + PCl5 → R–Cl + POCl3 + HCl | Also PBr3, P + I2 |
| From alkenes | CH3CH=CH2 + HBr → CH3CHBrCH3 | Markownikoff (Unit 2) |
| Halogen exchange (Finkelstein) | R–Cl + NaI ──acetone──▶ R–I + NaCl↓ | NaCl precipitates in acetone, driving the reaction |
| Swarts reaction | R–Br + AgF → R–F + AgBr | Makes alkyl fluorides |

### Reactions of alkyl halides

**Nucleophilic substitution (Nu⁻ replaces X⁻):**

| Reagent | Product | Name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aqueous KOH | R–OH | Alcohol |
| NaOR' (Williamson synthesis) | R–O–R' | Ether |
| KCN (alcoholic) | R–CN | Nitrile (one carbon more) |
| AgCN | R–NC | Isocyanide |
| NH3 (excess) | R–NH2 (plus 2°, 3° amines, quaternary salt) | Amine |
| AgNO2 | R–NO2 | Nitroalkane |
| R'COOAg | R'COOR | Ester |

**Elimination:** alcoholic KOH, heat → alkene (Unit 2).

**With metals:** Wurtz (2 RX + 2 Na → R–R) and Grignard reagent (R–X + Mg in dry ether → R–MgX).

## 3.2 SN2 reaction (bimolecular nucleophilic substitution)

**Kinetics:** rate = k[RX][Nu⁻] — **second order**. Doubling either reactant doubles the rate.

**Mechanism — one step, concerted, backside attack:**

```text
   HO⁻ attacks the carbon from the side opposite to Br (backside attack)

            H                          H                           H
            |                          |                           |
   HO⁻  →  C–Br       ──▶     [ HO·····C·····Br ]⁻     ──▶    HO–C      +  Br⁻
          /  \                        /  \                         /  \
       H3C    C2H5                 H3C    C2H5                  H3C    C2H5
   (H, CH3, C2H5 point       transition state: C bonded    the three groups have
    away from HO⁻)           partly to OH and Br; H, CH3,  flipped to the other side
                             C2H5 lie in one plane         (configuration inverted)

   The three groups turn over like an umbrella in a strong wind → inversion.
```

**Stereochemistry:** complete **inversion of configuration** (Walden inversion). An optically active (R)-2-bromobutane gives (S)-butan-2-ol with OH⁻.

**Order of reactivity:** CH3–X > 1° > 2° >> 3° (3° practically does not react by SN2).

Reason: **steric hindrance**. Bulky groups block the backside of the carbon and crowd the five-group transition state. Neopentyl bromide, (CH3)3C–CH2Br, is 1° but reacts extremely slowly for the same reason.

## 3.3 SN1 reaction (unimolecular nucleophilic substitution)

**Kinetics:** rate = k[RX] — **first order**. The rate does not depend on the nucleophile.

**Mechanism — two steps through a carbocation:**

```text
Step 1 (slow, rate-determining) — ionisation
    (CH3)3C–Br  ──▶  (CH3)3C⁺  +  Br⁻
                     planar sp2 carbocation

Step 2 (fast) — nucleophile attacks
    (CH3)3C⁺  +  H2O  ──▶  (CH3)3C–OH2⁺  ──–H⁺──▶  (CH3)3C–OH   2-methylpropan-2-ol
```

**Stereochemistry:** the carbocation is **planar**, so the nucleophile can attack from either face → **racemisation** (a mixture of retained and inverted products). In practice a little more inversion than retention is often seen, because the leaving halide briefly shields one face (ion pair).

**Order of reactivity:** 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3–X, following carbocation stability (+I effect and hyperconjugation of alkyl groups). Allylic and benzylic halides are also very reactive because their carbocations are resonance-stabilised.

**Rearrangement:** since a free carbocation forms, it can rearrange by a 1,2-hydride or methyl shift to a more stable cation, giving rearranged products.

```text
   (CH3)3C–CH2–Br  ──slow──▶  (CH3)3C–C⁺H2  (1°)  ──CH3 shift──▶  (CH3)2C⁺–CH2CH3 (3°)
   neopentyl bromide                                              + H2O → (CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3
                                                                    2-methylbutan-2-ol
   (rearranged product, not neopentyl alcohol)
```

## 3.4 SN1 versus SN2

| Feature | SN1 | SN2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Molecularity | Unimolecular | Bimolecular |
| Kinetics | First order, rate = k[RX] | Second order, rate = k[RX][Nu] |
| Steps | Two; carbocation intermediate | One; transition state only |
| Stereochemistry | Racemisation (partial inversion) | Complete inversion (Walden) |
| Substrate order | 3° > 2° > 1° > CH3 | CH3 > 1° > 2° > 3° |
| Rearrangement | Possible | Never |
| Nucleophile | Weak, low concentration is enough (H2O, ROH) | Strong, high concentration (OH⁻, CN⁻, RS⁻, I⁻) |
| Solvent | Polar protic (water, alcohols, formic acid) | Polar aprotic (acetone, DMSO, DMF) |
| Competes with | E1 | E2 |
| Example | (CH3)3CBr + H2O | CH3CH2Br + OH⁻ |

### Factors affecting SN1 and SN2

1. **Structure of the substrate** — steric crowding stops SN2; carbocation stability speeds SN1. 2° halides can go either way depending on the other factors.
2. **Nucleophile** — strength and concentration matter only for SN2. Negatively charged nucleophiles are stronger than their neutral forms (OH⁻ > H2O, RO⁻ > ROH). In protic solvents, nucleophilicity of halides is I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ (large ions are less solvated and more polarisable).
3. **Leaving group** — a good leaving group is a weak base that is stable on its own: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻. It speeds both SN1 and SN2. –OH is a poor leaving group; protonating it (–OH2⁺ leaves as water) is why alcohols need acid to react with HX.
4. **Solvent** — polar protic solvents stabilise both the carbocation and the leaving anion by solvation → favour SN1. Polar aprotic solvents dissolve salts but do not hydrogen-bond the anion, leaving the nucleophile "naked" and reactive → favour SN2.
5. **Temperature** — higher temperature favours elimination over substitution.

## 3.5 Structure and uses of important halogen compounds

| Compound | Structure | Preparation (one line) | Uses / notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ethyl chloride (chloroethane) | CH3CH2Cl, gas that liquefies easily (bp about 12 °C) | CH2=CH2 + HCl (AlCl3); or ethanol + HCl / ZnCl2 | Spray as a local anaesthetic: rapid evaporation freezes the skin for minor surgery and sports injuries; earlier a general anaesthetic |
| Chloroform (trichloromethane) | CHCl3, heavy sweet-smelling liquid (bp 61 °C) | Ethanol or acetone + bleaching powder (haloform reaction) | Solvent for extraction of alkaloids, fats and resins; preservative (chloroform water); formerly a general anaesthetic, abandoned because it damages liver and heart |
| Trichloroethylene | ClCH=CCl2 | 1,1,2,2-tetrachloroethane + lime, Ca(OH)2 (loses HCl) | Industrial degreasing and extraction solvent; formerly an inhaled analgesic/anaesthetic (must not be used with soda lime: forms toxic dichloroacetylene) |
| Tetrachloroethylene (perchloroethylene) | Cl2C=CCl2 | Chlorination of trichloroethylene, then removal of HCl | Dry-cleaning solvent; formerly an anthelmintic against hookworm |
| Dichloromethane (methylene chloride) | CH2Cl2, volatile (bp about 40 °C) | Chlorination of methane or chloromethane | Extraction solvent, paint stripper, solvent in coating and in synthesis |
| Tetrachloromethane (carbon tetrachloride) | CCl4, non-flammable | CS2 + 3 Cl2 → CCl4 + S2Cl2 (catalyst); or full chlorination of methane | Solvent for fats, oils and iodine; formerly used in fire extinguishers and as an anthelmintic; now restricted because it is toxic to the liver and depletes ozone |
| Iodoform (triiodomethane) | CHI3, yellow crystals, strong odour (mp about 119 °C) | Ethanol or acetone + I2 + NaOH (or Na2CO3), warm | Antiseptic for wounds (releases iodine slowly), used in dusting powders and dental/ENT packing pastes |

### Chloroform in more detail (often asked)

**Preparation (laboratory, from ethanol and bleaching powder):**

```text
   CaOCl2 + H2O → Ca(OH)2 + Cl2
   CH3CH2OH + Cl2 → CH3CHO + 2 HCl            (oxidation)
   CH3CHO + 3 Cl2 → CCl3CHO + 3 HCl            (chlorination → chloral)
   2 CCl3CHO + Ca(OH)2 → 2 CHCl3 + (HCOO)2Ca   (hydrolysis)
```

**Oxidation in air and light:** 2 CHCl3 + O2 → 2 COCl2 (phosgene, poisonous) + 2 HCl.
**Storage:** dark amber bottles filled to the brim, kept cool; about 1% ethanol is added to convert any phosgene into harmless diethyl carbonate.
**Purity test:** shake with silver nitrate solution — no white precipitate if free of HCl (and therefore of decomposition).

**Reactions:** carbylamine reaction with primary amines (foul-smelling isocyanide, Unit 5); Reimer–Tiemann reaction with phenol (via dichlorocarbene :CCl2); with acetone and KOH gives chlorobutanol.

### Haloform (iodoform) reaction

Compounds containing **CH3–CO–** or **CH3–CH(OH)–** (which is oxidised to CH3CO–) give iodoform with I2 and NaOH.

```text
   CH3–CO–R + 3 I2 + 3 NaOH → CI3–CO–R + 3 NaI + 3 H2O   (α-H replaced by I)
   CI3–CO–R + NaOH → CHI3↓ (yellow) + R–COONa
```

Positive: ethanol, acetaldehyde, acetone, propan-2-ol, butan-2-one. Negative: methanol, propan-1-ol, formaldehyde, pentan-3-one.

## 3.6 Alcohols

Alcohols contain an –OH group on an sp3 carbon. Monohydric alcohols: CnH2n+1OH.

**Classification:** by number of –OH groups — monohydric (ethanol), dihydric/glycols (propylene glycol), trihydric (glycerol), polyhydric (sorbitol); by the carbon carrying –OH — primary (RCH2OH), secondary (R2CHOH), tertiary (R3COH).

**Nomenclature:** replace -e of the alkane by **-ol**: methanol, ethanol, propan-2-ol, butane-1,3-diol, propane-1,2,3-triol.

**Physical properties:** hydrogen bonding between molecules gives high boiling points (ethanol 78 °C against propane −42 °C of similar mass); lower alcohols mix with water because they hydrogen-bond with it; solubility falls as the chain grows.

**Acidity:** alcohols are very weak acids, weaker than or comparable to water (methanol and water both about pKa 15.5–15.7; ethanol about 16). They react with sodium but not with NaOH. Order: CH3OH > 1° > 2° > 3° (alkyl groups push electrons onto the alkoxide oxygen and destabilise it).

### Preparation of alcohols (two methods + others)

| Method | Reaction |
| --- | --- |
| Hydration of alkenes | CH2=CH2 + H2O ──H3PO4, heat, pressure (or H2SO4 then water)──▶ CH3CH2OH (Markownikoff) |
| Hydrolysis of alkyl halides | R–X + KOH (aqueous) → R–OH + KX |
| Reduction of carbonyl compounds | Aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol (NaBH4, LiAlH4, or H2/Ni) |
| Grignard reagent + carbonyl | HCHO → 1°; other aldehydes → 2°; ketones → 3° alcohol (after hydrolysis) |
| Fermentation | Sugars → ethanol + CO2 (yeast enzymes) |

### Reactions of alcohols

| Reaction | Equation / result |
| --- | --- |
| With sodium | 2 R–OH + 2 Na → 2 R–ONa + H2↑ (acidic nature) |
| Esterification | R–OH + R'COOH ⇌ R'COOR + H2O (conc. H2SO4); fruity smell |
| With HX / PCl5 / SOCl2 | R–OH → R–X |
| Dehydration | Conc. H2SO4 or Al2O3, heat → alkene (higher temperature) or ether (lower temperature, excess alcohol) |
| Oxidation (K2Cr2O7 / H2SO4) | 1° → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° → ketone; 3° resists (only breaks under drastic conditions) |
| Dehydrogenation over Cu at about 300 °C | 1° → aldehyde; 2° → ketone; 3° → alkene (dehydration) |

### Qualitative tests for alcohols

| Test | Reagent and observation | Shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium metal | Effervescence of H2 (dry sample) | –OH group |
| Ceric ammonium nitrate | Yellow solution turns red | Alcoholic –OH |
| Ester test | Warm with acetic acid + conc. H2SO4, pour into water: fruity smell | Alcohol |
| Lucas test (conc. HCl + anhydrous ZnCl2) | 3°: turbidity at once; 2°: within about 5 minutes; 1°: no turbidity at room temperature | 1°, 2°, 3° class |
| Chromic acid (Jones) | Orange colour changes to green/blue-green | 1° or 2° alcohol (3° negative) |
| Iodoform | Yellow CHI3 precipitate | CH3CH(OH)– group (ethanol, propan-2-ol) |

Lucas test works because the alcohol forms the chloride by an SN1 path: 3° carbocations form fastest, and the insoluble chloride makes the solution cloudy.

## 3.7 Structure and uses of important alcohols

### Ethyl alcohol (ethanol), CH3CH2OH

- **Preparation:** (1) **fermentation** of molasses: C12H22O11 + H2O ──invertase──▶ glucose + fructose; C6H12O6 ──zymase──▶ 2 C2H5OH + 2 CO2. (2) **Hydration of ethylene** with phosphoric acid catalyst at high temperature and pressure.
- **Grades:** rectified spirit (about 95%, forms a constant-boiling mixture with water so cannot be purified further by simple distillation); absolute alcohol (water removed by azeotropic distillation or drying agents); denatured (methylated) spirit — made unfit to drink with methanol and other additives.
- **Uses:** solvent for tinctures, elixirs and extracts; antiseptic and disinfectant at about 70% (penetrates cells better than absolute alcohol); preservative; vehicle in many oral liquids; fuel.
- **Tests:** positive iodoform test; ethyl acetate odour with acetic acid + H2SO4.

### Methyl alcohol (methanol, wood spirit), CH3OH

- **Preparation:** (1) **from synthesis gas**: CO + 2 H2 → CH3OH (zinc oxide–chromium oxide or copper catalyst, about 300 °C, high pressure). (2) **Destructive distillation of wood** (old method, hence "wood spirit").
- **Toxicity:** oxidised in the body to formaldehyde and formic acid → metabolic acidosis and damage to the optic nerve (blindness); even small amounts can kill. Ethanol (or fomepizole) is used as treatment because it competes for alcohol dehydrogenase.
- **Uses:** industrial solvent, denaturant of ethanol, antifreeze, raw material for formaldehyde.
- **Distinguish from ethanol:** methanol gives a **negative iodoform test**; heated with salicylic acid and conc. H2SO4 it gives the wintergreen odour of methyl salicylate.

### Chlorobutanol (1,1,1-trichloro-2-methylpropan-2-ol)

```text
              OH
              |
   CH3 –– C –– CCl3          prepared by adding chloroform to acetone
              |              in the presence of solid KOH (cold):
             CH3              CH3COCH3 + CHCl3 ──KOH──▶ (CH3)2C(OH)CCl3
```

- White crystals with a camphor-like odour; usually supplied as the hemihydrate.
- **Uses:** antimicrobial **preservative** (about 0.5%) in injections, eye drops and ear drops; mild sedative–hypnotic and local anaesthetic action.
- Note: it is volatile and is partly lost on autoclaving and through plastic containers.

### Cetostearyl alcohol

- A mixture of solid fatty alcohols, mainly **cetyl alcohol** (hexadecan-1-ol, C16H33OH) and **stearyl alcohol** (octadecan-1-ol, C18H37OH).
- **Preparation:** catalytic reduction (hydrogenation) of fatty acids or their esters from vegetable oils or tallow.
- **Uses:** stiffening agent and emollient in creams and ointments; stabilises oil-in-water emulsions; mixed with sodium lauryl sulfate it forms **emulsifying wax** (base of emulsifying ointment and aqueous cream).

### Benzyl alcohol (phenylmethanol), C6H5CH2OH

- **Preparation:** (1) hydrolysis of benzyl chloride with aqueous sodium carbonate or NaOH: C6H5CH2Cl + NaOH → C6H5CH2OH + NaCl. (2) **Cannizzaro reaction** of benzaldehyde (Unit 4).
- A primary alcohol (the –OH is on a CH2, not on the ring), so it is **not a phenol** and gives no violet colour with FeCl3.
- **Uses:** antimicrobial preservative in multi-dose injections; mild local anaesthetic; solvent. Preserved injections should not be given to newborn babies, in whom benzyl alcohol can be toxic.

### Glycerol (glycerin, propane-1,2,3-triol), CH2OH–CHOH–CH2OH

- **Preparation:** (1) by-product of **saponification** of fats in soap making: fat + 3 NaOH → 3 soap + glycerol. (2) **Synthesis from propene** via allyl chloride (high-temperature chlorination) and hydrolysis steps.
- **Reactions:** (1) heated with KHSO4 → **acrolein** (CH2=CH–CHO), sharp irritating smell (acrolein test); (2) with conc. HNO3 + conc. H2SO4 → **glyceryl trinitrate** (nitroglycerin).
- **Test:** borax–phenolphthalein test — the pink colour of borax with phenolphthalein disappears on adding glycerol (glycerol forms an acidic borate complex) and returns on heating.
- **Uses:** humectant (keeps creams moist), sweetener, solvent and vehicle (glycerites, e.g. tannic acid glycerin), laxative suppositories, plasticiser in gelatin capsule shells, emollient; glyceryl trinitrate is used for angina.

### Propylene glycol (propane-1,2-diol), CH3–CHOH–CH2OH

- **Preparation:** (1) hydration of **propylene oxide** with water and a little acid; (2) from propene via the chlorohydrin (propene + HOCl, then hydrolysis).
- **Uses:** cosolvent in injections and oral solutions for poorly water-soluble drugs; humectant; antimicrobial preservative at higher concentrations; plasticiser. It replaces the related **ethylene glycol**, which is toxic and must never be used in medicines (diethylene glycol contamination has caused mass poisonings).

## Remember

- C–X reactivity R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F; SOCl2 gives the purest alkyl chlorides.
- SN2: one step, rate = k[RX][Nu], inversion (Walden), CH3 > 1° > 2° > 3°, polar aprotic solvent.
- SN1: two steps, rate = k[RX], carbocation, racemisation, rearrangement, 3° > 2° > 1°, polar protic solvent.
- Good leaving groups are weak bases (I⁻ best); –OH must be protonated to leave.
- CHCl3 → phosgene in air: amber bottle, full, 1% ethanol. Iodoform = haloform reaction of CH3CO– or CH3CH(OH)–.
- Lucas: 3° immediate, 2° about 5 minutes, 1° none at room temperature.
- Chlorobutanol = acetone + chloroform + KOH; benzyl alcohol and chlorobutanol are injection preservatives; glycerol gives acrolein with KHSO4.
