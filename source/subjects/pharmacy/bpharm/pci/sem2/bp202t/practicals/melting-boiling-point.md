# Melting point and boiling point of organic compounds

**Aim:** To determine the melting point of a solid organic compound by the capillary method and the boiling point of a liquid by Siwoloboff's method.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Heat the paraffin bath slowly and never above its smoking point; hot oil causes severe burns.

## Principle

- **Melting point** is the temperature at which a solid changes to liquid at atmospheric pressure. A pure compound melts sharply, over a range of about 1 °C. Impurities **lower** the melting point and **widen** the range. If an unknown and a known sample are mixed and the melting point does not fall (**mixed melting point**), the two are identical.
- **Boiling point** is the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the external pressure. In Siwoloboff's method, a capillary sealed at the top is dipped open end down in the liquid; air and then vapour escape as bubbles, and when heating stops, the liquid is drawn into the capillary at the moment its vapour pressure falls just below atmospheric pressure — that temperature is the boiling point.

## Requirements

Capillary tubes (sealed at one end), Thiele tube or melting point apparatus, liquid paraffin, thermometer (0–360 °C), rubber band or thread, fusion tube (ignition tube), burner, watch glass, glass rod, pure samples for calibration (e.g. benzoic acid).

## Procedure

**Melting point (capillary method)**

1. Powder the dry sample finely on a watch glass.
2. Press the open end of a capillary into the powder and tap the sealed end on the bench until 2–3 mm of tightly packed solid sits at the bottom.
3. Tie the capillary to the thermometer so that the sample is level with the middle of the bulb.
4. Immerse in the Thiele tube filled with liquid paraffin (keep the open end above the oil).
5. Heat the side arm quickly to about 15 °C below the expected melting point, then slowly (about 2 °C per minute).
6. Record **t1** when the first drop of liquid appears and **t2** when the solid has completely melted.
7. Repeat with a fresh capillary for a concordant value.

**Boiling point (Siwoloboff's method)**

8. Put about 0.5 ml of the liquid in a fusion tube; drop in a capillary (sealed at its upper end) open end down.
9. Tie the fusion tube to the thermometer and heat in the paraffin bath.
10. A slow stream of bubbles first appears (expanding air). Continue heating until a rapid, continuous stream comes out.
11. Remove the flame. Note the temperature at which bubbling stops and the liquid just begins to enter the capillary: this is the boiling point.

## Precautions

1. The sample must be dry and finely powdered; pack it tightly.
2. Heat slowly near the melting point (about 2 °C per minute).
3. Use a fresh capillary for every determination; a melted sample may change on resolidifying.
4. Keep the thermometer bulb and sample at the same level, and stir the bath (Thiele tube shape helps convection).
5. Note the boiling point while cooling, when bubbling stops, not while bubbles are still streaming.

## Viva questions

1. **Why does an impurity lower the melting point?** It disturbs the crystal lattice and lowers the vapour pressure of the liquid phase (freezing point depression).
2. **What is a mixed melting point?** The melting point of a 1 : 1 mixture of the unknown with a known sample; no depression means they are identical.
3. **Why is liquid paraffin used in the bath?** It is clear, non-volatile, stable up to about 200–250 °C and does not catch fire easily.
4. **Why does the liquid enter the capillary in Siwoloboff's method?** When the vapour pressure inside falls just below atmospheric pressure, the outside pressure pushes the liquid in.
5. **Why should the heating be slow near the melting point?** So that the thermometer and the sample stay at the same temperature.
6. **How does pressure affect boiling point?** Lower pressure lowers the boiling point (used in vacuum distillation).
