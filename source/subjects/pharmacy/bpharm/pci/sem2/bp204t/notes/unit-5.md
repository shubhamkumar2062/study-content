# Unit 5: Infectious diseases and sexually transmitted diseases

The last unit covers infections that are common and important in India: meningitis, typhoid, leprosy, tuberculosis and urinary tract infections, and the sexually transmitted diseases AIDS, syphilis and gonorrhoea. It is a short unit (7 hours), but almost every paper has at least one short question from it — tuberculosis, typhoid and AIDS are the favourites. For each disease learn: **causative organism, mode of spread, pathogenesis, clinical features, complications and diagnosis**.

## 5.1 Basic terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Infection | Entry and multiplication of a microorganism in the body |
| Pathogen | Organism able to cause disease |
| Virulence | Degree of disease-producing power |
| Incubation period | Time from entry of the organism to the first symptoms |
| Carrier | Person who harbours and spreads the organism without being ill |
| Opportunistic infection | Infection by an organism that causes disease mainly when immunity is low (e.g. in AIDS) |
| Communicable disease | Spreads from person to person |

**Routes of spread**: droplets and air (TB, meningococcus), faeco-oral (typhoid, cholera), sexual contact (HIV, syphilis, gonorrhoea), blood and needles (HIV, hepatitis B), mother to child (HIV, syphilis), insects (malaria, dengue), skin contact.

## 5.2 Meningitis

**Definition.** Inflammation of the **meninges** (the membranes covering the brain and spinal cord — pia and arachnoid, with the cerebrospinal fluid in the subarachnoid space).

### Causes

| Type | Organisms | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acute bacterial (pyogenic) | *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Neisseria meningitidis* (meningococcus, epidemics), *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (children, now reduced by vaccine); *E. coli*, group B streptococcus and *Listeria* in newborns | All ages |
| Viral (aseptic) | Enteroviruses, mumps, herpes simplex | Usually milder, self-limiting |
| Tuberculous | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* | Slow onset over weeks; common in India; high risk of disability |
| Fungal | *Cryptococcus neoformans* | Immunocompromised (AIDS) |

### Pathogenesis of bacterial meningitis

```text
Bacteria colonise the nose/throat
→ invade the bloodstream (bacteraemia)
→ cross the blood–brain barrier (choroid plexus) into the CSF
   (or spread directly: ear or sinus infection, skull fracture)
→ multiply in CSF (few defences there)
→ bacterial products trigger cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1) → neutrophils pour in
→ purulent exudate, raised permeability, brain oedema
→ ↑ intracranial pressure, ↓ cerebral blood flow, vasculitis, nerve damage
```

### Clinical features

- **Triad**: fever, headache, **neck stiffness**; also vomiting, photophobia, confusion, drowsiness, seizures.
- **Kernig's sign**: with the hip flexed, extension of the knee is painful and resisted. **Brudzinski's sign**: flexing the neck causes the hips and knees to flex.
- Meningococcal disease: a non-blanching **purpuric (petechial) rash** and rapid shock.
- Infants: irritability, poor feeding, bulging fontanelle.

### CSF findings (lumbar puncture)

| Feature | Bacterial | Viral | Tuberculous |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Turbid | Clear | Clear or slightly turbid; fibrin web on standing |
| Main cells | Neutrophils, many | Lymphocytes | Lymphocytes |
| Protein | High | Normal or slightly high | High |
| Glucose | Low | Normal | Low |

**Complications**: brain abscess, hydrocephalus, deafness (especially after pneumococcal meningitis), seizures, cranial nerve palsies, intellectual disability, septic shock, death.

## 5.3 Typhoid (enteric) fever

**Cause.** *Salmonella enterica* serotype **Typhi** (typhoid) and serotypes Paratyphi A, B, C (paratyphoid, milder). Gram-negative, motile bacilli. Humans are the only natural host.

**Spread.** **Faeco-oral** — food or water contaminated with faeces or urine of patients or **carriers** (the bacteria can persist in the gallbladder). Flies, poor sanitation and street food add to the risk. Incubation about 1–3 weeks (commonly 10–14 days).

### Pathogenesis

```text
Ingested bacilli survive stomach acid (large dose or low acid)
→ invade the small-intestine mucosa via M cells of Peyer's patches
→ taken up by macrophages, survive inside them
→ carried to mesenteric lymph nodes → blood (first, silent bacteraemia)
→ multiply in liver, spleen, bone marrow (reticuloendothelial system)
→ second, large bacteraemia → FEVER and symptoms (end of incubation)
→ re-infect the gallbladder → bile → intestine
→ Peyer's patches become inflamed, necrose and ulcerate (2nd–3rd week)
   → risk of HAEMORRHAGE and PERFORATION
```

### Clinical features (classic course)

| Week | Features |
| --- | --- |
| 1st | Gradual **step-ladder** rise of fever, headache, malaise, abdominal pain, dry cough, constipation (or diarrhoea in children); relative bradycardia (pulse slower than expected for the fever) |
| 2nd | High continuous fever, **rose spots** (faint pink spots on trunk), enlarged spleen and liver, coated tongue, toxic look, confusion ("typhoid state") |
| 3rd | Complications: intestinal bleeding, perforation (peritonitis), encephalopathy, myocarditis |
| 4th | Gradual recovery in survivors |

**Diagnosis**
- **Blood culture** — best test in the first week (bone marrow culture is the most sensitive).
- Stool and urine culture positive later (2nd–3rd week).
- **Widal test** (antibodies to O and H antigens) — becomes positive from the second week; a rising titre is more meaningful than a single value; many false positives in endemic areas such as India.

**Complications**: intestinal haemorrhage and perforation (most serious), hepatitis, cholecystitis, pneumonia, myocarditis, osteomyelitis, relapse, **chronic carrier state** (gallbladder, especially with gallstones).

Prevention: safe water, sanitation, hand washing, food hygiene, typhoid vaccines.

## 5.4 Leprosy (Hansen's disease)

**Cause.** ***Mycobacterium leprae*** — an acid-fast bacillus that grows very slowly (doubling time about 12–14 days), cannot be grown on artificial media, and prefers cooler body parts (skin, peripheral nerves, nose, eyes, testes). India has a large share of the world's new cases.

**Spread.** Mainly by nasal droplets from untreated multibacillary patients, after prolonged close contact. Incubation is long (often 3–5 years or more). Most exposed people do not develop the disease.

### Pathogenesis

The bacillus enters Schwann cells of peripheral nerves and macrophages. The **form of disease depends on the cell-mediated (T-cell) immunity of the patient**.

| Feature | Tuberculoid leprosy | Lepromatous leprosy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immunity | Good cell-mediated immunity (Th1) | Poor cell-mediated immunity (Th2) |
| Bacilli | Very few | Very many (globi in foamy macrophages) |
| Skin lesions | Few, large, well-defined, dry, hairless, **anaesthetic** hypopigmented patches | Many, symmetrical nodules and plaques; thickened skin of face (**leonine facies**), loss of eyebrows |
| Nerves | Early, asymmetrical thickening and damage | Late, symmetrical (glove-and-stocking) |
| Lepromin test | Positive | Negative |
| Histology | Granulomas | Foamy macrophages full of bacilli, no good granulomas |
| Infectivity | Low | High |

Borderline forms lie between (Ridley–Jopling scale: TT, BT, BB, BL, LL). For treatment, **WHO** classifies patients as **paucibacillary** (1–5 skin lesions, no bacilli in skin smear) or **multibacillary** (more than 5 lesions, or nerve involvement, or a positive smear).

### Clinical features and complications

- Hypopigmented or reddish skin patches with **loss of sensation** — the key sign.
- **Thickened, tender peripheral nerves** (ulnar, common peroneal, great auricular).
- Nerve damage → numbness → repeated unnoticed injuries and burns → ulcers, loss of fingers and toes; claw hand, foot drop; eye damage (inability to close eyes → blindness).
- **Lepra reactions**: type 1 (reversal reaction — sudden worsening of skin lesions and nerve pain, cell-mediated) and type 2 (erythema nodosum leprosum — painful red nodules, fever, immune-complex mediated, in lepromatous leprosy).

**Diagnosis**: clinical signs, slit-skin smear for acid-fast bacilli, skin biopsy.

## 5.5 Tuberculosis

**Cause.** ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** — a slow-growing, aerobic, **acid-fast** bacillus (resists decolourisation by acid alcohol in Ziehl–Neelsen staining because of mycolic acids in its waxy cell wall). *M. bovis* (from unpasteurised milk) causes some intestinal TB. India carries the largest share of the global TB burden.

**Spread.** **Airborne droplet nuclei** coughed out by patients with open pulmonary TB. Risk factors: close contact, overcrowding, malnutrition, **HIV infection**, diabetes, smoking, alcohol, silicosis, corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants, poverty.

### Pathogenesis

```text
Inhaled bacilli reach alveoli → engulfed by alveolar macrophages
→ bacilli survive and multiply inside (block phagosome–lysosome fusion)
→ after about 3 weeks, Th1 cell-mediated immunity develops:
   T cells release IFN-γ → macrophages activated → kill bacilli
   → epithelioid cells + Langhans giant cells + lymphocytes = GRANULOMA (tubercle)
   → centre undergoes CASEOUS NECROSIS
   (hypersensitivity → tuberculin/Mantoux test becomes positive)
```

**Primary TB** (first infection, often children)
- **Ghon focus**: a small caseous lesion, usually in the lower part of the upper lobe or upper part of the lower lobe, near the pleura.
- **Ghon complex** = Ghon focus + caseous hilar lymph nodes.
- In most people it heals by fibrosis and calcification; bacilli may stay dormant (**latent TB**).
- In infants and the immunocompromised it may progress: progressive primary TB, **miliary TB** (blood spread — tiny millet-seed lesions in many organs), tuberculous meningitis.

**Secondary (post-primary, reactivation) TB** (adults)
- Reactivation of dormant bacilli (or re-infection) when immunity falls.
- Lesion usually in the **apex of the upper lobe** (high oxygen).
- Caseation erodes into a bronchus → **cavity** → bacilli coughed out (highly infectious) → spread within the lungs; erosion of a vessel → haemoptysis.

### Clinical features of pulmonary TB

- Cough for **2 weeks or more**, sputum, haemoptysis (blood in sputum), chest pain.
- **Evening rise of fever, night sweats**, loss of appetite, weight loss, fatigue.

**Extrapulmonary TB**: lymph nodes (cervical — "scrofula"), pleura (effusion), spine (**Pott's disease** — vertebral collapse, gibbus), meninges, abdomen and intestine, kidneys and genital tract, bones and joints, pericardium.

**Complications**: haemoptysis, pleural effusion and empyema, pneumothorax, spread to other organs, lung fibrosis and bronchiectasis, respiratory failure, amyloidosis; **drug-resistant TB** (MDR-TB — resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin) from irregular treatment.

**Diagnosis**: sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli, nucleic-acid tests (CBNAAT/GeneXpert — also detects rifampicin resistance), sputum culture, chest X-ray, tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release assay (show infection, not active disease), biopsy showing caseating granulomas.

**TB and HIV**: each worsens the other; TB is a leading cause of death in people with HIV.

## 5.6 Urinary tract infections (UTI)

**Definition.** Infection anywhere in the urinary tract — bladder (**cystitis**), urethra (urethritis), kidney and pelvis (**pyelonephritis**), prostate.

**Organisms**: ***Escherichia coli*** causes most cases; others are *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterococcus*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* (young women), *Pseudomonas* (in hospital and catheterised patients).

### Pathogenesis

Most UTIs are **ascending**: gut bacteria colonise the area around the urethra → enter the bladder → may ascend the ureters to the kidney. Bacterial **fimbriae (pili)** help them stick to the lining. Rarely infection reaches the kidney through the blood.

**Risk factors**
- **Female sex** — short urethra close to the anus; sexual activity; pregnancy; menopause.
- **Obstruction and stasis**: enlarged prostate, stones, tumours, strictures, neurogenic bladder.
- **Urinary catheters** (catheter-related UTI is one of the most common hospital-acquired infections).
- Vesicoureteric reflux in children; diabetes mellitus; immunosuppression.

### Clinical features

| Lower UTI (cystitis) | Upper UTI (acute pyelonephritis) |
| --- | --- |
| Burning or painful urination (dysuria), frequency, urgency | High fever with chills and rigors |
| Lower abdominal (suprapubic) pain | Loin (flank) pain and tenderness |
| Cloudy, foul-smelling urine, sometimes blood | Nausea, vomiting; may also have cystitis symptoms |
| Usually no fever | Can lead to sepsis |

Elderly patients may present only with confusion.

**Diagnosis**: urine dipstick (leukocyte esterase, nitrites), microscopy (pus cells), **urine culture** (significant bacteriuria: commonly 10⁵ colony-forming units per ml or more in a clean-catch midstream sample).

**Complications**: recurrent infection, chronic pyelonephritis and kidney scarring → chronic kidney disease, renal abscess, urosepsis, problems in pregnancy (preterm birth).

## 5.7 Sexually transmitted diseases

Infections spread mainly by sexual contact; many also spread by blood and from mother to child. They often occur together, and genital ulcers increase the risk of HIV transmission.

### AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome)

**Cause.** **Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV-1, HIV-2)** — an RNA **retrovirus** (lentivirus) with the enzyme **reverse transcriptase**. HIV-1 causes most cases worldwide; HIV-2 is less virulent.

**Spread**: unprotected sexual contact (most common), infected blood and blood products, shared needles and syringes, needle-stick injuries, mother to child (pregnancy, delivery, breastfeeding). **Not** spread by touching, hugging, sharing food, mosquitoes or coughing.

**Pathogenesis**

```text
HIV gp120 binds CD4 molecule + co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4) on
CD4+ T helper cells, macrophages, dendritic cells
→ fusion (gp41) → viral RNA enters
→ reverse transcriptase makes DNA copy
→ integrase inserts it into host DNA (provirus — lifelong infection)
→ new virions made (protease cleaves proteins) and released
→ CD4+ T cells destroyed (direct killing, immune attack, apoptosis)
→ steady fall in CD4 count → loss of cell-mediated immunity
→ OPPORTUNISTIC INFECTIONS and CANCERS
```

**Stages**

| Stage | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Acute HIV infection (2–4 weeks after exposure) | Flu-like or glandular-fever-like illness: fever, sore throat, rash, lymph node swelling; very high viral load, very infectious |
| Clinical latency (chronic, asymptomatic) | Lasts years without treatment; virus keeps multiplying in lymph nodes; CD4 count slowly falls; persistent generalised lymphadenopathy |
| Symptomatic phase | Weight loss, fever, diarrhoea, oral thrush, herpes zoster |
| AIDS | CD4 count **below 200 cells/µL** or an AIDS-defining illness |

**AIDS-defining conditions**: *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia, **tuberculosis** (most common in India), oesophageal candidiasis, cryptococcal meningitis, toxoplasmosis of the brain, cytomegalovirus retinitis, *Mycobacterium avium* complex, **Kaposi sarcoma** (HHV-8), non-Hodgkin lymphoma, invasive cervical cancer, HIV dementia, HIV wasting syndrome.

**Diagnosis**: antibody (or combined antigen–antibody) tests such as ELISA/rapid tests, confirmed by further tests; viral load and CD4 count to follow the disease.

### Syphilis

**Cause.** ***Treponema pallidum*** — a thin spirochaete that cannot be grown on ordinary media; seen by dark-ground microscopy.

**Spread**: sexual contact, mother to fetus across the placenta (**congenital syphilis**), rarely blood transfusion.

**Stages**

| Stage | Time after infection (approx.) | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary | About 3 weeks (9–90 days) | **Chancre** — single, painless, firm, clean-based ulcer at the site of entry (genitals, mouth); painless enlarged nearby lymph nodes; heals by itself in a few weeks |
| Secondary | 6 weeks to 6 months | Spread through blood: generalised non-itchy rash including **palms and soles**, mucous patches in the mouth, condylomata lata (moist wart-like lesions), fever, generalised lymphadenopathy, patchy hair loss; very infectious |
| Latent | Years | No symptoms; only blood tests positive; early latent can relapse |
| Tertiary (untreated, about one-third) | Years to decades | **Gummas** (destructive granulomas in skin, bone, liver); **cardiovascular** syphilis (aortitis, aortic aneurysm, aortic regurgitation); **neurosyphilis** (tabes dorsalis, general paresis, Argyll Robertson pupils) |

**Congenital syphilis**: stillbirth, rash, snuffles, saddle nose, Hutchinson's notched teeth, sabre shins, deafness.

**Diagnosis**: dark-ground microscopy of chancre fluid; non-treponemal tests (**VDRL**, RPR — for screening and following treatment); treponemal tests (**TPHA**, FTA-ABS — confirm infection, stay positive for life).

### Gonorrhoea

**Cause.** ***Neisseria gonorrhoeae*** (gonococcus) — a **gram-negative, kidney-shaped diplococcus**, often seen inside neutrophils in pus. Incubation 2–7 days.

**Pathogenesis**: pili attach the bacteria to columnar epithelium of the urethra, cervix, rectum, throat and eye; bacteria invade and cause acute purulent inflammation. Its antigens keep changing, so one infection gives no lasting immunity and reinfection is common.

**Clinical features**
- **Men**: acute **urethritis** with **burning urination** and **thick yellow-green purulent discharge**; may spread to the epididymis (epididymitis) and prostate.
- **Women**: often **without symptoms**; cervicitis with vaginal discharge, painful urination; spread upwards gives **pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** → infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain.
- Other sites: rectum, throat.
- **Disseminated gonococcal infection**: fever, skin pustules, septic arthritis.
- **Newborns**: **ophthalmia neonatorum** (purulent conjunctivitis from an infected mother at birth) → blindness if not treated.

**Complications**: urethral stricture in men, PID and infertility in women, sterility from epididymitis in men, increased HIV transmission, growing **antibiotic resistance**.

**Diagnosis**: gram stain of urethral discharge (gram-negative intracellular diplococci), culture, nucleic-acid amplification tests.

## Remember

- Meningitis: fever, headache, neck stiffness, Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs; bacterial CSF = turbid, neutrophils, high protein, low glucose.
- Typhoid: *Salmonella* Typhi, faeco-oral, Peyer's patches, step-ladder fever, rose spots, relative bradycardia; blood culture in week 1; perforation in week 3; gallbladder carriers.
- Leprosy: *M. leprae*, anaesthetic patches + thick nerves; tuberculoid (good immunity, few bacilli) vs lepromatous (poor immunity, many bacilli, leonine face); WHO paucibacillary ≤ 5 lesions.
- TB: airborne, granuloma with caseous necrosis, Ghon focus/complex in primary, apical cavity in secondary; cough ≥ 2 weeks, evening fever, night sweats; HIV is the strongest risk factor.
- UTI: *E. coli*, ascending route; women, obstruction and catheters are risks; cystitis vs pyelonephritis; 10⁵ CFU/ml.
- HIV: retrovirus, gp120 binds CD4 + CCR5/CXCR4, reverse transcriptase; AIDS = CD4 < 200 cells/µL or AIDS-defining illness.
- Syphilis: painless chancre → rash on palms and soles → latent → gumma, aortitis, neurosyphilis; VDRL screens, TPHA confirms.
- Gonorrhoea: gram-negative diplococci, purulent urethritis in men, often silent in women → PID; ophthalmia neonatorum.
