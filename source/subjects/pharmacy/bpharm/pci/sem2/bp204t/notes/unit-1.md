# Unit 1: Cell injury, adaptation, inflammation and repair

Pathophysiology explains **why** a disease happens (aetiology), **how** it develops (pathogenesis), and **what** it does to the body (signs, symptoms and complications). This unit builds the base for the whole subject: how a cell keeps its balance, how it adapts to stress, how it gets injured or dies, how the body reacts with inflammation, and how tissue is repaired. It is the longest unit of the syllabus and gives at least one long question in most papers.

## 1.1 Introduction and definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Pathology | Study of disease: structural and functional changes in cells, tissues and organs |
| Pathophysiology | Study of the disturbed physiology (functions) that produce the signs and symptoms of a disease |
| Aetiology | The cause of a disease (bacteria, toxin, gene defect, unknown = idiopathic) |
| Pathogenesis | The sequence of events from the cause to the disease |
| Lesion | The structural change produced in a tissue |
| Sign | What the doctor finds on examination (fever, enlarged liver) |
| Symptom | What the patient feels and reports (pain, nausea) |
| Syndrome | A set of signs and symptoms that occur together |
| Prognosis | The expected outcome of a disease |
| Acute / chronic | Sudden and short-lived / slow and long-lasting |

## 1.2 Homeostasis and feedback systems

**Homeostasis** is the ability of the body to keep its internal environment (temperature, pH, blood glucose, blood pressure, water and electrolytes) nearly constant in spite of changes outside. Disease appears when homeostasis fails.

### Components of a feedback system

```text
Stimulus (change)
      ↓
Receptor (sensor) ── detects the change
      ↓ afferent (input) pathway
Control centre (brain, hypothalamus, endocrine gland) ── compares with set point
      ↓ efferent (output) pathway
Effector (muscle, gland) ── produces a response
      ↓
Response changes the controlled variable  ──► fed back to the receptor
```

### Types of feedback

| Type | What the response does | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Negative feedback | Opposes (reverses) the original change; keeps the variable stable. Most body systems | Body temperature control, baroreceptor control of blood pressure, insulin–glucagon control of blood glucose, thyroid hormone control of TSH |
| Positive feedback | Strengthens the original change until an end event stops it | Oxytocin and uterine contractions in labour, blood clotting cascade, LH surge before ovulation |
| Feed-forward | Body responds before the change happens | Insulin release when food is seen or tasted |

A positive feedback that runs out of control is harmful, e.g. in shock a fall in cardiac output lowers coronary flow, which weakens the heart further.

## 1.3 Cell injury

### Causes of cellular injury

1. **Hypoxia** (oxygen deficiency) — the most common cause. Ischaemia (reduced blood supply), anaemia, CO poisoning, lung disease.
2. **Physical agents** — trauma, extreme heat or cold, radiation, electric shock, sudden pressure change.
3. **Chemical agents and drugs** — poisons (cyanide, arsenic), alcohol, carbon tetrachloride, drug overdose (paracetamol), even high oxygen or salt.
4. **Infectious agents** — viruses, bacteria, fungi, parasites and their toxins.
5. **Immunological reactions** — hypersensitivity, autoimmune disease.
6. **Genetic defects** — sickle cell anaemia, enzyme deficiencies.
7. **Nutritional imbalance** — protein-energy malnutrition, vitamin deficiency, obesity.
8. **Ageing** — reduced ability to repair damage.

### Pathogenesis (mechanisms) of cell injury

The same few biochemical events are seen with most causes.

| Target | What happens | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mitochondria | Hypoxia and toxins stop oxidative phosphorylation; mitochondrial permeability transition pores open; cytochrome c leaks out | ATP depletion, more free radicals, apoptosis |
| Cell membrane | ATP loss stops the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump → Na⁺ and water enter; free radicals cause lipid peroxidation; phospholipases break phospholipids | Cell swelling, loss of permeability, leakage of enzymes |
| Ribosomes | Swelling of rough endoplasmic reticulum and detachment of ribosomes | Protein synthesis falls |
| Nucleus (DNA) | Free radicals, radiation and toxins damage DNA | Apoptosis (if repair fails) or mutation |
| Calcium | Raised cytosolic Ca²⁺ activates phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases and ATPases | Destruction of membranes, proteins and chromatin |
| Free radicals | Superoxide, H2O2, hydroxyl radical from reperfusion, radiation, drugs | Lipid peroxidation, protein cross-links, DNA breaks |

**Sequence in hypoxic (ischaemic) injury**

```text
↓ O2 → ↓ oxidative phosphorylation → ↓ ATP
   ├─ ↓ Na+/K+ pump → Na+ and water enter → cell swelling, ER swelling
   ├─ ↑ anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acid → ↓ pH → chromatin clumping
   └─ ribosomes detach → ↓ protein synthesis
        (all REVERSIBLE if O2 returns)
Continued hypoxia → severe membrane damage + mitochondrial damage + Ca2+ influx
   → lysosomal enzymes leak → NECROSIS (IRREVERSIBLE)
```

Restoring blood flow can sometimes worsen damage (**reperfusion injury**) because fresh oxygen produces a burst of free radicals and brings in calcium and inflammatory cells.

## 1.4 Morphology of cell injury: adaptive changes

When stress is mild or long-lasting, the cell adapts instead of dying.

| Adaptation | Definition | Physiological example | Pathological example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Atrophy | Decrease in the size of cells (and organ) | Thymus after puberty, uterus after menopause | Muscle wasting after nerve injury or plaster, brain atrophy in Alzheimer's, reduced blood supply |
| Hypertrophy | Increase in the size of cells (no new cells) | Skeletal muscle in body builders, uterus in pregnancy | Left ventricular hypertrophy in hypertension and aortic stenosis |
| Hyperplasia | Increase in the number of cells | Breast in puberty and lactation, liver regrowth after partial removal | Endometrial hyperplasia (excess oestrogen), benign prostatic hyperplasia, warts |
| Metaplasia | One adult cell type is replaced by another adult cell type | — | Ciliated columnar → squamous epithelium in smokers' bronchi; squamous → columnar (intestinal type) in lower oesophagus with acid reflux (Barrett's) |
| Dysplasia | Disordered growth: cells vary in size and shape, nuclei large and dark, loss of normal arrangement | — | Cervix, bronchus; a pre-cancerous change that may turn into carcinoma |

Hypertrophy and hyperplasia often occur together (uterus in pregnancy). Hypertrophy alone happens in cells that cannot divide (heart and skeletal muscle). Metaplasia and dysplasia are reversible if the cause is removed, but dysplasia can progress to cancer.

## 1.5 Reversible changes: cell swelling and intracellular accumulation

### Cell swelling (hydropic change)

The first change in almost every injury. Na⁺ and water enter because the pump fails. The cell looks pale and enlarged with small clear vacuoles. The organ becomes heavy and pale. Fully reversible.

### Intracellular accumulations

| Substance | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Lipids (fatty change, steatosis) | Fatty liver in alcoholism, diabetes, obesity, malnutrition, CCl4 poisoning |
| Cholesterol | Foam cells (macrophages full of cholesterol) in atherosclerosis; xanthomas |
| Proteins | Reabsorbed protein droplets in kidney tubules in proteinuria; Russell bodies (immunoglobulin) in plasma cells; Mallory bodies in alcoholic liver |
| Glycogen | Diabetes mellitus, glycogen storage diseases |
| Exogenous pigments | Carbon (anthracosis) in the lungs of city dwellers and coal miners, tattoo ink |
| Endogenous pigments | Lipofuscin ("wear-and-tear" brown pigment of ageing), melanin, haemosiderin (iron overload, old bruise), bilirubin (jaundice) |

## 1.6 Pathological calcification

Abnormal deposition of calcium salts in soft tissues.

| Feature | Dystrophic calcification | Metastatic calcification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tissue | Dead or degenerating tissue | Normal (living) tissue |
| Serum calcium | Normal | Raised (hypercalcaemia) |
| Causes | Atheromas, damaged heart valves, caseous necrosis of TB, dead parasites, fat necrosis | Hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D excess, bone destruction by tumours, chronic renal failure |
| Common sites | Any site of necrosis | Kidneys, lungs, stomach, blood vessels |

## 1.7 Enzyme leakage and cell death

When the membrane of an injured cell is damaged, its enzymes leak into the blood. Measuring them helps diagnosis.

| Enzyme / marker | Organ it points to |
| --- | --- |
| ALT (SGPT), AST (SGOT) | Liver (ALT more specific); AST also heart and muscle |
| Cardiac troponin I or T, CK-MB | Heart muscle (myocardial infarction) |
| Creatine kinase (total) | Skeletal muscle |
| Amylase, lipase | Pancreas |
| Alkaline phosphatase | Bone and bile ducts |
| LDH | Many tissues (non-specific) |

### Necrosis

Death of cells in a living tissue after severe injury, with swelling, membrane rupture and inflammation around it.

| Type | Features | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coagulative | Cell outline kept for some days; proteins denatured | Infarct of heart, kidney (not brain) |
| Liquefactive | Tissue digested into a liquid | Brain infarct, abscess |
| Caseous | Cheese-like, white, crumbly; granuloma around it | Tuberculosis |
| Fat | Fat broken by lipase; calcium soaps form | Acute pancreatitis, breast trauma |
| Fibrinoid | Pink fibrin-like material in vessel wall | Malignant hypertension, vasculitis |
| Gangrene | Necrosis of a large tissue mass; dry (arterial block) or wet (with infection) | Diabetic foot, intestine |

### Apoptosis versus necrosis

| Feature | Apoptosis | Necrosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Programmed, controlled ("cell suicide") | Uncontrolled, accidental |
| Cells involved | Single cells | Groups of cells |
| Cell size | Shrinks | Swells |
| Membrane | Intact; forms apoptotic bodies | Ruptured |
| Nucleus | Chromatin condenses, breaks into fragments | Pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis |
| Inflammation | None | Present |
| Enzymes | Caspases | Lysosomal enzymes |
| Examples | Embryo development, removal of old cells, virus-infected cells, DNA damage | Infarction, burns, toxins |

## 1.8 Acidosis and alkalosis

Normal arterial blood: pH 7.35–7.45, PaCO2 35–45 mmHg, HCO3⁻ 22–26 mEq/L. Body defends pH with **buffers** (bicarbonate, phosphate, proteins, haemoglobin), the **lungs** (remove CO2 in minutes) and the **kidneys** (excrete H⁺ and make HCO3⁻ over days).

```text
CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3-
pH = 6.1 + log [HCO3-] / (0.03 × PaCO2)
```

| Disorder | Primary change | Common causes | Compensation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | ↓ HCO3⁻ | Diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis (shock), renal failure, severe diarrhoea, methanol or salicylate poisoning | Hyperventilation (Kussmaul breathing) lowers CO2 |
| Metabolic alkalosis | ↑ HCO3⁻ | Prolonged vomiting, nasogastric suction, loop or thiazide diuretics, excess antacid, hypokalaemia | Hypoventilation raises CO2 |
| Respiratory acidosis | ↑ PaCO2 | Hypoventilation: COPD, severe asthma, opioid or sedative overdose, chest wall disease | Kidneys retain HCO3⁻ |
| Respiratory alkalosis | ↓ PaCO2 | Hyperventilation: anxiety, fever, high altitude, early salicylate poisoning, pulmonary embolism | Kidneys excrete HCO3⁻ |

**Anion gap** = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO3⁻), normally about 8–12 mEq/L. It rises in acidosis from added acids (ketones, lactate, renal failure, poisons) and stays normal when bicarbonate is lost (diarrhoea).

Effects: acidosis causes depressed heart contraction, low blood pressure, confusion and hyperkalaemia; alkalosis causes tingling, muscle cramps and tetany (less ionised calcium), arrhythmias and hypokalaemia.

## 1.9 Electrolyte imbalance

| Disorder | Normal range | Causes | Main effects |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hyponatraemia | Na⁺ 135–145 mEq/L | Excess water (SIADH, heart failure), diuretics, vomiting, diarrhoea | Brain swelling: headache, confusion, seizures |
| Hypernatraemia | | Water loss (diabetes insipidus, fever, poor intake) | Thirst, brain shrinkage, confusion, coma |
| Hypokalaemia | K⁺ 3.5–5.0 mEq/L | Diuretics, vomiting, diarrhoea, insulin, hyperaldosteronism | Muscle weakness, paralytic ileus, flat T and U waves on ECG, arrhythmias, raised digoxin toxicity |
| Hyperkalaemia | | Renal failure, K⁺-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors, acidosis, cell breakdown | Peaked T waves, arrhythmias, cardiac arrest |
| Hypocalcaemia | Total Ca 8.5–10.5 mg/dL | Hypoparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, renal failure | Tetany, carpopedal spasm, seizures, prolonged QT |
| Hypercalcaemia | | Hyperparathyroidism, cancer, vitamin D excess | "Stones, bones, groans and moans": kidney stones, bone pain, constipation, confusion |

## 1.10 Inflammation

**Inflammation** is the protective response of living vascularised tissue to injury. It aims to remove the cause and the dead cells and to start repair. If it is excessive or misdirected it causes disease (asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis).

### Clinical (cardinal) signs

| Sign | Latin | Cause |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Redness | Rubor | Vasodilatation, more blood |
| Heat | Calor | Increased blood flow |
| Swelling | Tumor | Exudate leaking from vessels |
| Pain | Dolor | Bradykinin, prostaglandins, pressure on nerves |
| Loss of function | Functio laesa | Pain and swelling (added later by Virchow) |

The first four were described by Celsus.

### Types of inflammation

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Duration | Acute (minutes to days; fluid exudate and neutrophils) and chronic (weeks to years; lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells, fibrosis, new vessels) |
| Exudate | Serous (watery: blister, pleural effusion), fibrinous (fibrin-rich: pericarditis), suppurative or purulent (pus: abscess, boil), haemorrhagic, catarrhal (mucus: common cold) |
| Special pattern | Ulcer (loss of surface epithelium: peptic ulcer); granulomatous (collection of epithelioid macrophages and giant cells: tuberculosis, leprosy, sarcoidosis) |

**Acute versus chronic inflammation**

| Feature | Acute | Chronic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Fast | Slow |
| Main cells | Neutrophils | Macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells |
| Tissue damage | Usually mild, self-limited | Progressive |
| Fibrosis | Little | Marked |
| Examples | Appendicitis, boil, sore throat | Tuberculosis, rheumatoid arthritis, peptic ulcer |

## 1.11 Mechanism of acute inflammation

### A. Vascular events: changes in blood flow and permeability

1. **Brief vasoconstriction** of arterioles (seconds).
2. **Vasodilatation** (histamine, nitric oxide, prostaglandins) → increased flow → redness and heat.
3. **Increased vascular permeability** — protein-rich fluid (exudate) leaks into the tissue → swelling. Mechanisms: contraction of endothelial cells in venules (histamine, bradykinin, leukotrienes; quick and short-lived), direct endothelial injury (burns, toxins; prolonged), leukocyte-mediated injury, increased transcytosis (VEGF), and leaky new vessels.
4. **Stasis** — loss of fluid makes blood thick and slow; leukocytes move to the vessel edge.

**Exudate versus transudate**

| Feature | Exudate (inflammation) | Transudate (non-inflammatory, e.g. heart failure) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protein | High | Low |
| Specific gravity | Above 1.020 | Below 1.012 |
| Cells | Many | Few |
| Cause | Increased permeability | Raised hydrostatic or low oncotic pressure |

### B. Cellular events: migration of white blood cells

```text
Margination → Rolling → Firm adhesion → Transmigration (diapedesis) → Chemotaxis → Phagocytosis
              (selectins)  (integrins on WBC +    (through junctions,        (towards C5a,   (recognition, engulfment,
                           ICAM-1/VCAM-1 on        PECAM-1 / CD31)            LTB4, IL-8,      killing, digestion)
                           endothelium)                                       bacterial products)
```

- **Margination**: slow flow pushes leukocytes to the periphery of the vessel.
- **Rolling**: weak, temporary binding through selectins (E-, P- and L-selectin).
- **Adhesion**: firm binding of leukocyte integrins to endothelial ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, switched on by TNF and IL-1.
- **Transmigration**: cells squeeze between endothelial cells, mainly in venules.
- **Chemotaxis**: movement along a chemical gradient.
- **Phagocytosis**: microbes coated with opsonins (C3b, IgG) are recognised, engulfed into a phagosome, fused with lysosomes and killed by reactive oxygen species (NADPH oxidase, myeloperoxidase forming hypochlorite) and enzymes.

Neutrophils dominate the first 6–24 hours; monocytes/macrophages take over after 24–48 hours.

### C. Mediators of inflammation

| Mediator | Source | Main actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Histamine | Mast cells, basophils, platelets | Vasodilatation, increased permeability (the earliest mediator) |
| Serotonin (5-HT) | Platelets | Vasodilatation, permeability |
| Prostaglandins (PGE2, PGI2) | Cyclooxygenase pathway of arachidonic acid | Vasodilatation, pain, fever |
| Thromboxane A2 | Platelets (COX) | Vasoconstriction, platelet aggregation |
| Leukotrienes | Lipoxygenase pathway | LTB4: chemotaxis; LTC4, LTD4, LTE4: bronchoconstriction, permeability |
| Platelet-activating factor | Many cells | Platelet aggregation, bronchoconstriction, permeability |
| Cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) | Macrophages, lymphocytes | Endothelial activation, fever, acute-phase proteins (CRP) |
| Chemokines (IL-8) | Many cells | Chemotaxis |
| Nitric oxide | Endothelium, macrophages | Vasodilatation, microbial killing |
| Complement (C3a, C5a, C3b, C5b–9) | Plasma | C3a/C5a anaphylatoxins (histamine release); C5a chemotaxis; C3b opsonin; membrane attack complex lyses microbes |
| Bradykinin (kinin system) | Plasma | Permeability, vasodilatation, pain |
| Coagulation and fibrinolysis products | Plasma | Thrombin activates endothelium; fibrin split products increase permeability |

```text
Membrane phospholipids
        │ phospholipase A2 (blocked by corticosteroids)
        ▼
  Arachidonic acid
   ├── Cyclooxygenase (blocked by NSAIDs) → PGE2, PGI2, PGF2α, TXA2
   └── 5-Lipoxygenase → LTB4, LTC4, LTD4, LTE4
```

### Systemic effects and outcomes

Fever, raised ESR and CRP, leukocytosis, malaise. Outcomes of acute inflammation: complete resolution, healing by fibrosis (scar), abscess formation, or progression to chronic inflammation.

## 1.12 Basic principles of wound healing in the skin

**Repair** restores tissue by regeneration (same cells grow back — epithelium, liver) and/or by **fibrosis** (connective-tissue scar — where cells cannot divide, or the framework is destroyed).

### Phases of skin wound healing

| Phase | Time (approx.) | Events |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemostasis | Minutes | Vasoconstriction, platelet plug, fibrin clot forms a scaffold |
| Inflammation | Days 1–3 | Neutrophils, then macrophages clear debris; growth factors released (PDGF, TGF-β, VEGF, FGF) |
| Proliferation | Days 3–14 | **Granulation tissue** (new capillaries + fibroblasts + loose matrix, pink granular look); epithelium spreads over the surface; collagen (type III first) laid down; myofibroblasts begin contraction |
| Maturation (remodelling) | Weeks to a year or more | Type III collagen replaced by stronger type I; scar becomes pale; strength rises slowly but never fully returns to normal |

### Healing by first and second intention

| Feature | First (primary) intention | Second (secondary) intention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wound | Clean, edges close together (surgical incision with sutures) | Large, gaping, edges apart, infected or with tissue loss (ulcers, burns, abscess) |
| Granulation tissue | Little | Abundant, fills the gap from the base |
| Wound contraction | Minimal | Marked (myofibroblasts) |
| Scar | Thin, neat | Large, may be deforming |
| Time | Fast | Slow |
| Infection risk | Low | High |

### Factors affecting wound healing

- **Local**: infection (the most important), poor blood supply, foreign bodies, movement, large size, radiation, pressure.
- **Systemic**: poor nutrition (protein, vitamin C — needed for collagen hydroxylation, zinc), diabetes mellitus, corticosteroids (suppress inflammation and fibrosis), old age, anaemia, poor circulation, cancer chemotherapy.

Complications: wound infection, wound dehiscence (opening), excess scar (hypertrophic scar, keloid), contractures, incisional hernia.

## 1.13 Pathophysiology of atherosclerosis

**Atherosclerosis** is a chronic inflammatory disease of the intima of large and medium arteries (aorta, coronary, carotid, cerebral, leg arteries) in which lipid-rich plaques (**atheromas**) form, narrow the lumen and may rupture.

### Risk factors

| Non-modifiable | Modifiable |
| --- | --- |
| Increasing age, male sex (women catch up after menopause), family history, genetic lipid disorders | High LDL cholesterol, hypertension, smoking, diabetes mellitus, obesity, physical inactivity, low HDL, raised lipoprotein(a) and homocysteine, chronic inflammation (raised CRP) |

### Pathogenesis: response-to-injury hypothesis

```text
1. Endothelial injury / dysfunction (high BP, smoking, LDL, glucose, toxins)
        ↓  ↑ permeability, ↑ adhesion molecules
2. LDL enters intima → oxidised LDL
        ↓
3. Monocytes adhere, enter, become macrophages
        ↓ take up oxidised LDL via scavenger receptors
4. FOAM CELLS → FATTY STREAK (earliest visible lesion, even in children)
        ↓ cytokines and growth factors (PDGF)
5. Smooth muscle cells migrate from media to intima, multiply, make collagen
        ↓
6. FIBROUS (ATHEROMATOUS) PLAQUE = fibrous cap + lipid/necrotic core
        ↓
7. Complicated plaque: calcification, ulceration, rupture → thrombosis,
   haemorrhage into plaque, embolism, aneurysm
```

### Consequences

- Slow narrowing → reduced blood flow: stable angina, intermittent claudication, chronic ischaemia.
- Plaque rupture + thrombus → sudden block: myocardial infarction, stroke, gangrene of a limb.
- Weakening of the wall → aneurysm, mainly abdominal aorta.

## Remember

- Homeostasis is mostly negative feedback; positive feedback examples: labour (oxytocin), clotting, LH surge.
- ATP depletion, membrane damage, calcium influx and free radicals are the core mechanisms of cell injury.
- Atrophy ↓ size, hypertrophy ↑ size, hyperplasia ↑ number, metaplasia = one adult cell type replaced by another, dysplasia = disordered pre-cancerous growth.
- Dystrophic calcification: dead tissue, normal calcium. Metastatic: normal tissue, high calcium.
- Apoptosis: shrinkage, no inflammation, caspases. Necrosis: swelling, inflammation.
- Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO3⁻); diarrhoea causes normal-gap acidosis, vomiting causes metabolic alkalosis.
- Five signs of inflammation: rubor, calor, tumor, dolor, functio laesa.
- WBC sequence: margination → rolling (selectins) → adhesion (integrins) → transmigration (PECAM-1) → chemotaxis → phagocytosis.
- Steroids block phospholipase A2; NSAIDs block cyclooxygenase.
- Primary intention = clean, close edges, thin scar; secondary = gaping wound, much granulation tissue and contraction.
- Atheroma story: endothelial injury → LDL oxidation → foam cells → fatty streak → fibrous plaque → rupture and thrombosis.
