# Unit 4: Bowel and liver diseases, bone and joint diseases, principles of cancer

This unit covers inflammatory bowel disease, jaundice, viral hepatitis and alcoholic liver disease; rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis and gout; and the general principles of cancer. Comparison tables (Crohn's vs ulcerative colitis, types of jaundice, hepatitis viruses, benign vs malignant tumours) are easy marks, and cancer pathogenesis is a common long question.

## 4.1 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

**Definition.** Chronic, relapsing, immune-mediated inflammation of the intestine that is not caused by a known infection. The two main forms are **Crohn's disease** and **ulcerative colitis**.

### Aetiology and pathogenesis

The exact cause is unknown. Current view:

```text
Genetic susceptibility (e.g. NOD2 gene in Crohn's, family history)
        +
Abnormal gut bacteria (dysbiosis) and a leaky epithelial barrier
        +
Environmental triggers (smoking worsens Crohn's, Western diet, infections, NSAIDs)
        ↓
Inappropriate, persistent immune response (Th1/Th17 in Crohn's, Th2-like in UC)
→ TNF-α, IL-12, IL-23 and other cytokines → chronic inflammation and tissue damage
```

Young adults (15–30 years) are most often affected.

### Crohn's disease versus ulcerative colitis

| Feature | Crohn's disease | Ulcerative colitis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Any part of the gut from mouth to anus; terminal ileum most common | Colon only; always starts at the **rectum** and spreads upwards |
| Distribution | **Skip lesions** (patchy, normal bowel between) | **Continuous** |
| Depth | **Transmural** (full thickness) | Mucosa and submucosa only |
| Gross look | Cobblestone mucosa, deep fissuring ulcers, thick wall, "string sign" narrowing | Broad shallow ulcers, pseudopolyps, loss of folds ("lead-pipe" colon) |
| Microscopy | Non-caseating **granulomas** | Crypt abscesses, no granulomas |
| Fistulae, strictures | Common | Rare |
| Bleeding per rectum | Less common | Common — bloody diarrhoea with mucus |
| Smoking | Worsens | Seems protective |
| Cancer risk | Raised | Raised more (long-standing pancolitis) |
| Special complication | Malabsorption (B12 in ileal disease), perianal disease, obstruction | **Toxic megacolon**, severe haemorrhage |

**Common symptoms**: chronic diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss, fever, tiredness, anaemia; Crohn's often presents with right lower abdominal pain, UC with frequent bloody stools and urgency.

**Extra-intestinal features** (both): arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, uveitis, erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, primary sclerosing cholangitis (mainly UC), gallstones and kidney stones (Crohn's).

## 4.2 Jaundice

**Definition.** Yellow colour of the skin, sclera and mucous membranes due to a raised **bilirubin** in the blood. Normal serum bilirubin is below about 1 mg/dL; jaundice becomes visible at about 2–3 mg/dL. The sclera shows it first.

### Bilirubin metabolism

```text
Old red cells (spleen) → haemoglobin → haem → biliverdin → UNCONJUGATED bilirubin
   (fat-soluble, carried on albumin, NOT in urine)
        ↓ liver uptake
   Conjugation with glucuronic acid (enzyme UDP-glucuronyl transferase)
        ↓
   CONJUGATED bilirubin (water-soluble) → bile → intestine
        ↓ bacteria
   Urobilinogen → stercobilin (brown stool colour)
              → part reabsorbed → urobilinogen in urine
```

### Types of jaundice

| Feature | Pre-hepatic (haemolytic) | Hepatic (hepatocellular) | Post-hepatic (obstructive, cholestatic) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Excess breakdown of red cells: haemolytic anaemias, malaria, mismatched transfusion, neonatal jaundice | Liver cell damage: viral hepatitis, alcohol, drugs (paracetamol overdose, isoniazid, rifampicin), cirrhosis; Gilbert syndrome | Block to bile flow: gallstones in the common bile duct, cancer of the head of pancreas, biliary stricture; drug-induced cholestasis |
| Bilirubin raised | Unconjugated | Both | Conjugated |
| Urine bilirubin | Absent ("acholuric") | Present | Present (dark urine) |
| Urine urobilinogen | Increased | Variable | Absent (complete block) |
| Stool | Dark | Normal or pale | **Pale, clay-coloured** |
| Itching | No | Sometimes | Common (bile salts in skin) |
| Liver enzymes | Normal | High ALT, AST | High alkaline phosphatase and GGT |

**Neonatal (physiological) jaundice**: common in the first week because liver conjugation is immature and red cells break down fast. Very high unconjugated bilirubin can enter the newborn brain (**kernicterus**).

## 4.3 Viral hepatitis (A, B, C, D, E and the so-called F)

**Hepatitis** is inflammation of the liver. Viral hepatitis can be acute (less than 6 months) or chronic (more than 6 months).

| Virus | Type | Spread | Incubation (approx.) | Chronic infection | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A (HAV) | RNA, picornavirus | **Faeco-oral** (contaminated food and water) | 2–6 weeks | No | Children often mild; outbreaks; vaccine available |
| B (HBV) | **DNA**, hepadnavirus | **Blood, sexual, mother to child**, shared needles | 1–6 months | Yes — under about 5% of infected adults, but about 90% of infants infected at birth | Cirrhosis and liver cancer; vaccine available; markers HBsAg, HBeAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs |
| C (HCV) | RNA, flavivirus | **Blood** (transfusion before screening, injecting drug use, unsafe injections), less often sexual | 2 weeks–6 months | Yes — the majority (about 70%) | Often silent for years; cirrhosis and liver cancer; no vaccine; curable with antivirals |
| D (HDV) | Defective RNA virus | Blood, sexual | — | Yes | **Needs HBV** (uses HBsAg coat): co-infection or superinfection; worsens hepatitis B |
| E (HEV) | RNA virus | **Faeco-oral** (water-borne epidemics, common in India) | 2–8 weeks | Rarely (only in the immunocompromised) | High death rate in **pregnant women** (fulminant hepatitis) |
| "F" | Not established | — | — | — | Proposed after some cases were not explained by A–E; the virus was never confirmed, so "hepatitis F" is not a recognised separate disease |

Easy memory: **A and E come through the bowel ("vowels → bowels")**; B, C, D come through blood and body fluids.

### Pathogenesis

Hepatitis viruses mostly do not kill liver cells directly; liver injury comes largely from the **host's immune response** (cytotoxic T cells attacking infected hepatocytes). A strong response clears the virus (acute hepatitis); a weak response lets the virus persist (chronic hepatitis).

### Clinical course of acute hepatitis

1. **Prodrome (pre-icteric)**: fever, malaise, loss of appetite, nausea, distaste for smoking, aching in the right upper abdomen.
2. **Icteric phase**: jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, tender enlarged liver; patient often feels better as jaundice appears.
3. **Recovery** over weeks.

**Outcomes**: full recovery; **fulminant hepatic failure** (encephalopathy, bleeding — rare); chronic hepatitis → **cirrhosis** → **hepatocellular carcinoma** (B, C, D); carrier state (B).

**Laboratory**: very high ALT and AST, raised bilirubin, viral markers (anti-HAV IgM, HBsAg, anti-HCV, anti-HEV IgM).

## 4.4 Alcoholic liver disease

Long-term heavy alcohol intake damages the liver in three overlapping stages.

```text
Heavy drinking
   ↓
1. FATTY LIVER (steatosis) – fat in liver cells; enlarged, yellow liver;
   usually no symptoms; REVERSIBLE if drinking stops
   ↓
2. ALCOHOLIC HEPATITIS – liver cell swelling and necrosis, neutrophils,
   Mallory bodies; fever, jaundice, tender liver; can be fatal
   ↓
3. ALCOHOLIC CIRRHOSIS – fibrous bands + regenerating nodules
   (micronodular); IRREVERSIBLE
```

### Pathogenesis

- Alcohol is oxidised by alcohol dehydrogenase (and CYP2E1 in heavy drinkers) to **acetaldehyde**, which is toxic: it binds proteins, damages microtubules and mitochondria.
- Oxidation produces excess **NADH**, which favours fat synthesis and blocks fatty acid oxidation → fatty liver.
- CYP2E1 activity generates **free radicals** (oxidative stress).
- Gut bacterial endotoxin reaches the liver → Kupffer cells release TNF-α → inflammation.
- Stellate (Ito) cells become activated and lay down collagen → fibrosis.
- Poor diet adds to the damage.

### Complications of cirrhosis (any cause)

| Problem | Mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| Portal hypertension | Fibrosis blocks portal flow → oesophageal varices (bleeding, haematemesis), splenomegaly, caput medusae |
| Ascites and oedema | Portal hypertension + low albumin + salt retention (RAAS) |
| Hepatic encephalopathy | Ammonia and other toxins not cleared: confusion, flapping tremor, coma |
| Bleeding tendency | Fewer clotting factors (raised PT) and low platelets |
| Jaundice | Failing liver cells |
| Hormonal changes | Less oestrogen breakdown: gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, spider naevi, palmar erythema |
| Hepatorenal syndrome, infections | Kidney failure, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis |
| Hepatocellular carcinoma | Risk raised in cirrhosis |

**Drug relevance**: impaired drug metabolism and low albumin → dose adjustment; avoid hepatotoxic drugs and sedatives.

## 4.5 Rheumatoid arthritis

**Definition.** A chronic, systemic, **autoimmune** inflammatory disease mainly affecting the synovium of joints, giving **symmetrical polyarthritis** of small joints, which can destroy cartilage and bone. Women are affected about three times more than men, usually between 30 and 50 years.

### Pathogenesis

```text
Genetic susceptibility (HLA-DR4, shared epitope) + trigger (smoking, infection?)
   ↓ citrullination of proteins → loss of tolerance
Autoreactive CD4 T cells and B cells → autoantibodies:
   rheumatoid factor (IgM against Fc of IgG), anti-CCP (anti-citrullinated protein)
   ↓ cytokines TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6
Synovium becomes inflamed, thick and proliferates = PANNUS
   (granulation tissue with lymphocytes, plasma cells, new vessels)
   ↓ pannus grows over and erodes cartilage and bone (osteoclast activation)
Joint destruction → fibrous / bony ankylosis and deformity
```

### Clinical features

- Pain, swelling and warmth of small joints of hands (MCP, PIP) and feet, wrists, knees — **symmetrical**; distal interphalangeal joints usually spared.
- **Morning stiffness lasting more than an hour**, improving with use.
- Deformities: **ulnar deviation** of fingers, **swan-neck** and **boutonnière** deformities, Z-thumb.
- Systemic: fatigue, low fever, weight loss, anaemia of chronic disease.
- Extra-articular: rheumatoid nodules (elbows), dry eyes and mouth (Sjögren's), lung fibrosis, pericarditis, vasculitis, raised cardiovascular risk, carpal tunnel syndrome.
- Tests: raised ESR and CRP, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP (more specific), X-ray erosions near joint margins.

**RA vs osteoarthritis**: RA — autoimmune inflammation, small joints, symmetrical, long morning stiffness, systemic; OA — "wear and tear" of cartilage, weight-bearing joints (knee, hip) and DIP joints, stiffness under 30 minutes, worse with use, no systemic illness.

## 4.6 Osteoporosis

**Definition.** A systemic skeletal disease with **low bone mass** and **deterioration of bone micro-architecture**, giving fragile bones and a high risk of fracture. The bone that is present is normally mineralised (compare osteomalacia, where mineralisation is poor). WHO definition by DEXA scan: bone mineral density **T-score of −2.5 or lower**; −1 to −2.5 = osteopenia.

### Pathogenesis

Bone is constantly remodelled: **osteoclasts** remove bone and **osteoblasts** form it. Osteoporosis results when resorption exceeds formation.

| Type | Cause |
| --- | --- |
| Primary type I (postmenopausal) | Loss of oestrogen after menopause → more RANKL, more osteoclast activity → rapid loss of trabecular bone → vertebral and wrist fractures |
| Primary type II (senile) | Ageing in both sexes: fewer osteoblasts, less calcium absorption, less vitamin D → loss of cortical and trabecular bone → hip fractures |
| Secondary | Long-term **corticosteroids** (most common drug cause), heparin, anticonvulsants, excess thyroxine; hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing's syndrome, hypogonadism, chronic kidney and liver disease, rheumatoid arthritis, malabsorption, immobilisation |

**Risk factors**: female sex, age, early menopause, thin build, family history, low calcium and vitamin D intake, lack of exercise, smoking, alcohol.

**Features**: a "silent disease" until fracture. Fractures of vertebrae (loss of height, kyphosis or "dowager's hump", back pain), wrist (Colles' fracture), and hip (neck of femur — high disability and death in the elderly).

## 4.7 Gout

**Definition.** A metabolic disease in which raised serum **uric acid** (hyperuricaemia) leads to deposition of **monosodium urate crystals** in and around joints, causing very painful attacks of arthritis, and in tissues and kidneys. Uric acid is the end product of **purine** metabolism (xanthine oxidase converts hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid). Blood becomes saturated with urate above about 6.8 mg/dL.

### Causes of hyperuricaemia

| Over-production (about 10%) | Under-excretion by the kidney (about 90%) |
| --- | --- |
| High-purine diet (red meat, organ meat, seafood), alcohol (beer), fructose drinks | Kidney disease |
| Rapid cell turnover: leukaemia, lymphoma, cancer chemotherapy (tumour lysis), psoriasis | Drugs: thiazide and loop diuretics, low-dose aspirin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, ciclosporin |
| Enzyme defects (Lesch–Nyhan syndrome, HGPRT deficiency) | Lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis (compete for excretion), obesity, hypertension |

### Pathogenesis of an acute attack

```text
Urate crystals form in the joint (cool, distal joints; trauma, alcohol, dehydration)
→ crystals are taken up by neutrophils and macrophages
→ NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β → intense acute inflammation
→ hot, red, swollen, exquisitely painful joint
```

### Clinical stages

1. **Asymptomatic hyperuricaemia** — most people never get gout.
2. **Acute gouty arthritis** — sudden severe pain, often at night, usually in **one joint**: classically the **first metatarsophalangeal joint (big toe) = podagra**; also ankle, knee. Settles in days to two weeks.
3. **Intercritical period** — free intervals between attacks.
4. **Chronic tophaceous gout** — **tophi** (chalky urate deposits) in ear helix, around joints, tendons; joint destruction.

**Kidney effects**: uric acid stones, urate nephropathy.

**Diagnosis**: needle-shaped, **negatively birefringent** crystals in joint fluid under polarised light (pseudogout crystals are rhomboid and positively birefringent). Serum uric acid can be normal during an attack.

## 4.8 Principles of cancer

**Neoplasm (tumour)**: an abnormal mass of tissue whose growth is excessive, uncoordinated with normal tissue, and continues after the stimulus stops. **Cancer** is the common term for malignant neoplasms. **Oncology** is the study of tumours.

### Classification

**By behaviour**

| Feature | Benign tumour | Malignant tumour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth rate | Slow | Usually fast |
| Capsule | Often present | Absent |
| Spread | Expands, pushes tissue aside | **Invades** surrounding tissue |
| Metastasis | Never | Common |
| Differentiation | Well differentiated, like the parent tissue | Poorly differentiated to undifferentiated (anaplasia) |
| Cells | Uniform | Pleomorphic, large dark nuclei, many abnormal mitoses |
| Recurrence after removal | Rare | Common |
| Effect on patient | Usually by pressure or hormones | Can kill (cachexia, organ failure) |

**By tissue of origin (naming)**

| Origin | Benign | Malignant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covering / glandular epithelium | Papilloma, adenoma | **Carcinoma** (squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma) |
| Connective tissue (mesenchyme) | Lipoma, fibroma, osteoma, chondroma, leiomyoma | **Sarcoma** (liposarcoma, osteosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma) |
| Blood-forming cells | — | Leukaemia, lymphoma, myeloma |
| Melanocytes | Naevus (mole) | Melanoma |
| Germ cells | Mature teratoma | Seminoma, malignant teratoma |
| Embryonic tissue | — | Blastomas (retinoblastoma, neuroblastoma, Wilms' tumour) |

Note: melanoma, lymphoma and seminoma are malignant even though the names end in "-oma".

### Aetiology (causes and risk factors)

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Chemical carcinogens | Tobacco smoke (lung, mouth, bladder), gutka and betel quid (oral cancer — very common in India), polycyclic hydrocarbons, aromatic amines (bladder), aflatoxin (liver), asbestos (mesothelioma), benzene (leukaemia), alkylating drugs (cyclophosphamide) |
| Radiation | UV light (skin cancer, melanoma), ionising radiation (leukaemia, thyroid cancer) |
| Viruses (oncogenic) | Human papillomavirus (cervical cancer), hepatitis B and C (liver cancer), Epstein–Barr virus (Burkitt's lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma), HTLV-1 (T-cell leukaemia), HHV-8 (Kaposi sarcoma) |
| Bacteria | *Helicobacter pylori* (gastric cancer, gastric lymphoma) |
| Hormones | Oestrogen (endometrium, breast) |
| Heredity | BRCA1/BRCA2 (breast, ovary), RB gene (retinoblastoma), APC (familial polyposis colon cancer) |
| Others | Age, obesity, alcohol, chronic inflammation (ulcerative colitis), immunosuppression |

### Pathogenesis (molecular basis)

Cancer is a **genetic disease of cells**: non-lethal damage to DNA accumulates in a single cell, which then grows into a clone (monoclonal origin). Four classes of genes are targets:

| Gene class | Normal role | In cancer | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proto-oncogenes → **oncogenes** | Promote normal growth | Gain-of-function mutation → constant "accelerator" | RAS, MYC, HER2 (ERBB2), BCR-ABL |
| **Tumour suppressor genes** | Brake on cell growth | Loss-of-function (both copies — Knudson's "two-hit" idea) → "brakes fail" | p53 ("guardian of the genome"), RB, APC, BRCA1/2 |
| Apoptosis-regulating genes | Remove damaged cells | Anti-apoptotic genes overactive → damaged cells survive | BCL-2 over-expression |
| DNA repair genes | Fix DNA errors | Defective repair → mutations pile up | Mismatch repair genes, BRCA |

```text
Carcinogen / virus / radiation / inherited mutation
        ↓
DNA damage escapes repair
        ↓
Activation of oncogenes + loss of tumour suppressors + loss of apoptosis
        ↓
Clonal growth → more mutations (tumour progression)
        ↓
Hallmarks gained: self-sufficient growth signals, ignoring growth inhibitors,
escape from apoptosis, unlimited replication (telomerase),
new blood vessels (angiogenesis, VEGF), invasion and metastasis,
altered metabolism (Warburg effect), escape from immune attack
```

**Multistep carcinogenesis**: initiation (irreversible DNA damage) → promotion (growth of initiated cells by promoters such as hormones, phorbol esters) → progression (more mutations, invasion).

### Invasion and metastasis

Tumour cells loosen from each other (loss of E-cadherin), attach to and digest the basement membrane (metalloproteinases), migrate, enter vessels, survive in blood, exit at a distant site and grow there.

| Route | Typical of | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lymphatic | Carcinomas | Breast cancer → axillary nodes |
| Blood (haematogenous) | Sarcomas, also many carcinomas | Liver and lungs are common sites; bone (prostate, breast) |
| Seeding of body cavities (transcoelomic) | | Ovarian cancer across the peritoneum |

**Grading** (how differentiated the cells look) and **staging** (how far it has spread — TNM: Tumour size, Node involvement, Metastasis) guide treatment and prognosis; stage matters more.

**Effects on the patient**: local pressure and obstruction, bleeding, infection, pain, **cachexia** (wasting), paraneoplastic syndromes (hormone-like effects, e.g. hypercalcaemia), anaemia.

## Remember

- Crohn's: mouth-to-anus, skip lesions, transmural, granulomas, fistulae. UC: rectum upwards, continuous, mucosal, crypt abscesses, bloody diarrhoea, toxic megacolon.
- Jaundice visible above about 2–3 mg/dL bilirubin. Haemolytic = unconjugated, no bilirubin in urine; obstructive = conjugated, dark urine, pale stools, itching, high ALP.
- Hepatitis A and E faeco-oral, no chronic state (E dangerous in pregnancy); B (DNA), C, D blood-borne, can become chronic; D needs B; "F" is not an established virus.
- Alcoholic liver disease: fatty liver → alcoholic hepatitis (Mallory bodies) → cirrhosis; acetaldehyde, NADH, free radicals.
- RA: autoimmune, symmetrical small joints, pannus, RF and anti-CCP, morning stiffness > 1 h, ulnar deviation.
- Osteoporosis: T-score ≤ −2.5; postmenopausal (oestrogen loss) and senile; steroids are the main drug cause.
- Gout: urate crystals (needle, negatively birefringent), podagra, tophi; diuretics and alcohol raise uric acid.
- Benign vs malignant: invasion and metastasis decide. Carcinoma = epithelial, sarcoma = mesenchymal.
- Cancer genes: oncogenes (RAS, MYC), tumour suppressors (p53, RB), apoptosis (BCL-2), DNA repair.
