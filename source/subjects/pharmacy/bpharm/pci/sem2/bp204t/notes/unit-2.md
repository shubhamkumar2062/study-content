# Unit 2: Cardiovascular, respiratory and renal diseases

This unit covers the common chronic diseases a pharmacist meets every day at the counter: high blood pressure, heart failure, angina and heart attack, asthma and COPD, and kidney failure. For each disease learn the same five headings: **definition, causes (aetiology), pathogenesis, signs and symptoms, complications**. Hypertension, congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction and asthma are favourite long questions.

## 2.1 Hypertension

**Definition.** A persistent rise of arterial blood pressure. In Indian and most international practice, hypertension is diagnosed when repeated office readings are **140/90 mmHg or above** (some newer guidelines use 130/80 mmHg as the threshold for treatment in high-risk people). Normal is below 120/80 mmHg.

| Category (office BP) | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | below 120 | below 80 |
| High normal / prehypertension | 120–139 | 80–89 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 140–159 | 90–99 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | 160 or more | 100 or more |

### Types

1. **Primary (essential) hypertension** — no single cause; about 90–95% of patients. Genetic tendency plus environment.
2. **Secondary hypertension** — caused by another disease:
   - Renal: chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis (renovascular), glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney.
   - Endocrine: primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome), Cushing's syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, hyperthyroidism.
   - Others: coarctation of aorta, pregnancy (pre-eclampsia), drugs (oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, NSAIDs, decongestants), sleep apnoea.

### Pathogenesis

```text
Blood pressure = Cardiac output × Total peripheral resistance
                 (heart rate × stroke volume)   (arteriolar tone)
```

Factors that raise either side cause hypertension:

| Mechanism | How it raises BP |
| --- | --- |
| Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system | Angiotensin II constricts arterioles, releases aldosterone (Na⁺ and water retention), stimulates thirst and sympathetic activity |
| Sympathetic overactivity | Stress → noradrenaline → raised heart rate, contractility and vasoconstriction; renin release |
| Sodium and water retention | High salt intake or kidney defect → larger blood volume |
| Endothelial dysfunction | Less nitric oxide, more endothelin → vasoconstriction |
| Structural change | Vessel walls thicken (remodelling), so resistance stays high |
| Others | Obesity, insulin resistance, genetics, ageing (stiff arteries raise systolic BP) |

```text
↓ renal perfusion → kidney releases RENIN
Angiotensinogen (liver) --renin--> Angiotensin I --ACE (lungs)--> Angiotensin II
Angiotensin II → vasoconstriction + aldosterone (Na+/H2O retention) + ADH + thirst
            → ↑ peripheral resistance + ↑ blood volume → ↑ BP
```

### Signs and symptoms

Usually **none** — the "silent killer" — found on routine check. Some patients have morning headache at the back of the head, dizziness, palpitations, nosebleed, blurred vision.

### Complications (target-organ damage)

| Organ | Damage |
| --- | --- |
| Heart | Left ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction |
| Brain | Stroke (haemorrhage and infarct), hypertensive encephalopathy, dementia |
| Kidney | Nephrosclerosis, chronic kidney disease |
| Eyes | Hypertensive retinopathy (narrowed arteries, haemorrhages, papilloedema) |
| Vessels | Accelerated atherosclerosis, aortic dissection, aneurysm, peripheral arterial disease |

**Hypertensive crisis**: a very high reading (often around 180/120 mmHg or more). If organ damage is present (encephalopathy, pulmonary oedema, MI) it is an emergency.

## 2.2 Congestive heart failure

**Definition.** The heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the needs of the tissues, or can do so only at raised filling pressures. "Congestive" because blood backs up in the lungs and/or the veins of the body.

### Causes

- Ischaemic heart disease (most common), myocardial infarction.
- Hypertension (pressure overload).
- Valve disease (aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation), cardiomyopathy, myocarditis.
- Arrhythmias, congenital heart disease.
- High-output states: severe anaemia, thyrotoxicosis, beriberi.
- Precipitating factors: infection, uncontrolled BP, non-adherence, excess salt or fluid, NSAIDs.

### Types

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Function | Systolic failure (weak contraction, reduced ejection fraction) and diastolic failure (stiff ventricle, poor filling, ejection fraction preserved) |
| Side | Left-sided and right-sided (most patients end up with both) |
| Speed | Acute (e.g. after MI) and chronic |
| Output | Low-output (most) and high-output (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis) |

### Pathogenesis: compensation that becomes harmful

```text
Damaged heart → ↓ cardiac output → ↓ BP and renal perfusion
   ├─ Sympathetic activation → ↑ HR, contractility, vasoconstriction (more workload)
   ├─ RAAS activation → Na+/water retention, vasoconstriction (more preload + afterload)
   ├─ ADH release → water retention
   ├─ Frank–Starling: more filling → stronger beat (only up to a point)
   └─ Ventricular remodelling/hypertrophy → stiff, enlarged, failing heart
These keep output up at first, but in the long run they overload the heart
and the cycle worsens → oedema, congestion.
```

### Signs and symptoms

| Left heart failure (lung congestion) | Right heart failure (systemic congestion) |
| --- | --- |
| Breathlessness on exertion (dyspnoea), orthopnoea (breathless lying flat), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea | Swelling of ankles and legs (pitting oedema) |
| Cough, frothy pink sputum in acute pulmonary oedema | Raised jugular venous pressure |
| Fine crackles at lung bases, third heart sound | Enlarged tender liver, ascites |
| Fatigue, cold peripheries | Loss of appetite, nausea (gut congestion) |

Most common cause of right heart failure is left heart failure. Right failure due to lung disease is called **cor pulmonale**.

**NYHA functional classes**: I no limitation; II slight limitation, symptoms on ordinary activity; III marked limitation, symptoms on less than ordinary activity; IV symptoms at rest.

**Complications**: pulmonary oedema, arrhythmias, sudden death, kidney failure, thromboembolism, liver congestion (cardiac cirrhosis), cachexia.

## 2.3 Ischaemic heart disease

**Definition.** A group of conditions caused by an imbalance between the oxygen supply of the heart muscle (coronary blood flow) and its oxygen demand. More than 90% of cases are due to **atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries** (coronary artery disease).

```text
Supply ↓ : atherosclerotic narrowing, thrombosis, spasm, anaemia, hypotension
Demand ↑ : exercise, tachycardia, hypertension, LVH, emotion, cold
               ↓
          MYOCARDIAL ISCHAEMIA
   ┌───────────┼───────────┬────────────┐
 Angina     Acute MI    Chronic IHD   Sudden cardiac death
```

### Angina pectoris

Chest pain from transient ischaemia **without** muscle death. Typically a squeezing or heavy pain behind the sternum, spreading to the left arm, neck or jaw, lasting a few minutes.

| Type | Mechanism | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stable (classic, exertional) | Fixed atherosclerotic narrowing; demand exceeds supply | Comes on exercise, emotion or cold; relieved by rest or sublingual nitrate in minutes |
| Unstable (crescendo) | Plaque rupture with a non-occlusive thrombus | Pain at rest, more frequent or longer; high risk of MI. Part of acute coronary syndrome |
| Variant (Prinzmetal) | Coronary artery spasm | Pain at rest, often early morning; ST elevation during the attack; relieved by nitrates and calcium channel blockers |

### Myocardial infarction (heart attack)

**Death (necrosis) of heart muscle** due to prolonged ischaemia, usually from a thrombus on a ruptured coronary plaque.

**Pathogenesis**

```text
Unstable plaque ruptures → platelets adhere and aggregate (TXA2, ADP)
→ coagulation → occlusive thrombus → no flow
→ ATP depletion within seconds, loss of contraction within about a minute
→ irreversible injury after roughly 20–40 minutes of severe ischaemia
→ necrosis spreads from subendocardium to the whole wall over a few hours
```

Hence "time is muscle" — opening the artery early (thrombolysis or angioplasty) saves myocardium.

**Types**: transmural (full-thickness, usually STEMI) and subendocardial (inner layer, usually NSTEMI).

**Clinical features**: severe crushing chest pain over 20–30 minutes, not relieved by rest or nitrates; sweating, nausea, vomiting, breathlessness, anxiety, sense of impending death. Elderly and diabetic patients may have a "silent" MI with little pain.

**Diagnosis**
- **ECG**: ST-segment elevation (STEMI) or ST depression/T inversion (NSTEMI); pathological Q waves later.
- **Cardiac markers**: troponin I or T (most specific; rises within a few hours and stays high for 1–2 weeks), CK-MB, myoglobin (early but non-specific).

**Complications**: arrhythmias (ventricular fibrillation — main cause of early death), heart failure and cardiogenic shock, rupture of wall, septum or papillary muscle, ventricular aneurysm, pericarditis, mural thrombus with embolism, recurrent infarction.

### Atherosclerosis and arteriosclerosis

**Arteriosclerosis** means "hardening of the arteries" — thickening and loss of elasticity of the arterial wall. It has three forms:

| Form | Vessels | Feature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atherosclerosis | Large and medium arteries | Lipid-rich intimal plaques (atheromas); the most important form (pathogenesis in Unit 1) |
| Mönckeberg medial calcific sclerosis | Medium muscular arteries, elderly | Calcium in the media; lumen not narrowed |
| Arteriolosclerosis | Small arteries and arterioles | Hyaline (hypertension, diabetes, ageing) or hyperplastic "onion-skin" (malignant hypertension) |

So every atherosclerosis is arteriosclerosis, but not every arteriosclerosis is atherosclerosis.

## 2.4 Bronchial asthma

**Definition.** A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways with **reversible** (variable) airflow obstruction, bronchial hyper-responsiveness and recurrent wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough, worse at night and early morning.

### Types

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Extrinsic (allergic, atopic) | Starts in childhood; triggered by allergens (pollen, house-dust mite, animal dander, moulds); raised IgE; family history of eczema, hay fever |
| Intrinsic (non-allergic) | Starts in adults; triggers are infection, cold air, exercise, stress, pollution; IgE not raised |
| Drug-induced | Aspirin and NSAIDs (shift arachidonic acid to leukotrienes), β-blockers |
| Occupational | Isocyanates, flour, wood dust |
| Exercise-induced | Cooling and drying of airways |

### Pathogenesis (allergic asthma)

```text
Sensitisation: allergen → dendritic cells → Th2 cells → IL-4, IL-5, IL-13
               → B cells make IgE → IgE binds to mast cells
Re-exposure:
 EARLY PHASE (minutes): allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells
   → histamine, leukotrienes C4/D4/E4, prostaglandin D2, PAF
   → bronchospasm, mucosal oedema, mucus secretion
 LATE PHASE (4–8 h): eosinophils, Th2 cells, neutrophils arrive
   → more inflammation, epithelial damage, hyper-responsiveness
Chronic: AIRWAY REMODELLING – thick basement membrane, smooth-muscle
   hypertrophy, goblet-cell hyperplasia, mucus plugs
```

**Signs and symptoms**: episodic wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness, dry cough (especially at night); prolonged expiration; reduced peak expiratory flow which improves after a bronchodilator.

**Complications**: status asthmaticus (severe attack not responding to usual treatment — an emergency), respiratory failure, pneumothorax, chest infections, side effects of long-term treatment.

## 2.5 Chronic obstructive airways disease (COPD)

**Definition.** A common, preventable disease with **persistent, poorly reversible** airflow limitation that usually progresses, caused by a long-term inflammatory response of the lungs to noxious particles or gases — mainly **cigarette smoking**, and in India also biomass (wood, dung) smoke from household cooking. Spirometry: post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.70.

It includes two overlapping conditions:

| Feature | Chronic bronchitis ("blue bloater") | Emphysema ("pink puffer") |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Productive cough for at least 3 months in each of 2 consecutive years | Permanent enlargement of airspaces beyond the terminal bronchiole with destruction of alveolar walls, without obvious fibrosis |
| Main lesion | Mucus gland hypertrophy, goblet-cell hyperplasia, inflamed narrow airways | Loss of alveolar walls and elastic recoil; airways collapse on expiration |
| Mechanism | Irritants → mucus hypersecretion → infection | Protease–antiprotease imbalance: smoke attracts neutrophils and macrophages releasing elastase, and oxidants inactivate α1-antitrypsin; inherited α1-antitrypsin deficiency gives panacinar emphysema |
| Picture | Cough with sputum, cyanosis, oedema, early cor pulmonale | Marked breathlessness, pursed-lip breathing, barrel chest, thin patient |
| Gases | Low O2, high CO2 early | O2 fairly kept until late |

Smoking gives **centriacinar** (centrilobular) emphysema of upper lobes; α1-antitrypsin deficiency gives **panacinar** emphysema of lower lobes.

**Complications**: acute exacerbations (often infection), respiratory failure, cor pulmonale, secondary polycythaemia, pneumothorax from ruptured bullae, lung cancer (shared smoking risk).

**Asthma vs COPD**: asthma — younger, allergic, reversible, eosinophils; COPD — older smokers, little reversibility, neutrophils, progressive.

## 2.6 Acute renal failure (acute kidney injury)

**Definition.** A sudden fall (hours to days) in kidney function, with rising serum creatinine and urea and often reduced urine output (**oliguria** = less than 400 ml/day; **anuria** = less than about 100 ml/day). It is often reversible.

### Causes

| Group | Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-renal (most common) | Kidney normal but under-perfused | Dehydration (vomiting, diarrhoea, burns), haemorrhage, shock, heart failure, NSAIDs or ACE inhibitors in a dry patient |
| Renal (intrinsic) | Damage to kidney tissue | Acute tubular necrosis (prolonged ischaemia; nephrotoxic drugs such as aminoglycosides, amphotericin B, contrast media; myoglobin, haemoglobin), acute glomerulonephritis, acute interstitial nephritis (drug allergy) |
| Post-renal | Block to urine outflow | Stones, enlarged prostate, tumours, blocked catheter |

### Pathogenesis of acute tubular necrosis

Ischaemia or toxins damage tubular cells → cells shed into the lumen and form casts that block tubules → filtrate leaks back through damaged walls → GFR falls; reflex vasoconstriction (via tubuloglomerular feedback) lowers GFR further.

### Phases

1. **Initiation** — the insult; output starts to fall.
2. **Oliguric (maintenance)** — low urine output; urea, creatinine, K⁺ and H⁺ rise; fluid overload.
3. **Diuretic (recovery) phase** — tubules heal but cannot yet concentrate urine → large urine volume; risk of dehydration, low K⁺ and Na⁺.
4. **Recovery** — function returns to normal over weeks.

**Effects**: fluid overload (oedema, pulmonary oedema, hypertension), hyperkalaemia (arrhythmias), metabolic acidosis, uraemia (nausea, confusion, pericarditis), drug accumulation — doses of renally excreted drugs must be reduced.

## 2.7 Chronic renal failure (chronic kidney disease)

**Definition.** Kidney damage or reduced function (GFR below 60 ml/min/1.73 m²) lasting **more than 3 months**; slowly progressive and irreversible. End-stage renal disease (GFR below 15) needs dialysis or a transplant.

| Stage | GFR (ml/min/1.73 m²) |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 90 or more, with kidney damage |
| 2 | 60–89 |
| 3a / 3b | 45–59 / 30–44 |
| 4 | 15–29 |
| 5 (kidney failure) | below 15 |

**Causes**: diabetic nephropathy (most common), hypertension, chronic glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, chronic pyelonephritis and obstruction, long-term analgesic or other nephrotoxic drug use.

**Pathogenesis**: nephrons are lost; the remaining nephrons hyperfilter to compensate → raised glomerular pressure → further glomerular sclerosis and loss of more nephrons (a vicious cycle). Proteinuria and angiotensin II speed the damage.

### Clinical features and complications

| System | Problem and cause |
| --- | --- |
| Fluid / electrolytes | Oedema, hypertension, hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, hyponatraemia |
| Blood | Normocytic anaemia — lack of **erythropoietin**; bleeding tendency |
| Bone | **Renal osteodystrophy** — less activation of vitamin D (1-α hydroxylation), phosphate retention, hypocalcaemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism |
| Uraemia | Nausea, vomiting, itching, metallic taste, confusion, pericarditis, neuropathy, uraemic breath |
| Heart | Hypertension, LVH, heart failure, accelerated atherosclerosis — the main cause of death |
| Urine | Early nocturia and polyuria (cannot concentrate); later oliguria |

**Acute vs chronic**: acute — sudden, often reversible, kidneys normal size; chronic — slow, irreversible, kidneys usually small and scarred, with anaemia and bone disease.

## Remember

- BP = cardiac output × peripheral resistance; RAAS and sympathetic system are central in hypertension. Essential hypertension ≈ 90–95%.
- Hypertension damages heart, brain, kidney, eyes and vessels; usually no symptoms.
- Heart failure compensations (SNS, RAAS, ADH, remodelling) help at first, then worsen failure. Left failure → lungs; right failure → legs, liver, neck veins.
- Stable angina: exertion, relieved by rest; unstable: at rest, plaque rupture; Prinzmetal: spasm.
- MI = necrosis; troponin is the most specific marker; ventricular fibrillation causes most early deaths.
- Arteriosclerosis is the umbrella term; atherosclerosis is its lipid-plaque form.
- Asthma: Th2, IgE, mast cells, early and late phases, reversible. COPD: smoking, neutrophils, protease–antiprotease, poorly reversible.
- AKI: pre-renal, renal (ATN), post-renal; oliguria < 400 ml/day; watch hyperkalaemia.
- CKD: GFR < 60 for > 3 months; anaemia (EPO), renal osteodystrophy (vitamin D), uraemia; diabetes is the top cause.
