# How to use this guide

Pathophysiology is a "story" subject: every disease follows the same chain — **cause → mechanism → what you see → what can go wrong**. Study one unit at a time: read the notes, draw each flow diagram once from memory, learn the comparison tables, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

The five units here follow the five units of the PCI syllabus. Unit 1 is large because the syllabus puts cell injury, acid–base and electrolytes, inflammation, wound healing and atherosclerosis together in it; read it over two days. Atherosclerosis is explained fully in Unit 1 (where the syllabus first names it) and only summarised again under ischaemic heart disease in Unit 2.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with a flow diagram and a comparison table.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Use the same skeleton for every disease answer: **Definition → Aetiology/risk factors → Pathogenesis (flow diagram) → Signs and symptoms → Complications → Diagnosis (one line)**. Examiners mark against these headings.
2. Draw a flow chart for pathogenesis (arrows from cause to effect). It earns marks quickly and shows you understand the mechanism.
3. "Differentiate" questions are frequent: learn the tables — apoptosis vs necrosis, acute vs chronic inflammation, exudate vs transudate, dystrophic vs metastatic calcification, type 1 vs type 2 diabetes, Crohn's vs UC, three types of jaundice, benign vs malignant tumour, tuberculoid vs lepromatous leprosy, left vs right heart failure, asthma vs COPD, acute vs chronic renal failure.
4. Give real Indian examples where they fit: hookworm and diet in iron deficiency, hepatitis E epidemics, gutka in oral cancer, TB and leprosy burden.
5. Learn the key numbers: pH 7.35–7.45, Na⁺ 135–145, K⁺ 3.5–5.0 mEq/L, BP 140/90, fasting glucose 126 mg/dL, HbA1c 6.5%, Hb 13/12 g/dL, CD4 < 200 cells/µL, T-score −2.5, oliguria < 400 ml/day, CKD GFR < 60 for > 3 months, visible jaundice at 2–3 mg/dL bilirubin.
6. Link to drugs in one line at the end where it helps (e.g. NSAIDs block COX, steroids block phospholipase A2, levodopa replaces dopamine in Parkinson's) — this subject is the base for pharmacology.
7. Spellings matter: aetiology, haemoglobin, oedema, ischaemia, diarrhoea; and write organism names in full the first time (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*).
