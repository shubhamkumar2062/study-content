# Unit 3: Respiratory system and urinary system

This unit joins two systems that work as a pair to keep the blood's composition steady. The lungs take in oxygen and remove carbon dioxide (and so control the acid part of blood pH quickly); the kidneys clean the blood of wastes, control water and salt, and correct pH slowly. Both are important routes for drugs: inhalers and anaesthetic gases enter through the lungs, and most drugs and their metabolites leave through the kidneys.

# Part A: Respiratory system

## 3.1 Organisation of the respiratory tract

```text
 UPPER RESPIRATORY TRACT
   Nose and nasal cavity  -- filters, warms, moistens air; smell
   Pharynx                -- naso-, oro-, laryngopharynx (shared with food)
   Larynx                 -- voice box; epiglottis guards it
 LOWER RESPIRATORY TRACT
   Trachea (C6 to T4/T5) -- divides at the CARINA into
   Right and left primary (main) bronchi
     -> secondary (lobar) bronchi -> tertiary (segmental) bronchi
     -> bronchioles -> terminal bronchioles          } CONDUCTING ZONE
                                                       (no gas exchange,
                                                        anatomical dead space
                                                        about 150 mL)
     -> respiratory bronchioles -> alveolar ducts
     -> alveolar sacs -> ALVEOLI                      } RESPIRATORY ZONE
                                                       (gas exchange)
```

| Structure | Key features |
| --- | --- |
| **Nose** | Nasal septum divides it; conchae (turbinates) increase surface; hairs and mucus trap dust; rich blood supply warms air |
| **Pharynx** | Muscular tube about 12–14 cm; tonsils (adenoids in the nasopharynx) |
| **Larynx** | Cartilages: thyroid (Adam's apple), cricoid (complete ring), epiglottis, paired arytenoids; contains the **vocal cords**; protects the airway during swallowing |
| **Trachea** | About 10–12 cm long, 16–20 **C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings** open behind (trachealis muscle) — keep the airway open but let the oesophagus expand; lined by **pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells** |
| **Bronchi** | Right main bronchus is shorter, wider and more vertical — inhaled objects usually enter it; cartilage plates decrease as bronchi divide |
| **Bronchioles** | No cartilage; walls rich in **smooth muscle** controlled by the ANS (β2 relaxes, muscarinic M3 constricts) — the site of bronchospasm in **asthma** and of action of **salbutamol** |

**Mucociliary escalator:** goblet cells make mucus that traps particles and microbes; cilia beat it upwards to the pharynx to be swallowed. Smoking paralyses cilia.

## 3.2 Anatomy of the lungs

The two lungs are cone-shaped spongy organs in the thoracic cavity, on either side of the mediastinum (which holds the heart). Each lung has an **apex** (rising about 2–3 cm above the clavicle), a **base** resting on the diaphragm, a **costal surface** against the ribs and a **mediastinal surface** with the **hilum**, where the root of the lung (main bronchus, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, bronchial vessels, nerves, lymphatics) enters.

| Feature | Right lung | Left lung |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lobes | **Three** (upper, middle, lower) | **Two** (upper, lower) |
| Fissures | Oblique and horizontal | Oblique only |
| Size | Shorter (liver below) and wider; heavier | Narrower, with the **cardiac notch** and **lingula** for the heart |
| Bronchopulmonary segments | 10 | 8–10 |

**Pleura:** a double serous membrane. The **visceral pleura** covers the lung; the **parietal pleura** lines the chest wall, diaphragm and mediastinum. Between them, the **pleural cavity** holds a thin film of fluid that lubricates and creates surface tension, so the lung follows the chest wall. Pressure in this cavity is normally **below atmospheric** (about 4 mmHg less at rest). Air in it (**pneumothorax**) collapses the lung; fluid is a pleural effusion; inflammation is pleurisy.

**Alveoli and the respiratory membrane**

```text
         alveolar air (O2 in, CO2 out)
   ____________________________________
  |  surfactant film / fluid layer     |
  |  TYPE I alveolar cell (thin, flat) |  <- most of the surface; gas exchange
  |  fused basement membranes          |
  |  capillary endothelium             |
  |____________________________________|
         blood in pulmonary capillary (RBC)

  Total thickness about 0.5 um; total area about 70 m2
  TYPE II cells (cuboidal): secrete SURFACTANT
  Alveolar macrophages (dust cells): remove particles
```

There are about **300 million alveoli**. **Surfactant** (mainly dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine) lowers surface tension so alveoli do not collapse on expiration and the lungs are easy to inflate. Premature babies may lack it — **respiratory distress syndrome**.

**Blood supply:** the **pulmonary circulation** (deoxygenated blood for gas exchange) and the **bronchial arteries** from the aorta (nourish lung tissue).

## 3.3 Mechanism of respiration

**Respiration** has four stages: pulmonary ventilation (breathing), external respiration (gas exchange in the lungs), transport of gases in blood, and internal respiration (exchange at the tissues). Cellular respiration (using O2 to make ATP) was covered in Unit 2.

Air moves because of pressure differences. By **Boyle's law** (at constant temperature, pressure × volume is constant), enlarging the thorax lowers lung pressure and air flows in.

**Inspiration (active)**

1. The respiratory centre sends impulses down the **phrenic nerves (C3–C5)** and intercostal nerves.
2. The **diaphragm** contracts and flattens, increasing the vertical size of the thorax (about 75% of quiet breathing).
3. The **external intercostal muscles** lift the ribs upwards and outwards ("bucket-handle" and "pump-handle" movements), increasing the front-to-back and side-to-side size.
4. The lungs, held to the chest wall by pleural fluid, expand. Intrapulmonary (alveolar) pressure falls to about **1 mmHg below atmospheric**.
5. Air flows in until pressures equalise.
6. In **forced inspiration**, accessory muscles help: sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, pectoralis minor.

**Expiration (passive at rest)**

1. The diaphragm and external intercostals relax.
2. **Elastic recoil** of the lungs and chest wall, and surface tension in the alveoli, reduce thoracic volume.
3. Alveolar pressure rises to about **1 mmHg above atmospheric** and air flows out.
4. **Forced expiration** (exercise, coughing) is active: **internal intercostals** pull ribs down and **abdominal muscles** push the diaphragm up.

```text
 INSPIRATION                         EXPIRATION
 diaphragm contracts (down)          diaphragm relaxes (up)
 ext. intercostals lift ribs         ribs fall back
 thoracic volume UP                  thoracic volume DOWN
 alveolar pressure ~ -1 mmHg         alveolar pressure ~ +1 mmHg
 air IN                              air OUT
```

**Factors that affect breathing:** **lung compliance** (stretchability; reduced in fibrosis, lack of surfactant), **airway resistance** (raised in asthma and chronic bronchitis), and elastic recoil (lost in emphysema).

**Normal rate:** about 12–18 breaths per minute in adults (higher in infants).

## 3.4 Regulation of respiration

Breathing is automatic but can be changed voluntarily (speaking, holding the breath). Control has nervous and chemical parts.

**A. Nervous (neural) control — respiratory centres in the brainstem**

```text
 HIGHER CENTRES (cerebral cortex, hypothalamus, limbic system)
   voluntary control, emotion, temperature
          |
 PONS:  PNEUMOTAXIC CENTRE ----| limits inspiration, sets rate
        APNEUSTIC CENTRE ------> prolongs inspiration
          |
 MEDULLA: DORSAL RESPIRATORY GROUP (DRG) -- inspiratory rhythm (basic pacemaker area)
          VENTRAL RESPIRATORY GROUP (VRG) -- active in forced breathing
          (pre-Botzinger complex generates the basic rhythm)
          |
 Spinal cord --> phrenic and intercostal nerves --> diaphragm, intercostals
```

**Reflexes that adjust breathing**

- **Hering–Breuer (inflation) reflex:** stretch receptors in the bronchial walls, via the vagus, stop inspiration when the lungs are well inflated — protects against over-inflation (important in infants and deep breathing).
- **Irritant receptors** in the airways: cough, sneeze, bronchoconstriction.
- **J (juxtacapillary) receptors:** stimulated by lung congestion and oedema — rapid shallow breathing.
- **Proprioceptors** in moving muscles and joints raise breathing at the start of exercise.
- **Baroreceptors:** a sharp rise in BP slightly depresses breathing.

**B. Chemical control** — keeps arterial PCO2, PO2 and H+ normal.

| Chemoreceptor | Location | Responds to | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Central** | Ventral surface of the medulla | **H+ in CSF**, produced when CO2 crosses the blood–brain barrier (CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 → H+ + HCO3−) | Most important; a small rise in **PCO2** strongly increases ventilation |
| **Peripheral** | **Carotid bodies** (at the carotid bifurcation; glossopharyngeal nerve) and **aortic bodies** (vagus nerve) | Fall in **PO2** (especially below about 60 mmHg), rise in PCO2 and H+ | Main sensor of **hypoxia** |

**Carbon dioxide is the most powerful stimulus** in health. In long-standing CO2 retention (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) the central receptors adapt, and breathing depends on the hypoxic drive — high-flow oxygen can then depress breathing, so oxygen is given in controlled amounts. Opioids, barbiturates and alcohol depress the respiratory centre.

## 3.5 Lung volumes and capacities

Measured with a **spirometer**; the record is a **spirogram**. Values are average for a young adult male (women about 20–25% lower).

```text
 volume
  (mL)
  ~5800 _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _ _  top of max inspiration
          |        |                    |
          |  IRV   |                    |
          | ~3000  |  IC  ~3500         |
  ~2800 __|__/\/\/\|____ end of normal inspiration      VC ~4600   TLC ~5800
          |  TV ~500                    |
  ~2300 __|__ end of normal expiration  |
          |  ERV   |                    |
          | ~1100  |  FRC ~2300         |
  ~1200 __|________|______ end of max expiration _______|
          |  RV ~1200 (cannot be expired; not measured by spirometer)
     0  __|_____________________________________________
```

| Volume (single) | Definition | Normal (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | Air breathed in or out in one quiet breath | 500 mL |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | Extra air that can be breathed in after a normal inspiration | 3000 mL |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | Extra air that can be forced out after a normal expiration | 1100 mL |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | Air left in the lungs after maximal expiration | 1200 mL |

| Capacity (sum of volumes) | Formula | Normal (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inspiratory capacity (IC)** | TV + IRV | 3500 mL |
| **Functional residual capacity (FRC)** | ERV + RV | 2300 mL |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | IRV + TV + ERV — maximum air expelled after a maximum inspiration | 4600 mL |
| **Total lung capacity (TLC)** | VC + RV | 5800 mL |

**Related terms**

- **Pulmonary (minute) ventilation** = TV × rate = 500 × 12 = **6 L/min**.
- **Alveolar ventilation** = (TV − dead space) × rate = (500 − 150) × 12 = **4.2 L/min** — the air that actually reaches the alveoli. Rapid shallow breathing therefore ventilates the alveoli poorly.
- **Dead space:** anatomical about 150 mL (conducting zone); physiological = anatomical + alveoli without blood flow.
- **FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in 1 second):** normally about 80% of the forced vital capacity. **FEV1/FVC is low in obstructive disease** (asthma, COPD) and normal or high with a reduced VC in **restrictive disease** (fibrosis).

**Vital capacity** is reduced by weak muscles, lung fibrosis, pleural effusion, pregnancy, obesity and lying down; it is higher in athletes.

## 3.6 Transport of respiratory gases

Gases move by diffusion from higher to lower **partial pressure**.

| Site | PO2 (mmHg) | PCO2 (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atmospheric air | about 160 | about 0.3 |
| Alveolar air | 100–104 | 40 |
| Arterial blood | about 95–100 | 40 |
| Venous blood (and tissues) | 40 | 45–46 |

So O2 moves alveoli → blood → tissues, and CO2 moves tissues → blood → alveoli. CO2 diffuses about 20 times faster than O2, so its smaller gradient is enough.

**Oxygen transport**

- About **97–98.5%** is carried bound to haemoglobin as **oxyhaemoglobin**; about 1.5–3% is dissolved in plasma.
- Each haemoglobin molecule binds 4 O2; **1 g of Hb carries about 1.34 mL of O2**. With 15 g Hb/dL, fully saturated blood carries about 20 mL O2 per 100 mL.
- At rest the tissues take about 5 mL O2 from each 100 mL of blood (about 25% of the content).

**Oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve** — plots % saturation of Hb against PO2; it is **S-shaped (sigmoid)** because binding of one O2 makes the next easier (cooperative binding).

```text
 % Hb sat
 100 |                     ___________  arterial (PO2 ~100): ~97%
     |                 ___/
  75 |              _-/  <- venous (PO2 40): ~75%
     |            /
  50 |-----------/  P50 ~26-27 mmHg
     |         /
     |       /     Shift to RIGHT (Bohr effect): more O2 released
     |     /         -> high CO2, high H+ (low pH), high temperature,
     |   /              high 2,3-BPG (exercise, altitude, anaemia)
     |__/          Shift to LEFT: O2 held more tightly
   0 +---------------------------------> PO2 (mmHg)  -> fetal Hb, low CO2,
     0   20   40   60   80   100            low temperature, CO poisoning
```

The flat upper part means blood stays well saturated even if alveolar PO2 falls somewhat; the steep middle part releases large amounts of O2 at tissue PO2. **Carbon monoxide** binds Hb about 200–250 times more strongly than O2 and shifts the curve to the left — very dangerous.

**Carbon dioxide transport**

| Form | Share (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| **As bicarbonate (HCO3−) in plasma** | About 70% |
| As **carbamino compounds** (bound to amino groups of Hb) | About 23% |
| Dissolved in plasma | About 7% |

```text
 TISSUE CAPILLARY (inside RBC)
 CO2 + H2O --carbonic anhydrase--> H2CO3 --> H+ + HCO3-
   H+  is buffered by haemoglobin (reduced Hb is a good buffer)
   HCO3- leaves the RBC into plasma; Cl- enters in exchange
         = CHLORIDE SHIFT (Hamburger phenomenon)
 In the LUNGS the whole process reverses and CO2 is breathed out.
```

**Haldane effect:** deoxygenated blood carries more CO2 than oxygenated blood, so unloading O2 at the tissues helps blood pick up CO2, and loading O2 in the lungs helps release CO2. The Bohr and Haldane effects work together.

## 3.7 Artificial respiration and resuscitation methods

**Artificial respiration** is any method of moving air in and out of the lungs of a person who has stopped breathing or is breathing inadequately (drowning, electric shock, poisoning, drug overdose, head injury). Brain cells begin to die within a few minutes without oxygen, so it must start at once.

**Manual methods (older, now mainly historical but asked in exams)**

| Method | Position | Principle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Schafer's prone pressure method** | Face down, head turned to one side | Rhythmic pressure on the lower back pushes air out; releasing pressure lets air in (about 12 per minute) |
| **Holger–Nielsen method** (back pressure, arm lift) | Face down | Pressure on the back (expiration) alternated with lifting the arms (inspiration) |
| **Silvester's method** | Face up | Raising the arms over the head expands the chest; bringing them down and pressing on the chest expels air |
| **Eve's rocking method** | Patient strapped to a stretcher | Tilting head-down and head-up moves the abdominal organs against the diaphragm (used for infants and drowning) |

**Mouth-to-mouth (expired air) ventilation:** with the airway opened (head tilt–chin lift), the rescuer seals the patient's mouth, pinches the nose and blows in until the chest rises. Expired air still holds about 16% oxygen, enough to keep the patient alive. Mouth-to-nose, mouth-to-mask and **bag–valve–mask (Ambu bag)** ventilation work the same way.

**Mechanical methods:** **positive-pressure ventilators** push air into the lungs through an endotracheal tube (used in ICUs and anaesthesia); the older **negative-pressure** "iron lung" (Drinker's respirator) lowered the pressure around the chest.

**Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR)** — basic life support for cardiac arrest (no response, no normal breathing):

1. Check the scene is safe; check response; call for help and an **automated external defibrillator (AED)**.
2. Start **chest compressions** on the centre of the chest (lower half of the sternum), on a firm surface: depth about **5–6 cm** in adults, rate **100–120 per minute**, letting the chest recoil fully.
3. Give **30 compressions to 2 rescue breaths** (each breath about 1 second, enough to make the chest rise). Untrained rescuers may give compression-only CPR.
4. Attach the AED as soon as it arrives and follow its prompts (it shocks ventricular fibrillation).
5. Continue until the person recovers, trained help takes over, or the rescuer is exhausted.

The modern sequence is **C-A-B** (circulation by compressions, then airway, then breathing). **Oxygen therapy** and drugs such as adrenaline are part of advanced life support given by trained staff.

# Part B: Urinary system

## 3.8 Anatomy of the urinary tract

```text
   KIDNEYS (two) -- form urine
       |
   URETERS (two, about 25-30 cm) -- carry urine by peristalsis
       |
   URINARY BLADDER -- stores urine
       |
   URETHRA -- carries urine out (female about 4 cm; male about 18-20 cm)
```

**Kidneys:** bean-shaped organs on the posterior abdominal wall, **retroperitoneal**, from about **T12 to L3**; the **right kidney is slightly lower** because of the liver. Each is about **11 cm long, 6 cm wide, 3 cm thick**, weighing about **150 g**. The adrenal gland sits on top. The medial **hilum** admits the renal artery and lets out the renal vein and ureter.

**Coverings** (inside out): fibrous **renal capsule**, **perirenal fat** (adipose capsule), **renal fascia**.

**Internal structure (coronal section)**

```text
          RENAL CAPSULE
        __________________________
       /  CORTEX (outer, granular; \
      /   renal corpuscles, PCT,     \
     |    DCT)                        |
     |   /\    /\    /\               |
     |  /  \  /  \  /  \  <- MEDULLA: |
     | /PYRA\/MID \/    \   renal     |
     | \MID /      \    /   pyramids  |
     |  \  /        \  /   (8-18;     |
     |   \/ PAPILLA  \/    loops,     |      renal artery -->
     |    MINOR CALYX       collecting|      renal vein   <--  HILUM
     |      \      /        ducts)    |      ureter       <--
     |      MAJOR CALYX                |
     |          \                     /
      \       RENAL PELVIS ---> URETER
       \_________________________ /
       renal columns of cortex dip between the pyramids
```

- **Cortex:** outer layer; contains glomeruli and convoluted tubules; dips between pyramids as **renal columns**.
- **Medulla:** 8–18 cone-shaped **renal pyramids** (striated look from loops and collecting ducts); each apex (**papilla**) opens into a **minor calyx**.
- Minor calyces join into 2–3 **major calyces** → **renal pelvis** → ureter.

**Blood supply** — the kidneys receive about **1200–1300 mL/min**, about **20–25% of cardiac output**:

```text
 Abdominal aorta -> renal artery -> segmental -> interlobar -> arcuate
 -> interlobular (cortical radiate) -> AFFERENT ARTERIOLE -> GLOMERULUS
 -> EFFERENT ARTERIOLE -> peritubular capillaries (around cortical tubules)
                          and VASA RECTA (around loops in the medulla)
 -> interlobular vein -> arcuate -> interlobar -> renal vein -> IVC
```

The glomerulus is a capillary bed between two arterioles, which keeps glomerular pressure high for filtration.

**Ureters:** muscular tubes about 25–30 cm long; urine moves by peristalsis; they enter the bladder obliquely, which prevents backflow. Kidney stones commonly lodge at their narrow points, causing colic.

**Urinary bladder:** hollow muscular organ in the pelvis behind the pubic symphysis. Wall of smooth muscle — the **detrusor muscle** — lined by **transitional epithelium** (stretches). The **trigone** is a smooth triangle between the two ureteric openings and the urethral opening. The **internal urethral sphincter** (smooth muscle, involuntary) and **external urethral sphincter** (skeletal muscle, voluntary, pudendal nerve) control outflow. Comfortable capacity about 400–600 mL.

**Urethra:** female about 4 cm (short — why UTIs are commoner in women); male about 18–20 cm, carrying both urine and semen (prostatic, membranous and spongy parts).

## 3.9 Nephron: the functional unit

Each kidney has about **1 million nephrons**.

```text
  afferent arteriole --> GLOMERULUS (capillary tuft) --> efferent arteriole
                     \__ BOWMAN'S CAPSULE _/  = RENAL CORPUSCLE (in cortex)
                              |
                PROXIMAL CONVOLUTED TUBULE (PCT) -- brush border, cortex
                              |
                LOOP OF HENLE: thin DESCENDING limb (into medulla)
                               |   (water-permeable)
                               U  hairpin bend
                               |
                               thin -> THICK ASCENDING limb
                                     (impermeable to water; pumps out Na+, K+, Cl-)
                              |
         JUXTAGLOMERULAR APPARATUS (where DCT touches its own afferent arteriole)
                              |
                DISTAL CONVOLUTED TUBULE (DCT) -- cortex
                              |
                COLLECTING DUCT (shared by several nephrons)
                              |
                papillary duct --> minor calyx
```

| Part | Structure | Main job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Renal corpuscle** | Glomerulus inside double-layered Bowman's capsule; inner layer of **podocytes** with filtration slits | **Filtration** |
| **PCT** | Cuboidal cells with dense **brush border** and many mitochondria | Reabsorbs about 65% of filtered Na+ and water, all glucose and amino acids, most HCO3−; secretes H+, drugs |
| **Loop of Henle** | Descending (permeable to water) and ascending (impermeable to water, actively removes NaCl) limbs | Creates the **medullary concentration gradient** (countercurrent multiplier) |
| **DCT** | Cuboidal cells, few microvilli | Fine-tunes Na+, K+, Ca2+ (PTH acts here), H+; aldosterone acts on the late DCT |
| **Collecting duct** | Principal and intercalated cells | Final water reabsorption under **ADH**; Na+/K+ under **aldosterone**; H+ secretion |

**Types of nephron:** **cortical** (about 85%; short loops, mostly in the cortex) and **juxtamedullary** (about 15%; long loops deep in the medulla with vasa recta; needed to make concentrated urine).

**Juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA):**

- **Juxtaglomerular (granular) cells** in the wall of the afferent arteriole — secrete **renin**; act as stretch receptors.
- **Macula densa** — specialised DCT cells that sense **NaCl** in the tubular fluid.
- **Extraglomerular mesangial (lacis) cells.**

## 3.10 Functions of the kidneys and urinary tract

**Kidneys**

1. **Excretion** of metabolic wastes — urea, uric acid, creatinine — and foreign substances, including drugs.
2. **Water balance** — volume and concentration of urine (with ADH).
3. **Electrolyte balance** — Na+, K+, Ca2+, phosphate, Mg2+.
4. **Acid–base balance** — excrete H+, conserve and generate HCO3−.
5. **Regulation of blood pressure** — renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and salt/water balance.
6. **Endocrine:** **erythropoietin** (RBC production; low in kidney failure → anaemia), **renin**, **calcitriol** (active vitamin D, final hydroxylation), prostaglandins.
7. **Gluconeogenesis** during prolonged fasting.

**Urinary tract:** ureters transport urine, the bladder stores it, the urethra voids it, and the sphincters give continence.

## 3.11 Physiology of urine formation

Three processes: **glomerular filtration → tubular reabsorption → tubular secretion**.

```text
 Blood plasma
     |  1. GLOMERULAR FILTRATION (about 180 L/day)
     v
 Filtrate in Bowman's capsule
     |  2. TUBULAR REABSORPTION (tubule --> blood; about 99% of water)
     |  3. TUBULAR SECRETION   (blood --> tubule)
     v
 Urine (about 1-1.5 L/day)
 Excretion = Filtration - Reabsorption + Secretion
```

**1. Glomerular filtration**

- Blood is filtered through the **filtration membrane**: fenestrated capillary endothelium, basement membrane and podocyte filtration slits. Water and small solutes (glucose, amino acids, salts, urea, many drugs) pass; **blood cells and plasma proteins (above about 70 kDa, e.g. albumin) do not**. Protein-bound drug is not filtered.
- **Net filtration pressure (NFP)** = glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure − (Bowman's capsule hydrostatic pressure + plasma colloid osmotic pressure) = about **60 − (18 + 32) = 10 mmHg**.
- **Glomerular filtration rate (GFR)** — volume filtered by both kidneys per minute: about **125 mL/min (about 180 L/day)**. About 20% of renal plasma flow is filtered (**filtration fraction**).
- GFR is kept steady over a wide BP range (about 80–180 mmHg) by **autoregulation** (myogenic response and **tubuloglomerular feedback** via the macula densa). It is measured by the clearance of **inulin** (gold standard) or estimated from **creatinine clearance** or serum creatinine.

**2. Tubular reabsorption** — useful substances return to blood by active and passive transport.

| Segment | Reabsorbed |
| --- | --- |
| PCT | About 65% of Na+, Cl−, K+ and water; **100% of glucose and amino acids** (normally); about 80–90% of HCO3−; phosphate, urea (partly) |
| Descending limb | Water |
| Thick ascending limb | Na+, K+, Cl− (Na+–K+–2Cl− co-transporter — blocked by **furosemide**); Ca2+, Mg2+; no water |
| DCT | Na+ and Cl− (blocked by **thiazides**); Ca2+ (PTH) |
| Collecting duct | Na+ (aldosterone; blocked by **spironolactone**); **water (ADH via aquaporin-2)**; urea |

Glucose is reabsorbed by carriers with a limited capacity. When blood glucose exceeds the **renal threshold (about 180 mg/dL)**, carriers saturate (the transport maximum) and glucose appears in the urine — **glycosuria** in diabetes mellitus.

**Concentration of urine:** the loop of Henle (**countercurrent multiplier**) and vasa recta (**countercurrent exchanger**) build a high osmolarity in the deep medulla (up to about 1200 mOsm/L). With **ADH**, the collecting duct becomes permeable to water, which leaves into the concentrated medulla — small volume of concentrated urine. Without ADH, a large volume of dilute urine forms (as in **diabetes insipidus**).

**3. Tubular secretion** — substances move from peritubular blood into the tubule: **H+** (acid–base balance), **K+** (under aldosterone), NH3/NH4+, creatinine (a little), and many **drugs** — penicillin, probenecid, para-aminohippuric acid (PAH, used to measure renal plasma flow), diuretics. **Probenecid** competes for the same anion carrier and so prolongs the action of penicillin.

**Composition of normal urine:** volume about 1–1.5 L/day; pale yellow (urochrome); pH about 6 (range about 4.5–8); specific gravity about 1.010–1.025; contains water (about 95%), urea (about 25–30 g/day), creatinine, uric acid, Na+, K+, Cl−, phosphate, sulphate. **Abnormal constituents:** glucose (glycosuria), protein (proteinuria), ketone bodies, blood (haematuria), bile pigments, pus cells.

## 3.12 Micturition reflex

**Micturition** is the emptying of the urinary bladder.

```text
 Urine collects; bladder fills (detrusor relaxed, sympathetic T11-L2 helps storage)
        |  at about 300-400 mL
        v
 STRETCH RECEPTORS in bladder wall fire
        |  pelvic (parasympathetic) afferent fibres
        v
 SACRAL MICTURITION CENTRE (S2-S4) in spinal cord  <---- pons (micturition centre)
        |                                                 and cerebral cortex:
        |  pelvic parasympathetic efferents               voluntary permission
        v                                                 or postponement
 DETRUSOR CONTRACTS + INTERNAL SPHINCTER OPENS
        |
 Voluntary relaxation of EXTERNAL SPHINCTER (pudendal nerve inhibited)
        v
 URINE FLOWS OUT; urine in urethra sends more impulses -> contraction continues
 (positive feedback) until bladder is empty
```

- Desire to void is first felt at about 150–300 mL and becomes strong at about 400 mL.
- In infants, micturition is a pure spinal reflex; voluntary control develops at about 2–3 years.
- After **spinal cord injury** above the sacral segments, the bladder empties reflexly without control (automatic bladder); damage to the sacral segments or pelvic nerves causes an atonic, overflowing bladder.
- **Drugs:** anticholinergics relax the detrusor (used for overactive bladder; cause **urinary retention** as a side effect); α1 blockers (tamsulosin) relax the bladder neck and prostate in enlarged prostate.

## 3.13 Role of the kidneys in acid–base balance

Normal arterial **pH is 7.35–7.45**. Buffers act within seconds and the lungs within minutes, but only the kidneys can remove fixed (non-volatile) acids from the body and regenerate bicarbonate — over hours to days.

1. **Reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate** — mainly in the PCT. H+ secreted by tubular cells (Na+/H+ exchange) combines with filtered HCO3− to form H2CO3 → CO2 + H2O (carbonic anhydrase on the brush border); CO2 enters the cell and is converted back to HCO3−, which returns to blood. No net H+ is lost, but almost all HCO3− is saved.
2. **Excretion of H+ with phosphate buffer (titratable acid):** secreted H+ joins HPO4^2− to form H2PO4^−, which is excreted; each H+ secreted this way adds one **new HCO3−** to blood.
3. **Ammonia buffer:** tubular cells make NH3 from **glutamine**; NH3 binds secreted H+ to form **NH4+**, which is trapped and excreted; again a new HCO3− is added to blood. This mechanism increases most in chronic acidosis.
4. **In alkalosis**, the kidneys secrete less H+ and excrete more HCO3−.

```text
 TUBULAR CELL                               TUBULE LUMEN
 CO2 + H2O --CA--> H2CO3 --> H+ ---------->  H+ + HCO3- (filtered) -> reabsorbed
                     |                       H+ + HPO4-- -> H2PO4- (excreted)
                     v                       H+ + NH3    -> NH4+   (excreted)
                  HCO3- ---> BLOOD (new bicarbonate)
```

**Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** (acetazolamide) reduce H+ secretion and HCO3− reabsorption — alkaline urine and mild metabolic acidosis.

## 3.14 Role of the renin–angiotensin system (RAS) in the kidney

```text
 STIMULI for renin release:
   fall in BP / renal perfusion (JG cells sense low stretch)
   low NaCl at the MACULA DENSA
   sympathetic stimulation (beta-1 receptors on JG cells)
            |
            v
     RENIN (from JG cells)
            |
 ANGIOTENSINOGEN (plasma protein from LIVER) --> ANGIOTENSIN I (inactive decapeptide)
            |
            |  ANGIOTENSIN-CONVERTING ENZYME (ACE), mainly in LUNG capillaries
            v
     ANGIOTENSIN II (active octapeptide) acts on AT1 receptors:
       1. Strong VASOCONSTRICTION -> BP up
       2. ADRENAL CORTEX -> ALDOSTERONE -> Na+ and water retained, K+ lost (DCT, CD)
       3. Posterior pituitary -> ADH -> water retained
       4. Hypothalamus -> THIRST
       5. Constricts EFFERENT arteriole more -> GFR maintained
       6. Increases Na+ reabsorption in PCT; promotes sympathetic activity
            |
            v
     BP and blood volume restored --> renin falls (negative feedback)
```

**Drug link:** **ACE inhibitors** (enalapril, ramipril; cough from accumulated bradykinin), **angiotensin receptor blockers** (losartan, telmisartan), **aldosterone antagonists** (spironolactone) and **direct renin inhibitors** (aliskiren) treat hypertension and heart failure. Beta-blockers reduce renin release.

## 3.15 Disorders of the kidney

| Disorder | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **Acute kidney injury** | Sudden fall in kidney function over hours to days (shock, dehydration, nephrotoxic drugs such as aminoglycosides, obstruction); urine output falls; often reversible |
| **Chronic kidney disease** | Gradual, permanent loss of nephrons (commonly diabetes, hypertension); raised urea and creatinine (uraemia), anaemia, bone disease, high K+; end stage needs dialysis or transplant |
| **Glomerulonephritis** | Inflammation of glomeruli (often after streptococcal throat infection); haematuria, oedema, hypertension |
| **Nephrotic syndrome** | Heavy proteinuria, low plasma albumin, generalised oedema, high cholesterol |
| **Renal calculi (kidney stones)** | Calcium oxalate (commonest), uric acid, phosphate; severe colicky pain radiating to the groin, haematuria |
| **Urinary tract infection** | Cystitis (bladder) or pyelonephritis (kidney); usually *E. coli*; burning and frequent urination; commoner in women |
| Polycystic kidney disease | Inherited (autosomal dominant); many cysts enlarge the kidneys |
| Diabetes insipidus | Lack of ADH or kidney unresponsiveness; large volumes of dilute urine |
| Urinary incontinence and retention | Loss of control, or inability to empty (prostate enlargement, anticholinergic drugs) |

**Dose adjustment:** drugs cleared mainly by the kidneys (aminoglycosides, digoxin, metformin, many antibiotics) need lower doses in kidney failure, guided by creatinine clearance.

## Remember

- Conducting zone ends at terminal bronchioles (dead space about 150 mL); exchange occurs in respiratory bronchioles, ducts, sacs and alveoli.
- Right lung 3 lobes (2 fissures), left lung 2 lobes with cardiac notch; type II cells make surfactant.
- Inspiration is active (diaphragm, external intercostals; phrenic C3–C5); quiet expiration is passive recoil.
- Centres: DRG and VRG in the medulla; pneumotaxic and apneustic in the pons; CO2 (via H+ in CSF) is the strongest stimulus; carotid bodies sense low O2.
- TV 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100, RV 1200; VC 4600, FRC 2300, TLC 5800 mL; minute ventilation 6 L/min.
- O2: 97–98.5% as oxyhaemoglobin; 1 g Hb carries 1.34 mL O2; curve sigmoid; right shift by CO2, H+, heat, 2,3-BPG.
- CO2: 70% bicarbonate, 23% carbamino, 7% dissolved; chloride shift.
- CPR: 30:2, 100–120 compressions/min, depth 5–6 cm, early AED.
- Kidneys T12–L3, about 150 g; about 1 million nephrons each; 20–25% of cardiac output.
- Nephron: corpuscle → PCT → loop of Henle → DCT → collecting duct; JGA makes renin.
- GFR 125 mL/min (180 L/day); NFP about 10 mmHg; urine 1–1.5 L/day; glucose threshold about 180 mg/dL.
- ADH → aquaporins in collecting duct; aldosterone → Na+ retained, K+ lost.
- Micturition: stretch at 300–400 mL → S2–S4 parasympathetic → detrusor contracts; external sphincter voluntary (pudendal).
- Kidneys reabsorb HCO3−, excrete H+ as titratable acid and NH4+, and generate new HCO3−.
- RAS: renin → angiotensin I → ACE (lungs) → angiotensin II → vasoconstriction, aldosterone, ADH, thirst.
