# Unit 1: Nervous system and central nervous system

Part I of this subject (BP101T) covered nervous tissue in outline and the peripheral nervous system (cranial nerves, spinal nerves and the autonomic system). This unit goes inside the neuron: how a nerve fibre carries an impulse, how one neuron talks to the next at a synapse, and which chemicals carry the message. The second half covers the brain and spinal cord: their coverings, the fluid around them, the parts of the brain and the tracts and reflexes of the spinal cord. Almost every drug that acts on the CNS (anaesthetics, sedatives, antidepressants, antiepileptics, opioids) works at one of the steps described here.

## 1.1 Organisation of the nervous system

```text
NERVOUS SYSTEM
 |-- CNS: brain (in the skull) + spinal cord (in the vertebral canal)
 |        integrates information, stores memory, makes decisions
 |-- PNS: 12 pairs of cranial nerves, 31 pairs of spinal nerves, ganglia
          |-- afferent (sensory) limb: receptor --> CNS
          |-- efferent (motor) limb:  CNS --> effector
                 |-- somatic (skeletal muscle, voluntary)
                 |-- autonomic (heart, smooth muscle, glands; involuntary)
```

The nervous system does three linked jobs: **sensory input** (receptors detect a change), **integration** (the CNS analyses it) and **motor output** (muscles contract or glands secrete). Together with the endocrine system (Unit 4) it keeps homeostasis; the nervous system acts in milliseconds, hormones over minutes to days.

## 1.2 Neuron: the structural and functional unit

A neuron is a cell specialised to receive, conduct and pass on electrical signals. Mature neurons do not divide, so lost neurons are not replaced.

```text
          dendrites (receive)
            \  |  /
             \ | /
        +---------------+
        |  CELL BODY    |  nucleus with prominent nucleolus,
        |  (soma)       |  Nissl bodies (rough ER), neurofibrils
        +-------+-------+
                |  axon hillock (trigger zone: impulse starts here)
                |
   ==[myelin]==|==[myelin]==|==[myelin]==   <- axon
             node of     node of
             Ranvier     Ranvier
                                    \
                                     >--- axon terminals --- synaptic knobs
                                    /     (vesicles of transmitter)
```

| Part | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell body (soma) | Nucleus, Nissl bodies, Golgi, mitochondria, neurofibrils | Makes proteins and transmitter; keeps the cell alive |
| Dendrites | Short, branched, many; carry Nissl bodies | Receive signals and carry them **towards** the cell body |
| Axon | Single, long, uniform; begins at the axon hillock; no Nissl bodies | Carries impulses **away** from the cell body |
| Myelin sheath | Lipid-rich wrapping; gaps = nodes of Ranvier | Insulates and speeds conduction |
| Axon terminals | Synaptic knobs with vesicles | Release neurotransmitter |

**Classification of neurons**

- By number of processes: **multipolar** (one axon, many dendrites — motor neurons, most CNS neurons), **bipolar** (one axon, one dendrite — retina, olfactory epithelium, inner ear), **pseudounipolar** (one process that splits in a T — dorsal root ganglion sensory neurons), unipolar (embryonic).
- By function: **sensory (afferent)**, **motor (efferent)**, **interneurons** (association neurons inside the CNS; about 90% of all neurons).
- By axon length: Golgi type I (long axon, e.g. pyramidal cells) and Golgi type II (short axon, local circuits).

**Axonal transport.** Proteins and vesicles made in the soma move down the axon (anterograde, along microtubules, kinesin motor); material returns to the soma (retrograde, dynein). Tetanus toxin and rabies virus travel to the CNS by retrograde transport.

## 1.3 Neuroglia

Neuroglia (glial cells) are non-conducting support cells. They outnumber neurons and, unlike neurons, can divide; most brain tumours (gliomas) arise from them.

| Location | Glial cell | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CNS | Astrocytes (star-shaped, largest, most numerous) | Support neurons; end-feet on capillaries help form the **blood–brain barrier**; take up excess K+ and glutamate; form scars after injury |
| CNS | Oligodendrocytes | Form **myelin in the CNS** (one cell myelinates segments of several axons) |
| CNS | Microglia | Phagocytes of the CNS (derived from monocytes); remove debris and microbes |
| CNS | Ependymal cells | Ciliated cells lining the ventricles and central canal; help form and circulate **CSF** (choroid plexus) |
| PNS | Schwann cells | Form **myelin in the PNS** (one cell for one segment of one axon); help regeneration of cut peripheral nerves |
| PNS | Satellite cells | Surround cell bodies in ganglia; regulate their environment |

**Blood–brain barrier (BBB):** tight junctions between brain capillary endothelial cells, a thick basement membrane and astrocyte end-feet. Lipid-soluble drugs (general anaesthetics, diazepam) cross easily; ionised and large water-soluble drugs (penicillin, dopamine) cross poorly. That is why **levodopa**, which crosses on a carrier, is given for Parkinsonism instead of dopamine.

## 1.4 Nerve fibres: classification and properties

A **nerve fibre** is an axon with its coverings. A **nerve** is a bundle of fibres held by connective tissue (epineurium around the nerve, perineurium around each fascicle, endoneurium around each fibre).

**Classification**

1. **By myelination:** myelinated (fast, e.g. motor fibres to skeletal muscle) and unmyelinated (slow, e.g. postganglionic autonomic, many pain fibres).
2. **By direction:** afferent (sensory), efferent (motor), mixed nerves (most spinal nerves).
3. **By origin:** cranial and spinal nerves.
4. **By transmitter released:** cholinergic (acetylcholine) and adrenergic (noradrenaline).
5. **By diameter and conduction velocity (Erlanger–Gasser):**

| Type | Myelin | Diameter (µm) | Velocity (m/s) | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aα | Yes | 12–20 (largest) | 70–120 | Motor to skeletal muscle; muscle spindle (Ia) |
| Aβ | Yes | 5–12 | 30–70 | Touch, pressure |
| Aγ | Yes | 3–6 | 15–30 | Motor to muscle spindle |
| Aδ | Yes, thin | 2–5 | 12–30 | Fast, sharp pain; cold |
| B | Yes, thin | Under 3 | 3–15 | Preganglionic autonomic |
| C | No | 0.4–1.2 (smallest) | 0.5–2 | Slow, dull pain; warmth; postganglionic autonomic |

Local anaesthetics block small fibres (C, Aδ) before large ones, so pain goes first and motor power last. Pressure on a nerve (as when a leg "goes to sleep") blocks large A fibres first.

**Properties of nerve fibres**

- **Excitability:** responds to an adequate stimulus by producing an action potential.
- **Conductivity:** carries the impulse along its whole length.
- **All-or-none law:** a stimulus below threshold gives no impulse; any stimulus at or above threshold gives a full-size impulse. Strength of sensation is coded by the **frequency** of impulses and the **number** of fibres firing, not by impulse size.
- **Refractory period:** for a short time after an impulse the fibre cannot fire again — **absolute** (no stimulus works; sodium channels inactivated) then **relative** (only a stronger stimulus works). This limits the firing rate and makes conduction one-way.
- **Summation:** two subthreshold stimuli close together can add up to reach threshold.
- **Accommodation:** a slowly rising stimulus may fail to excite because sodium channels inactivate.
- **Infatigability:** nerve fibres hardly fatigue (synapses do).
- **Adaptation** belongs to receptors (see 1.7).

## 1.5 Electrophysiology: resting membrane potential

Every neuron has a voltage across its membrane: the inside is negative relative to the outside. At rest this **resting membrane potential (RMP)** is about **−70 mV** in a neuron (about −90 mV in skeletal muscle).

| Ion | Inside (mmol/L, approx.) | Outside (mmol/L, approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| K+ | 140 | 4–5 |
| Na+ | 10–15 | 140–145 |
| Cl− | about 10 | about 110 |
| Proteins (negative) | High, cannot leave | Low |

**How the RMP is produced**

1. **Selective permeability:** at rest the membrane is far more permeable to K+ (leak channels open) than to Na+. K+ diffuses out along its gradient, leaving negative proteins behind; this is the main cause of the negative inside.
2. **Na+/K+ ATPase pump:** moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in for each ATP. It keeps the gradients and adds a few millivolts of negativity (electrogenic).
3. **Non-diffusible anions** (proteins, phosphates) stay inside.

The potential at which an ion's diffusion is exactly balanced by the voltage is its **equilibrium potential** (Nernst equation): about −90 mV for K+ and +60 mV for Na+. The RMP lies close to the K+ value because K+ permeability dominates.

## 1.6 Action potential and the nerve impulse

An **action potential** is a rapid, brief reversal of membrane potential that travels along the fibre. A travelling action potential is the **nerve impulse**.

```text
 mV
 +30 |            /\        overshoot
     |           /  \
   0 |----------/----\---------------------
     |         /      \
     |        / depol  \ repolarisation
 -55 |.......+..........\........ threshold
     |       |           \
 -70 |_______|            \____________ RMP
     |                     \__/
 -80 |                   hyperpolarisation (after-potential)
     +--------------------------------------> time (about 1-2 ms)
        stimulus
```

**Phases**

1. **Resting state:** −70 mV; voltage-gated Na+ and K+ channels closed.
2. **Depolarisation:** a stimulus opens some Na+ channels; when the membrane reaches the **threshold (about −55 mV)**, many voltage-gated Na+ channels open at once (positive feedback). Na+ rushes in and the potential rises to about **+30 to +35 mV** (overshoot).
3. **Repolarisation:** Na+ channels inactivate; slower voltage-gated K+ channels open; K+ leaves and the inside becomes negative again.
4. **Hyperpolarisation:** K+ channels close slowly, so the potential dips below the RMP for a moment.
5. **Recovery:** the Na+/K+ pump restores ion distribution over time (very few ions actually move in one impulse).

**Propagation**

- In an **unmyelinated** fibre, local currents depolarise the next patch of membrane, so the impulse moves continuously and slowly.
- In a **myelinated** fibre, Na+ channels are packed at the **nodes of Ranvier**, and the impulse jumps from node to node — **saltatory conduction**. It is up to about 50 times faster and uses less energy.
- Conduction velocity increases with **fibre diameter** and **myelination**; it falls with cold and in demyelinating diseases (multiple sclerosis in the CNS, Guillain–Barré syndrome in the PNS).

**Drugs:** local anaesthetics (lidocaine) and tetrodotoxin block voltage-gated Na+ channels, so no action potential forms. Phenytoin and carbamazepine stabilise inactivated Na+ channels to stop the repeated firing of epilepsy.

## 1.7 Receptors

A **sensory receptor** is a structure that detects a change (stimulus) and converts it into nerve impulses (**transduction**). The change it produces first is a graded **receptor (generator) potential**; if large enough it fires action potentials in the sensory fibre.

**Classification**

| Basis | Types and examples |
| --- | --- |
| Stimulus (modality) | **Mechanoreceptors** (touch: Meissner's and Pacinian corpuscles; stretch: muscle spindle, Golgi tendon organ; baroreceptors; hair cells of ear), **thermoreceptors** (warm, cold), **nociceptors** (free nerve endings: pain), **chemoreceptors** (taste, smell, carotid body), **photoreceptors** (rods, cones), osmoreceptors (hypothalamus) |
| Location | **Exteroceptors** (skin, special senses), **interoceptors / visceroceptors** (internal organs, vessels), **proprioceptors** (muscles, tendons, joints: body position) |
| Adaptation | **Rapidly adapting (phasic)** — Pacinian, Meissner's; **slowly adapting (tonic)** — pain receptors, muscle spindles, baroreceptors |

**Properties:** specificity (each receptor responds best to one kind of stimulus — **adequate stimulus**), generator potential, **adaptation** (response falls during a steady stimulus; this is why we stop noticing clothes), coding of intensity by frequency, and Müller's law of specific nerve energies (stimulating a pathway gives the same sensation whatever the stimulus, e.g. a blow to the eye is "seen" as light).

## 1.8 Synapse

A **synapse** is the junction where one neuron passes a signal to another neuron (or to a muscle or gland cell, then called a neuroeffector junction).

**Types**

- By mode: **chemical** (most; transmitter released) and **electrical** (gap junctions; very fast, two-way; in heart muscle and some brain areas).
- By site of contact: **axodendritic** (commonest), **axosomatic**, **axoaxonic** (used for presynaptic inhibition).

```text
   PRESYNAPTIC TERMINAL
  +-------------------------------+
  |  mitochondria   o  o  o       |  synaptic vesicles (transmitter)
  |   Ca2+ channels  o o o        |
  +======|=======|========|=======+  presynaptic membrane
         v  exocytosis    v
  ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ synaptic cleft (about 20 nm) ~ ~ ~ ~ ~   enzyme (e.g. AChE)
  +=====[R]=====[R]======[R]======+  postsynaptic membrane with receptors
  |   POSTSYNAPTIC NEURON          |  --> EPSP or IPSP
  +--------------------------------+
```

**Steps of chemical transmission**

1. Action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal.
2. Voltage-gated **Ca2+ channels** open; Ca2+ enters.
3. Ca2+ makes vesicles fuse with the membrane and release transmitter by **exocytosis**.
4. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds **receptors** on the postsynaptic membrane.
5. Ion channels open:
   - Na+ entry → small depolarisation = **excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)**.
   - Cl− entry or K+ exit → hyperpolarisation = **inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)**.
6. EPSPs and IPSPs add up at the axon hillock (**spatial and temporal summation**); if threshold is reached the postsynaptic neuron fires.
7. Transmitter action ends by **enzyme breakdown** (acetylcholinesterase splits ACh), **reuptake** into the terminal (noradrenaline, serotonin, dopamine), or diffusion away.

**Properties of synapses**

- **One-way conduction** (Bell–Magendie law): only the presynaptic side releases transmitter.
- **Synaptic delay** of about 0.5 ms per synapse; counting delays shows how many synapses a reflex has.
- **Fatigue:** repeated firing exhausts transmitter stores.
- **Summation**, **convergence** (many neurons to one) and **divergence** (one neuron to many).
- **Facilitation** and **inhibition** (postsynaptic, presynaptic, feed-forward, Renshaw cell recurrent inhibition).
- Sensitive to **hypoxia, acidosis and drugs** — most CNS drugs act here.

## 1.9 Neurotransmitters

A **neurotransmitter** is a chemical made by a neuron, stored in vesicles, released on stimulation, which acts on specific receptors of the next cell and is then quickly removed.

| Class | Transmitter | Main sites | Action and drug link |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ester | **Acetylcholine (ACh)** | NMJ, autonomic ganglia, parasympathetic endings, basal forebrain | Nicotinic and muscarinic receptors; low in Alzheimer's disease (donepezil inhibits AChE) |
| Monoamines (catecholamines) | **Noradrenaline** | Sympathetic endings, locus coeruleus | Alertness, mood; antidepressants block its reuptake |
| | **Dopamine** | Substantia nigra → striatum; mesolimbic pathway | Movement, reward; loss → Parkinson's disease; excess activity linked to schizophrenia (antipsychotics block D2) |
| Monoamines (indoleamine) | **Serotonin (5-HT)** | Raphe nuclei | Mood, sleep, appetite; SSRIs (fluoxetine) block reuptake |
| | **Histamine** | Hypothalamus | Wakefulness; older antihistamines cause sleepiness |
| Amino acids | **Glutamate** | Most of the brain | Main **excitatory** transmitter (NMDA, AMPA receptors) |
| | **GABA** | Brain | Main **inhibitory** transmitter in the brain; benzodiazepines enhance GABA-A |
| | **Glycine** | Spinal cord, brainstem | Inhibitory; strychnine blocks it (convulsions) |
| Neuropeptides | Substance P | Pain fibres (dorsal horn) | Pain transmission |
| | Endorphins, enkephalins | Brain, spinal cord | Natural pain relief; morphine acts on the same opioid receptors |
| Gases | Nitric oxide (NO) | Brain, blood vessels | Made on demand, diffuses freely; vasodilatation |
| Purines | ATP, adenosine | Widespread | Adenosine is inhibitory; caffeine blocks its receptors |

## 1.10 Meninges

The brain and spinal cord are wrapped in three membranes, from outside in:

```text
  SKULL BONE
  |== DURA MATER ==|   tough, fibrous, two layers in the skull
  |  subdural space (potential space)
  |-- ARACHNOID MATER --|   thin, web-like
  |  SUBARACHNOID SPACE  <-- contains CSF and large vessels
  |-- PIA MATER --|   delicate, vascular, follows every groove
  BRAIN SURFACE
```

| Layer | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Dura mater** | Thick and fibrous. In the skull it has an outer (periosteal) and inner (meningeal) layer; they separate to enclose the **dural venous sinuses** (e.g. superior sagittal sinus). The inner layer forms folds: **falx cerebri** (between the two hemispheres), **tentorium cerebelli** (between cerebrum and cerebellum), falx cerebelli, diaphragma sellae. In the spinal canal the **epidural space** (fat and veins) separates dura from bone — the site of epidural anaesthesia. |
| **Arachnoid mater** | Thin, avascular; joined to the pia by fine strands. **Arachnoid villi (granulations)** project into the venous sinuses and return CSF to the blood. |
| **Pia mater** | Thin, vascular, adherent to the brain and cord; helps form the choroid plexus; in the cord it forms denticulate ligaments and the **filum terminale**. |

**Spaces:** epidural (only in the spinal canal normally), subdural, **subarachnoid** (filled with CSF; enlarged as cisterns). **Meningitis** is infection of the meninges (neck stiffness, fever, headache); diagnosed by examining CSF from a lumbar puncture.

## 1.12 Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)

**Formation:** mainly by the **choroid plexuses** (tufts of capillaries covered by ependyma) in the lateral, third and fourth ventricles, by filtration and active secretion. About **500 mL is formed per day**, while the total volume at any time is about **150 mL**, so CSF is replaced about three times a day.

**Circulation:**

```text
Choroid plexus of lateral ventricles --> interventricular foramina --> third ventricle
--> cerebral aqueduct --> fourth ventricle --> foramina of Luschka and Magendie
--> subarachnoid space (around brain and spinal cord)
--> arachnoid villi --> dural venous sinuses (superior sagittal sinus) --> venous blood
```

**Composition:** clear, colourless, almost protein-free (about 20–40 mg/dL), glucose a little lower than plasma (about 50–80 mg/dL), very few cells (lymphocytes, under 5 per mm³), specific gravity about 1.005.

**Functions**

1. **Protection:** the brain floats in CSF (buoyancy reduces its effective weight from about 1.4 kg to about 50 g) and CSF cushions it against jolts.
2. **Chemical stability:** keeps a constant ionic environment for neurons; its H+ concentration drives the central chemoreceptors of breathing (Unit 3).
3. **Nutrition and waste removal** for nervous tissue.
4. **Pressure balance:** adjusts volume when blood volume in the skull changes.

**Applied:** **lumbar puncture** is done between L3–L4 or L4–L5, below the end of the spinal cord, to collect CSF or give spinal anaesthesia or intrathecal drugs. Blocked CSF flow (e.g. at the aqueduct) or poor absorption causes **hydrocephalus** — enlarged ventricles and raised intracranial pressure.

## 1.14 Cerebrum

**Structure.** The largest part. Two **cerebral hemispheres** separated by the longitudinal fissure and joined by the **corpus callosum** (the largest commissure). The surface is folded into **gyri** (ridges) and **sulci** (grooves), which increase its area. Major landmarks: **central sulcus** (separates frontal and parietal lobes), **lateral sulcus** (separates temporal lobe from frontal and parietal), parieto-occipital sulcus.

- **Cerebral cortex:** outer **grey matter** (2–4 mm thick; nerve cell bodies, six layers).
- **White matter:** beneath it, made of tracts — association fibres (within one hemisphere), commissural fibres (between hemispheres, e.g. corpus callosum), projection fibres (to and from lower centres, e.g. internal capsule).
- **Basal ganglia (basal nuclei):** masses of grey matter deep in the white matter — caudate nucleus, putamen and globus pallidus (with substantia nigra and subthalamic nucleus functionally). They control posture and smooth movement; damage causes Parkinsonism or chorea.
- **Limbic system:** hippocampus, amygdala, cingulate gyrus and connections — emotion, memory, behaviour.

**Functional areas of the cortex** (Brodmann numbers in brackets)

| Lobe | Area | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Frontal | **Primary motor area** (4), precentral gyrus | Voluntary movement of the opposite side of the body; body is mapped upside down (motor homunculus) |
| | Premotor area (6) | Plans and sequences learned movements |
| | **Broca's motor speech area** (44, 45), usually left | Forming spoken words; damage → expressive (motor) aphasia |
| | Frontal eye field (8) | Voluntary eye movements |
| | Prefrontal area | Personality, judgement, planning, intellect |
| Parietal | **Primary somatosensory area** (3, 1, 2), postcentral gyrus | Touch, pressure, pain, temperature, position sense from the opposite side |
| | Somatosensory association area | Recognising objects by touch (stereognosis) |
| Temporal | **Primary auditory area** (41, 42) | Hearing |
| | **Wernicke's area** (22), usually left | Understanding spoken and written language; damage → receptive (sensory) aphasia |
| | Hippocampus (medial temporal) | Forming new memories |
| Occipital | **Primary visual area** (17) | Seeing |
| | Visual association areas (18, 19) | Interpreting what is seen |
| Insula | Deep in lateral sulcus | Taste, visceral sensation |

**Functions of the cerebrum (summary):** sensory perception, voluntary movement, speech and language, memory, learning, intelligence, reasoning, emotion, consciousness. **Hemispheric specialisation:** in most people the left hemisphere leads in language and logic, the right in spatial and artistic ability.

## 1.15 Diencephalon (in brief)

- **Thalamus:** paired oval mass on each side of the third ventricle; the **relay station** for all sensory pathways except smell, on the way to the cortex; crude awareness of pain.
- **Hypothalamus:** below the thalamus; controls the autonomic system, body temperature, hunger and satiety, thirst and water balance (ADH), sleep–wake rhythm, emotions, and the pituitary gland (Unit 4).
- **Epithalamus:** contains the **pineal gland** (melatonin).

## 1.16 Brainstem

The brainstem links the cerebrum with the spinal cord. It carries all ascending and descending tracts, contains nuclei of cranial nerves III–XII and the **reticular formation**.

| Part | Structure | Functions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Midbrain** | Cerebral peduncles in front; tectum behind with **superior colliculi** (visual reflexes) and **inferior colliculi** (auditory reflexes); red nucleus, substantia nigra; cerebral aqueduct passes through | Nuclei of III and IV; pupillary light reflex and eye movements; startle reflex to sound; substantia nigra makes dopamine (motor control) |
| **Pons** | Bulge in front of the cerebellum; transverse fibres link to cerebellum | Nuclei of V–VIII; relays from cortex to cerebellum; **pneumotaxic and apneustic centres** that fine-tune breathing |
| **Medulla oblongata** | Lowest part, continuous with the spinal cord at the foramen magnum; **pyramids** in front where most corticospinal fibres cross (**decussation of pyramids**); olives on the sides | Nuclei of IX–XII; **vital centres**: cardiac centre, **vasomotor centre**, **respiratory centre**; reflex centres for vomiting, coughing, sneezing, swallowing, salivation |

**Reticular formation and RAS:** a network of neurons through the brainstem. The **reticular activating system** keeps the cortex awake and alert; damage causes coma, and general anaesthetics and sedatives depress it. Because the medulla holds the vital centres, an overdose of opioids or barbiturates kills mainly by **respiratory depression**.

## 1.17 Cerebellum

**Structure.** Lies behind the pons and medulla, below the occipital lobes, separated from them by the tentorium cerebelli. Two **hemispheres** joined by the midline **vermis**. Outer grey cortex with fine folds (**folia**); inner white matter shaped like a tree (**arbor vitae**) with deep nuclei (dentate is the largest). Its cortex contains large **Purkinje cells**. Joined to the brainstem by three pairs of **cerebellar peduncles** (superior to midbrain, middle to pons, inferior to medulla).

**Functions** (it works below consciousness):

1. **Coordination** of voluntary movements — compares intended movement (from the motor cortex) with actual movement (from proprioceptors) and corrects errors.
2. **Maintenance of posture and equilibrium** with the vestibular system.
3. **Muscle tone.**
4. **Motor learning** of skilled movements (writing, cycling).

**Cerebellar lesions** affect the same side of the body: **ataxia** (unsteady, wide-based gait), **intention tremor**, **dysmetria** (past-pointing in the finger–nose test), **dysdiadochokinesia** (cannot do rapid alternating movements), nystagmus, hypotonia, scanning (slurred) speech. Alcohol intoxication produces a similar picture.

## 1.18 Spinal cord: gross structure

- Cylindrical cord about **45 cm** long in adults, lying in the vertebral canal from the **foramen magnum** (continuous with the medulla) to the lower border of **L1 or upper L2**.
- Two swellings: **cervical enlargement** (nerves to upper limbs) and **lumbar enlargement** (nerves to lower limbs).
- It tapers into the **conus medullaris**; a thread of pia, the **filum terminale**, anchors it to the coccyx.
- Below L2 the vertebral canal holds a bundle of lumbar and sacral nerve roots — the **cauda equina** ("horse's tail"). This is why lumbar puncture below L2 is safe.
- **31 pairs of spinal nerves** leave it (8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, 1 coccygeal), each formed by a **dorsal (sensory) root** with its **dorsal root ganglion** and a **ventral (motor) root**.

**Cross-section**

```text
                 posterior median sulcus
              ___________|___________
             /   dorsal   |  dorsal   \      <- posterior (dorsal) white column
            /    horn \   |   / horn   \        (fasciculus gracilis + cuneatus)
  dorsal   |           \  |  /          |
  root  -->|  lateral   \ | /  lateral  |    <- lateral white column
           |  horn (T1-L2)>o< (autonomic)|        (spinothalamic, corticospinal)
           |            /  |  \  central |
           |           /   |   \  canal  |
  ventral<-|  ventral  /    |    \ ventral|    <- anterior white column
  root      \  horn   /     |     \ horn /
             \_______/______|______\____/
                    anterior median fissure

  GREY matter (H or butterfly shape, inside): cell bodies
  WHITE matter (outside): myelinated tracts
```

| Region | Contents | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dorsal (posterior) horn** | Sensory neurons and interneurons | Receives sensory input |
| **Lateral horn** (T1–L2, S2–S4) | Autonomic preganglionic neurons | Sympathetic (T1–L2) and parasympathetic (S2–S4) outflow |
| **Ventral (anterior) horn** | Large **α and γ motor neurons** | Motor output to skeletal muscle (lower motor neurons) |
| White columns (funiculi) | Ascending and descending tracts | Conduction to and from the brain |

## 1.19 Spinal cord tracts (afferent and efferent)

A **tract** is a bundle of nerve fibres in the CNS with the same origin, course and function. Tracts are named origin-to-destination (spinothalamic = cord to thalamus).

**Ascending (sensory, afferent) tracts**

| Tract | Carries | Crossing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dorsal column** (fasciculus gracilis from lower body, fasciculus cuneatus from upper body) → medial lemniscus | Fine (discriminative) touch, vibration, **conscious proprioception** (position sense), two-point discrimination | Crosses in the **medulla** |
| **Lateral spinothalamic** | **Pain and temperature** | Crosses in the **spinal cord** within one or two segments |
| **Anterior spinothalamic** | Crude touch and pressure | Crosses in the spinal cord |
| **Spinocerebellar** (dorsal and ventral) | Unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum (posture, coordination) | Mostly uncrossed or double-crossed |

Typical sensory pathway: **first-order neuron** (cell body in dorsal root ganglion) → **second-order neuron** (dorsal horn or medulla; crosses over) → **third-order neuron** (thalamus) → sensory cortex.

**Descending (motor, efferent) tracts**

| Tract | Origin | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lateral corticospinal** (pyramidal) | Motor cortex; about 80–90% of fibres cross at the **pyramids** of the medulla | Skilled voluntary movements of limbs, especially hands and fingers |
| **Anterior corticospinal** | Motor cortex; uncrossed fibres that cross at the level they end | Voluntary movement of axial (trunk) muscles |
| **Rubrospinal** (extrapyramidal) | Red nucleus, midbrain | Tone of flexor muscles, coordinated limb movement |
| **Vestibulospinal** | Vestibular nuclei | Balance; tone of antigravity extensor muscles |
| **Reticulospinal** | Reticular formation | Posture, muscle tone, autonomic control |
| **Tectospinal** | Superior colliculus | Turning head and eyes towards visual or sound stimuli |

**Upper vs lower motor neuron lesion**

| Feature | Upper motor neuron (UMN) lesion (e.g. stroke) | Lower motor neuron (LMN) lesion (e.g. polio) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Motor cortex or corticospinal tract | Anterior horn cell or peripheral nerve |
| Paralysis | Spastic | Flaccid |
| Muscle tone | Increased | Decreased |
| Tendon reflexes | Exaggerated | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar response | Extensor (**Babinski sign positive**) | Flexor or absent |
| Muscle wasting | Little (disuse only) | Marked |

## 1.20 Reflex activity

A **reflex** is a rapid, automatic, predictable response to a stimulus, carried out through the CNS without conscious control (we may become aware of it afterwards).

**Reflex arc — five components**

```text
 (1) RECEPTOR (skin / muscle spindle)
        |  stimulus
        v
 (2) AFFERENT (sensory) NEURON --> dorsal root --> dorsal root ganglion
        |
        v
 (3) CENTRE in spinal cord grey matter
        |  (monosynaptic: direct synapse;
        |   polysynaptic: one or more interneurons)
        v
 (4) EFFERENT (motor) NEURON --> ventral root
        |
        v
 (5) EFFECTOR (muscle contracts / gland secretes) = RESPONSE
```

**Classification of reflexes**

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Number of synapses | **Monosynaptic** (stretch reflex, e.g. knee jerk) and **polysynaptic** (withdrawal reflex) |
| Centre | Spinal (knee jerk), bulbar/medullary (cough, vomiting), midbrain (pupillary light reflex), cortical (conditioned reflexes) |
| Effector | Somatic (skeletal muscle) and autonomic/visceral (heart rate, micturition, pupil) |
| Development | **Inborn (unconditioned)** — present at birth (sucking, blinking); **acquired (conditioned)** — learned (Pavlov's dog salivating at a bell) |
| Site of receptor | **Superficial** (skin or mucosa: corneal, abdominal, plantar), **deep / tendon** (stretch reflexes: knee, ankle, biceps, triceps), **visceral** (pupillary, carotid sinus) |

**Stretch reflex (knee jerk).** Tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps; muscle spindles fire through Ia fibres directly onto α motor neurons in segments **L2–L4**; the quadriceps contracts and the leg kicks forward. At the same time interneurons relax the hamstrings (**reciprocal inhibition**). It tests the integrity of that spinal segment.

**Withdrawal (flexor) reflex.** Touching something hot activates nociceptors; through several interneurons the flexors of that limb contract to pull it away, and through the **crossed extensor reflex** the opposite limb extends to support the body.

**Properties of reflexes:** one-way conduction, reflex time (delay), summation, fatigue, irradiation (a stronger stimulus spreads to more muscles), recruitment, and occlusion.

**Importance:** protection (withdrawal, blinking, coughing), posture and muscle tone, control of internal organs (BP, breathing, micturition), and clinical diagnosis — reflexes tell the examiner which spinal segment or tract is damaged.

## Remember

- Neuron: dendrites carry impulses to the soma, axon carries them away; impulse starts at the axon hillock.
- CNS myelin = oligodendrocytes; PNS myelin = Schwann cells; microglia are the CNS phagocytes; astrocytes support the blood–brain barrier.
- Aα fibres are the fastest (70–120 m/s); C fibres are unmyelinated and slowest (0.5–2 m/s).
- RMP about −70 mV (K+ leak + Na+/K+ pump); threshold about −55 mV; overshoot about +30 mV; Na+ in = depolarisation, K+ out = repolarisation.
- Saltatory conduction at nodes of Ranvier is fast and energy-saving.
- Synapse: Ca2+ entry triggers transmitter release; EPSP excites, IPSP inhibits; delay about 0.5 ms; conduction one-way.
- Glutamate is the main excitatory and GABA the main inhibitory brain transmitter; glycine inhibits in the spinal cord.
- Meninges: dura, arachnoid, pia; CSF lies in the subarachnoid space.
- CSF: made by choroid plexus, about 500 mL/day, total about 150 mL; absorbed by arachnoid villi; lumbar puncture at L3–L4 or L4–L5.
- Ventricles: lateral → foramen of Monro → third → aqueduct of Sylvius → fourth → foramina of Luschka and Magendie.
- Area 4 motor, 3-1-2 sensory, 17 visual, 41-42 auditory, 44-45 Broca, 22 Wernicke.
- Medulla holds cardiac, vasomotor and respiratory centres; pyramids decussate there.
- Cerebellum: coordination, posture, tone; lesion → ataxia, intention tremor.
- Spinal cord ends at L1–L2; 31 pairs of spinal nerves; dorsal root sensory, ventral root motor.
- Pain and temperature cross in the cord (spinothalamic); fine touch and position cross in the medulla (dorsal column).
- Knee jerk L2–L4, monosynaptic; UMN lesion → spasticity and Babinski sign.
