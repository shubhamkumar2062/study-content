# Unit 4: Endocrine system

The endocrine system is a set of ductless glands that release **hormones** into the blood. Hormones travel to distant **target cells** that carry the matching receptor and change their activity. The nervous system (Unit 1) acts within milliseconds through nerves; the endocrine system acts more slowly but its effects last longer. The two are linked at the **hypothalamus–pituitary** axis. This unit is the physiological base of endocrine pharmacology: insulin and oral antidiabetic drugs, thyroid and antithyroid drugs, corticosteroids, and sex hormones and contraceptives all act on the systems described here.

## 4.1 Endocrine glands and hormones: an overview

```text
                 HYPOTHALAMUS  (releasing / inhibiting hormones; ADH, oxytocin)
                      |
                 PITUITARY GLAND  (anterior and posterior lobes)
                      |
   ---------------------------------------------------------------
   |            |             |            |            |         |
 PINEAL     THYROID      PARATHYROIDS   THYMUS      ADRENALS    PANCREAS
 (melatonin) (T3, T4,     (PTH)         (thymosin)  (cortex:     (islets:
             calcitonin)                            steroids;    insulin,
                                                    medulla:     glucagon)
                                                    adrenaline)
                      GONADS: testes (testosterone), ovaries (oestrogen,
                      progesterone)  -- covered in Unit 5
 Other organs with endocrine cells: kidney (renin, erythropoietin, calcitriol),
 heart (atrial natriuretic peptide), GI tract (gastrin, secretin, CCK),
 adipose tissue (leptin), placenta (hCG)
```

**Features of hormones:** secreted in tiny amounts (nano- or picograms per mL), carried by blood (free or bound to plasma proteins), act only on cells with **specific receptors**, are inactivated mainly in the liver and kidney, and are mostly controlled by **negative feedback**.

**Modes of chemical signalling**

| Mode | Distance | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Endocrine** | Via blood to distant cells | Insulin, thyroxine |
| **Paracrine** | To neighbouring cells by diffusion | Histamine on parietal cells; somatostatin in islets |
| **Autocrine** | On the same cell that released it | Some growth factors, interleukin-2 on T cells |
| Neuroendocrine | Released by neurons into blood | ADH, oxytocin |

## 4.2 Classification of hormones

**A. By chemical nature**

| Class | Made from | Examples | Solubility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Amine (amino acid–derived)** | Tyrosine, tryptophan | Adrenaline, noradrenaline, dopamine (water-soluble); **thyroid hormones T3, T4** (lipid-soluble); melatonin (from tryptophan) | Mixed |
| **Peptides and proteins** | Amino acid chains | Insulin, glucagon, GH, prolactin, PTH, calcitonin, ADH, oxytocin, ACTH, releasing hormones | Water-soluble |
| **Glycoproteins** | Proteins with carbohydrate | TSH, FSH, LH, hCG | Water-soluble |
| **Steroids** | Cholesterol | Cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, calcitriol | Lipid-soluble |
| **Eicosanoids** | Arachidonic acid | Prostaglandins, leukotrienes, thromboxanes (local hormones) | Lipid-soluble |

**B. By site of receptor (and so mechanism)**

- **Group I — lipid-soluble hormones with intracellular receptors:** steroids and thyroid hormones. Carried in blood bound to proteins; long half-life; act by changing gene transcription.
- **Group II — water-soluble hormones with cell-surface receptors:** peptides, proteins, catecholamines. Travel free in plasma; short half-life; act through **second messengers**.

**C. By the gland of origin** (pituitary, thyroid, adrenal and so on) and **D. by function** (metabolic, growth, reproductive, electrolyte-regulating).

## 4.3 Mechanism of hormone action

**1. Hormones acting on cell-surface receptors (second messenger mechanisms)**

**(a) cAMP (adenylyl cyclase) pathway** — adrenaline (β receptors), glucagon, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, PTH, calcitonin, ADH (V2):

```text
 Hormone (first messenger) binds receptor on membrane
        |
 G-protein (Gs) activated (GDP -> GTP)
        |
 ADENYLYL CYCLASE activated
        |
 ATP --> cyclic AMP (cAMP) = SECOND MESSENGER
        |
 cAMP activates PROTEIN KINASE A
        |
 Kinase PHOSPHORYLATES enzymes / channels --> cell response
        |  (e.g. glycogen breakdown in liver)
 cAMP destroyed by PHOSPHODIESTERASE (inhibited by theophylline, caffeine)
 Gi proteins do the opposite: inhibit adenylyl cyclase (alpha-2, M2 receptors)
```

**(b) Phospholipase C — IP3/DAG/Ca2+ pathway** — ADH (V1), oxytocin, TRH, GnRH, adrenaline (α1), angiotensin II: Gq protein → **phospholipase C** splits membrane PIP2 into **IP3** (releases Ca2+ from the ER) and **DAG** (activates protein kinase C). Ca2+ binds **calmodulin** to activate further enzymes.

**(c) Receptor tyrosine kinase** — **insulin**, IGF-1, growth factors: the receptor itself is an enzyme; hormone binding makes it phosphorylate tyrosine residues on itself and on insulin-receptor substrates, starting a kinase cascade (e.g. moving GLUT4 transporters to the membrane).

**(d) JAK–STAT pathway** — **growth hormone, prolactin**, erythropoietin, cytokines: the receptor activates attached Janus kinases, which activate STAT proteins that move to the nucleus to switch on genes.

**(e) cGMP pathway** — atrial natriuretic peptide (membrane guanylyl cyclase), nitric oxide (soluble guanylyl cyclase).

**Amplification:** one hormone molecule activates many G-proteins, each making many cAMP molecules, so a tiny amount of hormone gives a large response.

**2. Hormones acting on intracellular receptors** — steroids, thyroid hormones, calcitriol:

```text
 Lipid-soluble hormone crosses the plasma membrane
        |
 Binds receptor in CYTOPLASM (steroids) or NUCLEUS (thyroid hormones)
        |
 Hormone-receptor complex binds a HORMONE RESPONSE ELEMENT on DNA
        |
 TRANSCRIPTION of specific genes --> mRNA --> new PROTEINS (enzymes etc.)
        |
 Cell response -- slow (hours) but long-lasting
```

This is why corticosteroids take hours to act even when given intravenously.

**Regulation of hormone secretion**

- **Negative feedback** (most hormones): the hormone or its effect inhibits further release — e.g. high T4 suppresses TRH and TSH; high blood glucose → insulin → glucose falls → insulin falls.
- **Positive feedback** (few): oestrogen triggering the **LH surge** before ovulation; oxytocin during labour.
- **Nervous control:** sympathetic nerves → adrenal medulla; suckling → oxytocin and prolactin.
- **Rhythms:** circadian (cortisol peaks in the early morning; melatonin at night), monthly (menstrual cycle).

## 4.4 Hypothalamus and pituitary gland

**Location and structure.** The pituitary (hypophysis) is a pea-sized gland weighing about 0.5 g, lying in the **sella turcica** (hypophyseal fossa) of the sphenoid bone, attached to the hypothalamus by the **infundibulum** (pituitary stalk). The optic chiasma lies just above it — so a pituitary tumour can cause loss of the outer halves of the visual fields (bitemporal hemianopia).

```text
                HYPOTHALAMUS
     supraoptic and paraventricular   other hypothalamic neurons
     nuclei (make ADH, oxytocin)      (make releasing/inhibiting hormones)
            |  axons (hypothalamo-         |  release into
            |  hypophyseal tract)          |  primary capillary plexus
            |                              v
            |                    HYPOPHYSEAL PORTAL VEINS (in the stalk)
            |                              |
            v                              v
   POSTERIOR PITUITARY          ANTERIOR PITUITARY (secondary capillary plexus)
   (neurohypophysis:            (adenohypophysis: glandular tissue,
    nervous tissue; STORES       from Rathke's pouch; MAKES
    and RELEASES ADH and         GH, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, PRL)
    oxytocin)
               (intermediate lobe: MSH -- small in adults)
```

**Hypothalamic hormones controlling the anterior pituitary**

| Hypothalamic hormone | Effect on anterior pituitary |
| --- | --- |
| GHRH (growth hormone–releasing hormone) | ↑ GH |
| **Somatostatin (GHIH)** | ↓ GH (and TSH) |
| **TRH** (thyrotropin-releasing hormone) | ↑ TSH (and prolactin) |
| **CRH** (corticotropin-releasing hormone) | ↑ ACTH |
| **GnRH** (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) | ↑ FSH and LH |
| **Dopamine (prolactin-inhibiting hormone)** | ↓ Prolactin |

**Hormones of the anterior pituitary**

| Hormone | Target | Main actions | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Growth hormone (GH, somatotropin)** | Most tissues; liver | Growth of bones (via **IGF-1** from liver acting on epiphyseal plates) and muscle; protein synthesis; fat breakdown; raises blood glucose (anti-insulin) | GHRH ↑, somatostatin ↓; secreted in pulses, most during deep sleep; raised by exercise, fasting, hypoglycaemia |
| **TSH** (thyrotropin) | Thyroid | Growth of the gland; synthesis and release of T3, T4 | TRH ↑; T3/T4 negative feedback |
| **ACTH** (corticotropin) | Adrenal cortex | Secretion of **cortisol** (and adrenal androgens); growth of the cortex | CRH ↑; cortisol negative feedback; stress ↑ |
| **FSH** | Ovary; testis | Females: growth of follicles and oestrogen secretion. Males: spermatogenesis (via Sertoli cells) | GnRH ↑; oestrogen and inhibin feedback |
| **LH** | Ovary; testis | Females: **ovulation** (LH surge) and formation of corpus luteum (progesterone). Males: **testosterone** from Leydig cells (also called ICSH) | GnRH ↑; sex steroid feedback |
| **Prolactin (PRL)** | Breast | Milk **production** after childbirth | Dopamine ↓; suckling ↑; TRH ↑ |
| MSH | Melanocytes | Skin pigmentation | — |

**Hormones of the posterior pituitary** (made in the hypothalamus)

| Hormone | Main actions | Stimulus for release |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ADH (antidiuretic hormone, vasopressin)** | Inserts **aquaporin-2** channels in collecting ducts → water reabsorbed → concentrated urine; in large amounts constricts blood vessels (V1) | Rise in plasma osmolarity (osmoreceptors), fall in blood volume or BP, pain, nausea; inhibited by **alcohol** (hence diuresis) |
| **Oxytocin** | **Contracts uterus** during labour; **milk ejection (let-down)** by contracting myoepithelial cells of the breast | Stretch of cervix (Ferguson reflex — positive feedback); suckling |

**Disorders of the pituitary**

| Disorder | Cause | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Gigantism** | Excess GH **before** epiphyses close (childhood) | Very tall stature, proportionate |
| **Acromegaly** | Excess GH **after** epiphyses close (adults; usually adenoma) | Large hands, feet, jaw (prognathism), coarse face, thick skin, diabetes, hypertension |
| **Pituitary dwarfism** | GH deficiency in childhood | Short but proportionate stature, normal intelligence |
| **Diabetes insipidus** | Lack of ADH (central) or kidney resistance (nephrogenic) | **Polyuria** (large volumes of dilute urine), thirst, polydipsia; no glucose in urine |
| SIADH | Excess ADH (tumours, some drugs) | Water retention, low plasma Na+ |
| Hyperprolactinaemia | Prolactinoma; drugs that block dopamine | Galactorrhoea, absent periods, infertility |
| **Cushing's disease** | ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma | Features of cortisol excess (see 4.7) |
| Hypopituitarism (Simmonds' disease; Sheehan's syndrome after postpartum haemorrhage) | Loss of anterior pituitary | Loss of several hormones — fatigue, hypothyroidism, adrenal and gonadal failure |

## 4.5 Thyroid gland

**Structure.** A butterfly-shaped gland in the front of the neck, below the larynx, in front of the trachea. Two **lateral lobes** joined by an **isthmus** (over the 2nd–4th tracheal rings); sometimes a pyramidal lobe. Weighs about 20–25 g; very rich blood supply.

```text
            larynx (thyroid cartilage)
          ________________
   RIGHT |                |  LEFT
   LOBE  |    ISTHMUS     |  LOBE
         |________________|
              trachea

 Microscopic:   FOLLICLES = spheres of cuboidal FOLLICULAR CELLS
                filled with COLLOID (thyroglobulin) --> make T3, T4
                PARAFOLLICULAR (C) CELLS between follicles --> CALCITONIN
```

**Hormones:** **thyroxine (T4, tetraiodothyronine)** — about 90% of the output; **triiodothyronine (T3)** — about 10% but 3–5 times more active (most T4 is converted to T3 in the tissues by deiodinase); **calcitonin** from C cells.

**Synthesis, storage and release of thyroid hormones**

```text
 1. IODIDE TRAPPING: Na+/I- symporter actively takes I- from blood
        |   (blocked by perchlorate, thiocyanate)
 2. OXIDATION: I- --> active iodine by THYROID PEROXIDASE (TPO)
        |
 3. ORGANIFICATION: iodine attaches to TYROSINE residues of
    THYROGLOBULIN in colloid --> MIT (1 iodine), DIT (2 iodines)
        |   (steps 2-3 blocked by carbimazole, propylthiouracil)
 4. COUPLING (TPO): MIT + DIT --> T3 ;  DIT + DIT --> T4
        |
 5. STORAGE in colloid as part of thyroglobulin (enough for 2-3 months)
        |
 6. RELEASE: TSH --> follicle cells take in colloid by endocytosis
    --> lysosomal enzymes split thyroglobulin --> free T4, T3 into blood
        |   (large doses of iodide inhibit release)
 7. TRANSPORT: about 99% bound to plasma proteins (mainly
    thyroxine-binding globulin, TBG); only free hormone acts
```

**Regulation:** hypothalamus (TRH) → pituitary (TSH) → thyroid (T3, T4), with negative feedback by T3/T4 on both. Iodine deficiency reduces hormone, raises TSH and enlarges the gland (**goitre**).

**Functions of thyroid hormones**

1. **Raise BMR** and oxygen consumption in most tissues (more Na+/K+ ATPase, more mitochondria); **calorigenic** — heat production.
2. **Growth and development:** essential with GH for skeletal growth; essential for **brain development** in the fetus and infant.
3. **Metabolism:** increase glucose absorption and use, fat breakdown, lower plasma cholesterol; protein synthesis (catabolic in excess).
4. **Cardiovascular:** raise heart rate, force and cardiac output (increase β-receptors — hence β-blockers relieve symptoms of hyperthyroidism).
5. **Nervous system:** alertness, speed of reflexes.
6. Normal reproductive function, gut motility, and skin and hair.

**Calcitonin:** lowers blood calcium by inhibiting **osteoclasts** (bone resorption) and increasing renal excretion of calcium; released when blood Ca2+ is high. Used in Paget's disease and some cases of osteoporosis.

**Disorders of the thyroid**

| Disorder | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Cretinism** (congenital hypothyroidism) | Dwarfism, **mental retardation**, coarse features, protruding tongue, pot belly, delayed milestones; preventable by early detection (newborn screening) and thyroxine |
| **Myxoedema** (adult hypothyroidism; often Hashimoto's autoimmune thyroiditis) | Low BMR, weight gain, **cold intolerance**, slow pulse, puffy face (non-pitting oedema), dry skin, hair loss, constipation, sluggishness, hoarse voice; high TSH; treated with **levothyroxine** |
| **Hyperthyroidism — Graves' disease** (autoimmune; antibodies stimulate the TSH receptor) | High BMR, **weight loss with good appetite**, **heat intolerance**, sweating, tachycardia and palpitations, tremor, nervousness, diarrhoea, **exophthalmos** (protruding eyes), diffuse goitre; low TSH |
| **Simple (endemic) goitre** | Enlarged gland due to **iodine deficiency**, usually with normal function; prevented by **iodised salt** |
| Toxic nodular goitre | Overactive nodules |
| Thyroid carcinoma | Thyroid nodule; medullary type arises from C cells |

## 4.6 Parathyroid glands

**Structure:** usually **four** small glands (each about the size of a rice grain, about 30–50 mg) on the **posterior surface of the thyroid lobes**, two on each side. **Chief cells** secrete **parathyroid hormone (PTH, parathormone)**; oxyphil cells have no known function.

**PTH raises blood calcium and lowers phosphate.** Its release is controlled by blood Ca2+ itself through a calcium-sensing receptor (low Ca2+ → more PTH).

| Target | Action of PTH |
| --- | --- |
| **Bone** | Stimulates **osteoclasts** (indirectly, via osteoblasts) → bone resorption → Ca2+ and phosphate released into blood |
| **Kidney** | ↑ Ca2+ reabsorption in the DCT; ↓ phosphate reabsorption (phosphaturia); activates **1α-hydroxylase** → active vitamin D (**calcitriol**) |
| **Intestine** (indirect, via calcitriol) | ↑ Ca2+ and phosphate absorption |

**Calcium balance** — normal plasma calcium about **9–11 mg/dL**: PTH and calcitriol raise it; calcitonin lowers it.

```text
 Low blood Ca2+ --> PTH up --> bone resorption, kidney Ca2+ kept,
                               calcitriol --> gut absorption --> Ca2+ rises
 High blood Ca2+ --> PTH down, CALCITONIN up --> Ca2+ falls
```

Calcium is needed for nerve and muscle excitability, muscle contraction, blood clotting, bone and teeth, hormone release and heart rhythm.

**Disorders**

| Disorder | Features |
| --- | --- |
| **Hypoparathyroidism** (often after accidental removal in thyroid surgery) | Low Ca2+ → increased nerve excitability → **tetany**: muscle spasms, tingling, carpopedal spasm; **Trousseau's sign** (carpal spasm when a BP cuff is inflated on the arm) and **Chvostek's sign** (facial twitch when the facial nerve is tapped); laryngeal spasm can be fatal. Treated with calcium (IV calcium gluconate in an emergency) and vitamin D |
| **Hyperparathyroidism** (usually an adenoma) | High Ca2+: weak decalcified bones (fractures, bone pain, cysts), **kidney stones**, abdominal pain, peptic ulcer, muscle weakness, depression ("bones, stones, groans and moans") |

## 4.7 Adrenal (suprarenal) glands

**Structure.** Two triangular glands, one on top of each kidney, each about 4–5 g. Each has two parts with different origins and functions: outer **cortex** (about 80–90%, from mesoderm; steroid hormones) and inner **medulla** (from neural crest; a modified sympathetic ganglion; catecholamines).

```text
  CAPSULE
  |--------------------------------------------|
  | ZONA GLOMERULOSA  (outer, thin)  -> MINERALOCORTICOIDS (aldosterone)  "SALT"
  |--------------------------------------------|
  | ZONA FASCICULATA  (middle, thickest)        |
  |                                  -> GLUCOCORTICOIDS (cortisol)        "SUGAR"
  |--------------------------------------------|
  | ZONA RETICULARIS  (inner)        -> ANDROGENS (DHEA)                  "SEX"
  |============================================|  CORTEX ends
  | MEDULLA: chromaffin cells   -> ADRENALINE (about 80%), NORADRENALINE (about 20%)
  |--------------------------------------------|
```

**A. Mineralocorticoids — aldosterone**

- Acts on the **DCT and collecting duct**: **Na+ reabsorption** (and so water), **K+ and H+ secretion**.
- Increases blood volume and BP.
- Controlled mainly by **angiotensin II** (renin–angiotensin system, Unit 3) and by **high plasma K+**; ACTH has a minor effect.

**B. Glucocorticoids — cortisol (hydrocortisone)**

- **Metabolism:** raises blood glucose (gluconeogenesis in the liver; less glucose use by tissues — diabetogenic); breaks down protein in muscle, skin and bone; mobilises fat (redistributes it to face and trunk).
- **Anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive:** stabilise lysosomes, reduce capillary permeability, inhibit phospholipase A2 (fewer prostaglandins and leukotrienes), reduce lymphocytes and eosinophils — the basis of prednisolone and other steroid drugs in asthma, allergy, arthritis and transplantation.
- **Stress response:** essential for survival in stress (injury, infection, surgery); helps catecholamines maintain BP (**permissive action**).
- Others: reduce bone formation, raise gastric acid, affect mood, retain some Na+.
- **Control:** CRH → ACTH → cortisol, with negative feedback; **circadian rhythm** (highest early morning, lowest about midnight); stress raises it. Long-term steroid therapy suppresses the axis, so it must be **withdrawn gradually**.

**C. Adrenal androgens** (dehydroepiandrosterone, DHEA): weak; responsible for pubic and axillary hair in females and part of the pubertal growth spurt.

**D. Adrenal medulla — catecholamines** (secreted in response to sympathetic preganglionic stimulation; "fight or flight"):

| Effect | Adrenaline / noradrenaline |
| --- | --- |
| Heart | ↑ rate and force (β1) |
| Blood vessels | Constriction in skin and gut (α1); dilatation in skeletal muscle by adrenaline (β2) |
| BP | Raised (noradrenaline mainly raises both systolic and diastolic) |
| Bronchi | Dilated (β2, adrenaline) |
| Metabolism | ↑ blood glucose (glycogenolysis), ↑ fatty acids, ↑ BMR |
| Eye | Pupil dilated |
| Others | Alertness; sweating; reduced gut motility |

**Disorders of the adrenal gland**

| Disorder | Cause | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cushing's syndrome** | Excess cortisol — long-term steroid drugs (commonest), pituitary ACTH adenoma (Cushing's disease), adrenal tumour | **Moon face**, buffalo hump, central obesity with thin limbs, purple **striae**, thin skin and easy bruising, hypertension, hyperglycaemia, osteoporosis, muscle weakness, poor healing |
| **Addison's disease** | Destruction of the adrenal cortex (autoimmune, tuberculosis) — low cortisol and aldosterone | Weakness, weight loss, **hypotension**, **skin pigmentation** (high ACTH/MSH), low Na+ and high K+, hypoglycaemia; risk of adrenal crisis |
| **Conn's syndrome** (primary hyperaldosteronism) | Aldosterone-secreting adenoma | Hypertension, **low K+** (muscle weakness), metabolic alkalosis |
| Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (adrenogenital syndrome) | Enzyme defect → excess androgens | Virilisation in girls, early puberty in boys |
| **Phaeochromocytoma** | Tumour of the adrenal medulla | Episodes of severe hypertension, headache, sweating, palpitations |

## 4.8 Pancreas (endocrine part): islets of Langerhans

About 1–2 million **islets of Langerhans** are scattered among the exocrine acini (Unit 2), forming about 1–2% of the pancreatic mass; more numerous in the tail.

| Cell type | Share (approx.) | Hormone | Main effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Beta (β) cells** | 60–70% (centre of islet) | **Insulin** | Lowers blood glucose |
| **Alpha (α) cells** | 20–25% | **Glucagon** | Raises blood glucose |
| **Delta (δ) cells** | About 5–10% | **Somatostatin** | Inhibits insulin and glucagon (paracrine) and GI activity |
| PP (F) cells | Small | Pancreatic polypeptide | Inhibits pancreatic exocrine secretion |

**Insulin** — a protein of two chains (A with 21 and B with 30 amino acids) joined by disulphide bridges; made as proinsulin, which is split into insulin and **C-peptide** (C-peptide measurement shows how much insulin the patient's own pancreas makes).

- **Stimulus:** rise in blood glucose (also amino acids, GI hormones such as GLP-1 and GIP — the **incretins**, vagal stimulation). Glucose enters β cells by GLUT2 → ATP rises → ATP-sensitive K+ channels close → depolarisation → Ca2+ entry → insulin release. **Sulphonylureas** (glibenclamide) close the same K+ channel.
- **Actions** (the hormone of the fed state; anabolic):

| Metabolism | Effect of insulin |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrate | ↑ glucose uptake into muscle and fat (moves **GLUT4** to the membrane); ↑ glycogen synthesis; ↓ glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis → **blood glucose falls** |
| Fat | ↑ fat synthesis and storage; ↓ lipolysis; ↓ ketone formation |
| Protein | ↑ amino acid uptake and protein synthesis; ↓ breakdown |
| Electrolytes | Moves **K+ into cells** (used to treat high K+) |
| Growth | Acts with GH for growth |

**Glucagon** — the hormone of fasting (catabolic): stimulated by low blood glucose, amino acids and exercise; inhibited by glucose and insulin. Acts mainly on the **liver**: glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and ketogenesis → **raises blood glucose**. Injected glucagon is used for severe hypoglycaemia.

**Blood glucose regulation**

```text
 After a meal: glucose HIGH --> beta cells --> INSULIN --> uptake, storage --> glucose falls
 Fasting:      glucose LOW  --> alpha cells --> GLUCAGON --> liver releases glucose --> rises
 Other hormones that raise glucose (counter-regulatory): adrenaline, cortisol, GH, thyroxine
 Normal fasting plasma glucose: about 70-100 mg/dL
```

**Disorders**

- **Diabetes mellitus** — persistent hyperglycaemia from lack of insulin, insulin resistance or both.
  - **Type 1:** autoimmune destruction of β cells; usually young people; absolute insulin deficiency; prone to **ketoacidosis**; needs insulin.
  - **Type 2:** insulin resistance with relative deficiency; adults, obesity, family history; most common type; treated with diet, exercise, metformin and other oral drugs, insulin later.
  - Classic features: **polyuria** (osmotic diuresis), **polydipsia**, **polyphagia**, weight loss, glycosuria, fatigue, slow healing, infections. Long-term complications: retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy, atherosclerosis.
  - Diagnosis (common criteria): fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, 2-hour value in an oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 200 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥ 6.5%.
- **Hypoglycaemia** (glucose below about 70 mg/dL): sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, confusion, fits, coma; commonly from excess insulin or sulphonylureas, or an insulinoma; treated with oral or IV glucose.

## 4.9 Pineal gland

A small pine-cone-shaped gland in the **epithalamus**, at the back of the third ventricle; made of **pinealocytes** and glial cells. Calcium deposits ("brain sand") appear with age and are visible on X-rays.

- Secretes **melatonin** (made from serotonin, ultimately from tryptophan). Secretion is **high in darkness and low in light**: light falling on the retina inhibits it through a pathway from the retina to the hypothalamus (suprachiasmatic nucleus, the body's master clock) and sympathetic fibres.
- **Functions:** regulates the **circadian (sleep–wake) rhythm** and promotes sleep; may help regulate the timing of puberty (inhibits gonadotropins); antioxidant.
- **Applied:** melatonin and melatonin agonists are used for jet lag and some sleep disorders; disruption with shift work. Pineal tumours in children can cause early puberty.

## 4.10 Thymus

A two-lobed lymphoid and endocrine gland in the **upper anterior mediastinum**, behind the sternum. It is **large in infancy and childhood**, reaches its largest absolute size around puberty, then **involutes** and is gradually replaced by fat. Each lobule has an outer **cortex** (packed with immature T lymphocytes) and inner **medulla** with **Hassall's corpuscles**.

- **Hormones:** **thymosin**, thymopoietin, thymulin and thymic humoral factor.
- **Functions:** maturation and "education" of **T lymphocytes** — cells that would attack the body's own tissues are removed and useful ones are released to the lymph nodes and spleen (cell-mediated immunity).
- **Disorders:** **DiGeorge syndrome** (congenital absence of thymus and parathyroids → severe infections and hypocalcaemia), thymoma; thymic abnormalities are common in **myasthenia gravis**, and thymectomy may help.

| Gland | Hyposecretion | Hypersecretion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anterior pituitary (GH) | Dwarfism (child) | Gigantism (child), acromegaly (adult) |
| Posterior pituitary (ADH) | Diabetes insipidus | SIADH |
| Thyroid | Cretinism (child), myxoedema (adult) | Graves' disease, toxic goitre |
| Parathyroid | Tetany | Bone decalcification, kidney stones |
| Adrenal cortex | Addison's disease | Cushing's syndrome, Conn's syndrome |
| Adrenal medulla | (no definite disease) | Phaeochromocytoma |

| Thymus | DiGeorge syndrome (immune deficiency) | Thymoma |

## Remember

- Hormones: amines, peptides/proteins, glycoproteins, steroids, eicosanoids; steroids and thyroid hormones use intracellular receptors and act on genes.
- Peptides and catecholamines act through second messengers: cAMP (Gs, adenylyl cyclase, PKA), IP3/DAG/Ca2+ (Gq, phospholipase C), tyrosine kinase (insulin), JAK–STAT (GH, prolactin).
- Most hormones are regulated by negative feedback; LH surge and oxytocin in labour are positive feedback.
- Pituitary in sella turcica; anterior lobe makes GH, TSH, ACTH, FSH, LH, PRL (portal system); posterior lobe stores ADH and oxytocin made in supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei.
- GH excess: gigantism (child), acromegaly (adult); ADH deficiency: diabetes insipidus.
- Thyroid: T4 about 90%, T3 more active; steps — trapping, oxidation (TPO), organification, coupling, storage, release; carbimazole blocks TPO.
- Hypothyroidism: cretinism, myxoedema (cold intolerance, weight gain); Graves' disease: weight loss, heat intolerance, exophthalmos; iodine deficiency → goitre.
- Calcitonin lowers Ca2+; PTH raises Ca2+ (bone, kidney, calcitriol); low PTH → tetany (Trousseau, Chvostek).
- Adrenal cortex: glomerulosa – aldosterone (salt), fasciculata – cortisol (sugar), reticularis – androgens (sex); medulla – adrenaline 80%, noradrenaline 20%.
- Cushing's: moon face, buffalo hump, striae; Addison's: hypotension, pigmentation, low Na+, high K+.
- Islets: β 60–70% insulin, α glucagon, δ somatostatin; insulin uses GLUT4 and lowers glucose; glucagon raises it.
- Pineal: melatonin at night, circadian rhythm; thymus: thymosin, T-cell maturation, involutes after puberty.
