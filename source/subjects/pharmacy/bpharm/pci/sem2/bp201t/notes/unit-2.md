# Unit 2: Digestive system and energetics

The digestive system breaks food into molecules small enough to be absorbed and delivers them to the blood. For a pharmacy student it matters twice over: most drugs are swallowed and absorbed here, and many common drugs act on it (antacids, proton pump inhibitors, H2 blockers, laxatives, antiemetics). The syllabus asks for special attention to the **stomach** — how acid is made, how the parasympathetic system controls it and how pepsin digests protein. The last part, **energetics**, explains how the energy in absorbed food is stored as ATP and creatine phosphate, and how the body's basal energy use (BMR) is measured.

## 2.1 Organisation of the digestive system

```text
 MOUTH (teeth, tongue, salivary glands)
   |
 PHARYNX
   |
 OESOPHAGUS (about 25 cm)
   |
 STOMACH  <------------- (liver and gallbladder, pancreas
   |                       pour secretions into the duodenum)
 SMALL INTESTINE: duodenum -> jejunum -> ileum
   |
 LARGE INTESTINE: caecum (+ appendix) -> ascending -> transverse
                  -> descending -> sigmoid colon -> rectum
   |
 ANAL CANAL -> anus
```

- **Alimentary canal (GI tract):** a muscular tube about 8–9 m long in a cadaver (shorter in life because of muscle tone), from mouth to anus.
- **Accessory organs:** teeth, tongue, salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, pancreas.
- **Processes:** ingestion → secretion → mixing and propulsion → digestion (mechanical and chemical) → absorption → defecation.

## 2.2 General structure of the GI tract wall

From the oesophagus to the anal canal the wall has four layers.

```text
 LUMEN
  |-- MUCOSA:       epithelium (stratified squamous in mouth/oesophagus/anus,
  |                 simple columnar in stomach and intestines) + lamina propria
  |                 + muscularis mucosae
  |-- SUBMUCOSA:    loose connective tissue, blood vessels, glands,
  |                 SUBMUCOSAL (MEISSNER'S) PLEXUS -> controls secretion
  |-- MUSCULARIS EXTERNA: inner circular + outer longitudinal smooth muscle
  |                 MYENTERIC (AUERBACH'S) PLEXUS between them -> controls motility
  |-- SEROSA (visceral peritoneum)  [adventitia in the oesophagus]
 OUTSIDE
```

The two plexuses form the **enteric nervous system**, which can work alone but is modulated by the autonomic system: **parasympathetic (vagus, pelvic nerves) increases secretion and motility; sympathetic decreases them** and closes sphincters.

## 2.3 Mouth, teeth and salivary glands

**Mouth (oral cavity):** bounded by lips, cheeks, hard and soft palate, and tongue. The **tongue** mixes food, forms the bolus, starts swallowing and carries taste buds (covered in BP101T).

**Teeth.** Children have 20 **deciduous (temporary) teeth**; adults have 32 **permanent** teeth.

| Set | Dental formula (one half of upper / lower jaw) | Total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deciduous | I 2, C 1, M 2 / I 2, C 1, M 2 | 20 |
| Permanent | I 2, C 1, PM 2, M 3 / I 2, C 1, PM 2, M 3 | 32 |

(I = incisors, C = canines, PM = premolars, M = molars.) A tooth has a crown, neck and root; its layers are **enamel** (hardest substance in the body), dentine, and pulp with nerves and vessels.

**Salivary glands** — three pairs of major glands:

| Gland | Location | Duct | Type of secretion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parotid** (largest) | In front of and below the ear | Parotid (Stensen's) duct, opens opposite the upper second molar | Serous (watery, rich in amylase) |
| **Submandibular** | Under the body of the mandible | Submandibular (Wharton's) duct, opens beside the frenulum of the tongue | Mixed, mainly serous; gives most of the resting saliva |
| **Sublingual** (smallest) | Floor of the mouth under the tongue | Several small ducts (of Rivinus) | Mainly mucous |

**Saliva:** about 1–1.5 L per day; pH about 6.0–7.4; 99.5% water.

| Constituent | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Salivary amylase (ptyalin) | Starts starch digestion → maltose, dextrins |
| Lingual lipase | Begins fat digestion (acts mainly in the stomach) |
| Mucin | Lubricates food, forms bolus, protects mucosa |
| Lysozyme, IgA, thiocyanate | Antibacterial |
| Water and bicarbonate | Dissolve food for taste, buffer acids, clean the mouth |

Other functions: helps speech, keeps the mouth moist, excretes some substances (heavy metals, some drugs). **Control** is purely nervous: parasympathetic stimulation (facial and glossopharyngeal nerves) gives a large flow of watery saliva; sympathetic gives a small amount of thick saliva. Atropine-like drugs (and many antidepressants and antihistamines) cause **dry mouth** by blocking the parasympathetic supply.

## 2.4 Pharynx and oesophagus

The **pharynx** is a common passage for food and air. The **oesophagus** is a muscular tube about 25 cm long, behind the trachea, passing through the diaphragm to the stomach. Its upper third has skeletal muscle, the lower third smooth muscle. The **lower oesophageal sphincter** prevents reflux of acid; when weak, it causes **gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD)** with heartburn.

## 2.5 Stomach: anatomy

A J-shaped dilated part of the tract in the upper left abdomen (epigastric and left hypochondriac regions), below the diaphragm. Empty capacity about 50 mL; it can expand to hold about 1–1.5 L or more.

```text
                 cardiac orifice
     oesophagus  |
         \       v      ___ FUNDUS (above the cardia; holds gas)
          \_____ CARDIA/    \
                |            \
   LESSER       |   BODY      \   GREATER CURVATURE
   CURVATURE    |  (rugae =    |  (convex, left)
   (concave,    |   folds)     |
    right)       \            /
                  \  PYLORIC /
                   \ ANTRUM /
                    \______/
                       | PYLORIC SPHINCTER
                       v
                   DUODENUM
```

**Parts:** cardia, fundus, body, pyloric part (antrum and canal) ending at the **pyloric sphincter**. The empty stomach shows folds called **rugae**. Its muscularis has **three layers** (outer longitudinal, middle circular, inner oblique) for churning.

**Gastric glands** (in the fundus and body) open into gastric pits.

| Cell | Secretion | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface and neck mucous cells | Mucus and bicarbonate | Protective gel layer against acid and pepsin |
| **Parietal (oxyntic) cells** | **HCl** and **intrinsic factor** | Acid; intrinsic factor needed for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum |
| **Chief (peptic) cells** | **Pepsinogen**, gastric lipase | Protein and some fat digestion |
| **G cells** (antrum) | Gastrin (hormone, into blood) | Stimulates acid secretion and motility |
| Enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells | Histamine | Stimulates parietal cells (H2 receptors) |
| D cells | Somatostatin | Inhibits gastrin and acid |

## 2.6 Functions of the stomach

1. **Storage** of food, releasing it slowly to the duodenum.
2. **Mechanical mixing** — churning turns food into semi-liquid **chyme**.
3. **Secretion of gastric juice** (about 1.5–2 L per day; pH about 1–2 because of HCl).
4. **Digestion** — protein by pepsin; a little fat by gastric lipase; salivary amylase works until the acid stops it.
5. **Antimicrobial action** of HCl.
6. **Intrinsic factor** for B12 absorption — its loss causes pernicious anaemia.
7. **Limited absorption** of water, alcohol, some weak-acid drugs such as aspirin.
8. **Hormone secretion** (gastrin; ghrelin, the hunger hormone).
9. Regulated **gastric emptying** — liquids leave fastest, then carbohydrates, proteins, and fats slowest.

## 2.7 Acid production in the stomach

Parietal cells secrete H+ into the gland lumen at a concentration about a million times that of blood, using energy from many mitochondria.

```text
     BLOOD side                PARIETAL CELL                  LUMEN (gland)
                         CO2 + H2O
                            |  carbonic anhydrase
                            v
                         H2CO3
                          /   \
     HCO3- <--(Cl-/HCO3-  HCO3-  H+ --[H+/K+ ATPase]--> H+   (PROTON PUMP,
      to blood exchanger)                <-- K+               uses ATP)
     ("alkaline tide")      Cl- ------------------------> Cl-   (Cl- channel)
       Cl- --> in                                       H+ + Cl- = HCl
```

**Steps**

1. CO2 (from metabolism and blood) combines with water inside the cell, catalysed by **carbonic anhydrase**, to form carbonic acid, which splits into **H+ and HCO3−**.
2. **H+ is pumped into the lumen in exchange for K+** by the **H+/K+ ATPase (proton pump)** on the apical membrane — the final common step of acid secretion.
3. **HCO3− leaves into the blood** in exchange for Cl− (chloride–bicarbonate exchanger). Blood leaving the stomach after a meal is therefore slightly alkaline — the **alkaline tide**.
4. Cl− that entered from the blood passes out through apical Cl− channels; K+ recycles through K+ channels.
5. H+ and Cl− together form **HCl** in the lumen.

**Functions of HCl:** activates pepsinogen to pepsin and gives its optimum pH; denatures proteins; kills most swallowed microbes; helps absorption of iron (keeps it in the Fe2+ form) and calcium; stimulates secretin release in the duodenum.

**Drug link:** proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole, pantoprazole) block the H+/K+ ATPase irreversibly and are the strongest acid suppressants; antacids neutralise acid already made.

## 2.8 Regulation of acid secretion

Three chemical messengers stimulate the parietal cell, and they strengthen each other:

| Stimulant | Source | Receptor on parietal cell | Blocked by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Acetylcholine** | Vagus nerve (parasympathetic) endings via enteric neurons | **Muscarinic M3** | Atropine, pirenzepine (M1 on ganglia/ECL) |
| **Gastrin** | G cells of antrum, into blood | CCK2 (gastrin) receptor | — |
| **Histamine** | ECL cells (paracrine) | **H2** | Ranitidine, famotidine |

**Parasympathetic regulation** (syllabus focus): the **vagus nerve** is the main nervous drive for acid.

```text
 Sight, smell, taste, thought of food; chewing; swallowing
            |
            v
 Dorsal motor nucleus of VAGUS (medulla)
            |  vagal efferent fibres
            v
 Enteric neurons in stomach wall release ACETYLCHOLINE
     |                     |                         |
     v                     v                         v
 Parietal cells (M3)   ECL cells -> histamine    G cells: GRP (bombesin)
  -> HCl directly       -> H2 -> more HCl         -> GASTRIN -> more HCl
                                                  (ACh also inhibits D cells,
                                                   less somatostatin)
```

Cutting the vagus (vagotomy, once used for ulcers) cuts acid output sharply. Sympathetic stimulation has little direct effect but reduces blood flow and motility.

**Phases of gastric secretion**

| Phase | Trigger | Mechanism | Share of acid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cephalic** | Sight, smell, taste, thought of food | Purely nervous: vagus → ACh and gastrin | About 30% |
| **Gastric** | Food in stomach — distension, peptides | Local and vago-vagal reflexes; peptides and amino acids stimulate gastrin | About 60% |
| **Intestinal** | Chyme in duodenum | Small stimulation at first (intestinal gastrin), then **inhibition**: acid, fat and hypertonic chyme release **secretin, CCK, GIP** and trigger the enterogastric reflex, which slow acid secretion and gastric emptying | About 10% |

**Negative feedback:** when antral pH falls below about 2, D cells release somatostatin, which suppresses gastrin — acid limits its own production.

## 2.9 Role of pepsin in protein digestion

- **Chief cells** secrete inactive **pepsinogen**, which protects them from digesting themselves.
- In the lumen, **HCl** (below about pH 5) converts pepsinogen to active **pepsin** by removing a small peptide; pepsin then activates more pepsinogen (**autocatalysis**).
- Pepsin works best at **pH about 1.5–2.5** and is inactivated in the neutral pH of the duodenum.
- It is an **endopeptidase**: it splits peptide bonds inside protein chains, preferring those next to aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan), giving **proteoses, peptones and polypeptides**.
- It digests about 10–20% of dietary protein and is especially good at digesting **collagen** in meat; it also curdles milk. Digestion is completed in the small intestine by pancreatic and brush-border enzymes, so people who lack pepsin can still digest protein.

**Protection of the stomach wall:** mucus–bicarbonate barrier, tight junctions, rapid cell renewal, good blood flow and **prostaglandins** (which increase mucus and bicarbonate). NSAIDs block prostaglandin synthesis — the reason they cause gastric ulcers.

## 2.10 Small intestine

The longest part of the tract, about 5–6 m, from pylorus to ileocaecal valve.

| Part | Length (approx.) | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Duodenum** | 25 cm, C-shaped around the head of pancreas | Receives bile and pancreatic juice at the **hepatopancreatic ampulla** (ampulla of Vater, sphincter of Oddi); **Brunner's glands** secrete alkaline mucus |
| **Jejunum** | About 2.5 m | Thick wall, many tall circular folds, rich blood supply; main site of absorption |
| **Ileum** | About 3.5 m | Thinner, fewer folds; **Peyer's patches** (lymphoid tissue); absorbs **vitamin B12** and **bile salts**; ends at the ileocaecal valve |

**Adaptations for absorption** — surface area increased about 600 times:

```text
 Circular folds (plicae circulares)  -> x3
        |
 VILLI (finger-like, 0.5-1 mm)       -> x10
        |    each villus has: columnar absorptive cells (enterocytes),
        |    goblet cells, capillary network, central LACTEAL (lymph)
        |
 MICROVILLI = BRUSH BORDER           -> x20
             (carry brush-border enzymes)

     ___      ___      ___   <- villi
    |   |    |   |    |   |
    | L |    | L |    | L |   L = lacteal (absorbs fat as chylomicrons)
    |___|____|___|____|___|
        |crypt|  |crypt|      <- crypts of Lieberkuhn (intestinal glands;
                                 new cells, Paneth cells)
```

**Functions:** completes digestion (pancreatic juice, bile, brush-border enzymes); absorbs almost all nutrients, most water and electrolytes, and most oral drugs; secretes intestinal juice (succus entericus, about 1.5–2 L/day, pH about 7.6) and hormones (secretin, CCK, GIP, motilin); immune defence (Peyer's patches, IgA).

**Brush-border enzymes:** maltase, sucrase, **lactase**, isomaltase (carbohydrates); aminopeptidases and dipeptidases (proteins); intestinal lipase; **enterokinase** (activates trypsinogen). Lactase deficiency causes lactose intolerance.

## 2.11 Large intestine

About 1.5 m long, from the ileocaecal valve to the anus: **caecum** with the **vermiform appendix**, **ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid colon**, **rectum** and **anal canal** (internal sphincter of smooth muscle, involuntary; external sphincter of skeletal muscle, voluntary).

**Distinctive features:** longitudinal muscle gathered into three bands (**taeniae coli**), pouches (**haustra**), fat tags (appendices epiploicae), no villi, many goblet cells.

**Functions**

1. **Absorption of water and electrolytes** — of about 1.5–2 L of fluid entering each day, most is absorbed, leaving about 100–200 mL in the faeces.
2. **Formation and storage of faeces.**
3. **Bacterial action:** gut flora ferment undigested fibre (producing short-chain fatty acids and gas) and synthesise **vitamin K** and some B vitamins. Long courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics reduce this flora.
4. **Secretion of mucus** (lubrication).
5. **Defecation.**
6. Rectal absorption of drugs (suppositories), partly avoiding first-pass metabolism in the liver.

## 2.12 Pancreas (exocrine part)

A soft gland about 12–15 cm long, behind the stomach, with a **head** (in the curve of the duodenum), **body** and **tail** (reaching the spleen). Most of it is **exocrine acini** (about 98%); the **islets of Langerhans** (endocrine, Unit 4) are scattered among them. The **main pancreatic duct** joins the common bile duct at the ampulla of Vater.

**Pancreatic juice:** about 1.2–1.5 L per day; alkaline (pH about 8) because of **bicarbonate**, which neutralises acid chyme.

| Food | Enzyme (secreted form → active form) | Product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protein | Trypsinogen → **trypsin** (by enterokinase); chymotrypsinogen → chymotrypsin (by trypsin); procarboxypeptidase → carboxypeptidase; proelastase → elastase | Peptides, amino acids |
| Starch | **Pancreatic amylase** | Maltose, maltotriose, dextrins |
| Fat | **Pancreatic lipase** (with colipase), phospholipase A2, cholesterol esterase | Monoglycerides, fatty acids, glycerol |
| Nucleic acids | Ribonuclease, deoxyribonuclease | Nucleotides |

Pancreatic **trypsin inhibitor** stops early activation inside the gland; if enzymes activate inside the pancreas they digest it — **acute pancreatitis**.

**Control:** **secretin** (released when acid enters the duodenum) → water and bicarbonate; **cholecystokinin (CCK)** (released by fat and protein in the duodenum) → enzyme-rich juice and gallbladder contraction; vagus → enzymes.

## 2.13 Liver and biliary system

**Anatomy.** The largest gland, about 1.5 kg, in the right hypochondrium under the diaphragm. Divided by the **falciform ligament** into a large **right lobe** and smaller **left lobe**; the **caudate** and **quadrate** lobes lie on the undersurface. The **porta hepatis** admits the **hepatic artery** (oxygen-rich, about 25% of flow) and **portal vein** (nutrient-rich blood from the gut, about 75%), and lets out the hepatic ducts. Blood leaves by the **hepatic veins** to the inferior vena cava.

**Hepatic lobule** — the structural unit:

```text
          portal triad                 portal triad
     (hepatic artery branch,      .  /
      portal vein branch,      .    /
      bile duct)            .  rows of hepatocytes
            \           .     radiating like spokes,
             \       .        with SINUSOIDS between
              \   .           (lined by KUPFFER CELLS)
       --------(CENTRAL VEIN)--------   hexagon-shaped lobule
              /   .           blood flows: triad -> sinusoid
             /       .                     -> central vein
            /           .     bile flows the opposite way:
     portal triad          .  canaliculi -> bile duct in triad
```

**Biliary tract:** right and left hepatic ducts → common hepatic duct → joined by the **cystic duct** from the **gallbladder** → **common bile duct** → joins the pancreatic duct → ampulla of Vater → duodenum. The gallbladder (pear-shaped, under the liver) **stores and concentrates bile** 5–10 times and contracts in response to CCK after a fatty meal.

**Bile:** about 0.5–1 L per day; yellow-green, alkaline. Contains water, **bile salts** (sodium glycocholate and taurocholate), **bile pigments** (bilirubin, biliverdin), cholesterol, phospholipids, electrolytes. **Bile salts** emulsify fat and form micelles, allowing fat and fat-soluble vitamin (A, D, E, K) absorption; about 95% are reabsorbed in the ileum and returned to the liver (**enterohepatic circulation**). Bile has no digestive enzymes.

**Functions of the liver**

| Group | Functions |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrate | Stores glucose as glycogen (glycogenesis), releases it (glycogenolysis), makes new glucose (gluconeogenesis) — keeps blood sugar steady |
| Fat | Makes cholesterol, lipoproteins and bile salts; oxidises fatty acids; forms ketone bodies |
| Protein | Makes **plasma proteins** (albumin, globulins, fibrinogen, prothrombin and other clotting factors); deamination of amino acids; converts ammonia to **urea** |
| Storage | Glycogen, vitamins A, D, B12, iron (ferritin), copper |
| **Detoxification and drug metabolism** | Phase I (oxidation by cytochrome P450, reduction, hydrolysis) and phase II (conjugation with glucuronic acid, sulphate, glycine) reactions inactivate drugs, alcohol and toxins; **first-pass metabolism** of oral drugs |
| Excretion | Bilirubin, cholesterol, hormones and drugs into bile |
| Defence | **Kupffer cells** phagocytose bacteria and old blood cells |
| Haemopoiesis | Forms blood cells in the fetus |
| Heat production | A large share of resting body heat |
| Vitamin activation | First hydroxylation of vitamin D (to 25-hydroxy D) |

## 2.14 Movements of the GI tract

| Movement | Where | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mastication** | Mouth | Chewing breaks food and mixes it with saliva |
| **Deglutition (swallowing)** | Mouth → stomach | **Oral (voluntary)** stage: tongue pushes bolus into pharynx; **pharyngeal (involuntary)** stage: soft palate closes nasopharynx, larynx rises, epiglottis covers it, breathing stops briefly (swallowing centre in medulla); **oesophageal** stage: peristalsis carries bolus to the stomach in about 5–10 s |
| **Peristalsis** | Whole tract | Wave of contraction behind and relaxation ahead of the contents (law of the intestine), moving them forward; controlled by the myenteric plexus |
| **Receptive relaxation and churning** | Stomach | Stomach relaxes to receive food; mixing waves every 20 s or so; **gastric emptying** through the pylorus |
| **Segmentation** | Small intestine | Rings of contraction chop and mix chyme with juices and expose it to the mucosa, with little forward movement |
| **Migrating motor complex** | Stomach and small intestine, fasting | Waves every 90 minutes or so that sweep residue onward ("housekeeper"); linked to motilin |
| **Haustral churning** | Colon | Slow mixing to aid water absorption |
| **Mass movement** | Colon | Strong peristalsis a few times a day, often after a meal (**gastrocolic reflex**), pushing faeces into the rectum |
| **Defecation** | Rectum, anus | Rectal distension → urge; spinal reflex (S2–S4, parasympathetic) contracts rectum and relaxes the internal sphincter; voluntary relaxation of the external sphincter, aided by straining |
| **Vomiting** | Reverse | Reflex controlled by the vomiting centre in the medulla and the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) — the target of antiemetics |

## 2.15 Digestion and absorption of nutrients

**Carbohydrates**

```text
 Starch --(salivary amylase, pancreatic amylase)--> maltose, maltotriose, dextrins
 Brush border: maltase / isomaltase -> glucose; sucrase -> glucose + fructose;
               lactase -> glucose + galactose
 Absorption (mainly duodenum, jejunum):
   glucose, galactose : Na+-glucose co-transporter SGLT1 (secondary active)
   fructose           : GLUT5 (facilitated diffusion)
   all exit to blood  : GLUT2 --> portal vein --> liver
```

**Proteins**

```text
 Protein --(pepsin, stomach)--> polypeptides
         --(trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, carboxypeptidase)--> small peptides
         --(brush-border aminopeptidases, dipeptidases)--> amino acids,
           di- and tripeptides
 Absorption: amino acids by Na+-linked carriers; di/tripeptides by H+-linked
             PepT1; split inside the cell; amino acids --> portal blood
```

**Fats**

```text
 Large fat globules --(bile salts: EMULSIFICATION)--> small droplets
   --(pancreatic lipase + colipase)--> monoglycerides + free fatty acids
   --(bile salts)--> MICELLES carry them to the brush border
   --> diffuse into enterocyte --> re-formed into triglycerides
   --> packed with cholesterol, phospholipid, protein as CHYLOMICRONS
   --> LACTEALS (lymph) --> thoracic duct --> blood
 Short- and medium-chain fatty acids go straight to portal blood.
```

**Water, vitamins and minerals**

- **Water:** about 8–9 L enters the gut daily (food, drink, secretions); about 80% is absorbed in the small intestine by osmosis following solutes, most of the rest in the colon.
- **Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)** go with fat in micelles — impaired when bile is lacking.
- **Water-soluble vitamins** by diffusion or carriers; **vitamin B12** binds intrinsic factor and is absorbed in the **terminal ileum**.
- **Iron** (as Fe2+) and **calcium** (vitamin D-dependent) mainly in the **duodenum**.
- **Sodium** by several carriers; it drives glucose and amino acid absorption — the basis of **oral rehydration salts (ORS)**, which work even in cholera.

| Disorder | Key points |
| --- | --- |
| **Peptic ulcer** | Ulcer in the stomach or duodenum where acid–pepsin overcome mucosal defence; main causes ***Helicobacter pylori*** and **NSAIDs**; treated with PPIs, H2 blockers and *H. pylori* eradication |
| GERD | Acid reflux through a weak lower oesophageal sphincter; heartburn |
| Gastritis | Inflammation of gastric mucosa (alcohol, NSAIDs, *H. pylori*) |

| Inflammatory bowel disease | Ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease |
| Appendicitis | Inflammation of the appendix; pain moving to the right iliac fossa |
| Haemorrhoids (piles) | Dilated veins of the anal canal |

| Pancreatitis | Autodigestion of the pancreas; gallstones and alcohol are common causes |

## 2.17 Energetics: formation and role of ATP

**ATP (adenosine triphosphate)** is the energy currency of the cell. It is made of **adenine + ribose + three phosphate groups**; the last two phosphates are linked by **high-energy bonds**. Splitting one releases about **7.3 kcal per mole** under standard conditions.

```text
 Adenine--Ribose--P ~ P ~ P          (~ = high-energy bond)
              ATP + H2O  -->  ADP + Pi  + energy (used for work)
              ADP + Pi + energy (from food) --> ATP
```

**Formation of ATP**

1. **Substrate-level phosphorylation** — a phosphate is transferred directly from a high-energy intermediate to ADP: in **glycolysis** (cytoplasm, also without oxygen) and in the **Krebs cycle** (as GTP).
2. **Oxidative phosphorylation** — in the inner mitochondrial membrane. NADH and FADH2 from glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation and the Krebs cycle give electrons to the **electron transport chain**; the energy pumps H+ outwards, and H+ flowing back through **ATP synthase** makes ATP (**chemiosmotic theory**). Oxygen is the final electron acceptor, forming water. This gives most of the ATP.
3. (Photophosphorylation occurs only in plants.)

Complete oxidation of one glucose molecule gives about 30–32 ATP (older textbooks give 36–38); anaerobic glycolysis gives only 2 ATP and produces lactate. Cyanide blocks the electron transport chain, so cells cannot make ATP even with oxygen available.

**Roles of ATP**

- **Muscle contraction** — cross-bridge cycling and relaxation (Ca2+ pump).
- **Active transport** — Na+/K+ ATPase, proton pump, Ca2+ ATPase.
- **Biosynthesis** of proteins, glycogen, fats, nucleic acids.
- **Nerve function** — restoring ion gradients after impulses.
- **Secretion** (exocytosis) of hormones, enzymes, transmitters.
- **Cell signalling** — source of **cAMP** (second messenger) and of phosphate for protein kinases.
- **Heat production** to keep body temperature.

## 2.18 Creatine phosphate (phosphocreatine)

The syllabus writes "creatinine phosphate"; the energy store is in fact **creatine phosphate** — creatinine is its waste product.

- Creatine is made from amino acids (arginine, glycine, methionine) in the kidney and liver and taken up by muscle and brain.
- In resting muscle, spare ATP phosphorylates creatine; **creatine phosphate** stores the energy. Resting muscle holds several times more creatine phosphate than ATP.
- In the first seconds of intense exercise, **creatine kinase (CK)** transfers phosphate back to ADP (**Lohmann reaction**):

```text
                       creatine kinase
 Creatine phosphate + ADP  <=========>  Creatine + ATP
     (rest: right to left, store energy; exercise: left to right, release it)
```

- It is the **fastest** way to regenerate ATP, supporting maximal effort for about 5–10 seconds (sprinting, weight-lifting), before glycolysis and oxidative pathways take over.
- Creatine phosphate breaks down non-enzymatically at a steady rate to **creatinine**, which the kidneys excrete. Daily creatinine output depends on muscle mass and is fairly constant, so **serum creatinine** and **creatinine clearance** are used to judge kidney function (and to adjust doses of renally cleared drugs).
- **CK-MB** in blood is an older marker of heart muscle damage (myocardial infarction).

## 2.19 Basal metabolic rate (BMR)

**Definition:** the rate of energy use of a person at complete physical and mental rest, in a comfortable environment, at least 12 hours after the last meal — the minimum energy needed to keep vital functions going (heartbeat, breathing, kidney work, ion pumping, body temperature).

**Basal conditions for measurement:** overnight fast (12–14 h); measured in the morning after a good sleep; lying down awake and relaxed; no exercise beforehand; comfortable room temperature (about 20–25 °C); no fever, no stimulant drugs (caffeine, nicotine).

**Normal value:** about **40 kcal per square metre of body surface per hour** in adult men and about **37 kcal/m²/h** in women; roughly 1,500–1,800 kcal a day for an average adult. BMR is expressed per body surface area because heat loss depends on surface.

**Measurement:** usually indirect calorimetry — oxygen consumed in a measured time is found with a **Benedict–Roth apparatus** (a closed spirometer) and multiplied by the energy equivalent of oxygen (about **4.825 kcal per litre** of O2 on a mixed diet). Direct calorimetry measures heat output in an insulated chamber; it is accurate but impractical. The **respiratory quotient (RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 consumed)** is 1.0 for carbohydrate, about 0.7 for fat, about 0.8 for protein and about 0.82 on a mixed diet.

**Factors affecting BMR**

| Raises BMR | Lowers BMR |
| --- | --- |
| Larger body surface area; male sex (more muscle) | Small body size; female sex |
| Childhood and growth | Old age |
| **Thyroid hormones (hyperthyroidism)** — the most important hormonal factor | **Hypothyroidism** |
| Adrenaline, sympathetic activity, stress | Sleep (about 10% lower) |
| **Fever** — about 13% per 1 °C rise in body temperature | Starvation and undernutrition |
| Pregnancy, lactation | |
| Cold climate | Hot climate (slightly) |
| Muscular build, athletes | Obesity with little muscle |
| Drugs: caffeine, nicotine, amphetamines | Anaesthesia, sedatives |
| Recent food intake (specific dynamic action, highest for protein) | |

**Applied:** BMR was once used to diagnose thyroid disease (now done by blood TSH, T3, T4); it is the base for calculating daily calorie needs in diet planning and nutrition support.

## Remember

- Wall layers: mucosa, submucosa (Meissner's plexus), muscularis externa (Auerbach's plexus), serosa.
- Dental formula: temporary 2-1-0-2 = 20 teeth; permanent 2-1-2-3 = 32 teeth.
- Parotid → Stensen's duct (serous); submandibular → Wharton's duct (most resting saliva); sublingual (mucous).
- Parietal cells: HCl + intrinsic factor; chief cells: pepsinogen; G cells: gastrin; ECL: histamine.
- Acid: carbonic anhydrase → H+ pumped by H+/K+ ATPase in exchange for K+; HCO3− to blood = alkaline tide.
- Vagus (ACh, M3), gastrin and histamine (H2) stimulate acid; somatostatin and secretin inhibit it.
- Phases of gastric secretion: cephalic 30%, gastric 60%, intestinal 10%.
- Pepsinogen → pepsin by HCl and autocatalysis; optimum pH about 2; endopeptidase.
- Small intestine: duodenum 25 cm, jejunum about 2.5 m, ileum about 3.5 m; villi with lacteals; B12 and bile salts absorbed in ileum.
- Trypsinogen activated by enterokinase; secretin → bicarbonate; CCK → enzymes and gallbladder contraction.
- Liver lobule: central vein, sinusoids, Kupffer cells, portal triads; bile salts emulsify fat.
- Glucose via SGLT1 (with Na+), fructose via GLUT5; fats as chylomicrons into lacteals.
- ATP: adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates; mostly from oxidative phosphorylation; about 7.3 kcal/mol per bond.
- Creatine phosphate + ADP ⇌ creatine + ATP (creatine kinase); creatinine is the waste product.
- BMR about 40 kcal/m²/h (men); measured by Benedict–Roth apparatus; raised by thyroid hormones and fever.
