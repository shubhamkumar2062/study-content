# How to use this guide

Human Anatomy and Physiology II continues where Part I (BP101T) stopped. Part I covered cells, tissues, skin, bones, blood, the peripheral nervous system, special senses and the heart; this part covers the **brain and spinal cord, digestion, breathing, the kidneys, the endocrine glands, reproduction and basic genetics**. Like Part I it is a "draw and explain" subject: a neat labelled diagram, a clear flow chart of a mechanism and correct normal values earn most of the marks.

Study one unit at a time: read the notes, redraw every text diagram from memory on rough paper, learn the tables of normal values, attempt the question bank before reading the model answers, then finish with the unit's MCQs.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly; none has been split or joined. Units 3 and 5 each hold two syllabus topics (respiratory + urinary, reproductive + genetics), so their notes are divided into Part A and Part B. Where a syllabus topic overlaps Part I (the neuron, the autonomic control of organs), the notes give only what this syllabus adds and point back to BP101T. The syllabus writes "creatinine phosphate" under energetics; the notes explain that the energy store is creatine phosphate and creatinine is its waste product. The 16 practical guides follow the BP207P practical list.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it (some use 10 two-mark questions in Section A, or 5 of 7 seven-mark questions in Section C).

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

The question bank has long essays (10 marks), short answers (5 marks) and very short answers (2 marks, useful for class tests, sessionals and two-mark Section A papers).

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with a diagram and normal values.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Put a diagram in every long answer, even if the question does not ask for one: neuron, action potential graph, synapse, sagittal section of brain, cross-section of spinal cord, reflex arc, stomach, villus, liver lobule, respiratory tract, spirogram, O2 dissociation curve, kidney section, nephron, pituitary with portal system, thyroid follicle, adrenal zones, testis and seminiferous tubule, female reproductive organs, menstrual cycle chart, sperm, DNA double helix. Label at least six parts with straight lines.
2. Begin with a one-line definition, then a flow chart for any mechanism: HCl secretion, regulation of breathing, urine formation, RAS, cAMP pathway, thyroid hormone synthesis, menstrual cycle, spermatogenesis, transcription and translation, micturition reflex, Ferguson reflex.
3. Quote standard values: RMP −70 mV, threshold −55 mV; CSF 150 mL (500 mL/day); TV 500 mL, VC 4600 mL, TLC 5800 mL; GFR 125 mL/min (180 L/day); urine 1–1.5 L/day; renal threshold for glucose 180 mg/dL; fasting glucose 70–100 mg/dL; plasma calcium 9–11 mg/dL; 46 chromosomes; menstrual cycle 28 days with ovulation about day 14.
4. Use comparison tables for "differentiate" questions: UMN vs LMN lesion, spermatogenesis vs oogenesis, DNA vs RNA, type 1 vs type 2 diabetes, Cushing's vs Addison's, cortical vs juxtamedullary nephron, obstructive vs restrictive lung disease, myxoedema vs Graves' disease.
5. End with a pharmacy link — the drug that acts on that step: PPIs on the proton pump, H2 blockers on histamine, local anaesthetics on Na+ channels, benzodiazepines on GABA, furosemide on the loop of Henle, ACE inhibitors on RAS, carbimazole on thyroid peroxidase, sulphonylureas on the β-cell K+ channel, oral contraceptives on the LH surge, antibiotics on bacterial ribosomes. Examiners in a pharmacy course reward this.
6. Learn the "lists of names" that MCQs love: Brodmann areas (4, 3-1-2, 17, 41-42, 44-45, 22), the four ventricles and their connecting foramina, three salivary ducts (Stensen's, Wharton's, Rivinus), gastric cell types, the three zones of the adrenal cortex, the four islet cell types, stop and start codons.
7. For disorders, write a two-column table (cause → key features) rather than long paragraphs; it is quicker to write and easier to mark.
