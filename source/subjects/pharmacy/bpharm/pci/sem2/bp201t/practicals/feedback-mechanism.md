# Demonstration of positive and negative feedback mechanisms

**Aim:** To demonstrate negative feedback control of blood pressure and heart rate on changing posture, and to explain positive feedback with examples.

## Principle

**Homeostasis** keeps the internal environment steady through feedback loops of receptor → control centre → effector.

- In **negative feedback** the response opposes the original change, restoring the set point. Example: on standing up, blood pools in the legs and BP falls; **baroreceptors** in the carotid sinus and aortic arch fire less; the cardiovascular centres in the medulla increase sympathetic activity, so heart rate and vessel tone rise and BP returns to normal within seconds (baroreceptor reflex). Other examples: blood glucose and insulin, body temperature, thyroid hormone (TRH–TSH–T4).
- In **positive feedback** the response increases the original change until an outside event stops it. Examples: oxytocin and uterine contractions in labour (Ferguson reflex), the LH surge before ovulation, blood clotting, and the opening of Na+ channels in an action potential.

## Requirements

Sphygmomanometer and stethoscope (or a digital BP monitor), stopwatch, couch; charts of feedback loops (labour, clotting, LH surge).

## Procedure

**A. Negative feedback — posture and the baroreceptor reflex**

1. Let the subject lie on the couch for 5 minutes.
2. Record pulse rate and BP in the lying position.
3. Ask the subject to stand up quickly. Record pulse and BP **immediately**, then at 1, 2 and 3 minutes.
4. Note the immediate change and the return towards the resting values.

**B. Positive feedback — study from charts**

1. Draw the loop of the Ferguson reflex: baby's head stretches the cervix → oxytocin from posterior pituitary → stronger contractions → more stretch → more oxytocin, ending with delivery.
2. Draw the loops for blood clotting (thrombin activates more clotting factors) and the LH surge.

## Observations

| Position / time | Pulse (/min) | Systolic BP (mmHg) | Diastolic BP (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lying (rest) | | | |
| Standing — immediately | | | |
| Standing — 1 min | | | |
| Standing — 2 min | | | |
| Standing — 3 min | | | |

**Expected:** on standing, the systolic BP may fall slightly for a moment and the pulse rises by about 10–20 beats/min; within 1–2 minutes BP is restored (diastolic often slightly higher than lying) — the change is corrected, showing **negative feedback**. A fall of systolic BP of 20 mmHg or more that persists, with dizziness, is **orthostatic (postural) hypotension**, seen with some antihypertensive drugs (α-blockers), dehydration and autonomic neuropathy.

## Result

On changing posture from lying to standing, the heart rate rose by ______ /min and the BP returned to ______ mmHg within ______ minutes, demonstrating negative feedback. Positive feedback was explained with the examples of labour, clotting and the LH surge.

## Precautions

1. Stand close to the subject when they stand up, in case of dizziness.
2. Keep the cuff on and the arm at heart level for all readings.
3. Record readings quickly and at fixed times.
4. Do not do this test on anyone with a history of fainting without supervision.

## Viva questions

1. **Define homeostasis.** Maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite outside changes.
2. **Which type of feedback is more common in the body?** Negative feedback.
3. **Where are baroreceptors located?** In the carotid sinus and aortic arch.
4. **Give two examples of positive feedback.** Oxytocin in labour; blood clotting (also the LH surge).
5. **Why is positive feedback not used for routine control?** It amplifies change and would drive a value further from normal; it is used only for events that must be completed quickly.
6. **What is orthostatic hypotension?** A sustained fall in BP on standing, causing dizziness.
