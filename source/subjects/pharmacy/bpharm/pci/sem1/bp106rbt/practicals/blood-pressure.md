# Determination of blood pressure

**Aim:** To measure the arterial blood pressure of a person using a sphygmomanometer by the auscultatory method.

> Follow your lab manual and your teacher. Many laboratories now use aneroid or digital instruments; the principle is the same.

## Principle

**Blood pressure (BP)** is the pressure exerted by blood on the walls of the arteries. It is highest during ventricular contraction (**systolic pressure**) and lowest during relaxation (**diastolic pressure**), written as systolic/diastolic in **mmHg**, for example 120/80 mmHg.

A **sphygmomanometer** has an inflatable cuff, a rubber bulb with a valve, and a manometer (mercury column, aneroid dial or digital sensor). The cuff squeezes the **brachial artery** of the upper arm. When the cuff pressure is above systolic, the artery is closed and no sound is heard. As the pressure is released, blood begins to spurt through the partly opened artery, producing tapping sounds heard with a stethoscope, the **Korotkoff sounds**:

- The pressure at the **first clear tapping sound** = **systolic pressure**.
- The pressure at which the sounds **disappear** = **diastolic pressure**.

```text
 cuff pressure
   ^
 140|  silence (artery closed)
 120|  ---- first tapping sound  = SYSTOLIC
    |  sounds continue as pressure falls
  80|  ---- sounds disappear     = DIASTOLIC
    |  silence (blood flows freely)
    +-------------------------------> time (pressure released slowly)
```

**Derived values**

- **Pulse pressure** = systolic − diastolic (normally about 40 mmHg).
- **Mean arterial pressure (MAP)** ≈ diastolic + ⅓ × pulse pressure.

## Requirements

Sphygmomanometer with a cuff of correct size, stethoscope, chair and table.

## Procedure

1. Let the subject sit quietly for about 5 minutes, back supported, feet on the floor, arm resting on a table at the level of the heart. No smoking, coffee or exercise just before the test.
2. Wrap the deflated cuff snugly round the bare upper arm, its lower edge about 2–3 cm above the elbow crease, with the centre of the bladder over the brachial artery.
3. **Palpatory estimate:** feel the radial pulse at the wrist, inflate the cuff until the pulse disappears, and note this pressure; deflate fully. This avoids missing the first sound.
4. Place the stethoscope diaphragm over the brachial artery at the inner side of the elbow (below the cuff, not under it).
5. Inflate the cuff to about 20–30 mmHg above the palpatory value.
6. Open the valve and release pressure slowly, about 2–3 mmHg per second.
7. Note the pressure at the first clear tapping sound (systolic) and when the sounds disappear (diastolic).
8. Deflate completely. Wait 1–2 minutes and repeat; take the average of two readings.

**Worked example.** Systolic 120 mmHg, diastolic 80 mmHg → pulse pressure = 120 − 80 = **40 mmHg**; MAP ≈ 80 + 40/3 ≈ **93 mmHg**.

**Reference values for adults:** normal about 120/80 mmHg; persistently 140/90 mmHg or above suggests hypertension (diagnosis is made by a doctor on repeated readings).

## Precautions

1. The subject must be relaxed and seated; posture and activity change BP.
2. Keep the arm at heart level.
3. Use the correct cuff size; a small cuff gives falsely high readings.
4. Release pressure slowly and steadily.
5. Do not keep the cuff inflated for long; it is uncomfortable and changes readings.
6. Keep a mercury instrument upright and read at eye level.

## Viva questions

1. **Define systolic and diastolic pressure.** Pressure in arteries during ventricular contraction and relaxation respectively.
2. **What are Korotkoff sounds?** Tapping sounds of blood spurting through a partly compressed artery.
3. **Which artery is used?** The brachial artery.
4. **What is the normal pulse pressure?** About 40 mmHg.
5. **Why is the palpatory method done first?** To avoid missing the true systolic pressure because of a silent gap.
6. **Name two factors that raise BP.** Exercise, stress or anxiety (also smoking, salt intake).
