# Unit 4: Plants and mineral nutrition, and photosynthesis

Plants are the source of many drugs (alkaloids, glycosides, volatile oils), and the amount of drug a medicinal plant makes depends on how well it is nourished. This unit explains which mineral elements a plant needs, how it gets nitrogen, and how it makes its own food from light, water and carbon dioxide.

Some words first: **nutrition** is the process of taking in and using food; a **nutrient** is a substance needed for growth and life; an **element** is a basic chemical substance (carbon, nitrogen, iron); **mineral elements** are those a plant takes from the soil as ions (charged particles), as opposed to carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, which come from air and water.

## 4.1 How we know what a plant needs: hydroponics

In 1860 **Julius von Sachs** showed that plants can be grown to maturity in a nutrient solution without soil. Growing plants in a solution of known composition is called **hydroponics**. By leaving out one element at a time and watching whether the plant grows normally, scientists found which elements are essential. Hydroponics is also used commercially to grow tomatoes, seedless cucumbers and lettuce.

```text
   aerating tube (air bubbled in)
        |
   funnel for adding nutrient solution
        |
   [ plant held in cork ]
   [ ~~~ nutrient solution with known mineral salts ~~~ ]
   (roots kept in the dark and aerated)
```

## 4.2 Essential minerals

More than 60 elements are found in plants, but not all are needed. An element is **essential** only if it meets all three **criteria of essentiality**:

1. It must be **absolutely necessary** for normal growth and reproduction; without it the plant cannot complete its life cycle or set seed.
2. The requirement must be **specific**: no other element can replace it.
3. It must be **directly involved** in the plant's metabolism.

On this basis about **17 elements** are essential: carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) from air and water, and 14 mineral elements from the soil.

### Macro- and micronutrients

| Group | Definition | Elements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Macronutrients** | Present in large amounts, more than **10 mmol per kg** of dry matter | C, H, O, **N, P, K, S, Ca, Mg** |
| **Micronutrients (trace elements)** | Needed in very small amounts, less than 10 mmol per kg of dry matter | **Fe, Mn, Cu, Mo, Zn, B, Cl, Ni** |

A handy way to remember the micronutrients: "**Mn, Zn, Cu, Mo, B, Cl, Fe, Ni**".

Essential elements can also be grouped by **function**: as parts of body molecules (C, H, O, N); as parts of energy compounds (P in ATP, Mg in chlorophyll); as activators of enzymes (Zn, Mg, Mo); or by changing the osmotic potential of cells (K, which opens and closes stomata).

### Roles and deficiency symptoms

| Element | Absorbed as | Main roles | Deficiency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nitrogen** | NO3−, NO2−, NH4+ | Needed most; part of proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, vitamins, hormones | Yellowing (chlorosis) starting in older leaves; stunted growth |
| **Phosphorus** | H2PO4−, HPO4^2− | Part of cell membranes, nucleic acids, ATP; needed for phosphorylation reactions | Poor root growth, delayed flowering |
| **Potassium** | K+ | Keeps cation–anion balance; opens and closes stomata; activates enzymes; keeps cells turgid (firm with water) | Chlorosis and dead patches at leaf edges of older leaves |
| **Calcium** | Ca2+ | Forms calcium pectate in the middle lamella of cell walls; spindle formation in cell division | Death (necrosis) of growing tips and young leaves |
| **Magnesium** | Mg2+ | Centre of the **chlorophyll** molecule; activates enzymes of respiration and photosynthesis; holds ribosomes together | Chlorosis between veins of older leaves |
| **Sulphur** | SO4^2− | In amino acids cysteine and methionine, coenzyme A, some vitamins (thiamine, biotin), ferredoxin | Chlorosis of young leaves |
| **Iron** | Fe3+ (mainly) | In ferredoxin and cytochromes (electron carriers); needed to make chlorophyll | Chlorosis of young leaves |
| **Manganese** | Mn2+ | Splitting of water (releasing oxygen) in photosynthesis; activates enzymes | Chlorosis, grey spots |
| **Zinc** | Zn2+ | Activates enzymes (carboxylases); needed to make **auxin** (a growth hormone) | "Little leaf", rosette of small leaves |
| **Copper** | Cu2+ | Redox enzymes; overall metabolism | Necrosis of leaf tips |
| **Boron** | BO3^3−, B4O7^2− | Calcium uptake, pollen germination, cell elongation, carbohydrate movement | Death of shoot tip, poor flowering |
| **Molybdenum** | MoO2^2+ | Part of **nitrogenase** and nitrate reductase (nitrogen metabolism) | Chlorosis, poor nitrogen use |
| **Chlorine** | Cl− | Splitting of water in photosynthesis; solute balance | Wilting |
| **Nickel** | Ni2+ | Part of the enzyme urease | Urea build-up, leaf-tip damage |

### Reading deficiency symptoms

- **Chlorosis** = loss of chlorophyll, leaves turn yellow (N, K, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Mo).
- **Necrosis** = death of tissue, especially leaf tissue (Ca, Mg, Cu, K).
- **Mobile elements** (N, K, Mg) are moved from old to new leaves when short, so symptoms appear **first in older leaves**. **Less mobile elements** (S, Ca) stay locked in older tissue, so symptoms appear **first in young leaves and buds**.
- Too much of an element can also harm (**toxicity**). For example, excess manganese hinders the uptake of iron and magnesium, so its toxicity can look like their deficiency.

## 4.3 Nitrogen metabolism

Nitrogen is the element plants need most. Air is about **78% nitrogen gas (N2)**, but plants cannot use N2 directly because the two atoms are held by a very strong triple bond (N≡N). Nitrogen must first be "fixed" into ammonia or nitrate.

**Nitrogen fixation** = conversion of N2 into ammonia (NH3) or other usable compounds. It occurs by:

- **Lightning and ultraviolet radiation** — N2 + O2 → nitrogen oxides (NO, NO2, N2O), which reach the soil with rain.
- **Industrial fixation** — the Haber process makes ammonia for fertilisers.
- **Biological nitrogen fixation** — by living prokaryotes; the most important natural route.

### Nitrogen cycle

```text
              ATMOSPHERIC N2
           /                \
  biological / lightning     \  denitrification
   fixation                    \ (Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus)
        |                       \
        v                        \
   AMMONIA (NH3 / NH4+)      NITRATE (NO3-) in soil
        |   ^                    ^      |
        |   | ammonification     |      | absorbed by roots
        |   | (decay of dead     |      v
        |   |  plants, animals,  |   PLANT PROTEINS -> eaten -> ANIMAL PROTEINS
        |   |  excreta)          |         |                       |
        |   +--------------------|---------+-----------------------+
        |                        | (death, wastes)
        v                        |
  NITRITE (NO2-) ----------------+
  (Nitrosomonas,       nitrification
   Nitrococcus)        (Nitrobacter)
```

| Step | What happens | Organisms |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nitrogen fixation** | N2 → NH3 | Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria, etc. |
| **Ammonification** | Organic nitrogen of dead bodies and wastes → ammonia | Decay bacteria and fungi |
| **Nitrification** step 1 | Ammonia → nitrite (NO2−) | *Nitrosomonas*, *Nitrococcus* |
| **Nitrification** step 2 | Nitrite → nitrate (NO3−) | *Nitrobacter* |
| **Assimilation** | Plants absorb nitrate and ammonium and make amino acids and proteins | Plants |
| **Denitrification** | Nitrate → N2 gas returned to air | *Pseudomonas*, *Thiobacillus* |

Nitrifying bacteria are **chemoautotrophs**: they get energy from oxidising these inorganic compounds.

### Biological nitrogen fixation

Only some prokaryotes can fix nitrogen, because only they have the enzyme **nitrogenase**.

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Free-living, aerobic | *Azotobacter*, *Beijerinckia* |
| Free-living, anaerobic | *Clostridium*; *Rhodospirillum* (photosynthetic) |
| Cyanobacteria (free-living or symbiotic) | *Anabaena*, *Nostoc* (important in rice fields; *Anabaena* lives in the water fern *Azolla*) |
| **Symbiotic** (living in partnership with a plant) | ***Rhizobium*** in root nodules of **legumes** (pea, gram, groundnut — family Fabaceae); *Frankia* in root nodules of the non-legume *Alnus* |

**Overall reaction (needs a lot of energy):**

```text
 N2 + 8 e- + 8 H+ + 16 ATP  ---nitrogenase--->  2 NH3 + H2 + 16 ADP + 16 Pi
```

### Root nodule formation in legumes

```text
 1. Root releases chemicals; Rhizobium multiplies near the root and
    attaches to root hairs
 2. Root hair curls around the bacteria
 3. Bacteria enter through an INFECTION THREAD that grows into the
    root cortex
 4. Bacteria are released into cortical cells; these cells divide,
    forming a swelling = ROOT NODULE
 5. Bacteria change into nitrogen-fixing forms (bacteroids); the nodule
    gets vascular links with the root for exchange of food and nitrogen
```

- Nitrogenase is **destroyed by oxygen**, so the nodule keeps its inside low in oxygen. It does this with **leghaemoglobin**, a red-pink oxygen-binding pigment (similar to haemoglobin) that gives active nodules their pink colour.
- The plant gives the bacteria sugars and shelter; the bacteria give the plant fixed nitrogen. This is why legumes enrich the soil and are used in **crop rotation**.

### Fate of ammonia in the plant

At the cell's pH, ammonia exists as **ammonium ions (NH4+)**, which are toxic in excess, so they are quickly built into amino acids:

1. **Reductive amination** — ammonia reacts with α-ketoglutaric acid to form the amino acid **glutamic acid**.
2. **Transamination** — the amino group (−NH2) of glutamic acid is transferred to the keto group of other keto acids, making other amino acids. The enzymes are **transaminases**.
3. **Amides** — asparagine and glutamine are formed from amino acids by adding another amino group. They carry more nitrogen and are moved to other parts of the plant in the xylem. In soybean, fixed nitrogen is moved as ureides.

## 4.4 Photosynthesis

### Autotrophic nutrition

**Autotrophs** ("self-feeders") make their own food from inorganic materials. Most use light energy (**photoautotrophs**: green plants, algae, cyanobacteria); a few use chemical energy (**chemoautotrophs**: nitrifying bacteria). **Heterotrophs** depend on autotrophs directly or indirectly. So almost all life on earth depends on photosynthesis for food and for oxygen.

**Photosynthesis** is the process by which green plants use **light energy** absorbed by **chlorophyll** to make **carbohydrates** (glucose) from **carbon dioxide** and **water**, releasing **oxygen**.

```text
                         light energy, chlorophyll
 6 CO2  +  12 H2O  ------------------------------->  C6H12O6  +  6 O2  +  6 H2O
                                                    (glucose)
```

The oxygen released comes from **water**, not from CO2 (shown by van Niel and later with isotopes).

**Site.** Photosynthesis occurs mainly in the **mesophyll** cells of leaves, inside **chloroplasts** (green organelles).

```text
  CHLOROPLAST
  ____________________________________________
 | double membrane                            |
 |   [=][=]  [=][=]   <- GRANA (stacks of     |
 |   [=][=]  [=][=]      thylakoids):         |
 |      \____/           LIGHT REACTION       |
 |   stroma lamellae                          |
 |   ~~~ STROMA (fluid with enzymes,          |
 |       DNA, ribosomes): DARK REACTION ~~~   |
 |____________________________________________|
```

### Photosynthetic pigments

**Pigments** are substances that absorb light of particular wavelengths. They can be separated by **paper chromatography**.

| Pigment | Colour | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chlorophyll a** | Bright or blue-green | **Chief pigment**; forms the reaction centres of the photosystems |
| Chlorophyll b | Yellow-green | Accessory pigment |
| Xanthophylls | Yellow | Accessory pigment |
| Carotenoids (carotenes) | Yellow to yellow-orange | Accessory pigments; also protect chlorophyll from damage by strong light (photo-oxidation) |

- Chlorophyll absorbs mostly **blue and red light**; green light is reflected, which is why leaves look green.
- **Accessory pigments** absorb other wavelengths and pass the energy on to chlorophyll a, widening the range of light that can be used.
- An **absorption spectrum** shows how much light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength; an **action spectrum** shows the rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength. They match closely, proving chlorophyll does the work.

### Stages of photosynthesis

| | Light reaction (photochemical phase) | Dark reaction (biosynthetic phase, Calvin cycle) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Thylakoid membranes of grana | Stroma |
| Needs light directly | Yes | No (but depends on products of the light reaction) |
| Inputs | Light, water, ADP, NADP+ | CO2, ATP, NADPH |
| Outputs | **ATP, NADPH**, O2 | **Sugar** (glucose), ADP, NADP+ |

**Light reaction**

1. **Light absorption** by pigments grouped into two **photosystems**: **PS I** (reaction centre chlorophyll a absorbing at **700 nm**, called **P700**) and **PS II** (reaction centre **P680**).
2. **Splitting of water (photolysis)** at PS II: 2H2O → 4H+ + O2 + 4e−. This gives electrons to PS II and releases oxygen. Manganese and chlorine help here.
3. **Electron transport:** excited electrons pass from PS II along carriers (an electron transport chain) to PS I and then to **NADP+**, reducing it to **NADPH**. Drawn on an energy scale, the path looks like the letter Z, so it is called the **Z-scheme**.
4. **Photophosphorylation** — making ATP using light energy:
   - **Non-cyclic**: both photosystems, electrons do not return; produces **ATP + NADPH + O2**.
   - **Cyclic**: only PS I; electrons return to PS I; produces **ATP only** (no NADPH, no O2); occurs in stroma lamellae.
5. ATP is made by **chemiosmosis**: H+ ions build up inside the thylakoid and flow back out through the enzyme **ATP synthase**, which uses this flow to make ATP.

**Dark reaction — Calvin cycle (C3 pathway)**

Worked out by **Melvin Calvin** using radioactive carbon (14C). The first stable product is a **3-carbon** compound, **3-phosphoglyceric acid (PGA)**, so plants using only this route are called **C3 plants** (wheat, rice, most plants).

```text
 1. CARBOXYLATION: CO2 + RuBP (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate, 5 carbon)
       --RuBisCO--> 2 molecules of 3-PGA
 2. REDUCTION: 3-PGA --(ATP, NADPH)--> glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (a sugar);
       some leaves the cycle to make glucose and starch
 3. REGENERATION: the rest is used to remake RuBP (uses ATP), so the cycle
       continues
```

- **RuBisCO** (RuBP carboxylase–oxygenase) is the **most abundant enzyme on earth**.
- To make **one glucose** molecule, 6 turns of the cycle are needed, using **18 ATP and 12 NADPH**.

**C4 pathway (Hatch and Slack pathway)** — in plants of hot, dry tropical regions such as **maize, sugarcane and sorghum** (Poaceae):

- The first CO2 acceptor is **PEP (phosphoenolpyruvate)**, and the first stable product is a **4-carbon** compound, **oxaloacetic acid (OAA)**, made by **PEP carboxylase** in mesophyll cells.
- The 4-carbon acid moves to **bundle sheath cells** around the veins, releases CO2 there, and the Calvin cycle runs in those cells.
- C4 leaves show **Kranz anatomy** ("wreath"): large bundle sheath cells in a ring around each vein.
- C4 plants avoid **photorespiration** (a wasteful process in C3 plants in which RuBisCO binds O2 instead of CO2, losing fixed carbon), so they make more sugar in heat and strong light.

### Factors affecting photosynthesis

**Blackman's law of limiting factors (1905):** if a chemical process depends on several factors, its rate is controlled by the factor that is nearest to its minimum value; changing that factor changes the rate.

```text
 rate of photosynthesis
   |            ________  (limited by CO2 here)
   |          /
   |        /
   |      /   (limited by light here)
   |    /
   |__/_____________________ light intensity
```

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Light** | Rate rises with light intensity at low levels; at high intensity other factors limit it; very strong light can damage chlorophyll. Quality (wavelength) and duration also matter |
| **Carbon dioxide** | Major limiting factor because air has only about 0.03–0.04% CO2; raising it increases the rate (used in greenhouses for tomatoes and bell peppers); C4 plants saturate at lower CO2 levels than C3 plants |
| **Temperature** | Dark reactions are enzyme-controlled, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum and then falls; C4 plants have higher optima than C3 plants |
| **Water** | Shortage closes stomata (less CO2 enters) and makes leaves wilt; affects the plant more through these effects than as a direct raw material |
| Internal factors | Number, size and age of leaves, amount of chlorophyll, leaf orientation, CO2 concentration inside the leaf |

## Remember

- Hydroponics (von Sachs, 1860) identifies essential elements.
- Criteria: absolutely necessary, specific (not replaceable), directly involved in metabolism.
- Macronutrients (>10 mmol/kg dry matter): C, H, O, N, P, K, S, Ca, Mg. Micronutrients: Fe, Mn, Cu, Mo, Zn, B, Cl, Ni.
- Mg is in chlorophyll; Mo in nitrogenase; Zn for auxin; Mn and Cl for splitting water; K for stomata.
- Mobile elements (N, K, Mg): symptoms in older leaves; immobile (S, Ca): in young leaves.
- Nitrification: NH3 → NO2− (*Nitrosomonas*) → NO3− (*Nitrobacter*); denitrification: *Pseudomonas*, *Thiobacillus*.
- Nitrogenase needs anaerobic conditions; leghaemoglobin protects it in nodules; N2 fixation costs 16 ATP per N2.
- Ammonia → glutamic acid (reductive amination) → other amino acids (transamination); amides asparagine and glutamine.
- 6CO2 + 12H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O; O2 comes from water.
- Chlorophyll a is the chief pigment; absorbs blue and red; PS I = P700, PS II = P680.
- Light reaction (grana) gives ATP, NADPH, O2; Calvin cycle (stroma) uses 18 ATP + 12 NADPH per glucose; RuBisCO fixes CO2 into 3-PGA.
- C4 plants (maize, sugarcane): PEP carboxylase, OAA, Kranz anatomy, no photorespiration.
- Blackman: the factor in shortest supply limits the rate.
