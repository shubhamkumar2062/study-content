# Unit 2: Body fluids and circulation, digestion and absorption, breathing and respiration

This unit covers three systems of the human body that keep every cell supplied: blood and the heart carry materials, the digestive system brings in food, and the respiratory system brings in oxygen and removes carbon dioxide. Many common medicines act here (blood thinners, heart drugs, antacids, bronchodilators), so these basics come back in Human Anatomy and Physiology and in Pharmacology.

A few words used throughout: **physiology** is the study of how body parts work; an **organ** is a structure made of several tissues doing one job (heart, stomach); an **organ system** is a group of organs working together (the digestive system).

## 2.1 Body fluids

Most cells are bathed in fluid. The two circulating fluids in humans are **blood** and **lymph**.

### Composition of blood

**Blood** is a fluid connective tissue: a liquid matrix (**plasma**) with cells and cell fragments (**formed elements**) floating in it. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.

```text
                 BLOOD (about 5 L)
        ______________|______________
       |                             |
   PLASMA (about 55%)         FORMED ELEMENTS (about 45%)
   water 90-92%               red blood cells (erythrocytes)
   proteins 6-8%              white blood cells (leucocytes)
   salts, glucose, amino      platelets (thrombocytes)
   acids, lipids, hormones,
   urea, dissolved gases
```

**Plasma proteins**

| Protein | Function |
| --- | --- |
| **Albumin** | Keeps water inside blood vessels (osmotic balance); carries many drugs |
| **Globulins** | Defence (antibodies are gamma-globulins); transport |
| **Fibrinogen** | Blood clotting |

Plasma without the clotting factors is called **serum**.

**Formed elements**

| Element | Normal count (per mm³ of blood) | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Red blood cells (RBC)** | About 5–5.5 million (healthy adult man) | Biconcave discs; **no nucleus** in mammals; contain the red, iron-containing pigment **haemoglobin** (about 12–16 g per 100 ml of blood); made in red bone marrow; live about **120 days**; destroyed in the spleen ("graveyard of RBCs") | Carry oxygen and some carbon dioxide |
| **White blood cells (WBC)** | About 6,000–8,000 | Have a nucleus; colourless; shorter life | Defence against infection |
| **Platelets** | About 1.5–3.5 lakh | Cell fragments from megakaryocytes of bone marrow | Blood clotting |

**Types of WBC**

| Type | Approx. % of WBC | Main role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Neutrophils (granulocyte) | 60–65 | Engulf and destroy bacteria (phagocytosis) |
| Lymphocytes (agranulocyte) | 20–25 | Immunity; B cells make antibodies, T cells attack infected cells |
| Monocytes (agranulocyte) | 6–8 | Phagocytosis; become macrophages in tissues |
| Eosinophils (granulocyte) | 2–3 | Allergic reactions, worm infections |
| Basophils (granulocyte) | 0.5–1 | Release histamine, serotonin and heparin; inflammation |

### Blood groups

The surface of RBCs carries **antigens** (molecules that can trigger an immune reaction). The plasma may carry **antibodies** (proteins that react with a particular antigen). When incompatible blood is mixed, the antibodies make RBCs clump together (**agglutination**), which can be fatal in a transfusion.

**ABO system** (discovered by Karl Landsteiner)

| Blood group | Antigen on RBC | Antibody in plasma | Can donate to | Can receive from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | A | anti-B | A, AB | A, O |
| B | B | anti-A | B, AB | B, O |
| AB | A and B | none | AB only | A, B, AB, O (**universal recipient**) |
| O | none | anti-A and anti-B | A, B, AB, O (**universal donor**) | O only |

**Rh system.** The **Rh antigen** (named after the Rhesus monkey) is present in most people (**Rh-positive**). An **Rh-negative** person makes anti-Rh antibodies only after exposure to Rh-positive blood. In pregnancy, an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive baby may become sensitised during the first delivery; in a later pregnancy her antibodies can cross the placenta and destroy the baby's RBCs. This is **erythroblastosis fetalis** (haemolytic disease of the newborn). It is prevented by giving the mother anti-Rh antibodies soon after the first delivery.

### Coagulation of blood

**Coagulation (clotting)** turns liquid blood into a gel at a wound, preventing blood loss.

```text
 Injury --> platelets and damaged tissue release factors
                     |
                     v
   prothrombin activator (thrombokinase), needs Ca2+
                     |
   PROTHROMBIN (inactive) ---------> THROMBIN (active enzyme)
                                          |
   FIBRINOGEN (soluble) -----------> FIBRIN (insoluble threads)
                                          |
             fibrin network traps RBCs and platelets = CLOT
```

- **Calcium ions** are essential; this is why citrate or oxalate (which bind calcium) keep stored blood liquid.
- **Vitamin K** is needed by the liver to make prothrombin and other clotting factors.
- **Heparin** (from basophils and mast cells) prevents clotting inside vessels.
- Normal clotting time is a few minutes. **Haemophilia** is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing and bleeding does not stop easily.

### Lymph

As blood passes through capillaries, some plasma (without most proteins and RBCs) leaks into the spaces between cells; this is **tissue fluid** (interstitial fluid). Most of it returns to blood capillaries; the rest drains into thin **lymphatic vessels** and is then called **lymph**.

**Composition:** like plasma but with fewer proteins; contains many **lymphocytes**; no RBCs or platelets. After a fatty meal, lymph from the intestine looks milky because of absorbed fat.

**Functions of lymph**

1. Returns tissue fluid and leaked proteins to the blood (via the thoracic duct into a large vein).
2. Carries absorbed fats from the intestine (through the **lacteals** of the villi).
3. Defence: **lymph nodes** filter germs, and lymphocytes produce antibodies.
4. Supplies nutrients and oxygen to tissues and removes waste.

Blockage of lymph flow causes swelling of tissues (**oedema**).

## 2.2 Human circulatory system

The **circulatory system** = heart + blood vessels + blood. Humans have a **closed** system (blood always stays inside vessels) and **double circulation** (blood passes through the heart twice in one full round).

```text
       PULMONARY CIRCULATION               SYSTEMIC CIRCULATION
 right ventricle -> pulmonary artery   left ventricle -> aorta -> arteries
   -> lungs (blood takes O2,             -> body tissues (O2 delivered)
      gives CO2)                          -> veins -> venae cavae
   -> pulmonary veins -> left atrium      -> right atrium
```

Oxygen-rich (oxygenated) and oxygen-poor (deoxygenated) blood never mix in a healthy adult heart.

### Structure of the human heart

- A hollow, muscular organ, about the size of a closed fist, in the chest between the lungs, tilted slightly to the left.
- Enclosed in a double-walled sac, the **pericardium**, which contains **pericardial fluid** to reduce friction.
- Wall: outer **epicardium**, thick middle **myocardium** (cardiac muscle), inner **endocardium**.
- **Four chambers:** two thin-walled upper **atria** (receiving chambers) and two thick-walled lower **ventricles** (pumping chambers). The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps blood to the whole body.
- Partitions: **interatrial septum** and **interventricular septum**.

**Valves** (allow flow in one direction only)

| Valve | Position | Flaps (cusps) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tricuspid** | Between right atrium and right ventricle | Three |
| **Bicuspid (mitral)** | Between left atrium and left ventricle | Two |
| **Pulmonary semilunar** | At the opening of the pulmonary artery | Three half-moon cusps |
| **Aortic semilunar** | At the opening of the aorta | Three half-moon cusps |

```text
            superior vena cava        aorta
                    |                   ^
             [ RIGHT ATRIUM ]     [ LEFT ATRIUM ] <-- pulmonary veins
                    | tricuspid         | bicuspid (mitral)
                    v                   v
            [ RIGHT VENTRICLE ]   [ LEFT VENTRICLE ]
                    |                   |
          pulmonary semilunar    aortic semilunar
                    v                   v
            pulmonary artery          aorta
```

**Conducting system.** Heart muscle is **myogenic**: it beats on its own, without a nerve signal to start each beat.

```text
 SA node (sino-atrial node, wall of right atrium) = PACEMAKER, about 70-75 impulses/min
      |
      v  impulse spreads over both atria -> atria contract
 AV node (atrio-ventricular node)
      |
      v
 Bundle of His -> right and left bundle branches
      |
      v
 Purkinje fibres in ventricle walls -> ventricles contract
```

The nervous system only changes the rate: sympathetic nerves speed it up, parasympathetic (vagus) nerves slow it down.

### Blood vessels

| Feature | Arteries | Veins | Capillaries |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Carry blood | Away from heart | Towards heart | Between arteries and veins |
| Wall | Thick, elastic, muscular | Thinner, less muscle | One cell thick (endothelium only) |
| Lumen (inner space) | Narrow | Wide | Very narrow |
| Valves | Absent | Present (prevent backflow) | Absent |
| Pressure | High | Low | Falls through the bed |
| Blood | Oxygenated (except pulmonary artery) | Deoxygenated (except pulmonary veins) | Exchange of gases, nutrients, wastes |

Walls of arteries and veins have three layers: **tunica externa** (outer connective tissue), **tunica media** (smooth muscle and elastic fibres) and **tunica intima** (inner endothelium).

**Blood pressure** is the pressure of blood on artery walls. Normal adult value is about **120/80 mmHg**: 120 is **systolic** (during ventricular contraction), 80 is **diastolic** (during relaxation). Persistently 140/90 or higher indicates **hypertension**.

### Cardiac cycle

**Systole** = contraction; **diastole** = relaxation. The **cardiac cycle** is the sequence of events in one heartbeat. At about 72 beats per minute, one cycle lasts about **0.8 second**.

```text
 Joint diastole: all chambers relaxed; blood flows from veins
   into atria and on into ventricles (AV valves open, semilunar closed)
         |
 Atrial systole (about 0.1 s): atria push the last ~30% of blood
   into the ventricles
         |
 Ventricular systole (about 0.3 s): AV valves close -> first sound "LUBB";
   pressure rises, semilunar valves open, blood ejected into aorta
   and pulmonary artery
         |
 Ventricular diastole: pressure falls, semilunar valves close ->
   second sound "DUB"; AV valves reopen, cycle repeats
```

- **Heart sounds:** "lubb" (closure of tricuspid and bicuspid valves) and "dub" (closure of semilunar valves), heard with a stethoscope.
- **Stroke volume** — blood pumped by each ventricle in one beat, about **70 ml**.
- **Cardiac output** — blood pumped by each ventricle per minute = stroke volume × heart rate = 70 ml × 72 ≈ **5 litres per minute**. It rises greatly in exercise.

### Electrocardiogram (ECG)

An **ECG** is a graph of the electrical activity of the heart, recorded from electrodes on the wrists, ankle and chest by an **electrocardiograph**.

```text
          R
          |
   P      |       T
  / \     |      / \
 /   \_Q  |  S__/   \____
         \|/
     P wave  QRS complex  T wave
```

| Wave | Represents |
| --- | --- |
| **P wave** | Depolarisation (electrical excitation) of the atria → atrial contraction |
| **QRS complex** | Depolarisation of the ventricles → ventricular contraction |
| **T wave** | Repolarisation (return to resting state) of the ventricles |

Counting QRS complexes in a period gives the heart rate. Changes in the shape or timing of waves help doctors detect heart disease such as a heart attack or rhythm problems.

**Disorders (for awareness):** hypertension; coronary artery disease (fatty deposits narrow the arteries supplying heart muscle); angina (chest pain from too little oxygen to heart muscle); heart failure (heart cannot pump enough blood).

## 2.3 Digestion and absorption

**Digestion** is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble ones that can be absorbed. It is **mechanical** (chewing, churning) and **chemical** (by **enzymes**, proteins that speed up chemical reactions).

### Human alimentary canal

```text
 Mouth (buccal cavity: teeth, tongue)
   -> Pharynx (common passage for food and air)
   -> Oesophagus (food pipe, ~25 cm, moves food by peristalsis)
   -> Stomach (J-shaped; cardiac, fundic, body and pyloric parts)
   -> Small intestine: duodenum (C-shaped) -> jejunum -> ileum
   -> Large intestine: caecum (with vermiform appendix) -> colon
      (ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid) -> rectum
   -> Anus
```

- **Teeth:** humans have two sets in life (**diphyodont**), set in jaw sockets (**thecodont**), and of four kinds (**heterodont**): incisors, canines, premolars, molars. Adult **dental formula** (one half of upper jaw over lower jaw) = 2123/2123, giving **32** teeth.
- **Peristalsis** is the wave of muscle contraction that pushes food along.
- The wall of the canal has four layers: serosa (outer), muscularis, submucosa and mucosa (inner). The small intestine mucosa forms finger-like **villi**, each with blood capillaries and a lymph vessel called a **lacteal**; villi have tiny **microvilli** on their cells. These folds give a huge surface for absorption.

### Digestive glands

| Gland | Secretion | Opens into |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Salivary glands** (three pairs: parotid, submandibular, sublingual) | Saliva with salivary amylase (ptyalin) and lysozyme | Mouth |
| **Gastric glands** (in stomach wall) | **Chief (peptic) cells** — pepsinogen; **parietal (oxyntic) cells** — HCl and intrinsic factor (needed for vitamin B12 absorption); **mucus cells** — mucus | Stomach |
| **Liver** (largest gland, about 1.2–1.5 kg) | **Bile** (stored in the gall bladder); bile has no enzymes but has bile salts | Duodenum (via bile duct) |
| **Pancreas** (both exocrine and endocrine) | Pancreatic juice with enzymes; islets of Langerhans make insulin and glucagon (into blood) | Duodenum (via the hepato-pancreatic duct with bile) |
| **Intestinal glands** | Intestinal juice (succus entericus) with enzymes and mucus | Small intestine |

### Role of digestive enzymes

| Site | Enzyme | Acts on | Products |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mouth | Salivary amylase | Starch | Maltose (disaccharide); about 30% of starch digested here |
| Stomach | **Pepsin** (from pepsinogen, activated by HCl) | Proteins | Proteoses and peptones |
| Stomach (infants) | **Rennin** | Milk protein (casein) | Curdled milk (paracasein) |
| Stomach | Gastric lipase (small amount) | Fats | Fatty acids, glycerol |
| Duodenum | Pancreatic amylase | Starch | Maltose |
| Duodenum | **Trypsin** (from trypsinogen, activated by **enterokinase**), chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase | Proteins, peptones | Smaller peptides, dipeptides |
| Duodenum | Pancreatic **lipase** | Fats (after emulsification by bile) | Diglycerides, monoglycerides, fatty acids, glycerol |
| Duodenum | Nucleases | DNA, RNA | Nucleotides |
| Small intestine | Dipeptidases | Dipeptides | Amino acids |
| Small intestine | Maltase, lactase, sucrase | Maltose, lactose, sucrose | Glucose; glucose + galactose; glucose + fructose |
| Small intestine | Nucleotidases, nucleosidases | Nucleotides, nucleosides | Sugars, bases, phosphate |

**Role of HCl:** gives the acidic pH (about 1.8) needed by pepsin, converts pepsinogen to pepsin, and kills many swallowed microbes. **Role of bile:** bile salts **emulsify** fats (break them into tiny droplets) so lipase can act; bile also activates lipase. Mucus and bicarbonate protect the lining from acid.

### Digestion, absorption and assimilation

```text
 Carbohydrates -> monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose)
 Proteins      -> amino acids
 Fats          -> fatty acids + glycerol (+ monoglycerides)
 Nucleic acids -> sugars, nitrogen bases, phosphate
```

**Absorption** is the passage of digested food from the gut into blood or lymph. Most absorption takes place in the **small intestine**; the stomach absorbs only water, simple sugars, alcohol and some drugs; the large intestine absorbs water, some minerals and drugs.

| Mechanism | What is absorbed this way |
| --- | --- |
| **Simple diffusion** (high to low concentration) | Some glucose, amino acids, chloride ions |
| **Facilitated transport** (with a carrier, no energy) | Fructose, some amino acids |
| **Active transport** (against the concentration gradient, uses energy; often coupled with Na+) | Glucose, amino acids, Na+ |
| **Passive (osmotic) transport** | Water |

**Fats** are absorbed differently: bile salts carry fatty acids and monoglycerides as tiny **micelles** to the intestinal cells; inside the cells they are rebuilt into fat and coated with protein as **chylomicrons**, which pass into the **lacteals** (lymph) and later reach blood.

**Assimilation** is the use of absorbed nutrients by body cells to build tissues and to provide energy. **Egestion** is the removal of undigested waste (faeces) through the anus.

**Common disorders:** jaundice (yellow skin and eyes when bile pigments build up, often liver disease), diarrhoea, constipation, indigestion, vomiting.

## 2.4 Breathing and respiration

**Breathing** is the exchange of air between the atmosphere and the lungs (taking in oxygen-rich air, giving out CO2-rich air). **Cellular respiration** is the use of oxygen inside cells to break down food and release energy. The respiratory system serves breathing and gas exchange.

### Human respiratory system

```text
 External nostrils -> nasal cavity (air filtered, warmed, moistened)
   -> pharynx -> larynx (voice box; epiglottis closes it while swallowing)
   -> trachea (windpipe, kept open by C-shaped cartilage rings)
   -> right and left primary bronchi
   -> secondary and tertiary bronchi -> bronchioles
   -> terminal bronchioles -> alveoli (tiny thin-walled air sacs)
```

- Up to the terminal bronchioles is the **conducting part** (moves and conditions air); the **alveoli** are the **exchange part**, where gases cross into blood.
- The two **lungs** sit in the chest (thoracic) cavity, covered by a double **pleural membrane** with pleural fluid between the layers.
- The thoracic cavity is closed at the bottom by the dome-shaped muscle **diaphragm**, and its sides are formed by ribs with **intercostal muscles** between them.

### Mechanism of breathing

Air moves because of a pressure difference between the lungs and the atmosphere.

| Step | Inspiration (breathing in) — active | Expiration (breathing out) — mostly passive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diaphragm | Contracts and flattens | Relaxes, returns to dome shape |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract, lifting ribs and sternum | Relax |
| Chest volume | Increases (front–back and up–down) | Decreases |
| Pressure inside lungs | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric |
| Air flow | Into lungs | Out of lungs |

In forceful breathing, abdominal muscles and internal intercostals help push air out. A healthy adult breathes about **12–16 times per minute**.

### Exchange of gases

Gases move by **simple diffusion** from higher to lower **partial pressure** (the share of the total pressure due to one gas, written pO2 or pCO2).

| Site | pO2 (mmHg) | pCO2 (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Atmospheric air | 159 | 0.3 |
| Alveoli | 104 | 40 |
| Deoxygenated blood (arriving in lungs) | 40 | 45 |
| Oxygenated blood | 95 | 40 |
| Tissues | 40 | 45 |

So in the lungs O2 moves from alveoli into blood and CO2 from blood into alveoli; in tissues the reverse happens. CO2 is about 20–25 times more soluble than O2, so it crosses membranes much more easily. The **diffusion membrane** (alveolar wall + basement membrane + capillary wall) is less than a millimetre thick.

### Transport of gases

**Oxygen**

- About **97%** is carried bound to haemoglobin as **oxyhaemoglobin**; about 3% is dissolved in plasma.
- Each haemoglobin molecule can carry up to four O2 molecules. Binding is favoured by high pO2, low pCO2, low H+ and lower temperature (lungs); release is favoured by the opposite conditions (tissues). The S-shaped graph of % saturation against pO2 is the **oxygen dissociation curve**.
- Every 100 ml of oxygenated blood delivers about **5 ml of O2** to tissues under normal conditions.

**Carbon dioxide**

- About **20–25%** as **carbamino-haemoglobin** (bound to haemoglobin).
- About **70%** as **bicarbonate** (HCO3−). In RBCs the enzyme **carbonic anhydrase** speeds up:

```text
 CO2 + H2O  <=>  H2CO3  <=>  HCO3-  +  H+
 (in tissues: goes to the right; in lungs: goes to the left, CO2 released)
```

- About **7%** dissolved in plasma.
- Every 100 ml of deoxygenated blood delivers about **4 ml of CO2** to the alveoli.

### Regulation of respiration

Breathing continues automatically, but can be changed to suit the body's needs.

- **Respiratory rhythm centre** in the **medulla oblongata** (part of the brainstem) sets the basic rhythm.
- **Pneumotaxic centre** in the **pons** can shorten inspiration and so change the rate.
- **Chemosensitive area** near the rhythm centre responds mainly to a rise in **CO2 and H+** in blood and increases breathing to remove them.
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors** in the **aortic arch** and **carotid arteries** also sense CO2 and H+ (and low O2) and signal the centre.
- Oxygen has only a minor role in normal control; CO2 is the main driver.

### Respiratory volumes and capacities

Measured with a **spirometer** (a device that records the volume of air breathed).

| Volume | Meaning | Normal adult value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | Air breathed in or out in one normal breath | About 500 ml |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | Extra air that can be breathed in by force after a normal breath in | About 2,500–3,000 ml |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | Extra air that can be breathed out by force after a normal breath out | About 1,000–1,100 ml |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | Air left in the lungs even after forceful expiration | About 1,100–1,200 ml |

| Capacity | Made of |
| --- | --- |
| Inspiratory capacity (IC) | TV + IRV |
| Expiratory capacity (EC) | TV + ERV |
| Functional residual capacity (FRC) | ERV + RV |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | ERV + TV + IRV (maximum air breathed out after a maximum breath in) |
| Total lung capacity (TLC) | VC + RV |

**Minute volume** (pulmonary ventilation) = tidal volume × breaths per minute ≈ 500 ml × 12–16 = about 6,000–8,000 ml per minute.

**Disorders:** asthma (spasm of bronchi and bronchioles, wheezing), emphysema (alveolar walls damaged, often from smoking), occupational lung disease (from dust in industries such as stone grinding).

## Remember

- Plasma about 55%, formed elements about 45%; albumin keeps osmotic balance, globulins defend, fibrinogen clots.
- RBC about 5 million/mm³, life 120 days, no nucleus; neutrophils are the most common WBC; platelets 1.5–3.5 lakh/mm³.
- O is universal donor, AB universal recipient; Rh incompatibility causes erythroblastosis fetalis.
- Clotting: prothrombin → thrombin (needs Ca2+), fibrinogen → fibrin; vitamin K needed.
- Lymph: tissue fluid in lymph vessels; carries fat from lacteals; lymph nodes fight infection.
- Heart: 4 chambers, tricuspid right, bicuspid (mitral) left; SA node is the pacemaker; cycle 0.8 s; cardiac output ≈ 70 ml × 72 ≈ 5 L/min.
- ECG: P = atrial depolarisation, QRS = ventricular depolarisation, T = ventricular repolarisation.
- Pepsin from pepsinogen (HCl); trypsin from trypsinogen (enterokinase); bile emulsifies fats; fats absorbed as chylomicrons into lacteals.
- Inspiration: diaphragm and external intercostals contract; expiration is passive at rest.
- O2 97% as oxyhaemoglobin; CO2 about 70% as bicarbonate (carbonic anhydrase).
- Rhythm centre in medulla, pneumotaxic centre in pons; CO2 and H+ drive breathing.
- TV 500 ml; VC = TV + IRV + ERV; TLC = VC + RV.
