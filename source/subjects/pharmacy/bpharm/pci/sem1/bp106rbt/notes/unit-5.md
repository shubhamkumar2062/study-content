# Unit 5: Plant respiration, plant growth and development, the cell and tissues

This unit closes the course with the smallest units of life. First it shows how plants release energy from food (respiration) and how they grow under the control of hormones. Then it looks inside the cell, explains how cells divide, and describes the tissues that cells form in plants and animals. Fermentation, plant hormones and cell division are directly useful later: fermentation makes ethanol and antibiotics, synthetic plant hormones are used in farming, and many anticancer drugs act on dividing cells.

## 5.1 Plant respiration

**Respiration** is the breakdown of food molecules (mainly glucose) inside cells, with release of energy that is trapped as **ATP (adenosine triphosphate)**, the cell's "energy currency". The compounds broken down are called **respiratory substrates** (carbohydrates usually; fats and proteins in some conditions).

```text
 C6H12O6 + 6 O2  --->  6 CO2 + 6 H2O + energy (stored as ATP, part lost as heat)
```

Plants have no special breathing organs. Each part takes in and gives out gases by itself, through **stomata** (pores in leaves) and **lenticels** (pores in bark). Plants need relatively little oxygen because most of their cells are near the surface and their energy demand is lower than that of animals.

**Aerobic respiration** uses oxygen and completely breaks glucose to CO2 and water. **Anaerobic respiration (fermentation)** occurs without oxygen and breaks glucose only partly. Both begin with **glycolysis**.

### Glycolysis (EMP pathway)

**Glycolysis** ("splitting of sugar") is the breakdown of one glucose (6 carbons) into **two pyruvic acid** (3 carbons each) molecules. It was worked out by Gustav Embden, Otto Meyerhof and J. Parnas, so it is also called the **EMP pathway**. It takes place in the **cytoplasm**, does not need oxygen, and occurs in all living organisms.

```text
 Glucose (6C)
   | ATP -> ADP   (hexokinase)
 Glucose-6-phosphate
   |
 Fructose-6-phosphate
   | ATP -> ADP   (phosphofructokinase)
 Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (6C)
   |  split (aldolase)
 2 x triose phosphate (3C): glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (and DHAP, converted to it)
   |  2 NAD+ -> 2 NADH  (oxidation + phosphate added)
 2 x 1,3-bisphosphoglyceric acid
   |  2 ADP -> 2 ATP
 2 x 3-phosphoglyceric acid
   |
 2 x 2-phosphoglyceric acid
   |  (water removed)
 2 x phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)
   |  2 ADP -> 2 ATP
 2 x PYRUVIC ACID (3C)
```

**Energy account of glycolysis (per glucose)**

| Item | Number |
| --- | --- |
| ATP used | 2 |
| ATP formed | 4 |
| **Net ATP** | **2** |
| NADH formed | 2 |

In plants, glucose for glycolysis comes from sucrose (split by invertase into glucose and fructose) or from stored starch.

**Fate of pyruvic acid** depends on the cell's needs and on oxygen:

```text
                     PYRUVIC ACID
          ______________|______________________
         |                  |                  |
  no O2 (yeast,      no O2 (some        O2 present (most plant
  some plants)        bacteria, muscle)  and animal cells)
         |                  |                  |
  ethanol + CO2       lactic acid        Krebs cycle in mitochondria
  (alcoholic          (lactic acid       -> CO2 + H2O + much ATP
   fermentation)       fermentation)      (aerobic respiration)
```

### Fermentation (anaerobic respiration)

**Fermentation** is the incomplete breakdown of glucose in the absence of oxygen, giving ethanol or lactic acid plus a little energy.

**Alcoholic fermentation** (yeast, *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*):

```text
 Pyruvic acid --pyruvic acid decarboxylase--> Acetaldehyde + CO2
 Acetaldehyde + NADH --alcohol dehydrogenase--> Ethanol + NAD+

 Overall: C6H12O6 ---> 2 C2H5OH + 2 CO2 + energy (net 2 ATP)
```

**Lactic acid fermentation** (lactic acid bacteria such as *Lactobacillus*; animal muscle during hard exercise when oxygen runs short):

```text
 Pyruvic acid + NADH --lactate dehydrogenase--> Lactic acid + NAD+
```

**Key points**

- The NADH made in glycolysis is reused (turned back to NAD+) in fermentation, so glycolysis can keep going.
- Less than **7%** of the energy in glucose is released, and not all of it as ATP.
- Yeasts are poisoned and die when ethanol reaches about **13%**, which sets the upper alcohol content of naturally fermented drinks; higher strengths need distillation.
- Uses: bread (CO2 makes dough rise), alcoholic drinks and industrial alcohol, curd (lactic acid), vinegar, many antibiotics and vitamins are made by fermentation processes.

### Aerobic respiration (for completeness)

In mitochondria, pyruvic acid is converted to **acetyl CoA** (releasing CO2 and NADH), which enters the **Krebs cycle (citric acid / TCA cycle)**. The NADH and FADH2 formed pass electrons along the **electron transport system** in the inner mitochondrial membrane to **oxygen**, which is the final electron acceptor and forms water. ATP is made by **oxidative phosphorylation**. Complete oxidation of one glucose gives a net of about **38 ATP** in the classical textbook count (current estimates are lower, about 30–32).

| Aerobic respiration | Anaerobic respiration (fermentation) |
| --- | --- |
| Needs oxygen | No oxygen |
| Cytoplasm + mitochondria | Cytoplasm only |
| Glucose fully broken to CO2 and H2O | Partial breakdown to ethanol + CO2 or lactic acid |
| Large energy yield (about 38 ATP) | Small energy yield (2 ATP) |

### Respiratory quotient (RQ)

**RQ = volume of CO2 released ÷ volume of O2 used** in respiration.

| Substrate | RQ |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | 1.0 |
| Fats (e.g. tripalmitin) | Less than 1 (about 0.7) |
| Proteins | About 0.9 |
| Organic acids | More than 1 |
| Anaerobic respiration | Very high (CO2 released, no O2 used) |

## 5.2 Plant growth and development

**Growth** is a permanent, irreversible increase in the size, mass or volume of an organism or its part, usually with an increase in dry weight and in the number of cells.

- Plant growth is **indeterminate (open)**: plants keep growing throughout life because they have **meristems** (regions of dividing cells) at root and shoot tips and in the stem's sides.
- **Primary growth** (increase in length) comes from **apical meristems** at root and shoot tips; **secondary growth** (increase in thickness) from **lateral meristems** (vascular cambium and cork cambium) in dicots and gymnosperms.
- **Development** is the whole series of changes an organism goes through from seed germination to old age and death; it includes growth and **differentiation**.

### Phases of growth

```text
  Root or shoot tip:
  [ MERISTEMATIC ] -> [ ELONGATION ] -> [ MATURATION ]
  constantly dividing  cells enlarge,     cells reach full size,
  cells, dense          large vacuole,     thicker walls,
  cytoplasm, large      new wall material  change in structure
  nucleus, thin walls                      (differentiation)
```

### Rate of plant growth

**Growth rate** is the increase in growth per unit time.

| Type | Pattern | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Arithmetic growth** | After mitosis only one daughter cell keeps dividing; the other differentiates. Length increases at a constant rate | Lt = L0 + rt (Lt = length at time t, L0 at start, r = growth rate). Graph is a straight line |
| **Geometric (exponential) growth** | Both daughter cells keep dividing; growth is slow at first, then rapid | W1 = W0 e^(rt) (W = size, r = relative growth rate, e = base of natural logarithms) |

When cells, tissues or a whole organism grow geometrically with limited resources, growth slows down and the graph is **S-shaped (sigmoid)**:

```text
 size
   |                 ________ stationary (steady) phase
   |               /
   |             /   log (exponential) phase - fastest growth
   |           /
   |   ______/  lag phase - slow start
   |__________________________ time
```

**Absolute growth rate** = total growth per unit time; **relative growth rate** = growth per unit time per unit of initial size. Two leaves may grow by the same absolute amount, but the smaller one has the higher relative growth rate.

### Conditions for growth

| Condition | Why it is needed |
| --- | --- |
| **Water** | Cell enlargement needs turgidity (cells filled with water); medium for enzyme reactions |
| **Oxygen** | Respiration to release energy for growth |
| **Nutrients** | Macro- and micronutrients to build protoplasm and as energy sources |
| **Optimum temperature** | Enzymes work within a range; extremes damage or stop growth |
| **Light and gravity** | Influence some stages and the direction of growth (tropisms) |

### Differentiation, dedifferentiation, redifferentiation

- **Differentiation** — cells from meristems become specialised for particular jobs (for example, xylem cells lose their protoplasm and build strong walls for water transport).
- **Dedifferentiation** — permanent cells regain the ability to divide (for example, formation of cork cambium from mature parenchyma).
- **Redifferentiation** — cells formed by dedifferentiated tissue specialise again (for example, cork cells).
- **Plasticity** — a plant forms different structures in response to the environment or life stage. Example: **heterophylly**, different leaf shapes in young and mature plants of cotton, coriander and larkspur, or above-water and under-water leaves of buttercup.

### Introduction to plant growth regulators

**Plant growth regulators (PGRs)**, also called plant hormones or phytohormones, are small organic substances that, in tiny amounts, control growth and development. Some are **promoters** (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins) and some mainly **inhibitors** (abscisic acid); ethylene does both but is largely an inhibitor.

| PGR | Discovery (short) | Main physiological effects | Uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Auxins** — natural: indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), indole butyric acid (IBA); synthetic: NAA, 2,4-D | Charles and Francis Darwin noticed coleoptiles of canary grass bend towards light; F. W. Went isolated auxin from oat (*Avena*) coleoptile tips | Cell elongation; **apical dominance** (the growing tip suppresses side buds); root formation on stem cuttings; xylem differentiation | Rooting of cuttings; prevent early fruit and leaf drop; **parthenocarpy** (seedless fruits, e.g. tomato); 2,4-D kills broad-leaved (dicot) weeds in cereal crops |
| **Gibberellins** (more than 100 kinds; GA3 studied most) | E. Kurosawa found that the fungus *Gibberella fujikuroi* caused "foolish seedling" (bakanae) disease of rice, in which seedlings grow abnormally tall | Stem elongation; **bolting** (sudden stem growth before flowering) in cabbage and beet; break seed dormancy | Increase length of grape stalks; longer sugarcane stems (more sugar); speed up malting in brewing |
| **Cytokinins** — kinetin (from heated DNA), natural zeatin (from maize kernels, coconut milk) | F. Skoog and co-workers found a substance promoting cell division | **Cell division (cytokinesis)**; new leaves and shoots; overcome apical dominance; **delay leaf senescence** (ageing) | Keep cut flowers and vegetables fresh longer; tissue culture |
| **Abscisic acid (ABA)** | Identified as a growth inhibitor linked with leaf and fruit fall and dormancy | **Stress hormone**; closes stomata in drought; induces seed and bud **dormancy**; inhibits germination; aids seed maturation | — |
| **Ethylene** (a simple gas, C2H4) | H. H. Cousins found that ripe oranges released a gas that ripened stored bananas | **Fruit ripening** (raises respiration rate: climacteric); abscission (fall) of leaves and flowers; breaks dormancy; promotes female flowers in cucumber | **Ethephon** releases ethylene: ripening of fruits like tomato and apple, flowering in pineapple |

Plant growth and development also depend on day length (**photoperiodism**) and on cold treatment (**vernalisation**), which the syllabus does not cover in detail.

## 5.3 The cell — the unit of life

A **cell** is the smallest structural and functional unit of life. Robert Hooke first saw "cells" in cork (1665); Anton van Leeuwenhoek first saw living cells.

**Cell theory**

1. All living organisms are made of cells and cell products (Schleiden, for plants, 1838; Schwann, for animals, 1839).
2. All cells arise from pre-existing cells — "*omnis cellula e cellula*" (Rudolf Virchow, 1855).

### Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | Bacteria, cyanobacteria, mycoplasma | Protists, fungi, plants, animals |
| Nucleus | No true nucleus; DNA lies in the cytoplasm as the **nucleoid** | True nucleus with nuclear envelope |
| Membrane-bound organelles | Absent | Present (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, plastids) |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in cytoplasm (70S inside mitochondria and chloroplasts) |
| Size | Small, about 1–10 µm | Larger, about 10–100 µm |
| Extra DNA | Often **plasmids** (small circular DNA; can carry antibiotic resistance) | Not usual |

### Plant cell and animal cell

```text
   PLANT CELL                             ANIMAL CELL
   ____________________________           ______________________
  | cell wall (cellulose)       |        ( plasma membrane only  )
  | plasma membrane             |        (    nucleus            )
  |   [ large central vacuole ] |        (  small vacuoles       )
  |   nucleus     chloroplasts  |        (  centrioles (centrosome)
  |   mitochondria  Golgi  ER   |        (  mitochondria Golgi ER)
  |_____________________________|        (  lysosomes            )
```

| Feature | Plant cell | Animal cell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell wall | Present (cellulose) | Absent |
| Plastids (chloroplasts) | Present | Absent |
| Vacuole | One large central vacuole | Small, few or absent |
| Centrioles | Absent (in higher plants) | Present |
| Shape | Usually regular, rectangular | Usually rounded or irregular |
| Stored food | Starch | Glycogen |

### Structure and functions of cell organelles

| Structure | Structure in short | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cell wall** | Non-living rigid layer of plant cells: cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin; primary wall, secondary wall; neighbouring cells joined by the **middle lamella** (calcium pectate); **plasmodesmata** connect cells | Shape, protection, support; prevents bursting |
| **Plasma (cell) membrane** | **Fluid mosaic model** (Singer and Nicolson, 1972): a **lipid bilayer** (phospholipids, tails inward) with proteins scattered in it, some on the surface, some passing through; the lipids and proteins can move sideways | Controls what enters and leaves (**selectively permeable**); passive transport (diffusion, osmosis) and active transport using ATP (e.g. Na+/K+ pump) |
| **Nucleus** | Bounded by a double **nuclear envelope** with pores; contains **nucleoplasm**, **chromatin** (DNA + proteins, forms chromosomes during division) and one or more **nucleoli** | Controls all cell activity; stores hereditary information; nucleolus makes ribosomal RNA |
| **Endoplasmic reticulum (ER)** | Network of membrane tubes and sacs. **Rough ER** has ribosomes on its surface; **smooth ER** has none | Rough ER: protein synthesis and secretion. Smooth ER: lipid and steroid synthesis; detoxification of drugs (in liver cells) |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Stacks of flat sacs (cisternae) with a receiving (cis) and a shipping (trans) face | Packages and modifies proteins (adds sugars: glycoproteins), forms secretory vesicles and lysosomes |
| **Lysosomes** | Membrane sacs with **hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes** that work at acidic pH | Digest worn-out organelles and engulfed particles; "suicide bags" because they can digest the cell itself |
| **Vacuoles** | Membrane-bound spaces; in plants a large central vacuole bounded by the **tonoplast** | Store water, sap, wastes; keep the plant cell turgid |
| **Mitochondria** | Double membrane; inner membrane folded into **cristae**; inner space (**matrix**) has its own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes | "**Powerhouse of the cell**": aerobic respiration, ATP formation |
| **Plastids** | In plants. **Chloroplasts** (green, double membrane, grana and stroma, own DNA), **chromoplasts** (coloured carotenoids in fruits and petals), **leucoplasts** (colourless stores: amyloplasts for starch, elaioplasts for oil, aleuroplasts for protein) | Photosynthesis; colour; storage |
| **Ribosomes** | Non-membrane granules of RNA and protein; 80S (60S + 40S) in eukaryotes, 70S (50S + 30S) in prokaryotes | **Protein synthesis** |
| **Cytoskeleton** | Network of microtubules, microfilaments and intermediate filaments | Support, shape, movement of the cell and organelles |
| **Cilia and flagella** | Hair-like outgrowths; core (axoneme) of microtubules in a **9 + 2** pattern | Movement of the cell or of fluid over it |
| **Centrosome** | Two **centrioles** at right angles, each with nine triplets of microtubules (9 + 0) | Forms spindle fibres in animal cell division; base of cilia and flagella |
| **Microbodies** | Small vesicles with enzymes (peroxisomes, glyoxysomes) | Various reactions, e.g. breaking down hydrogen peroxide |

### Cell division

**Cell division** is how one cell becomes two. It allows growth, repair and reproduction.

**Cell cycle** — the sequence of events from one division to the next (about 24 hours in a human cell in culture):

```text
          INTERPHASE (about 95% of the time)
   G1 phase -----> S phase -----> G2 phase -----> M phase (division)
 (cell grows,    (DNA is copied,  (proteins made   mitosis + cytokinesis
  normal work)    doubled)         for division)        |
       ^________________________________________________|
 Cells that stop dividing leave G1 and enter G0 (quiescent stage).
```

### Mitosis (equational division)

In mitosis one cell divides into **two identical daughter cells**, each with the **same chromosome number** as the parent (2n → 2n). It happens in body (somatic) cells; in plants mainly in meristems.

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Prophase** | Chromatin condenses into visible **chromosomes**, each with two **chromatids** joined at the **centromere**; centrioles move to opposite poles and form spindle fibres (animal cells); nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear |
| **Metaphase** | Chromosomes line up at the cell's equator (**metaphase plate**); spindle fibres attach to the **kinetochores** at the centromeres. Best stage to count and study chromosomes |
| **Anaphase** | Centromeres split; sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles |
| **Telophase** | Chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil; nuclear envelope and nucleolus re-form; two nuclei |
| **Cytokinesis** | Cytoplasm divides: in animal cells by a **furrow** that pinches in; in plant cells by a **cell plate** formed from the centre outwards |

**Significance:** growth, repair and replacement of cells; keeps the chromosome number constant; asexual reproduction. Uncontrolled mitosis produces **cancer**.

### Meiosis (reductional division)

Meiosis occurs in cells that form **gametes** (or spores in plants). One diploid cell divides **twice** to give **four haploid cells** (2n → n), which are genetically different.

```text
 Diploid cell (2n)
      |  MEIOSIS I (reductional): homologous chromosomes pair and separate
      v
 2 haploid cells (n), chromosomes still double (two chromatids each)
      |  MEIOSIS II (like mitosis): sister chromatids separate
      v
 4 haploid cells (n)
```

**Prophase I** is long and has five substages:

| Substage | Event |
| --- | --- |
| Leptotene | Chromosomes become visible as long threads |
| Zygotene | **Homologous chromosomes** (matching pairs, one from each parent) pair up (**synapsis**), forming **bivalents** |
| Pachytene | **Crossing over** — exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of homologues (enzyme recombinase) |
| Diplotene | Homologues begin to separate but stay joined at the cross-over points (**chiasmata**) |
| Diakinesis | Chiasmata move to the ends; spindle forms; nuclear envelope and nucleolus disappear |

Then **metaphase I** (bivalents line up), **anaphase I** (homologous chromosomes, not chromatids, move apart), **telophase I**; a short gap (interkinesis, no DNA copying) and **meiosis II** (prophase II to telophase II).

| Mitosis | Meiosis |
| --- | --- |
| Body (somatic) cells | Reproductive cells (gamete or spore formation) |
| One division | Two divisions |
| 2 daughter cells | 4 daughter cells |
| Chromosome number stays the same (2n → 2n) | Halved (2n → n) |
| No pairing, no crossing over | Pairing and crossing over in prophase I |
| Daughter cells identical | Daughter cells genetically different |

**Significance of meiosis:** keeps the chromosome number constant from generation to generation (fertilisation restores 2n); crossing over and random sorting of chromosomes create **variation**, the raw material of evolution.

## 5.4 Tissues

A **tissue** is a group of cells, usually of similar structure and common origin, working together for a particular function. **Histology** is the study of tissues.

### Plant tissues

```text
                   PLANT TISSUES
          ________________|________________
         |                                 |
   MERISTEMATIC (dividing)           PERMANENT (no longer dividing)
   apical, intercalary, lateral      _________|__________
                                    |                    |
                              SIMPLE (one type     COMPLEX (several
                              of cell)             types of cell)
                              parenchyma           xylem
                              collenchyma          phloem
                              sclerenchyma
```

| Tissue | Location | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Apical meristem** | Tips of root and shoot | Small, dividing cells | Growth in length (primary growth) |
| **Intercalary meristem** | Base of internodes or leaves of grasses | Dividing cells between mature tissue | Regrowth of grazed grasses; elongation |
| **Lateral meristem** | Sides of stems and roots (vascular cambium, cork cambium) | Dividing cells in a cylinder | Growth in thickness (secondary growth) |
| **Parenchyma** | Cortex, pith, mesophyll, fruit pulp | Living, thin-walled, cellulose walls, spaces between cells | Storage, photosynthesis (as chlorenchyma), secretion |
| **Collenchyma** | Below epidermis of dicot stems, petioles | Living; walls thickened at the corners with cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin | Mechanical support to growing parts; flexibility |
| **Sclerenchyma** | Hypodermis of monocot stems, fruit walls, seed coats, around vascular bundles | Dead at maturity; thick walls with **lignin**; **fibres** (long) and **sclereids** (short, stony cells in nut shells, guava pulp, pear) | Strength and rigidity |
| **Xylem** | Vascular bundles | **Tracheids** and **vessels** (dead, lignified, conduct water), **xylem fibres** (support), **xylem parenchyma** (living, store food) | Conducts water and minerals upward from roots; support |
| **Phloem** | Vascular bundles | **Sieve tube elements** (living, no nucleus, sieve plates), **companion cells** (control sieve tubes), **phloem parenchyma**, **phloem fibres** (bast) | Conducts food (sugars) from leaves to other parts |

Vessels are the main water-conducting cells of angiosperms; gymnosperms have mainly tracheids. Companion cells are typical of angiosperms.

### Animal tissues

There are four basic animal tissues.

**1. Epithelial tissue** — covers the body surface and lines cavities, ducts and tubes. Cells are packed closely with very little space between them, resting on a **basement membrane**.

| Type | Structure | Location | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Squamous** (simple) | One layer of flat cells | Walls of blood vessels, air sacs (alveoli) of lungs | Diffusion, filtration |
| **Cuboidal** (simple) | One layer of cube-shaped cells | Ducts of glands, tubular parts of nephrons | Secretion, absorption |
| **Columnar** (simple) | One layer of tall cells, often with **microvilli** | Lining of stomach and intestine | Secretion, absorption |
| **Ciliated** | Cuboidal or columnar with cilia | Bronchioles, fallopian tubes | Move mucus or eggs along |
| **Compound (stratified)** | Several layers | Skin, lining of mouth and pharynx, inner lining of salivary and pancreatic ducts | Protection against chemical and mechanical stress |
| **Glandular** | Cells specialised for secretion | Exocrine glands (salivary, sweat) and endocrine glands | Secretion |

Cells are linked by **junctions**: **tight junctions** stop leakage, **adhering junctions** cement cells, **gap junctions** let ions and small molecules pass between cells.

**2. Connective tissue** — the most abundant and widely spread tissue; links and supports other tissues. Cells lie scattered in a **matrix** (ground substance) with fibres such as **collagen** and **elastin**.

| Type | Structure | Location | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Loose — areolar** | Fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, loose fibres | Beneath the skin | Packing, supports epithelium |
| **Loose — adipose** | Fat-storing cells | Under the skin, around kidneys | Stores fat, insulation, cushioning |
| **Dense regular** | Collagen fibres in parallel bundles | **Tendons** (muscle to bone), **ligaments** (bone to bone) | Strong attachment |
| **Dense irregular** | Fibres in many directions | Skin (dermis) | Strength in many directions |
| **Cartilage** | Solid, pliable matrix; cells (chondrocytes) in small cavities | Tip of nose, outer ear, ends of long bones, between vertebrae | Support with flexibility |
| **Bone** | Hard matrix rich in **calcium salts** and collagen; cells (osteocytes) in spaces called lacunae | Skeleton | Support, protection, movement; bone marrow forms blood cells |
| **Blood** | Fluid matrix (plasma) with RBC, WBC, platelets | Blood vessels | Transport (see Unit 2) |

**3. Muscular tissue** — made of long cells called **muscle fibres** that contain contractile filaments (actin and myosin) and can contract.

| Type | Features | Location | Control |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Skeletal (striated)** | Long, cylindrical, unbranched, many nuclei, cross stripes | Attached to bones (e.g. biceps) | Voluntary |
| **Smooth (unstriated)** | Spindle-shaped, one nucleus, no stripes | Walls of stomach, intestine, blood vessels, uterus | Involuntary |
| **Cardiac** | Branched, striated, usually one nucleus; joined by **intercalated discs** (gap junctions) so the heart contracts as a unit | Heart wall | Involuntary |

**4. Neural (nervous) tissue** — **neurons** (excitable cells that carry impulses, see Unit 3) and **neuroglia** (supporting cells that protect and support neurons and make up more than half the volume of neural tissue). Found in brain, spinal cord and nerves. Function: control and coordination.

## Remember

- Glycolysis: cytoplasm, no O2, glucose → 2 pyruvic acid, net 2 ATP + 2 NADH.
- Yeast: pyruvic acid → acetaldehyde + CO2 → ethanol (decarboxylase, alcohol dehydrogenase); muscle and *Lactobacillus*: lactic acid. Yeast dies at about 13% alcohol.
- RQ = CO2 released/O2 used: carbohydrate 1, fat about 0.7, protein about 0.9.
- Growth phases: meristematic → elongation → maturation; sigmoid curve: lag, log, stationary.
- Arithmetic Lt = L0 + rt; geometric W1 = W0 e^rt.
- Auxin: apical dominance, rooting, 2,4-D weedkiller; gibberellin: bolting, malting, sugarcane; cytokinin: cell division, delays senescence; ABA: stress, dormancy, stomata close; ethylene: ripening.
- Cell theory: Schleiden, Schwann; *omnis cellula e cellula* — Virchow.
- Fluid mosaic model: Singer and Nicolson (1972). Mitochondria = powerhouse; lysosomes = suicide bags; ribosomes 70S/80S.
- Mitosis: 2n → 2n, 2 identical cells, PMAT; meiosis: 2n → n, 4 different cells, crossing over in pachytene.
- Plant tissues: meristematic and permanent (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma; xylem, phloem).
- Animal tissues: epithelial, connective, muscular, neural; tendons join muscle to bone, ligaments bone to bone.
