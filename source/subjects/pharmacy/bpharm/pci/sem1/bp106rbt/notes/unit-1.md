# Unit 1: The living world and morphology of flowering plants

Many medicines come from plants, and many are tested on animals or act on the human body, so a pharmacist needs a working picture of living things. This unit starts from zero: what makes something "alive", how scientists name and group living things, and how a flowering plant is built from root to seed. Every new word is explained the first time it appears. Plant names are given with their **family** (a group of closely related plants), because pharmacognosy in later semesters uses families all the time.

## 1.1 What is a living organism?

An **organism** is any individual living thing: a bacterium, a mango tree, a frog, a human. **Biology** is the study of living things; **botany** studies plants and **zoology** studies animals.

There is no single test that separates living from non-living. Instead, living organisms show a set of characters together:

| Character | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cellular organisation | Body made of one or more **cells** (the smallest unit that can live on its own) | Amoeba is one cell; a human has trillions |
| Metabolism | The sum of all chemical reactions in the body: building up (anabolism) and breaking down (catabolism) | Digestion of food, making proteins |
| Growth | Increase in mass and number of cells, from inside (intrinsic) | A seedling becoming a tree. A crystal also grows, but only by adding material on the outside, so it is not living |
| Reproduction | Making new individuals of the same kind | Seeds, eggs, budding in yeast |
| Response to stimuli (consciousness) | Sensing and reacting to the surroundings | A plant bending towards light; pulling the hand away from heat |
| Homeostasis | Keeping the internal state (temperature, water, pH) steady | Sweating when hot |
| Movement | Movement of the whole body or of parts | Animals walk; plant cells show streaming of cytoplasm |

**Defining features.** Growth and reproduction are not enough on their own: a mule, a worker bee or an infertile couple do not reproduce, yet they are alive. **Metabolism, cellular organisation and consciousness (response to the environment)** are present in every living thing and absent in non-living things, so they are called the **defining characters** of life.

## 1.2 Diversity in the living world

**Biodiversity** means the number and variety of living organisms on earth. About 1.7–1.8 million species have been described, and many more remain unnamed. A **species** is a group of organisms that look alike and can breed with each other to give fertile offspring.

To study such variety we need three things:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Identification** | Finding the correct name of an organism |
| **Nomenclature** | Giving a correct, standard scientific name |
| **Classification** | Placing organisms into groups by shared characters |
| **Taxonomy** | The science that covers all three: identification, naming and classification |
| **Systematics** | Taxonomy plus the evolutionary relationships between organisms |

**Taxonomic hierarchy.** Groups are arranged in ranks, each rank called a **taxon** (plural taxa). Going down, each rank contains fewer organisms that are more similar to each other.

```text
Kingdom
  Phylum (animals) / Division (plants)
    Class
      Order
        Family
          Genus
            Species
```

| Rank | Human | Mango | Wheat |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kingdom | Animalia | Plantae | Plantae |
| Phylum/Division | Chordata | Angiospermae | Angiospermae |
| Class | Mammalia | Dicotyledonae | Monocotyledonae |
| Order | Primates | Sapindales | Poales |
| Family | Hominidae | Anacardiaceae | Poaceae |
| Genus | *Homo* | *Mangifera* | *Triticum* |
| Species | *sapiens* | *indica* | *aestivum* |

## 1.3 Binomial nomenclature

Common names differ from place to place (mango is *aam* in Hindi and *mamidi* in Telugu), so scientists use one Latin-based name that is the same everywhere. **Binomial nomenclature** ("two-name naming") was established by **Carolus Linnaeus**. Each scientific name has two parts: the **genus** (generic name) and the **specific epithet**.

**Rules**

1. Names are in Latin or are Latinised, whatever their origin.
2. The first word is the genus, the second is the specific epithet.
3. When printed, the name is in *italics*; when handwritten, the two words are underlined separately.
4. The genus starts with a capital letter; the specific epithet starts with a small letter.
5. The name of the scientist who first described the species may follow in short form, for example *Mangifera indica* L. (L. = Linnaeus).
6. Plant names follow the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi and plants; animal names follow the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature.

| Common name | Scientific name | Family |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mango | *Mangifera indica* | Anacardiaceae |
| Tulsi (holy basil) | *Ocimum tenuiflorum* (older name *O. sanctum*) | Lamiaceae |
| Neem | *Azadirachta indica* | Meliaceae |
| Datura | *Datura metel* | Solanaceae |
| Wheat | *Triticum aestivum* | Poaceae |
| Human | *Homo sapiens* | Hominidae |
| Housefly | *Musca domestica* | Muscidae |
| Tiger | *Panthera tigris* | Felidae |

## 1.4 Five-kingdom classification

Early on, Linnaeus used only two kingdoms, **Plantae** and **Animalia**. This was unsatisfactory: bacteria and fungi do not fit either. In 1969 **R. H. Whittaker** proposed **five kingdoms**. His main bases of classification were:

1. **Cell structure** — prokaryotic or eukaryotic. A **prokaryotic** cell has no true nucleus (its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm) and no membrane-bound organelles. A **eukaryotic** cell has a true nucleus inside a membrane and organelles such as mitochondria.
2. **Body organisation** — unicellular (one cell) or multicellular (many cells, forming tissues).
3. **Mode of nutrition** — **autotrophic** (makes its own food, mostly by photosynthesis) or **heterotrophic** (depends on others for food: saprophytic = feeding on dead matter, parasitic = feeding on a living host, holozoic = swallowing food).
4. **Reproduction** and **phylogenetic (evolutionary) relationships**.

| Feature | Monera | Protista | Fungi | Plantae | Animalia |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cell type | Prokaryotic | Eukaryotic | Eukaryotic | Eukaryotic | Eukaryotic |
| Cell wall | Present (non-cellulosic, peptidoglycan in most bacteria) | Present in some | Present (chitin) | Present (cellulose) | Absent |
| Body | Unicellular | Unicellular | Mostly multicellular, loose tissue | Multicellular, tissues/organs | Multicellular, tissues/organs/systems |
| Nutrition | Autotrophic or heterotrophic | Autotrophic or heterotrophic | Heterotrophic (saprophytic or parasitic) | Autotrophic (photosynthetic) | Heterotrophic (holozoic) |
| Examples | *Escherichia coli*, *Lactobacillus*, *Nostoc* | *Amoeba*, *Plasmodium*, *Euglena*, diatoms | *Penicillium*, yeast (*Saccharomyces*), mushroom (*Agaricus*) | Mango, neem, ferns, mosses | Frog, cockroach, human |

### Kingdom Monera

- All **bacteria** belong here; they are the most abundant organisms and live everywhere, even in hot springs and deep oceans.
- Shapes: **coccus** (spherical), **bacillus** (rod), **vibrio** (comma), **spirillum** (spiral).
- **Archaebacteria** live in extreme places: halophiles (salty water), thermoacidophiles (hot springs), methanogens (marshes and the gut of cattle, producing methane).
- **Eubacteria** ("true bacteria") have a rigid cell wall and many have a flagellum. **Cyanobacteria** (blue-green algae such as *Nostoc*, *Anabaena*) photosynthesise and some fix nitrogen. Most bacteria are heterotrophs; they help in curd making (*Lactobacillus*), antibiotic production (*Streptomyces*) and nitrogen fixation (*Rhizobium*), but some cause disease (cholera, typhoid, tetanus).
- **Mycoplasma** have no cell wall at all; they are the smallest living cells known.
- Reproduction mainly by **binary fission** (splitting into two).

### Kingdom Protista

- All single-celled **eukaryotes**, mostly aquatic. Forms a link between plants, animals and fungi.
- Groups: **chrysophytes** (diatoms — their silica walls form "diatomaceous earth", used as a filter aid), **dinoflagellates** (some cause "red tides"), **euglenoids** (*Euglena*: photosynthetic in light, heterotrophic in darkness), **slime moulds** and **protozoans**.
- Protozoans include *Amoeba* (moves by pseudopodia, "false feet"), *Paramecium* (cilia), *Trypanosoma* (causes sleeping sickness) and *Plasmodium* (causes malaria).

### Kingdom Fungi

- Heterotrophic, with cell walls of **chitin** (a tough polysaccharide). Body made of thread-like **hyphae** that form a network called **mycelium**. Yeast is a single-celled exception.
- Most are **saprophytes**; some are parasites (rusts and smuts on wheat); some live in partnership: **lichens** (fungus + alga) and **mycorrhiza** (fungus + plant root).
- Reproduce by fragmentation, spores (conidia, sporangiospores) and sexual spores.
- Pharmacy relevance: *Penicillium* gives penicillin; yeast is used in bread and alcohol; ergot (*Claviceps purpurea*) gives ergot alkaloids.

### Kingdom Plantae

- Eukaryotic, multicellular, with **chlorophyll** (green pigment) and cellulose cell walls; autotrophic. A few are partly heterotrophic: insectivorous plants (bladderwort, pitcher plant) and parasites (*Cuscuta*).
- Includes algae (in this scheme), bryophytes (mosses), pteridophytes (ferns), gymnosperms (pine, cycas) and angiosperms (flowering plants).
- Life cycle shows **alternation of generations** between a haploid (one set of chromosomes, n) gametophyte and a diploid (two sets, 2n) sporophyte.

### Kingdom Animalia

- Eukaryotic, multicellular, heterotrophic; **no cell wall**. Food is stored as **glycogen** and fat.
- Most show a definite growth pattern, can move about, and reproduce sexually.
- Ranges from sponges and worms to insects, fish, birds and mammals.

### Viruses (outside the five kingdoms)

Whittaker's system did not place viruses, because they are not truly "living" outside a host.

- A **virus** is a non-cellular particle of **nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) inside a protein coat** called a **capsid**. It has no metabolism of its own and multiplies only inside a living host cell. Outside a cell it can be crystallised like a chemical.
- **D. J. Ivanowsky** (1892) found that the agent of tobacco mosaic disease passed through filters that stopped bacteria; **M. W. Beijerinck** called it a "contagious living fluid"; **W. M. Stanley** (1935) crystallised tobacco mosaic virus (TMV).
- Plant viruses usually have RNA; animal viruses have DNA or RNA; **bacteriophages** (viruses that infect bacteria) usually have DNA.
- Diseases: common cold, influenza, mumps, measles, AIDS (HIV), polio in humans; mosaic and leaf curl in plants.
- **Viroids** are smaller still: free RNA without a protein coat (potato spindle tuber disease). **Prions** are infectious proteins.

## 1.5 Morphology of flowering plants

**Morphology** is the study of external form and structure. A flowering plant (angiosperm) has an underground **root system** and an aerial **shoot system** (stem, leaves, flowers, fruits).

```text
           flower ---> fruit ---> seed
             |
  leaf --- node --- stem (shoot system, above ground)
             |
        ---------- soil level ----------
             |
        primary (tap) root
          /    |    \
   lateral roots   root hairs   (root system, below ground)
```

### Root

The **root** grows downward from the **radicle** (embryonic root) of the seed. It anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals.

- **Tap root system** — the main root persists and gives lateral roots; typical of **dicotyledons** (plants whose seed has two seed-leaves, e.g. mustard, Brassicaceae).
- **Fibrous root system** — the main root is short-lived and many roots of equal size arise from the stem base; typical of **monocotyledons** (one seed-leaf, e.g. wheat, Poaceae).
- **Adventitious roots** — arise from any part other than the radicle (banyan, *Ficus benghalensis*, Moraceae).

Regions of a root tip: root cap → region of meristematic activity (dividing cells) → region of elongation → region of maturation (bears root hairs).

| Modification | Function | Example (family) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conical root | Food storage | Carrot (Apiaceae) |
| Fusiform root | Food storage | Radish (Brassicaceae) |
| Napiform root | Food storage | Turnip (Brassicaceae), beetroot (Amaranthaceae) |
| Tuberous root | Food storage | Sweet potato (Convolvulaceae) |
| Prop roots | Support from branches | Banyan (Moraceae) |
| Stilt roots | Support from lower nodes | Maize, sugarcane (Poaceae) |
| Pneumatophores | Breathing in swampy soil, grow upward | *Rhizophora* (Rhizophoraceae) |

### Stem

The **stem** develops from the **plumule** (embryonic shoot). It has **nodes** (where leaves arise) and **internodes** (portions between nodes); it bears buds, leaves, flowers and fruits, and conducts water and food.

| Modification | Function | Example (family) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tuber (underground, with "eyes" = buds) | Storage | Potato (Solanaceae) |
| Rhizome | Storage, perennation | Ginger (Zingiberaceae), turmeric (Zingiberaceae) |
| Corm | Storage | *Colocasia* (Araceae) |
| Bulb | Storage in fleshy leaves around a reduced stem | Onion (Amaryllidaceae; older books: Liliaceae) |
| Stem tendril | Climbing | Grapevine (Vitaceae), cucumber (Cucurbitaceae) |
| Thorn | Protection | *Citrus* (Rutaceae), *Bougainvillea* (Nyctaginaceae) |
| Phylloclade (flat green stem) | Photosynthesis in dry places | *Opuntia* (Cactaceae), *Euphorbia* (Euphorbiaceae) |
| Runner / stolon / offset | Vegetative spread | Grass (Poaceae), *Pistia*, *Eichhornia* |

### Leaf

A **leaf** is a flat, green outgrowth from a node, the main organ of photosynthesis. Parts: **leaf base** (with or without stipules, small side outgrowths), **petiole** (stalk) and **lamina** (blade) with veins.

- **Venation** (arrangement of veins): **reticulate** — veins form a net (dicots, e.g. peepal); **parallel** — veins run side by side (monocots, e.g. banana, grasses).
- **Simple leaf** — lamina entire or cut, but cuts do not reach the midrib (mango). **Compound leaf** — lamina divided into separate leaflets. **Pinnately compound**: leaflets along a central axis (neem, Meliaceae). **Palmately compound**: leaflets from one point (silk cotton, *Bombax*).
- **Phyllotaxy** (arrangement of leaves on the stem): **alternate** — one leaf per node (China rose, mustard); **opposite** — two leaves per node (*Calotropis*, Apocynaceae; guava, Myrtaceae); **whorled** — more than two per node (*Alstonia*, Apocynaceae).
- **Modifications**: tendrils (pea, Fabaceae), spines (cactus, *Opuntia*), fleshy storage leaves (onion), pitchers that trap insects (*Nepenthes*, Nepenthaceae), phyllode (flattened petiole replacing the lamina, Australian *Acacia*, Fabaceae).

### Inflorescence

An **inflorescence** is the arrangement of flowers on the floral axis.

| Type | Plan | Example (family) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Racemose** | Main axis keeps growing; flowers arise laterally, oldest at the base (acropetal order) | Mustard (Brassicaceae) |
| Spike | Racemose, flowers without stalks | *Achyranthes* (Amaranthaceae) |
| Umbel | Flower stalks arise from one point | Coriander (Apiaceae) |
| Capitulum (head) | Flat receptacle with many small florets | Sunflower (Asteraceae) |
| Spadix | Fleshy axis enclosed in a large bract (spathe) | *Colocasia* (Araceae), banana (Musaceae) |
| **Cymose** | Main axis ends in a flower; growth limited; oldest flower at the top (basipetal order) | Jasmine (Oleaceae) |
| Cyathium (special) | Cup-shaped bracts with one female and many male flowers | *Euphorbia* (Euphorbiaceae) |
| Verticillaster (special) | Clusters of flowers at each node | Tulsi (Lamiaceae) |
| Hypanthodium (special) | Hollow fleshy receptacle with flowers inside | Fig, *Ficus* (Moraceae) |

### Flower

The **flower** is the reproductive shoot. It sits on a stalk (**pedicel**) whose swollen end is the **thalamus** (receptacle). It has four whorls:

```text
   outermost                                       innermost
   CALYX ------- COROLLA ------- ANDROECIUM ------- GYNOECIUM
   sepals        petals          stamens            carpels/pistil
   (protect bud) (attract        (male: filament    (female: stigma,
                  pollinators)    + anther with      style, ovary with
                                  pollen)            ovules)
```

- **Bisexual** flower has both stamens and carpels (mustard); **unisexual** has only one set (cucumber).
- **Actinomorphic** (radial symmetry) can be cut into two equal halves along any vertical plane (mustard, *Datura*, chilli); **zygomorphic** only along one plane (pea, gulmohur).
- **Position of ovary on the thalamus:**
  - **Hypogynous** — ovary superior, other parts below it (mustard, China rose, brinjal).
  - **Perigynous** — other parts arise from a cup around the ovary, ovary half-inferior (plum, rose, peach).
  - **Epigynous** — thalamus grows round and fuses with the ovary; ovary inferior (guava, cucumber, ray florets of sunflower).
- **Aestivation** (arrangement of sepals or petals in the bud): **valvate** — margins just touch (*Calotropis*); **twisted** — each overlaps the next (China rose, cotton); **imbricate** — irregular overlap (gulmohur, *Cassia*); **vexillary** — one large petal (standard) covers two wings that cover two keel petals (pea, bean).
- **Placentation** (arrangement of ovules inside the ovary): **marginal** (pea), **axile** (China rose, tomato, lemon), **parietal** (mustard, *Argemone*), **free central** (*Dianthus*, primrose), **basal** (sunflower, marigold).

A flower is described in short form by a **floral formula** using symbols: ⚥ bisexual, ⊕ actinomorphic, K calyx, C corolla, A androecium, G gynoecium (G with a line below = superior ovary, line above = inferior). Mustard: ⊕ ⚥ K2+2 C4 A2+4 G(2) with superior ovary.

### Fruit

A **fruit** is a mature, ripened ovary formed after fertilisation. Its wall is the **pericarp** (outer **epicarp**, middle **mesocarp**, inner **endocarp**). A fruit formed without fertilisation is **parthenocarpic** (seedless banana).

| Group | Type | Example (family) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple fleshy | Berry | Tomato (Solanaceae), grape (Vitaceae) |
| Simple fleshy | Drupe (stony endocarp) | Mango (Anacardiaceae), coconut (Arecaceae) |
| Simple fleshy | Pepo | Cucumber (Cucurbitaceae) |
| Simple fleshy | Hesperidium | Orange, lemon (Rutaceae) |
| Simple fleshy | Pome (false fruit: edible part is thalamus) | Apple (Rosaceae) |
| Dry dehiscent (splits open) | Legume | Pea, bean (Fabaceae) |
| Dry dehiscent | Siliqua | Mustard (Brassicaceae) |
| Dry dehiscent | Capsule | Cotton (Malvaceae), *Datura* (Solanaceae) |
| Dry indehiscent (does not split) | Caryopsis (grain) | Wheat, rice, maize (Poaceae) |
| Dry indehiscent | Cypsela | Sunflower (Asteraceae) |
| Aggregate (from many free carpels of one flower) | Etaerio | Custard apple (Annonaceae), strawberry (Rosaceae) |
| Composite / multiple (from a whole inflorescence) | Sorosis, syconus | Jackfruit (Moraceae), pineapple (Bromeliaceae); fig (Moraceae) |

### Seed

A **seed** is a fertilised, ripened ovule. It contains an **embryo** (a baby plant: radicle + plumule + cotyledons) inside a seed coat.

| Feature | Dicot seed (gram, bean — Fabaceae) | Monocot seed (maize — Poaceae) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cotyledons (seed leaves) | Two, often fleshy with stored food | One, called the **scutellum** |
| Endosperm (food tissue) | Usually absent in the mature seed (used up) | Present and bulky |
| Seed coat | Testa (outer) and tegmen (inner) | Fused with the fruit wall |
| Protective sheaths | None | Coleorhiza over radicle, coleoptile over plumule |
| Scar | Hilum (where the seed was attached) and micropyle | Not easily seen |

Maize has a protein-rich **aleurone layer** around the endosperm.

## 1.6 Anatomy of root, stem and leaf (monocot and dicot)

**Anatomy** is the study of internal structure, seen in thin sections under the microscope. A few terms first:

- **Epidermis** — outermost protective layer (called **epiblema** in the root, where it bears root hairs).
- **Cortex** — layers of thin-walled cells inside the epidermis.
- **Endodermis** — innermost layer of cortex; in roots its cells have **Casparian strips** (bands of waxy suberin) that control water entry.
- **Pericycle** — layer just inside the endodermis; lateral roots arise from it.
- **Vascular bundle** — conducting strand of **xylem** (carries water and minerals) and **phloem** (carries food).
- **Conjoint** bundle has xylem and phloem in the same bundle (stems, leaves); **radial** arrangement has them on alternate radii (roots).
- **Open** bundle has **cambium** (a dividing layer) between xylem and phloem, so it can grow in thickness; **closed** bundle has none.
- **Protoxylem** is the first-formed xylem; **metaxylem** forms later. **Exarch** = protoxylem towards the outside (roots); **endarch** = protoxylem towards the centre (stems).
- **Pith** — central soft tissue.

### Root

| Feature | Dicot root (sunflower, bean) | Monocot root (maize) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Xylem bundles | 2 to 4 (diarch to tetrarch) | Many, more than 6 (polyarch) |
| Xylem arrangement | Radial, exarch | Radial, exarch |
| Pith | Small or absent | Large, well developed |
| Cambium / secondary growth | Formed later; secondary growth occurs | Absent |
| Pericycle | Gives lateral roots, cambium and cork cambium | Gives lateral roots only |

```text
 Dicot root (transverse section, simplified)
   epiblema with root hairs
     cortex (many layers of parenchyma)
       endodermis (Casparian strips)
         pericycle
           xylem arms (X) alternating with phloem patches (P)
                  P
             X         X        2-4 xylem arms, little or no pith
                  P
```

### Stem

| Feature | Dicot stem (sunflower) | Monocot stem (maize) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epidermis | With cuticle and multicellular hairs | With cuticle, no hairs |
| Hypodermis | **Collenchyma** (living, thick corners) | **Sclerenchyma** (dead, thick-walled) |
| Ground tissue | Divided into cortex, endodermis, pericycle and pith | Not divided; one mass of parenchyma |
| Vascular bundles | Few, in a **ring** | Many, **scattered** |
| Bundle type | Conjoint, collateral, **open** (cambium present), endarch | Conjoint, collateral, **closed**; each surrounded by a sclerenchyma **bundle sheath** |
| Phloem parenchyma | Present | Absent |
| Water cavity | Absent | Present in each bundle (protoxylem cavity) |
| Bundle shape | Wedge-shaped | Oval, "skull-like" |

```text
  Dicot stem                         Monocot stem
  ( o  o  o  o )  bundles            ( o   o  o )
  (o   pith   o)  in a ring          (  o  o   o)  bundles scattered
  ( o  o  o  o )                     ( o  o  o  )  in ground tissue
```

### Leaf

| Feature | Dicot leaf (dorsiventral, e.g. mango) | Monocot leaf (isobilateral, e.g. grass, maize) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Position | Lamina held at an angle; upper and lower surfaces differ | Held almost upright; both surfaces alike |
| Stomata (pores) | More on the lower surface | About equal on both surfaces |
| Mesophyll (inner green tissue) | Two kinds: **palisade** (long cells under upper epidermis) and **spongy** (loose cells, air spaces) | Not differentiated; all spongy-like |
| Bulliform cells | Absent | Present in the upper epidermis of grasses; they lose water and let the leaf roll up in dry weather |
| Veins | Net (reticulate) | Parallel |

```text
  Dicot leaf (section)
  ------------------------ cuticle + upper epidermis
  | | | | | | | | | | | |  palisade mesophyll (columns of cells)
  o  .  o  . (vein) .  o   spongy mesophyll with air spaces
  ------------------------ lower epidermis with stomata
```

## Remember

- Defining characters of life: metabolism, cellular organisation and response to surroundings; growth and reproduction are not enough alone.
- Taxonomy = identification + nomenclature + classification. Hierarchy: kingdom → phylum/division → class → order → family → genus → species.
- Binomial nomenclature (Linnaeus): genus capital, epithet small, italics in print, underline separately by hand.
- Whittaker (1969) five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia; bases: cell type, body organisation, nutrition, reproduction, relationships.
- Viruses are nucleic acid in a protein capsid; live only inside host cells; TMV crystallised by Stanley.
- Tap root in dicots, fibrous root in monocots; carrot conical, radish fusiform, turnip napiform.
- Placentation: marginal (pea), axile (China rose, tomato), parietal (mustard), free central (*Dianthus*), basal (sunflower).
- Mango and coconut are drupes; apple is a false fruit (pome); wheat grain is a caryopsis.
- Dicot root: 2–4 xylem, little pith; monocot root: many xylem, large pith; both radial and exarch.
- Dicot stem: bundles in a ring, open; monocot stem: scattered, closed, with bundle sheath. Dicot leaf dorsiventral; monocot leaf isobilateral with bulliform cells.
