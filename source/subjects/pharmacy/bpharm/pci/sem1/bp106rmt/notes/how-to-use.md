# How to use this guide

Remedial Mathematics is taken in Semester 1 by students who studied biology, not mathematics, in Class 12. It starts from the basics and builds up to the equations used in pharmacokinetics. Marks in this subject come from **working**, not from writing: read a section, then close the notes and redo every worked example on paper until you get the same answer without help. Then try the question bank.

**How the subject is examined.** In the PCI scheme, BP106RMT is a 2-credit theory subject (2 hours a week) marked as a **Non University Examination (NUE)**, so your own college sets and marks the paper. Marks: 15 internal (10 from a sessional exam of 1 hour, 5 from continuous assessment) + **35 end-semester (1.5 hours)** = 50. The PCI regulations print this pattern for a 35-mark paper, and the full mock paper in this app follows it. Confirm the exact pattern with your college.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Long answers — answer 1 of 2 | 1 × 10 = 10 |
| II | Short answers — answer 5 of 7 | 5 × 5 = 25 |
| | **Total** | **35** |

The question bank also has 2-mark very short questions, useful for the sessional exam and for papers that ask short definitions.

**How the units are arranged.** The five units follow the five syllabus units exactly (each about 6 teaching hours). Unit 4 joins two syllabus topics, analytical geometry and integration, because the syllabus puts them in the same unit.

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — this type of problem appears in most papers; practise several versions until you can do them quickly.
- **Medium** — appears every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from syllabus weight and common question patterns, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Problems that keep coming back |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Partial fractions, logs, limits | Resolve into partial fractions (linear, repeated, quadratic factors); laws of logarithms; characteristic and mantissa; limits using (x^n − a^n)/(x − a) and sin θ/θ; continuity at a point |
| 2 Matrices | Inverse of a 3 × 3 matrix; solving three equations by matrix method and Cramer's rule; verifying Cayley–Hamilton and using it for A^−1; properties of determinants |
| 3 Differentiation | Product and quotient rule problems; successive derivatives; maxima and minima of a cubic |
| 4 Geometry and integration | Distance, slope, equation of a line (parallel/perpendicular); integration by substitution, partial fractions and by parts; definite integrals |
| 5 DE and Laplace | Order and degree; separable, homogeneous, linear and exact equations; Laplace transforms of standard functions; solving a DE by Laplace; first-order kinetics and IV infusion |

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Write every step on its own line. Examiners give method marks even when the last number is wrong, but only if they can see the method.
2. Write the formula or rule you are using before substituting (for example "quotient rule: (vu′ − uv′)/v^2").
3. Check your answer whenever you can: put x = 0 into a partial fraction; multiply A by A^−1 to get I; put the solution back into the equations; differentiate an integral to get the integrand back.
4. Box the final answer and, for integrals, never forget "+ c".
5. In matrix questions, show |A| first. If |A| = 0, say the matrix is singular and has no inverse.
6. For maxima and minima, state the test (f′ = 0, sign of f″) and give both the point and the value.
7. Where a question says "application", add one pharmacy example: first-order kinetics (C = C0e^(−kt), t½ = 0.693/k), IV infusion, AUC = C0/k, Beer–Lambert calibration line, pH = −log[H+].
8. Learn the five standard tables in the revision sheet (logs laws, derivatives, integrals, Laplace pairs, DE methods); most marks depend on recalling them correctly.
