# Unit 4: Redox titrations

Redox (oxidation–reduction) titrations assay drugs that can gain or lose electrons: iron salts, hydrogen peroxide, ascorbic acid, copper salts, phenols, arsenicals and many more. The syllabus asks for the basic concepts and the principle and applications of six types: cerimetry, iodimetry, iodometry, bromatometry, dichrometry and titration with potassium iodate. Permanganometry, the oldest redox method, is included because it is part of the practicals.

## 4.1 Concepts of oxidation and reduction

| Concept | Oxidation | Reduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical (oxygen) | Addition of oxygen | Removal of oxygen |
| Classical (hydrogen) | Removal of hydrogen | Addition of hydrogen |
| Electronic | **Loss of electrons** | **Gain of electrons** |
| Oxidation number | Increase in oxidation number | Decrease in oxidation number |

- **Oxidising agent (oxidant)** — accepts electrons and is itself reduced (KMnO4, K2Cr2O7, Ce⁴⁺, I2, KBrO3, KIO3).
- **Reducing agent (reductant)** — donates electrons and is itself oxidised (Fe²⁺, oxalate, thiosulphate, As(III), ascorbic acid, H2O2 towards permanganate).
- Oxidation and reduction always occur together: Fe²⁺ + Ce⁴⁺ → Fe³⁺ + Ce³⁺.

**Oxidation number rules (short form):** element in free state 0; O usually −2 (−1 in peroxides); H +1 (−1 in metal hydrides); alkali metals +1; sum of oxidation numbers = charge on the species. Example: Mn in KMnO4: +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0 → x = +7. Cr in Cr2O7²⁻: 2x − 14 = −2 → x = +6.

**Balancing by the ion–electron (half-reaction) method:** write each half-reaction, balance atoms other than O and H, add H2O for O, H⁺ for H (acid medium), add electrons for charge, multiply so electrons cancel, add.

Example: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O and Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻ (×5):
**MnO4⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H2O**

### Electrode potential and the Nernst equation

Each redox couple has a **standard electrode potential (E°)** measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (0.000 V). The more positive E°, the stronger the oxidising agent.

| Couple | E° (V, approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| Ce⁴⁺/Ce³⁺ (in H2SO4) | +1.44 |
| MnO4⁻/Mn²⁺ | +1.51 |
| Cr2O7²⁻/Cr³⁺ | +1.33 |
| BrO3⁻/Br⁻ | +1.44 |
| Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ | +0.77 |
| I2/I⁻ | +0.54 |
| S4O6²⁻/S2O3²⁻ | +0.08 |

**Nernst equation** (25 °C): E = E° + (0.0591/n) log ([Ox]/[Red])

A reaction proceeds when the oxidant's couple has a higher potential than the reductant's; a difference of a few tenths of a volt makes it go to completion and gives a sharp potential jump at the equivalence point.

**Redox titration curve:** potential (E) against volume of titrant. Before equivalence E depends on the analyte couple; at half-way E = E° of the analyte couple; after equivalence it depends on the titrant couple. Equivalence potential for Fe²⁺/Ce⁴⁺: E = (E°1 + E°2)/2 ≈ (0.77 + 1.44)/2 ≈ 1.1 V (for n1 = n2 = 1).

### Redox indicators and end points

| Kind | How | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Self-indicator** | The titrant itself is coloured and its reduced form colourless | KMnO4 (first permanent pink); iodine (yellow-brown) |
| **Specific indicator** | Reacts specifically with one species | Starch with iodine (deep blue); thiocyanate with Fe³⁺ |
| **True redox indicator** | A substance whose oxidised and reduced forms have different colours; changes at a definite potential: E = E°In ± 0.059/n | Ferroin (red → pale blue, about 1.11 V); diphenylamine and barium diphenylamine sulphonate (colourless → violet, about 0.76–0.85 V); N-phenylanthranilic acid (colourless → purple-red, about 1.08 V); methylene blue |
| **External indicator** | Drop removed and tested outside | Potassium ferricyanide for Fe²⁺ (old dichromate method); starch–iodide paper |
| **Instrumental** | Potentiometric or dead-stop end point | Any redox titration |

**Equivalent weight in redox** = molecular weight ÷ number of electrons gained or lost per molecule.

| Reagent / analyte | Electrons | 0.1 N equals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| KMnO4 (acid) | 5 | 0.02 M |
| K2Cr2O7 | 6 | 0.01667 M |
| Ceric ammonium sulphate | 1 | 0.1 M |
| Iodine (I2) | 2 | 0.05 M |
| Sodium thiosulphate | 1 | 0.1 M |
| KBrO3 | 6 | 0.01667 M |
| KIO3 + excess KI in dilute acid (1 IO3⁻ ≡ 3 I2 ≡ 6 S2O3²⁻) | 6 (as thiosulphate equivalents) | 0.01667 M |
| KIO3 (to ICl, Andrews conditions) | 4 | 0.025 M |
| Fe²⁺ | 1 | — |
| H2O2 (with KMnO4) | 2 | — |

## 4.2 Permanganometry (permanganate titrations)

- **Titrant:** 0.02 M KMnO4 (0.1 N). Not a primary standard (contains MnO2, decomposes in light, reacts with organic matter in water). Prepared by dissolving about 3.2 g per litre, boiling, standing, filtering through glass wool, storing in an amber bottle. **Standardised** against oxalic acid or sodium oxalate in dilute H2SO4 at 60–70 °C (the reaction is slow at first; Mn²⁺ formed catalyses it — autocatalysis).
- **Medium:** dilute **sulphuric acid**. Not HCl (permanganate oxidises chloride to chlorine) and not HNO3 (an oxidant itself). In neutral/alkaline solution MnO4⁻ goes only to MnO2 (3 electrons) and a brown precipitate forms.
- **Indicator:** self-indicator — first permanent faint pink.
- **Applications:** hydrogen peroxide, ferrous sulphate, oxalates, nitrites, calcium (as oxalate, indirectly).

**Assay of hydrogen peroxide solution**

2MnO4⁻ + 5H2O2 + 6H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5O2 + 8H2O

1 ml of 0.02 M KMnO4 ≡ 0.001701 g H2O2 (Mw 34.01; n = 2: 34.01/2 × 0.1 = 1.701 mg).

**Volume strength:** "20 volume" H2O2 releases 20 times its own volume of oxygen at STP. 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2: 68 g gives 22.4 L, so 1 g gives about 0.329 L. A 6% w/v solution (60 g/L) gives 60 × 0.329 ≈ 19.7 L per L, i.e. about **20 volumes**.

## 4.3 Cerimetry

- **Titrant:** ceric ammonium sulphate (or ceric sulphate), 0.1 M, in sulphuric acid. Ce⁴⁺ + e⁻ → Ce³⁺ (n = 1, so molarity = normality).
- **Standardised** against arsenic trioxide (with osmic acid catalyst, ferroin indicator) or against ferrous ammonium sulphate.
- **Indicator:** **ferroin** (1,10-phenanthroline ferrous sulphate): red (reduced) → pale blue (oxidised). Also N-phenylanthranilic acid.

**Advantages over permanganate**

1. Very stable solutions — strength unchanged for long periods, even on boiling.
2. Ce⁴⁺ is a strong oxidant (E° about 1.44 V in H2SO4).
3. Single, simple electron change (Ce⁴⁺ → Ce³⁺), no intermediate products.
4. Can be used in the presence of high concentration of **HCl** (chloride is not oxidised in the cold), unlike KMnO4.
5. Ce³⁺ is colourless, so ceric solutions do not obscure indicator colours in dilute solution.
6. Ceric ammonium sulphate of high purity is available.

**Disadvantages:** costlier; cannot be used in neutral or alkaline solution (ceric hydroxide precipitates); yellow colour too faint to act as a reliable self-indicator.

**Applications:** ferrous salts (ferrous sulphate, ferrous fumarate, ferrous gluconate), paracetamol (after hydrolysis to p-aminophenol), menadione, tocopherol, nitrites, hydrogen peroxide, oxalates.

**Assay of ferrous sulphate (FeSO4·7H2O, Mw 278.01)**

Ce⁴⁺ + Fe²⁺ → Ce³⁺ + Fe³⁺. Dissolve about 0.5 g, accurately weighed, in a mixture of dilute H2SO4 and freshly boiled and cooled water; add ferroin; titrate with 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate to pale blue/greenish. **1 ml of 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate ≡ 0.02780 g FeSO4·7H2O.**

## 4.4 Iodimetry and iodometry

Iodine is a mild oxidising agent (I2 + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2I⁻, E° 0.54 V). Two quite different techniques use this couple.

| Point | **Iodimetry** (direct) | **Iodometry** (indirect) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Principle | A **reducing** analyte is titrated directly with a standard **iodine** solution | An **oxidising** analyte reacts with excess KI to **liberate iodine**, which is titrated with standard **sodium thiosulphate** |
| Titrant | 0.05 M iodine (in KI) | 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate |
| Reaction | I2 + 2e⁻ → 2I⁻ (from the analyte) | Oxidant + 2I⁻ → I2; I2 + 2S2O3²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S4O6²⁻ |
| End point | Starch: first permanent **blue** appears (or own yellow colour) | Starch added **near the end**: blue **disappears** |
| Examples | Ascorbic acid, sodium thiosulphate, arsenic trioxide, sulphites, antimony compounds | Copper sulphate, potassium dichromate, bromate, iodate, chlorine (bleaching powder), hydrogen peroxide, ferric salts |

**Iodine solution (0.05 M):** iodine is only slightly soluble in water but dissolves in potassium iodide solution as the tri-iodide ion (I2 + I⁻ → I3⁻), which also reduces loss by volatility. Standardised against arsenic trioxide (dissolved in NaOH, neutralised, buffered with sodium bicarbonate at pH about 8) or against standardised thiosulphate.

**Starch indicator** — forms an intense blue complex with iodine (iodine in the helix of amylose). In **iodometry**, starch is added only when most iodine has been titrated (pale straw colour), because a large amount of iodine binds strongly to starch and is released slowly, causing an error and a sluggish end point. Starch solution must be fresh (it decomposes, and hydrolysed starch gives a red-violet colour).

**Sources of error in iodine titrations and how to avoid them**

1. Loss of iodine by volatility — use excess KI, keep cool, titrate promptly, stoppered flasks.
2. Air oxidation of iodide in acid solution (4I⁻ + O2 + 4H⁺ → 2I2 + 2H2O), speeded by light — do not keep acid solutions of KI standing; work away from strong light.
3. pH: thiosulphate titrations must not be done in strong acid (S2O3²⁻ decomposes to S and SO2) or in alkaline solution above pH about 9 (iodine forms hypoiodite, which oxidises thiosulphate partly to sulphate).
4. Starch added too early.

**Assay of copper sulphate (CuSO4·5H2O, Mw 249.68) — iodometry**

2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI (white ppt) + I2; I2 + 2S2O3²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S4O6²⁻. So 2 Cu ≡ 1 I2 ≡ 2 S2O3²⁻, i.e. Cu : thiosulphate = 1 : 1.

Dissolve about 1 g, accurately weighed, in 50 ml water; add a little acetic acid and about 3 g KI; titrate the liberated iodine with 0.1 M thiosulphate, adding starch near the end. Near the end point add **potassium thiocyanate**: it converts the CuI precipitate to CuSCN, which releases iodine adsorbed on CuI and sharpens the end point. **1 ml of 0.1 M Na2S2O3 ≡ 0.02497 g CuSO4·5H2O.**

**Assay of ascorbic acid (C6H8O6, Mw 176.12) — iodimetry**

C6H8O6 + I2 → C6H6O6 + 2HI. Dissolve in water + dilute H2SO4 (acid slows air oxidation), titrate with 0.05 M iodine using starch to the first permanent blue. **1 ml of 0.05 M iodine ≡ 0.008806 g C6H8O6.**

## 4.5 Bromatometry

- **Titrant:** potassium bromate, 0.0167 M (0.1 N); KBrO3 is a primary standard (Mw 167.00).
- **Principle:** in acid with excess **potassium bromide**, bromate liberates a known amount of bromine:
  BrO3⁻ + 5Br⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3Br2 + 3H2O
  The bromine reacts with the analyte (bromination by substitution or addition, or oxidation). Usually an excess of bromate–bromide is used, and the unreacted bromine is determined by adding KI and titrating the liberated iodine with thiosulphate (**back titration**, iodometric finish):
  Br2 + 2I⁻ → 2Br⁻ + I2.
- **Direct titration** (e.g. arsenic(III), antimony(III), hydrazine) with methyl orange or methyl red as an **irreversible** indicator — excess bromine destroys (bleaches) the dye at the end point.
- **Applications:** phenol (forms 2,4,6-tribromophenol, uses 3 Br2 = 6 electrons), resorcinol, cresols, aniline, salicylic acid, isoniazid, metronidazole, hydrazine, 8-hydroxyquinoline (and metals as their oxinates), arsenic and antimony compounds.

**Phenol by bromination:** C6H5OH + 3Br2 → C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr. 1 mol phenol ≡ 3 mol Br2 ≡ 6 thiosulphate. 1 ml of 0.1 N bromine (0.05 M Br2) ≡ 94.11/6 × 0.1 = **1.569 mg phenol**.

## 4.6 Dichrometry

- **Titrant:** potassium dichromate, 0.01667 M (0.1 N); a primary standard (Mw 294.18) — can be weighed directly, stable for years, not decomposed by light or boiling.
- Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O (orange → green).
- **Indicators:** diphenylamine, **barium diphenylamine sulphonate**, sodium diphenylamine sulphonate, N-phenylanthranilic acid (colourless/green → violet/purple). Phosphoric acid is added in ferrous titrations — it complexes Fe³⁺, lowering the Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ potential so the indicator changes exactly at the equivalence point, and removes the yellow ferric colour.
- **Advantages:** primary standard; stable solution; can be used in HCl solution (does not oxidise chloride in the cold).
- **Disadvantages:** weaker oxidant than permanganate or ceric; green Cr³⁺ colour can mask the end point; chromium compounds are toxic and carcinogenic (waste disposal).
- **Applications:** ferrous salts, iron ores, determination of chemical oxygen demand, alcohol (oxidation), and as a primary standard for thiosulphate.

Cr2O7²⁻ + 6Fe²⁺ + 14H⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 6Fe³⁺ + 7H2O.

## 4.7 Titrations with potassium iodate

Potassium iodate (KIO3, Mw 214.00) is a primary standard and a strong oxidant. The products depend on the acid strength.

**(a) In dilute acid with excess iodide** — a source of iodine (iodometric standardisation):
IO3⁻ + 5I⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3I2 + 3H2O. Each iodate releases 3 I2, which need 6 thiosulphate ions, so for thiosulphate standardisation 1 IO3⁻ ≡ 6 S2O3²⁻ and the equivalent weight of KIO3 is 214.00/6 = 35.67 (1 ml of 0.1 M thiosulphate ≡ 0.003567 g KIO3). Used to standardise thiosulphate and to prepare iodine solutions of known strength.

**(b) Andrews' titration in strong HCl (about 3–6 M)** — reducing agents are oxidised and iodine ends up as **iodine monochloride (ICl)**, which is stable in strong HCl:
IO3⁻ + 2I⁻ + 6H⁺ + 3Cl⁻ → 3ICl + 3H2O
Overall, iodate changes from +5 to +1 (ICl) — **4 electrons**.
- **End point:** an immiscible organic solvent (chloroform or carbon tetrachloride) is shaken with the solution. During the titration, iodine first formed colours the organic layer **violet**. As titration continues the iodine is oxidised to ICl and the violet colour **disappears** — that is the end point. (Amaranth or other dyes may serve as internal indicators.)
- **Applications:** iodides (potassium iodide assay), hydrazine, arsenic(III), antimony(III), thallium(I), mercurous salts, sulphites, thiosulphates, hydroxylamine.

**Assay of potassium iodide (KI, Mw 166.00) by Andrews' method:** KIO3 + 2KI + 6HCl → 3ICl + 3KCl + 3H2O. 1 mol KIO3 ≡ 2 mol KI. 1 ml of 0.05 M KIO3 ≡ 2 × 0.05 × 166.00/1000 = **0.01660 g KI**.

## 4.8 Summary of redox methods

| Method | Titrant (usual strength) | Medium | Indicator | Typical assays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Permanganometry | 0.02 M KMnO4 | Dilute H2SO4 | Self (pink) | H2O2, FeSO4, oxalates |
| Cerimetry | 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate | H2SO4 (HCl tolerated) | Ferroin | FeSO4, ferrous fumarate, paracetamol, nitrites |
| Iodimetry | 0.05 M I2 in KI | Neutral/weak acid | Starch (blue appears) | Ascorbic acid, As2O3, thiosulphate |
| Iodometry | 0.1 M Na2S2O3 | Weak acid | Starch (blue disappears) | CuSO4, dichromate, bleaching powder, H2O2 |
| Bromatometry | 0.0167 M KBrO3 + KBr | Acid | Methyl orange (irreversible) or iodometric finish | Phenol, resorcinol, isoniazid, metronidazole |
| Dichrometry | 0.01667 M K2Cr2O7 | H2SO4/H3PO4 or HCl | Diphenylamine sulphonate | Ferrous salts |
| Iodate (Andrews) | 0.05 M KIO3 | Strong HCl + CHCl3 | Disappearance of violet in CHCl3 | KI, hydrazine, As(III) |

**Remember**

- OIL RIG: oxidation is loss, reduction is gain (of electrons). Equivalent weight = Mw ÷ electrons transferred.
- Nernst: E = E° + (0.0591/n) log [Ox]/[Red]; bigger E° difference = sharper end point.
- KMnO4 in H2SO4 only, self-indicator, standardised against oxalate at 60–70 °C; H2O2: 1 ml 0.02 M ≡ 0.001701 g.
- Ceric: stable, one-electron, works in HCl, ferroin; FeSO4·7H2O: 1 ml 0.1 M ≡ 0.02780 g.
- Iodimetry = titration with iodine (reductants); iodometry = titration of liberated iodine with thiosulphate (oxidants); starch late in iodometry.
- CuSO4·5H2O: 1 ml 0.1 M thiosulphate ≡ 0.02497 g; add KSCN near the end.
- Bromatometry: BrO3⁻ + 5Br⁻ + 6H⁺ → 3Br2; phenol takes 3 Br2.
- Dichromate: primary standard, diphenylamine sulphonate, H3PO4 added.
- Andrews: KIO3 in strong HCl → ICl; end point when violet colour in chloroform disappears.
