# Unit 2: Acid–base titrations and non-aqueous titrations

Most drugs are weak acids or weak bases, so neutralisation is the commonest way of assaying them. In water this works well for strong and moderately weak acids and bases, but very weak ones (pKa above about 7–8, many alkaloids and amine salts) give no sharp end point. Non-aqueous titration solves that by changing the solvent. This unit explains how indicators work, what happens to pH during each kind of titration, and how titrations are carried out in solvents other than water.

## 2.1 Acids and bases: quick recap

| Theory | Acid | Base |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arrhenius | Gives H+ in water | Gives OH− in water |
| Brønsted–Lowry | Proton donor | Proton acceptor |
| Lewis | Electron-pair acceptor | Electron-pair donor |

Brønsted–Lowry is the most useful here: HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺. A⁻ is the **conjugate base** of HA. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base and vice versa.

- **pH = −log[H⁺]**; pH + pOH = 14 at 25 °C.
- **Ka** (acid dissociation constant) and **pKa = −log Ka**. Smaller pKa = stronger acid. pKa + pKb = 14 for a conjugate pair.
- Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log [salt]/[acid] (buffer of a weak acid); pOH = pKb + log [salt]/[base].

## 2.2 Theories of acid–base indicators

An **acid–base indicator** is a weak organic acid or base whose ionised and un-ionised forms have different colours.

### Ostwald's theory (ionic theory)

- The un-ionised and ionised forms differ in colour.
- For an acid indicator: HIn ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ (HIn one colour, In⁻ another). KIn = [H⁺][In⁻]/[HIn], so **pH = pKIn + log [In⁻]/[HIn]**.
- **Phenolphthalein** (weak acid): un-ionised form colourless in acid; in alkali the ionised form is pink. Excess alkali shifts equilibrium to the pink ion.
- **Methyl orange** (weak base, MeOH ⇌ Me⁺ + OH⁻): ionised form red in acid, un-ionised yellow in alkali.
- The eye sees one colour clearly when that form is about 10 times the other, so the visible change happens between [In⁻]/[HIn] = 1/10 and 10/1: **colour change range = pKIn ± 1**.
- Limitation: does not explain why ionisation should change colour.

### Quinonoid (chromophore) theory

- Indicators exist in two tautomeric forms that are in equilibrium: a **benzenoid** form and a **quinonoid** form.
- The quinonoid form has a stronger chromophore (a conjugated quinone-like system) and is deeply coloured; the benzenoid form is light or colourless.
- A change of pH changes which form dominates. Phenolphthalein: benzenoid lactone (colourless) in acid → quinonoid dianion (pink) in alkali. Methyl orange: benzenoid (azo) yellow form in alkali → quinonoid red form in acid.
- Modern view combines both: ionisation causes a structural rearrangement that changes the colour.

### Common indicators

| Indicator | pH range | Acid colour → alkaline colour | pKIn (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Thymol blue (acid range) | 1.2–2.8 | Red → yellow | 1.7 |
| Methyl orange | 3.1–4.4 | Red → yellow | 3.7 |
| Bromocresol green | 3.8–5.4 | Yellow → blue | 4.7 |
| Methyl red | 4.4–6.2 | Red → yellow | 5.1 |
| Bromothymol blue | 6.0–7.6 | Yellow → blue | 7.1 |
| Phenol red | 6.8–8.4 | Yellow → red | 7.9 |
| Phenolphthalein | 8.3–10.0 | Colourless → pink | 9.4 |
| Thymolphthalein | 9.3–10.5 | Colourless → blue | 9.9 |

**Mixed and screened indicators** — two indicators (or indicator + inert dye) chosen so that the complementary colours at the end point give a sharp change to grey or another distinct colour; useful when the pH jump is small (e.g. methyl red + bromocresol green; methyl orange + xylene cyanol FF).

**Universal indicator** — a mixture showing a series of colours across pH 1–14; used for rough pH, not titrations.

**Choice of indicator** — its colour change range must fall within the steep part (pH jump) of the titration curve around the equivalence point.

## 2.3 Classification of acid–base titrations

1. **Acidimetry** — determination of a base by titration with a standard acid (e.g. NaOH, Na2CO3, borax with HCl).
2. **Alkalimetry** — determination of an acid with a standard alkali (e.g. HCl, acetic acid, citric acid, boric acid with NaOH).

By technique:

| Type | How | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct titration | Titrant added straight to sample | Citric acid with NaOH |
| Back (residual) titration | Excess standard reagent added, excess titrated back | Zinc oxide, aspirin (hydrolysis with excess NaOH, back-titrated with HCl), ammonium chloride (boil with excess NaOH) |
| Displacement titration | Salt of a weak acid/base titrated with strong acid/base which displaces the weak one | Borax with HCl, sodium carbonate with HCl |
| Indirect (via a reaction) | Analyte converted to an acid or base first | Ammonium salts with formaldehyde (formol titration) liberate HCl; boric acid with glycerin/mannitol |
| Double-indicator (mixed alkali) titration | Two end points with two indicators | NaOH + Na2CO3 mixture with phenolphthalein then methyl orange |
| Kjeldahl method | N in organic compound → ammonia, distilled into boric acid or excess acid, titrated | Nitrogen in proteins, drugs |

**Double-indicator example (NaOH + Na2CO3).** With phenolphthalein the titre V1 neutralises all NaOH and converts carbonate to bicarbonate. Then methyl orange; further titre V2 converts bicarbonate to CO2. Carbonate ≡ 2V2; hydroxide ≡ V1 − V2.

## 2.4 Neutralisation curves (theory of titrations)

A **neutralisation (titration) curve** is a plot of pH (y-axis) against volume of titrant (x-axis). The steep part near the equivalence point is the **pH jump**; its size decides whether a visual end point is possible and which indicator to use.

### (a) Strong acid × strong base (0.1 M HCl with 0.1 M NaOH)

- Start: pH = −log 0.1 = 1.
- Before equivalence: pH from the unreacted acid.
- At equivalence: only NaCl + water → **pH 7**.
- After: pH from excess OH⁻.
- The jump is large, about **pH 4 to 10** for 0.1 M solutions (smaller for more dilute solutions).
- **Indicators:** methyl orange, methyl red, phenolphthalein all suitable (phenolphthalein preferred for NaOH titrant).

### (b) Weak acid × strong base (0.1 M acetic acid, pKa 4.76, with 0.1 M NaOH)

- Start: pH = ½pKa − ½log C = 2.38 + 0.5 = 2.88.
- Buffer region: acid + acetate; at half-neutralisation **pH = pKa** (4.76).
- At equivalence: sodium acetate hydrolyses (A⁻ + H2O ⇌ HA + OH⁻), so pH is **above 7**: pH = 7 + ½pKa + ½log C. With salt concentration 0.05 M: pH = 7 + 2.38 − 0.65 = **8.73**.
- Jump about pH 7 to 10.
- **Indicator:** phenolphthalein (8.3–10.0). Methyl orange is unsuitable — it would change in the buffer region.

### (c) Strong acid × weak base (0.1 M HCl with 0.1 M ammonia, pKb 4.75)

- At equivalence: NH4Cl hydrolyses (NH4⁺ ⇌ NH3 + H⁺), so pH is **below 7**: pH = 7 − ½pKb − ½log C = 7 − 2.375 + 0.65 ≈ **5.3**.
- Jump about pH 4 to 6.5.
- **Indicator:** methyl orange or methyl red. Phenolphthalein unsuitable.

### (d) Weak acid × weak base (acetic acid with ammonia)

- pH changes gradually; no sharp jump. At equivalence pH ≈ 7 + ½(pKa − pKb).
- No ordinary indicator gives a sharp end point. Use a mixed indicator, or better, **conductometric** or **potentiometric** titration. Avoided in practice.

### (e) Very weak acids and very weak bases

For a visual end point in water, Ka × C must be at least about 10⁻⁸ (for 0.1 M, pKa below about 7). Very weak acids and bases fall below this.

- **Boric acid** (pKa about 9.2) cannot be titrated directly with NaOH. Adding a **polyhydric alcohol** — glycerin or mannitol — forms a much stronger complex acid (boroglycerin / boromannitol complex), which can then be titrated with NaOH using phenolphthalein. This is the IP assay of boric acid.
- **Phenol** (pKa about 10) and very weak bases such as **caffeine** or most alkaloid salts cannot be titrated in water; they are titrated in **non-aqueous** solvents.
- Salts of very weak acids (borax, sodium carbonate) behave as fairly strong bases and *can* be titrated with strong acid (displacement titration).

### (f) Polyprotic acids

H3PO4 (pKa 2.1, 7.2, 12.4) gives two visible end points: first (to H2PO4⁻) with methyl orange, second (to HPO4²⁻) with phenolphthalein. The third H⁺ is too weak to titrate in water. Sodium carbonate with HCl also shows two steps (phenolphthalein, then methyl orange).

## 2.5 Non-aqueous titrations

**Non-aqueous titration** is a titration carried out in a solvent other than water, used for acids and bases too weak to titrate in water or not soluble in water.

**Why water fails for weak bases and acids:** water is itself a weak acid and a weak base. It competes with a very weak base for the protons of the titrant (and with a very weak acid for the base), so the reaction never goes far enough to give a sharp end point. Changing to a solvent that is a weaker acid (for titrating bases) or a weaker base (for titrating acids) enhances the strength of the analyte.

**Advantages:** sharp end points for very weak acids/bases; organic drugs that are insoluble in water dissolve; mixtures can be titrated separately (differentiating solvents); simple and fast; many IP assays use it (amine salts, alkaloids, sulphonamides, barbiturates).

**Limitations:** solvents are costly and some toxic; water must be excluded (moisture spoils the end point); large temperature coefficient of expansion of organic solvents (correct the titrant for temperature); perchloric acid is corrosive and potentially explosive with organic matter.

### Solvents

| Type | Behaviour | Examples | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Aprotic** (inert) | Neither give nor accept protons; low dielectric constant; used as diluents | Benzene, toluene, chloroform, carbon tetrachloride, dioxane | Diluting, sharpening the end point |
| **Protophilic** (basic) | High affinity for protons; enhance the strength of weak acids | Liquid ammonia, pyridine, dimethylformamide (DMF), ethylenediamine, butylamine | Titration of weak acids (alkalimetry) |
| **Protogenic** (acidic) | Proton donors; enhance the strength of weak bases | Glacial acetic acid, formic acid, sulphuric acid, HF | Titration of weak bases (acidimetry) |
| **Amphiprotic** | Both donate and accept protons | Water, methanol, ethanol, glacial acetic acid (mainly acidic) | Depends on the system |

**Levelling and differentiating effects**

- A **levelling solvent** makes acids (or bases) of different strength appear equally strong. In water, HClO4, HCl and HNO3 are all fully ionised to H3O⁺, so water "levels" them. Glacial acetic acid, being acidic, levels all bases stronger than acetate into acetate-strength bases.
- A **differentiating solvent** shows the true differences between them. In glacial acetic acid, HClO4 is much stronger than HCl, so perchloric acid is chosen as the titrant for bases. Mixtures of acids of different strength can be titrated stepwise in a differentiating solvent such as methyl isobutyl ketone.

**Strength of the solution species in glacial acetic acid**

HClO4 + CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COOH2⁺ + ClO4⁻ (the onium ion CH3COOH2⁺ is a very strong proton donor)

CH3COOH2⁺ + B → BH⁺ + CH3COOH

Net: HClO4 + B → BH⁺ + ClO4⁻.

### Acidimetry in non-aqueous titration (titration of weak bases)

- **Titrant:** 0.1 M perchloric acid in glacial acetic acid. Made from 72% HClO4 + glacial acetic acid + **acetic anhydride**, which reacts with the water present ((CH3CO)2O + H2O → 2CH3COOH). Let it stand 24 hours before use.
- **Standardisation:** against **potassium hydrogen phthalate** dissolved in glacial acetic acid, crystal violet indicator. KHP behaves as a base here (phthalate takes up the proton). 1 ml of 0.1 M HClO4 ≡ 0.02042 g KHP.
- **Indicators:** crystal violet (violet → blue → blue-green / emerald green), oracet blue B (blue → pink), quinaldine red (magenta → near colourless), 1-naphtholbenzein (yellow → green); potentiometric end point with a glass–calomel electrode pair is also used.
- **Halide salts of bases** (hydrochlorides, hydrobromides): the halide ion is too weak a base to react, and HCl formed would interfere. **Mercuric acetate** is added: 2B·HCl + Hg(CH3COO)2 → 2B·H⁺CH3COO⁻ + HgCl2. HgCl2 is un-ionised and does not interfere; the acetate formed is titrated with HClO4.
- **Temperature correction:** glacial acetic acid expands markedly with temperature. If the titrant is used at a temperature different from that at standardisation: corrected M = M at standardisation × [1 + 0.0011 (t1 − t2)], where t1 is the standardisation temperature and t2 the temperature of use (approximate coefficient for acetic acid).

**Assay of sodium benzoate (C7H5NaO2, Mw 144.10)**

- The benzoate ion is a weak base. Dissolve a weighed quantity (about 0.25 g) in anhydrous glacial acetic acid (warm to dissolve, cool) and titrate with 0.1 M perchloric acid; crystal violet or 1-naphtholbenzein indicator (as your monograph directs). Perform a blank.
- C6H5COONa + HClO4 → C6H5COOH + NaClO4 (1 : 1).
- **1 ml of 0.1 M HClO4 ≡ 0.01441 g of C7H5NaO2.**
- % = (V − blank) × f × 0.01441 × 100 / w. Example: 0.2510 g used 17.30 ml (blank 0.10 ml), f = 1.005 → (17.20 × 1.005 × 0.01441 × 100)/0.2510 = **99.2%**.

**Assay of ephedrine hydrochloride (C10H15NO·HCl, Mw 201.70)**

- Dissolve a weighed sample in glacial acetic acid with warming, add **mercuric acetate solution**, titrate with 0.1 M perchloric acid; crystal violet indicator (to emerald green / blue-green). Blank titration.
- **1 ml of 0.1 M HClO4 ≡ 0.02017 g of C10H15NO·HCl.**

### Alkalimetry in non-aqueous titration (titration of weak acids)

- **Solvents:** DMF, ethylenediamine, pyridine, butylamine, or toluene–methanol.
- **Titrants:** 0.1 M sodium methoxide or lithium/potassium methoxide in toluene–methanol; **tetrabutylammonium hydroxide** in toluene–methanol (gives soluble salts, good for potentiometric work).
- **Standardisation:** against **benzoic acid** (Mw 122.12; 1 ml of 0.1 M ≡ 0.01221 g).
- **Indicators:** thymol blue (yellow → blue), azo violet, o-nitroaniline; or potentiometric.
- **Applications:** phenols, barbiturates, sulphonamides, enols, imides, some carboxylic acids and amino acids. Protect from atmospheric CO2 and moisture.

### Applications of non-aqueous titrations

| Drug class | Example | Titrant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amine salts and alkaloids | Ephedrine HCl, chlorpromazine HCl, atropine sulphate | Perchloric acid (+ mercuric acetate for halides) |
| Salts of organic acids | Sodium benzoate, sodium salicylate | Perchloric acid |
| Amino acids | Glycine, methionine | Perchloric acid |
| Weak acids | Phenobarbital, sulphonamides, phenols | Methoxide or tetrabutylammonium hydroxide |

**Remember**

- Indicator colour change range = pKIn ± 1; choose one whose range lies inside the pH jump.
- Strong/strong: pH 7 at equivalence, any indicator. Weak acid/strong base: pH about 8.7, phenolphthalein. Strong acid/weak base: about 5.3, methyl orange/red. Weak/weak: no sharp end point.
- Boric acid becomes titratable after adding glycerin or mannitol.
- Non-aqueous: protogenic solvent (glacial acetic acid) for weak bases with HClO4; protophilic solvent (DMF, pyridine) for weak acids with methoxide.
- Acetic anhydride removes water from HClO4 titrant; mercuric acetate for halide salts; standardise HClO4 with KHP, methoxide with benzoic acid.
- Sodium benzoate: 1 ml of 0.1 M HClO4 ≡ 0.01441 g; ephedrine HCl: ≡ 0.02017 g.
