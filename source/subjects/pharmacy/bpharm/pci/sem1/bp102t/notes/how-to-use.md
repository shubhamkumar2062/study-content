# How to use this guide

Pharmaceutical Analysis I is the first analytical chemistry paper of B.Pharm. It is half theory (principles, indicators, electrodes) and half calculation (molarity, standardisation factors, percentage purity, error statistics). Study one unit a day: read the notes, redo every worked calculation with a calculator without looking, write each reaction and equivalence statement from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Unit 3 is the largest (precipitation, complexometry, gravimetry and diazotisation), so give it two days.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ questions | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with reactions, equivalence statement and a worked calculation or diagram.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. For every assay answer write four things: the **reaction**, the **conditions** (medium, pH, temperature), the **indicator with its colour change**, and the **equivalence statement** ("1 ml of 0.1 M … ≡ … g of …"). Examiners look for all four.
2. Calculations: write the formula, substitute with units, show the factor (f = actual ÷ nominal molarity), and box the answer. Method marks are given even when arithmetic slips.
3. Check an equivalence statement yourself: g per ml = Mw × molarity of titrant × (moles of analyte per mole of titrant) ÷ 1000. If your number does not match the molecular weight this way, recheck the stoichiometry.
4. Remember the n factors: KMnO4 5, K2Cr2O7 6, KBrO3 6, Ce⁴⁺ 1, I2 2, thiosulphate 1, oxalic acid 2, H2SO4 2. Normality = molarity × n.
5. Draw the curve that goes with the topic: neutralisation curves with indicator ranges, conductometric V and two-break curves, potentiometric S curve with first and second derivatives, the polarogram with E½ and id, the Gutzeit apparatus, the calomel and glass electrodes, the DME.
6. Use comparison tables for "differentiate" questions: iodimetry vs iodometry, co- vs post-precipitation, accuracy vs precision, determinate vs indeterminate errors, primary vs secondary standards, Mohr vs Volhard.
7. In limit test answers state the principle, the reason for each reagent (nitric acid, citric acid, thioglycollic acid, lead acetate cotton wool, stannous chloride), and how the result is read (sample less than standard = passes).
8. Practise the statistics example (mean, SD, RSD) once a week; it is an easy 5 marks.
