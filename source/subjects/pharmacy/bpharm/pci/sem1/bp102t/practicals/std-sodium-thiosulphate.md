# Preparation and standardisation of 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate

**Aim:** To prepare 1000 ml of approximately 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate and standardise it using potassium bromate.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Potassium bromate is toxic and an oxidiser. Wear goggles, a lab coat and gloves.

## Principle

Sodium thiosulphate (Na2S2O3·5H2O, Mw 248.18) is not a primary standard: it may be efflorescent, and its solutions are decomposed by bacteria, CO2 and light. It is standardised **iodometrically**: a known weight of **potassium bromate** (primary standard, Mw 167.00) liberates iodine from excess potassium iodide in acid, and the iodine is titrated with thiosulphate using starch.

KBrO3 + 6KI + 6HCl → 3I2 + KBr + 6KCl + 3H2O

I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6

1 KBrO3 ≡ 3 I2 ≡ 6 Na2S2O3, so **1 ml of 0.1 M Na2S2O3 ≡ 0.002784 g KBrO3** (167.00 × 0.1/6/1000 = 0.002783; IP figure 0.002784).

## Requirements

Sodium thiosulphate pentahydrate, sodium carbonate, potassium bromate (dried), potassium iodide, 2 M (or dilute) HCl, starch solution (freshly prepared), freshly boiled and cooled water, iodine flasks (stoppered), burette, 250 ml volumetric flask, pipettes.

## Procedure

**Preparation**

1. Dissolve about 25 g of Na2S2O3·5H2O and 0.2 g of Na2CO3 in freshly boiled and cooled water and make up to 1000 ml. (Na2CO3 keeps the pH slightly alkaline; boiled water is free from CO2 and bacteria.)
2. Keep in an amber bottle; standardise after a day.

**Standardisation**

3. Accurately weigh about 0.2 g of dried KBrO3, dissolve in water and make up to 250 ml in a volumetric flask.
4. Pipette 50 ml into an iodine flask; add 2 g of KI and 3 ml of 2 M HCl; stopper and keep in the dark for about 5 minutes.
5. Titrate the liberated iodine with the thiosulphate solution until the colour is pale straw yellow.
6. Add 1–2 ml of starch solution (blue colour) and continue until the blue just disappears.
7. Repeat for concordant readings.

## Precautions

1. Add starch only near the end point (pale yellow).
2. Use stoppered flasks and keep in the dark before titration (iodine loss, air oxidation of iodide).
3. Do not let the solution become strongly acidic during titration.
4. Use fresh starch solution.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is Na2CO3 added to the thiosulphate solution?** To keep it slightly alkaline and stable against CO2 and bacteria.
2. **Why boiled water?** To remove CO2 and kill bacteria that decompose thiosulphate.
3. **Why is starch added late?** Large amounts of iodine bind strongly to starch and are released slowly, giving a late end point.
4. **Is this iodimetry or iodometry?** Iodometry (titration of liberated iodine).
5. **Equivalent weight of KBrO3 here?** Mw/6 = 27.83.
