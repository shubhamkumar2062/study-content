# Conductometric titration of strong acid against strong base

**Aim:** To determine the normality (molarity) of the given hydrochloric acid by conductometric titration with standard sodium hydroxide.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles, a lab coat and gloves.

## Principle

Conductance of a solution depends on the number and mobility of its ions. H⁺ (about 350 S cm² mol⁻¹) and OH⁻ (about 198) are much faster than Na⁺ (about 50) and Cl⁻ (about 76).

H⁺ + Cl⁻ + Na⁺ + OH⁻ → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ + H2O

- Before the end point, fast H⁺ is replaced by slow Na⁺, so conductance **falls**.
- After the end point, excess Na⁺ and fast OH⁻ are added, so conductance **rises** steeply.

A plot of conductance against volume of NaOH gives two straight lines (V shape); their **intersection** is the end point. No indicator is needed.

N(HCl) × V(HCl) = N(NaOH) × V(NaOH at end point)

## Requirements

Conductivity meter (or bridge) with a dip-type cell (platinised platinum electrodes), 0.1 N NaOH (standardised), the HCl sample (about 0.01 N), standard 0.01 M KCl (to check the cell constant), 100 ml beaker, magnetic stirrer, burette, pipette, graph paper.

## Procedure

1. Switch on the conductivity meter, let it warm up and calibrate/check the cell constant with standard KCl solution as directed.
2. Pipette 50 ml of the HCl solution into a clean beaker (dilute with water if needed so that the electrodes are covered).
3. Dip the conductivity cell, stir gently and record the initial conductance.
4. Add 0.1 N NaOH from the burette in 0.5 ml portions; stir, wait for a steady reading and record the conductance after each addition.
5. Continue until about 8–10 readings have been taken past the minimum.
6. Plot conductance (y-axis) against volume of NaOH (x-axis). Draw the best straight lines through the falling and rising points; read the volume at their intersection.

## Observations and calculation

| Volume of 0.1 N NaOH (ml) | Conductance (mS) |
| --- | --- |
| 0.0 |  |
| 0.5 |  |
| 1.0 |  |
| ... |  |

Correction for dilution (if the titrant is not much stronger than the sample): corrected G = observed G × (V + v)/V, where V is the initial volume and v the volume added.

**Worked example:** 50 ml HCl; lines intersect at 5.20 ml of 0.1000 N NaOH.

N(HCl) = 0.1000 × 5.20 / 50 = **0.0104 N** (0.0104 M). Strength = 0.0104 × 36.46 = 0.379 g/L.

## Result

The normality of the given hydrochloric acid is ___ N.

## Precautions

1. Keep the temperature constant; conductance changes about 2% per °C.
2. Rinse the cell with distilled water and then with the sample; do not touch the platinum plates.
3. Use a titrant about 10 times more concentrated than the sample to keep volume change small.
4. Stir after each addition and read only when steady.
5. Use points away from the end point to draw the lines.

## Viva questions

1. **Why does conductance fall at first?** Highly mobile H⁺ ions are replaced by slower Na⁺ ions.
2. **Why does it rise after the end point?** Excess NaOH adds fast OH⁻ ions.
3. **Why is AC used?** DC would electrolyse the solution and polarise the electrodes.
4. **What is the cell constant?** l/A of the cell; κ = G × cell constant.
5. **Advantage over indicator titration?** Works for coloured, turbid or very dilute solutions; no indicator error.
