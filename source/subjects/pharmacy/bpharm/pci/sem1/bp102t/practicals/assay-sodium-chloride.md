# Assay of sodium chloride by precipitation titration (Mohr's method)

**Aim:** To standardise 0.1 M silver nitrate and determine the percentage purity of sodium chloride by Mohr's method.

> Silver nitrate stains skin and clothes and is irritant; potassium chromate is toxic. Wear goggles, a lab coat and gloves.

## Principle

Chloride is titrated with silver nitrate in neutral solution, with **potassium chromate** as indicator:

Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl (white precipitate)

When all chloride has precipitated, the first excess of silver ion forms **red-brown silver chromate**:

2Ag⁺ + CrO4²⁻ → Ag2CrO4 (red-brown)

Silver chloride precipitates first because it needs a much lower silver ion concentration to reach its solubility product. The solution must be neutral or faintly alkaline (pH about 6.5–9).

**1 ml of 0.1 M AgNO3 ≡ 0.005844 g NaCl** (Mw 58.44).

## Requirements

Silver nitrate (about 17 g per litre for 0.1 M), sodium chloride (dried at 130 °C, primary standard grade), sodium chloride sample, potassium chromate solution (5% w/v), amber burette or bottle, conical flasks, analytical balance.

## Procedure

**Standardisation of 0.1 M AgNO3**

1. Accurately weigh about 0.1 g of dried primary-standard NaCl; dissolve in 50 ml water.
2. Add 1 ml of potassium chromate solution.
3. Titrate with AgNO3, swirling constantly, until a faint permanent reddish-brown tinge appears in the yellow suspension.
4. Do an indicator blank: 50 ml water + 1 ml chromate + a little CaCO3 (to imitate the white precipitate); titrate to the same tint and subtract.

**Assay**

5. Accurately weigh about 0.1 g of the sample; dissolve in 50 ml water.
6. Add 1 ml potassium chromate solution and titrate with standardised AgNO3 as above.
7. Repeat for concordant results.

## Observations and calculation

Standardisation: 0.1000 g NaCl used 17.05 ml (blank 0.05 ml); net 17.00 ml. Expected = 0.1000/0.005844 = 17.11 ml; f = 17.11/17.00 = **1.006**.

## Precautions

1. Keep pH between about 6.5 and 9; neutralise acidic samples with sodium bicarbonate or borax.
2. Swirl constantly; local excess of Ag⁺ gives a premature red colour.
3. Store AgNO3 in amber bottles; avoid direct light.
4. Do the indicator blank.
5. Collect silver residues for recovery.

## Viva questions

1. **Why must the solution be neutral?** In acid, chromate becomes dichromate and the end point is late; in alkali, silver oxide precipitates.
2. **Why does AgCl precipitate before Ag2CrO4?** Its solubility product is reached at a much lower silver ion concentration.
3. **Why is an indicator blank needed?** A small excess of Ag⁺ is required before the red colour is visible.
4. **Can Mohr's method be used for iodide?** No, AgI adsorbs chromate and the end point is unclear.
5. **Name two other methods for chloride.** Volhard's (back titration with thiocyanate and ferric alum) and Fajans' (adsorption indicator).
