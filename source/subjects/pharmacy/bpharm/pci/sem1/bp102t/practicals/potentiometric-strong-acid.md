# Potentiometric titration of strong acid against strong base

**Aim:** To determine the normality of the given hydrochloric acid by potentiometric titration with standard sodium hydroxide, locating the end point by the direct, first-derivative and second-derivative methods.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles, a lab coat and gloves.

## Principle

A **glass electrode** (indicator electrode, responds to H⁺) and a **saturated calomel** or Ag/AgCl electrode (reference) are dipped in the acid. The cell potential follows the Nernst equation, changing by about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C; the pH meter shows pH directly.

As NaOH is added, pH rises slowly, then jumps sharply near the equivalence point (about pH 4 to 10 for 0.1 N solutions), then levels off. The end point is:

- the **inflection point** of the pH vs volume curve;
- the **maximum** of ΔpH/ΔV plotted against mean volume (first derivative);
- the volume where **Δ²pH/ΔV²** changes sign, i.e. crosses zero (second derivative).

N(HCl) × V(HCl) = N(NaOH) × V(NaOH at end point)

## Requirements

pH meter with glass and reference electrodes (or a combination electrode), standard buffers (pH 4.0, 7.0, 9.2), standardised 0.1 N NaOH, HCl sample (about 0.1 N), beaker, magnetic stirrer, burette, pipette, graph paper.

## Procedure

1. Calibrate the pH meter with standard buffers at room temperature.
2. Pipette 25 ml of HCl into a beaker; add water if needed to cover the electrode; immerse the electrode and stir gently.
3. Record the initial pH.
4. Add NaOH in 1 ml portions, recording pH after each; near the expected end point (where pH changes quickly) add 0.1–0.2 ml portions.
5. Continue a few ml beyond the end point.
6. Plot (a) pH vs V, (b) ΔpH/ΔV vs mean V, (c) Δ²pH/ΔV² vs V.

N(HCl) = 0.1000 × 25.0/25.0 = **0.1000 N** (with a titrant factor, multiply accordingly; for example with 0.0998 N NaOH, N = 0.0998 N).

## Precautions

1. Calibrate the pH meter with buffers at the working temperature.
2. Rinse the electrode between solutions; keep the glass bulb wet and do not wipe it hard.
3. Add small increments near the end point.
4. Stir gently and wait for a stable reading.
5. Do not let the stirrer bar hit the electrode.

## Viva questions

1. **Which electrodes are used?** Glass electrode (indicator) and saturated calomel or Ag/AgCl (reference).
2. **Why is the second derivative most precise?** It locates the exact point where the slope is greatest, as a zero crossing.
3. **What is the pH at the equivalence point here?** About 7 (strong acid–strong base).
4. **Why calibrate with buffers?** To correct for the asymmetry potential and slope of the glass electrode.
5. **Advantages of potentiometric titration?** No indicator; works with coloured or turbid solutions, weak acids and non-aqueous media; can be automated.
