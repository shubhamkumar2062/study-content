# Neutralising capacity of aluminium hydroxide gel

**Aim:** To determine the acid-neutralising capacity of the given aluminium hydroxide gel.

> The weight of gel, volume of acid, temperature, contact time and minimum required capacity are fixed by the IP monograph. Use the values in the current IP and your lab manual; the figures in the worked example are for practice only.

## Principle

An antacid is useful only if it can neutralise a real amount of gastric acid. In this test a weighed quantity of the gel is kept in contact with a measured **excess of 0.1 M hydrochloric acid** at body temperature (37 °C), so that it reacts as it would in the stomach. The acid left over is then titrated with **0.1 M sodium hydroxide** to an end point of about **pH 3.5** (pH meter, or bromophenol blue as indicator). The difference gives the acid neutralised.

Al(OH)3 + 3HCl → AlCl3 + 3H2O (reaction with acid)
HCl (excess) + NaOH → NaCl + H2O (back titration)

The end point is taken at pH about 3.5 rather than 7 because aluminium chloride hydrolyses and would start precipitating aluminium hydroxide again at higher pH, and because an antacid only needs to raise gastric pH to about 3–4.

## Requirements

Conical flasks with stoppers, water bath or incubator at 37 °C, magnetic stirrer (if available), burettes, pipettes, pH meter (or bromophenol blue indicator), weighing balance; 0.1 M hydrochloric acid and 0.1 M sodium hydroxide (both standardised), aluminium hydroxide gel.

## Procedure

1. Shake the gel well and weigh accurately the specified quantity (W g) into a conical flask.
2. Add a measured excess of 0.1 M hydrochloric acid (V1 ml) from a burette or pipette.
3. Stopper and keep at 37 °C for the specified time, shaking or stirring frequently.
4. Cool if needed, add indicator (or place the pH meter electrode), and titrate the excess acid with 0.1 M sodium hydroxide to pH 3.5 (V2 ml).
5. Perform a blank (acid alone, titrated in the same way) to check the acid strength.
6. Repeat for concordant values.

If the molarities are not exactly 0.1, correct them:

**ml of 0.1 M HCl neutralised per g = [(V1 × M_HCl) − (V2 × M_NaOH)] ÷ (0.1 × W)**

**Worked example.** W = 2.00 g gel; V1 = 50.0 ml of 0.100 M HCl; V2 = 22.0 ml of 0.100 M NaOH.
HCl consumed = 50.0 − 22.0 = 28.0 ml of 0.1 M → 28.0 ÷ 2.00 = **14.0 ml of 0.1 M HCl per g of gel**.

## Result

## Precautions

1. Shake the gel well before weighing; the solid settles.
2. Keep the temperature at 37 °C and the contact time exactly as stated.
3. Stopper the flask to avoid evaporation.
4. Standardise both acid and alkali before use.
5. Approach the end point slowly; the gel reacts slowly, so wait for a stable reading.

## Viva questions

1. **What is acid-neutralising capacity?** The amount of acid that a given quantity of antacid can neutralise under set conditions.
2. **Why is the test done at 37 °C?** To mimic body temperature in the stomach.
3. **Why is back titration used?** Aluminium hydroxide reacts slowly and is insoluble, so excess acid is added and the remainder titrated.
4. **Why is the end point pH 3.5?** Aluminium chloride would reprecipitate aluminium hydroxide at higher pH, and pH 3–4 is enough for antacid action.
5. **Why is aluminium hydroxide often combined with magnesium hydroxide?** Aluminium causes constipation, magnesium causes loose stools; together they balance and act quickly and for long.
6. **Name two side effects of aluminium hydroxide.** Constipation and reduced phosphate absorption.
