# Limit test for chlorides and sulphates

**Aim:** To perform the limit tests for chloride and sulphate on the sample given by your teacher (for example boric acid).

> Quantities below follow the older IP general method used in most college manuals. Use the sample weight and volumes given in the monograph of your sample and in your lab manual; check the current IP.

## Principle

**Chloride.** Chloride ions react with silver nitrate in dilute nitric acid to give silver chloride, seen as an opalescence. Nitric acid prevents the precipitation of other silver salts (carbonate, phosphate).

Cl⁻ + AgNO3 → AgCl ↓ (white) + NO3⁻

**Sulphate.** Sulphate ions react with barium chloride (as barium sulphate reagent) in dilute hydrochloric acid to give a white turbidity of barium sulphate. The potassium sulphate in the reagent seeds the precipitate and the alcohol prevents supersaturation, giving a uniform turbidity.

SO4²⁻ + BaCl2 → BaSO4 ↓ (white) + 2Cl⁻

The turbidity of the test is compared with that of a standard containing a fixed amount of chloride or sulphate.

## Requirements

Nessler cylinders (matched pairs, 50 ml), glass rods, pipettes, measuring cylinders; dilute nitric acid, 0.1 M silver nitrate solution, standard sodium chloride solution (0.05845% w/v NaCl), dilute hydrochloric acid, barium sulphate reagent, standard potassium sulphate solution (0.1089% w/v K2SO4), purified water, sample.

## Procedure

**A. Chloride**

| Test | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Dissolve the specified quantity of sample in purified water in a Nessler cylinder. | 1. Take 1 ml of standard sodium chloride solution (0.05845% w/v) in a Nessler cylinder. |
| 2. Add 10 ml of dilute nitric acid. | 2. Add 10 ml of dilute nitric acid. |
| 3. Dilute to 50 ml with water. | 3. Dilute to 50 ml with water. |
| 4. Add 1 ml of 0.1 M silver nitrate, stir with a glass rod. | 4. Add 1 ml of 0.1 M silver nitrate, stir. |
| 5. Keep aside for 5 minutes away from direct light. | 5. Keep aside for 5 minutes away from direct light. |

Compare the opalescence by viewing across the cylinders against a black background.

**B. Sulphate**

| Test | Standard |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Dissolve the specified quantity of sample in water in a Nessler cylinder. | 1. Take 1 ml of standard potassium sulphate solution (0.1089% w/v). |
| 2. Add 2 ml of dilute hydrochloric acid. | 2. Add 2 ml of dilute hydrochloric acid. |
| 3. Dilute to 45 ml with water. | 3. Dilute to 45 ml with water. |
| 4. Add 5 ml of barium sulphate reagent, stir. | 4. Add 5 ml of barium sulphate reagent, stir. |
| 5. Keep aside for 5 minutes. | 5. Keep aside for 5 minutes. |

Compare the turbidity across the cylinders against a black background.

## Observations

| Test for | Observation in test | Observation in standard | Inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chloride | Opalescence: less / more than standard | Opalescence | Passes / fails |
| Sulphate | Turbidity: less / more than standard | Turbidity | Passes / fails |

**What the standard represents.** 1 ml of 0.05845% w/v NaCl contains 0.5845 mg NaCl, i.e. about 0.355 mg of chloride. If the sample weight is 1 g, the limit is about 355 ppm of chloride. Work out the limit for your sample weight in the same way.

## Result

The given sample **passes / does not pass** the limit tests for chloride and sulphate as per the IP.

## Precautions

1. Use matched Nessler cylinders of the same glass and diameter.
2. Add reagents in the same order and amounts to test and standard.
3. Stir each cylinder with a separate clean rod; do not touch the inner walls.
4. View turbidity across, not down, against a dark background.
5. Keep the chloride cylinders out of sunlight (silver chloride darkens).
6. Use purified (chloride- and sulphate-free) water and clean glassware.

## Viva questions

1. **What is a limit test?** A semi-quantitative test that shows whether an impurity is above or below a fixed limit, by comparing with a standard.
2. **Why is nitric acid used in the chloride test?** To prevent precipitation of other silver salts and to give a uniform opalescence; HCl would add chloride.
3. **Why is hydrochloric acid used in the sulphate test?** To stop barium carbonate and phosphate precipitating.
4. **Why does barium sulphate reagent contain potassium sulphate?** It gives seed crystals so the turbidity forms quickly and reproducibly.
5. **Why alcohol in the reagent?** It prevents supersaturation and gives a uniform turbidity.
6. **Why are Nessler cylinders used?** They are matched, so equal depths of liquid can be compared fairly.
7. **Why is chloride or sulphate tested if it is not toxic?** It indicates the quality of the process and of the water used.
