# Preparation of ferrous sulphate

**Aim:** To prepare ferrous sulphate heptahydrate from iron and dilute sulphuric acid and report the practical yield.

## Principle

Iron dissolves in dilute sulphuric acid with evolution of hydrogen, giving pale green ferrous sulphate:

Fe + H2SO4 → FeSO4 + H2 ↑

The solution is concentrated and cooled, and **FeSO4·7H2O** (green vitriol) crystallises. Iron is kept **in excess** so that all the acid is used and the ferrous salt is not oxidised to ferric salt (excess iron reduces any ferric ions formed: Fe + 2Fe³⁺ → 3Fe²⁺). A few drops of dilute sulphuric acid are added before crystallisation to prevent hydrolysis and oxidation.

Molecular weights: Fe 55.85; FeSO4·7H2O 278.0. So 55.85 g of iron gives 278.0 g of the heptahydrate.

## Requirements

Beakers or conical flask (250 ml), glass rod, tripod, wire gauze, burner, water bath, funnel and filter paper, crystallising dish, weighing balance; iron filings or iron wire (grease-free) about 3 g, dilute sulphuric acid (about 10% w/v) 25 ml, purified water.

## Procedure

1. Weigh accurately about 3 g of clean, grease-free iron filings into a conical flask.
2. Add about 25 ml of dilute sulphuric acid. Warm gently in a fume cupboard; hydrogen is evolved (keep flames away from the mouth of the flask).
3. When effervescence almost stops (some iron should remain undissolved), filter the hot solution quickly into a clean dish containing a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid.
4. Wash the undissolved iron on the filter with a little hot water, dry it and weigh it, to find how much iron dissolved.
5. Concentrate the filtrate on a water bath until crystals just start to appear at the edge (a drop placed on a cold glass rod crystallises).
6. Cover and allow to cool slowly. Pale bluish-green crystals separate.
7. Decant the mother liquor, wash crystals with a very little ice-cold water (or alcohol), and dry quickly between filter papers. Store in a tightly closed bottle.
8. Weigh the crystals and calculate the yield.

## Observations and calculation

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Weight of iron taken | ____ g |
| Weight of undissolved iron recovered | ____ g |
| Iron dissolved | taken − recovered |
| Theoretical yield of FeSO4·7H2O | iron dissolved × 278.0 / 55.85 |
| Practical yield | ____ g |
| Percentage yield | (practical ÷ theoretical) × 100 |

**Worked example.** Iron taken 3.00 g; recovered 1.88 g; dissolved 1.12 g. Theoretical yield = 1.12 × 278.0 / 55.85 = 5.57 g. Crystals obtained 4.20 g → % yield = **75.4%**.

**Identity checks.** Pale green crystals; dark blue precipitate with potassium ferricyanide (Fe²⁺); white precipitate with barium chloride insoluble in HCl (sulphate).

## Precautions

1. Keep iron in excess throughout to prevent formation of ferric salt.
2. Hydrogen is inflammable: no naked flame near the flask mouth; work in a fume cupboard.
3. Filter while hot to avoid crystallisation on the filter paper.
4. Do not evaporate to dryness or overheat; the salt loses water and oxidises.
5. Dry the crystals quickly and store in an airtight container away from moisture.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is iron kept in excess?** To use up all the acid and to reduce any ferric salt formed back to ferrous.
2. **Why is dilute sulphuric acid added to the filtrate?** To prevent hydrolysis and oxidation of ferrous sulphate.
3. **Why do ferrous sulphate crystals turn brown on storage?** Oxidation in moist air to basic ferric sulphate.
4. **What is dried ferrous sulphate?** Ferrous sulphate partly dehydrated by gentle heat; more stable, used in tablets.
5. **How is ferrous sulphate assayed?** By titration with ceric ammonium sulphate using ferroin indicator.
6. **What is its main use?** Treatment and prevention of iron-deficiency anaemia.
