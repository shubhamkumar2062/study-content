# Potassium iodate and free iodine in potassium iodide

**Aim:** To test the given potassium iodide for the impurities potassium iodate and free iodine, and to assay its potassium iodide content.

> Sample weights and time limits are given in the IP monograph for potassium iodide. Use the values in the current IP and your lab manual.

## Principle

**Why these impurities occur.** Potassium iodide is made from iodine and potassium hydroxide, which first gives a mixture of iodide and **iodate**; the iodate is reduced with charcoal. Incomplete reduction leaves iodate. On storage in moist air and light, iodide is slowly oxidised to **free iodine**, which makes the salt yellow.

**Test for iodate.** In acid solution, any iodate present reacts with iodide to liberate iodine, which turns starch blue.

KIO3 + 5KI + 3H2SO4 → 3I2 + 3K2SO4 + 3H2O

A pure sample gives no blue colour within the stated time (air slowly oxidises acidified iodide too, so a late faint colour is ignored).

**Test for free iodine.** Free iodine dissolves in chloroform with a violet colour, or gives a blue colour with starch. A pure sample gives a colourless chloroform layer.

**Assay of KI (Andrews' method).** KI is titrated with 0.05 M potassium iodate in strong hydrochloric acid. Iodine first released (violet in chloroform) is finally converted to iodine monochloride; the end point is the disappearance of the violet colour from the chloroform layer.

KIO3 + 2KI + 6HCl → 3ICl + 3KCl + 3H2O
Each ml of 0.05 M KIO3 ≡ 0.0166 g KI.

## Requirements

Test tubes, glass-stoppered conical flask (iodine flask), burette, pipettes, weighing balance; freshly boiled and cooled water, dilute sulphuric acid, starch solution, chloroform, concentrated hydrochloric acid, 0.05 M potassium iodate, potassium iodide sample.

## Procedure

**A. Test for iodate**

1. Dissolve the specified quantity of KI (about 1 g) in about 10 ml of freshly boiled and cooled water.
2. Add a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid and a few drops of starch solution.
3. Observe for a blue colour within the time stated in the IP.

**B. Test for free iodine**

1. Dissolve about 1 g of KI in about 10 ml of water.
2. Add 1–2 ml of chloroform, shake and allow to separate (or add starch solution).
3. Observe the colour of the chloroform layer.

**C. Assay**

1. Weigh accurately about 0.2 g of KI and dissolve in about 10 ml of water in an iodine flask.
2. Add about 35 ml of concentrated hydrochloric acid and 5 ml of chloroform.
3. Titrate with 0.05 M potassium iodate, shaking vigorously after each addition. The chloroform turns violet, then the colour fades.
4. Continue dropwise until the violet colour of the chloroform layer just disappears; this is the end point.

**Worked example.** W = 0.2000 g; V = 11.95 ml of 0.05 M KIO3. KI found = 11.95 × 0.0166 = 0.1984 g → **99.2% KI**.

## Precautions

1. Use freshly boiled and cooled water (free from dissolved oxygen) for the iodate test.
2. Read the iodate test within the stated time; a late faint colour is due to air oxidation.
3. Handle concentrated HCl in a fume cupboard.
4. Shake the iodine flask vigorously near the end point so iodine passes between the layers.
5. Store KI in well-closed, light-resistant containers.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is iodate an impurity in KI?** It is formed during manufacture from iodine and KOH and may not be fully reduced.
2. **Why does KI turn yellow on storage?** Air and light oxidise iodide to free iodine.
3. **What colour does iodine give in chloroform?** Violet.
4. **Why is strong HCl used in the assay?** To keep iodine monochloride stable so the end point is sharp.
5. **What is the end point in Andrews' titration?** Disappearance of the violet colour from the chloroform layer.
6. **Give two uses of potassium iodide.** Expectorant; with iodine before thyroid surgery and in thyroid blocking during radiation emergencies.
