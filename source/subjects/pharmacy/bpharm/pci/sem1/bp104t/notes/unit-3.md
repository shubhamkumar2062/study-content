# Unit 3: Gastrointestinal agents and topical antimicrobials

This unit covers inorganic compounds that act on the stomach and intestine (acidifiers, antacids and cathartics) and inorganic antimicrobials used on skin, wounds and water. Compounds marked with an asterisk in the syllabus (ammonium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, hydrogen peroxide and chlorinated lime) need preparation, properties, assay and uses. Antacids (ideal properties and combinations), hydrogen peroxide and chlorinated lime are the most repeated topics.

For every compound, learn four things in this order: **preparation (with equation) → properties → assay principle → uses**. That order is what examiners expect.

## Part A: Gastrointestinal agents

## 3.1 Gastric acid in brief

The parietal cells of the stomach secrete hydrochloric acid, so the fasting stomach has a pH of about 1–3. Acid helps digestion (activates pepsinogen to pepsin), kills swallowed micro-organisms, and helps the absorption of iron and calcium.

- **Achlorhydria / hypochlorhydria**: little or no acid secretion (in pernicious anaemia, some gastric cancers, old age). Treated with **acidifiers**.
- **Hyperacidity, heartburn, peptic ulcer, gastro-oesophageal reflux**: too much acid or acid in the wrong place. Relieved by **antacids**.

## 3.2 Acidifiers

Acidifiers are used to (a) increase gastric acidity in achlorhydria (**gastric acidifiers**) or (b) acidify the body fluids or urine (**systemic and urinary acidifiers**).

### Ammonium chloride* (NH4Cl, M = 53.49)

**Preparation.** By neutralising ammonia (or ammonium carbonate) with hydrochloric acid, then evaporating and crystallising; or by reaction of ammonium sulphate with sodium chloride and sublimation.

NH3 + HCl → NH4Cl
(NH4)2CO3 + 2HCl → 2NH4Cl + H2O + CO2

**Properties.** Colourless crystals or white crystalline powder, cooling saline taste, hygroscopic, freely soluble in water. Sublimes on heating without melting. Solution is slightly acidic (salt of a weak base and strong acid). On warming with sodium hydroxide it gives ammonia (smell, turns red litmus blue).

**Assay (principle): formol titration.** Ammonium chloride is treated with neutralised formaldehyde. Formaldehyde reacts with the ammonium ion to form hexamine and releases an equivalent amount of hydrochloric acid, which is titrated with standard sodium hydroxide using phenolphthalein.

4NH4Cl + 6HCHO → (CH2)6N4 + 4HCl + 6H2O
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O

Each ml of 0.1 M NaOH ≡ 0.005349 g NH4Cl.

The formaldehyde is neutralised before use because it usually contains some formic acid, which would add to the titre.

**Uses.**

- **Systemic acidifier**: in the liver, NH4⁺ is converted to urea, leaving H⁺ and Cl⁻; used for metabolic alkalosis.
- **Urinary acidifier**: acid urine helps the excretion of basic drugs (for example in some poisonings) and the action of some urinary antiseptics.
- **Expectorant** (Unit 4): irritates the stomach lining and reflexly increases bronchial secretion.
- Earlier used to potentiate mercurial diuretics.

It should be avoided in liver failure (ammonia toxicity) and kidney failure.

### Dilute hydrochloric acid

**Preparation.** Made by diluting concentrated hydrochloric acid (about 36% w/w HCl) with purified water. The IP dilute acid contains about **10% w/v HCl**.

**Properties.** Clear colourless liquid, strongly acidic, gives chloride tests.

**Assay (principle).** Acid–base titration of a weighed quantity with standard sodium hydroxide using methyl red or phenolphthalein.

**Uses.** In achlorhydria to replace gastric acid; it is taken well diluted and **through a straw** (to protect the teeth), usually with meals. It is also a laboratory reagent.

## 3.3 Antacids

An **antacid** is a weakly basic substance that neutralises gastric hydrochloric acid and so raises the gastric pH. It gives quick relief of heartburn and dyspepsia and helps ulcer healing.

### Ideal properties of an antacid

1. Should not be absorbed into the blood (no systemic alkalosis).
2. Should act quickly and its effect should last long.
3. Should not raise gastric pH too high; a pH of about 4–5 is enough (pepsin activity falls above pH 4) and avoids acid rebound.
4. Should not release carbon dioxide (no belching or distension).
5. Should not cause constipation or diarrhoea.
6. Should not interfere with absorption of other drugs or nutrients.
7. Should have a good buffering action in the pH range 4–5.
8. Should be palatable, cheap and stable.

No single antacid meets all these, so combinations are used.

### Classification

```text
ANTACIDS
├── Systemic (soluble, absorbed): sodium bicarbonate, sodium citrate
└── Non-systemic (insoluble, not absorbed in useful amounts)
       ├── Aluminium compounds: aluminium hydroxide gel, aluminium phosphate
       ├── Magnesium compounds: magnesium hydroxide, magnesium oxide,
       │                         magnesium carbonate, magnesium trisilicate
       ├── Calcium compounds: calcium carbonate
       └── Combinations: magaldrate (aluminium–magnesium complex)
```

### Combinations of antacids

| Combination | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Aluminium hydroxide + magnesium hydroxide (or trisilicate) | Al is slow and constipating; Mg is fast and laxative; together they give quick, lasting action with normal bowel habit |
| Antacid + simethicone (dimethicone) | Antifoaming agent breaks gas bubbles; relieves bloating |
| Antacid + oxetacaine (oxethazaine) | Local anaesthetic relieves pain of gastritis |
| Antacid + alginic acid / sodium alginate | Forms a viscous raft on the stomach contents; useful in reflux |

### Sodium bicarbonate* (NaHCO3, M = 84.01)

**Preparation.**

1. By passing carbon dioxide into a cold saturated solution of sodium carbonate:
   Na2CO3 + CO2 + H2O → 2NaHCO3
2. Industrially by the **Solvay (ammonia–soda) process**: carbon dioxide is passed into brine saturated with ammonia; the less soluble sodium bicarbonate separates.
   NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O → NaHCO3 + NH4Cl

**Properties.** White crystalline powder, saline slightly alkaline taste, soluble in water, insoluble in alcohol. Solution is mildly alkaline. On heating above about 50 °C, and in hot solution, it loses carbon dioxide and changes to sodium carbonate:
2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2
It effervesces with acids.

**Assay (principle).** Acid–base titration. A weighed sample in water is titrated with standard hydrochloric acid using methyl orange as indicator.

NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2
Each ml of 1 M HCl ≡ 0.08401 g NaHCO3.

**Uses.** Quick-acting antacid; systemic alkaliser for metabolic acidosis (IV in serious cases); urinary alkaliser (to help excretion of acidic drugs and prevent crystalluria); ingredient of effervescent granules; ear-drops to soften wax.

**Drawbacks as an antacid**: absorbed, so it can cause **systemic alkalosis**; releases **carbon dioxide** (belching, distension, risk in ulcers); **acid rebound**; high **sodium load** (unsuitable in heart failure, hypertension, oedema); milk-alkali syndrome with milk and calcium.

### Aluminium hydroxide gel

**Preparation.** By adding a solution of an aluminium salt (aluminium chloride or potash alum) to a solution of sodium carbonate (or ammonia) with stirring; the gelatinous aluminium hydroxide is washed free of soluble salts and suspended in water, with a flavour, sweetener and preservative.

2AlCl3 + 3Na2CO3 + 3H2O → 2Al(OH)3 + 6NaCl + 3CO2
2KAl(SO4)2 + 3Na2CO3 + 3H2O → 2Al(OH)3 + K2SO4 + 3Na2SO4 + 3CO2

**Properties.** White, viscous suspension; translucent in thin layers. Aluminium hydroxide is amphoteric: it dissolves in acids and in alkali hydroxides.
Al(OH)3 + 3HCl → AlCl3 + 3H2O

**Assay (principle).** Complexometric **back titration**: the sample is dissolved in acid, a known excess of disodium edetate is added and the mixture heated (aluminium reacts slowly with EDTA), then the excess EDTA is titrated with standard zinc sulphate using dithizone (or another suitable indicator). Results are expressed as Al2O3 (each ml of 0.05 M disodium edetate ≡ 0.002549 g Al2O3). The IP also checks **acid-neutralising capacity** (practical).

**Uses.** Antacid in hyperacidity and peptic ulcer (slow, prolonged action); phosphate binder in kidney failure; mild astringent and demulcent.

**Side effects.** Constipation; reduced absorption of phosphate (low phosphate with long use) and of some drugs such as tetracyclines; aluminium may accumulate in kidney failure.

### Magnesium hydroxide mixture (milk of magnesia)

**Preparation.** By adding sodium hydroxide solution to magnesium sulphate solution (or by hydration of magnesium oxide); the precipitate is washed and suspended in water.

MgSO4 + 2NaOH → Mg(OH)2 + Na2SO4

**Properties.** White, opaque suspension; magnesium hydroxide is practically insoluble in water and dissolves in dilute acids. Kept in well-closed containers (absorbs carbon dioxide).

**Assay (principle).** Dissolved in acid and titrated complexometrically with disodium edetate at pH about 10 using mordant black II (each ml of 0.05 M disodium edetate ≡ 0.002916 g Mg(OH)2).

**Uses.** Antacid in small doses; **osmotic laxative** in larger doses (unabsorbed magnesium holds water in the gut). Combined with aluminium hydroxide to cancel constipation. Avoided in kidney failure (magnesium retention).

### How an antacid neutralises acid

| Antacid | Reaction with HCl | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium bicarbonate | NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2 | Fast, CO2, systemic |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Al(OH)3 + 3HCl → AlCl3 + 3H2O | Slow, constipating |
| Magnesium hydroxide | Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl → MgCl2 + 2H2O | Fast, laxative |
| Calcium carbonate | CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2 | Potent, constipating, acid rebound |

## 3.4 Cathartics

**Cathartics (laxatives, purgatives)** promote bowel evacuation. Inorganic cathartics are mostly **saline (osmotic) laxatives**: their ions are poorly absorbed, so they hold water in the intestine by osmosis, increase the bulk of the contents and stimulate peristalsis. They act within a few hours and are used for bowel cleansing before procedures and to remove poisons and worms after anthelmintics. They should be taken with plenty of water, and they are avoided in dehydration and kidney failure (magnesium, phosphate).

### Magnesium sulphate (Epsom salt, MgSO4·7H2O, M = 246.5)

**Preparation.** From the mineral kieserite (MgSO4·H2O) by recrystallisation, or by treating magnesium carbonate (or magnesite) with dilute sulphuric acid:

MgCO3 + H2SO4 → MgSO4 + H2O + CO2

**Properties.** Colourless prismatic crystals, cooling saline bitter taste, efflorescent in warm dry air, very soluble in water.

**Assay (principle).** Complexometric titration with disodium edetate at pH 10 using mordant black II.

**Uses.** Saline purgative; **anticonvulsant in eclampsia** (by injection); treatment of magnesium deficiency; paste with glycerin to draw pus from boils (hygroscopic action). Overdose by injection causes respiratory depression; calcium gluconate is the antidote.

### Sodium orthophosphate (dibasic sodium phosphate, Na2HPO4·12H2O)

**Preparation.** By neutralising phosphoric acid with sodium carbonate, then crystallising:

H3PO4 + Na2CO3 → Na2HPO4 + H2O + CO2

**Properties.** Colourless transparent crystals, saline taste, strongly efflorescent (loses water in air), soluble in water, solution alkaline to phenolphthalein.

**Uses.** Saline cathartic; phosphate **enemas** and oral solutions for bowel preparation before colonoscopy or surgery; buffer component; phosphate supplement. Large doses can upset electrolyte balance, especially in elderly patients and kidney disease.

### Kaolin (light kaolin)

**Source.** A native hydrated **aluminium silicate** (china clay), purified by **levigation** (elutriation) with water to remove sand and coarse particles. "Light kaolin" is the finely divided form used internally; heavy kaolin is used for poultices.

**Properties.** Light, white, odourless, unctuous (smooth, soft) powder; insoluble in water, dilute acids and alkalis. Has a large surface area and strong **adsorbent** properties.

**Uses.** Although listed here, kaolin is not a purgative; it acts as an **adsorbent antidiarrhoeal** (adsorbs toxins and bacteria; kaolin mixtures); dusting powder; kaolin poultice (heavy kaolin with glycerin, boric acid and aromatic oils) to keep heat on inflamed areas; filtering aid and clarifying agent; diluent in tablets.

### Bentonite

**Source.** A native colloidal hydrated aluminium silicate, consisting mainly of the clay mineral **montmorillonite**. It is cleaned, dried and powdered.

**Properties.** Very fine, cream to greyish, odourless powder. Insoluble in water but **swells** to many times its volume, forming a colloidal suspension or gel (thixotropic). The IP checks its **swelling power** and gel formation.

**Uses.** **Suspending agent** and emulsion stabiliser (calamine lotion), base for ointments and lotions, clarifying agent; its water-holding swelling is the reason it is grouped with cathartics (bulk effect). Also an adsorbent.

## Part B: Antimicrobials

## 3.5 Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Antiseptic | Inhibits or kills micro-organisms on living tissue (skin, wounds, mucosa) |
| Disinfectant | Kills micro-organisms on non-living objects (floors, instruments, water) |
| Germicide (-cidal) | Kills micro-organisms |
| Bacteriostatic (-static) | Stops growth without killing |
| Sanitiser | Reduces micro-organisms to a safe public-health level |
| Sterilisation | Complete removal or killing of all living forms, including spores |

## 3.6 Mechanism and classification of inorganic antimicrobials

| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxidising agents | Release nascent oxygen or other oxidising species that oxidise –SH groups and other parts of microbial proteins and enzymes | Potassium permanganate, hydrogen peroxide, sodium perborate |
| Halogens and halogen-releasing compounds | Chlorine and iodine oxidise and halogenate proteins; chlorine forms hypochlorous acid in water | Iodine and its solutions, chlorinated lime, sodium hypochlorite |
| Protein precipitants (heavy metal ions) | Combine with –SH groups and precipitate proteins | Silver nitrate, mercury compounds, copper sulphate, zinc sulphate |
| Weak acids | Mild bacteriostatic action | Boric acid |

## 3.7 Potassium permanganate (KMnO4, M = 158.0)

**Preparation.** Manganese dioxide (pyrolusite) is fused with potassium hydroxide and an oxidising agent such as potassium chlorate to give green potassium manganate. The manganate is then oxidised to permanganate by passing carbon dioxide (or chlorine, or by electrolytic oxidation).

3MnO2 + 6KOH + KClO3 → 3K2MnO4 + KCl + 3H2O
3K2MnO4 + 2CO2 → 2KMnO4 + MnO2 + 2K2CO3

**Properties.** Dark purple, almost black crystals with a metallic sheen; soluble in water giving a deep purple solution. A strong oxidising agent, acting differently in acid (Mn²⁺, colourless) and in neutral or alkaline medium (brown MnO2). Stains skin brown. Explosive with some organic substances and concentrated acids.

**Assay (principle).** Iodometric: in acid, permanganate oxidises potassium iodide to iodine, which is titrated with sodium thiosulphate using starch.

2KMnO4 + 10KI + 8H2SO4 → 6K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 5I2 + 8H2O
Each ml of 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate ≡ 0.003161 g KMnO4.

**Uses.** Dilute solutions (about 1 in 5,000 to 1 in 10,000) as antiseptic for cleaning wounds and ulcers, soaks for weeping eczema and fungal infections of the feet, and gargles; gastric lavage in some poisonings (oxidises alkaloids and some organic poisons); disinfecting water and vegetables; oxidising agent and titrant in analysis. Concentrated solutions burn tissue.

## 3.8 Boric acid (H3BO3, M = 61.83)

**Preparation.** By adding hydrochloric or sulphuric acid to a hot concentrated solution of borax; boric acid crystallises on cooling.

Na2B4O7 + 2HCl + 5H2O → 4H3BO3 + 2NaCl
Na2B4O7 + H2SO4 + 5H2O → 4H3BO3 + Na2SO4

**Properties.** Colourless, shiny plates or white powder, unctuous to touch; soluble in water and more so in boiling water and glycerin. A very weak acid (pKa about 9.2); it acts as a Lewis acid, accepting OH⁻ from water. Burns with a green-edged flame when its alcoholic solution is ignited (borate test).

**Assay (principle).** Boric acid is too weak to titrate directly with sodium hydroxide. **Glycerin or mannitol** is added; it forms a complex (glyceroboric or mannitoboric acid) that is a much stronger acid and can be titrated with standard sodium hydroxide using phenolphthalein.

Each ml of 1 M NaOH ≡ 0.06183 g H3BO3.

**Uses.** Weak antiseptic and buffer in eye washes and eye drops; dusting powder; ear drops; in borate buffers. **Toxic** if absorbed in large amounts; it must not be applied to large raw areas or to infants' skin, and is not taken internally.

## 3.9 Hydrogen peroxide solution* (H2O2, M = 34.01)

**Preparation.**

1. Laboratory: by adding barium peroxide to cold dilute sulphuric acid; insoluble barium sulphate is filtered off.
   BaO2 + H2SO4 → BaSO4 + H2O2
2. Industrial: by the **anthraquinone (auto-oxidation) process**, in which an alkyl anthraquinol is oxidised by air to the anthraquinone, releasing hydrogen peroxide, and the anthraquinone is hydrogenated back for reuse. Electrolysis of persulphate was an older route.

**Properties.** Clear colourless liquid, slightly acidic, bitter taste. It decomposes slowly on standing and quickly in light, heat, alkali, dust or traces of metals, releasing oxygen:
2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2
Stabilisers (such as a little acid, phosphate or acetanilide) are added and it is stored in a cool, dark place in containers with a vent. It acts as an **oxidising agent** and also as a **reducing agent** (towards permanganate).

**Strength.** Expressed as **volume strength**: "20 volume" means 1 ml of solution gives 20 ml of oxygen at normal temperature and pressure. 20-volume solution contains about 6% w/v H2O2.

**Assay (principle).** Redox titration with standard potassium permanganate in sulphuric acid; permanganate acts as its own indicator (first permanent pink colour).

2KMnO4 + 5H2O2 + 3H2SO4 → K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 5O2 + 8H2O
Each ml of 0.02 M KMnO4 ≡ 0.001701 g H2O2.

**Uses.** Antiseptic and cleansing agent for wounds and ulcers: on contact with tissue catalase it releases oxygen with effervescence, which loosens debris and pus and acts against anaerobes. Mouthwash (diluted), ear drops to soften wax, bleaching of hair, disinfection of surfaces and contact lenses. It should not be injected into closed body cavities (risk of gas embolism).

## 3.10 Chlorinated lime* (bleaching powder)

**Composition.** A mixture of calcium hypochlorite, calcium chloride and calcium hydroxide, sometimes written as CaOCl2 (calcium chlorohypochlorite). Its activity is expressed as **available chlorine**: the chlorine set free when the powder is treated with acid.

**Preparation.** By passing chlorine gas over slaked lime (calcium hydroxide) spread in layers, at a temperature kept low:

Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 → CaOCl2 + H2O

**Properties.** Dull white powder with a smell of chlorine. Partly soluble in water. Decomposes in air and moisture, losing available chlorine (absorbs CO2): CaOCl2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + Cl2. With dilute acids it releases chlorine:
CaOCl2 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + Cl2

**Assay (principle): iodometry.** The powder is triturated with water, potassium iodide and acetic acid are added; the liberated chlorine displaces an equivalent amount of iodine, which is titrated with standard sodium thiosulphate using starch.

CaOCl2 + 2CH3COOH → (CH3COO)2Ca + H2O + Cl2
Cl2 + 2KI → 2KCl + I2
I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6
Each ml of 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate ≡ 0.003545 g available chlorine.

Acetic acid (not a mineral acid) is used so that calcium chlorate, if present, does not release extra iodine. The IP sets a minimum available chlorine content (older texts quote not less than 30% w/w; check the current IP).

**Uses.** Disinfection of drinking water, swimming pools, drains, toilets and hospital floors; deodorant; bleaching. It is used to prepare **surgical chlorinated soda solution (Dakin's solution)**, a wound antiseptic.

## 3.11 Iodine and its preparations

**Source and preparation.** Obtained from seaweed ash (kelp), from the iodate present in crude Chile saltpetre (caliche), and from oil-well brines. From iodate: the solution is reduced with sodium bisulphite to set free iodine, which is purified by **sublimation**.

2NaIO3 + 5NaHSO3 → 3NaHSO4 + 2Na2SO4 + I2 + H2O

**Properties.** Greyish-black, heavy, brittle plates with a metallic lustre; characteristic odour; sublimes on warming to give violet vapour. Very slightly soluble in water but readily soluble in solutions of iodides (forms the soluble triiodide ion, I2 + I⁻ ⇌ I3⁻), alcohol, chloroform (violet). Gives a deep blue colour with starch.

**Assay (principle).** Titration with standard sodium thiosulphate in the presence of potassium iodide, using starch as indicator near the end.
I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6
Each ml of 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate ≡ 0.01269 g I.

**Preparations**

| Preparation | Composition (outline) | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aqueous iodine solution (Lugol's solution) | 5% iodine and 10% potassium iodide in water | Before thyroid surgery; iodine source |
| Weak iodine solution (iodine tincture) | Iodine with potassium iodide in alcohol | Skin antiseptic |
| Strong iodine solution | Higher iodine content in alcohol | Counter-irritant (older use) |
| Povidone-iodine | Iodine complexed with polyvinylpyrrolidone (an iodophor) | Skin and wound antiseptic, surgical scrub; less irritant, does not stain much |
| Iodised salt | Common salt with potassium iodate or iodide | Prevention of iodine-deficiency goitre |

**Uses.** Skin disinfection before injections and surgery, wound antiseptic, fungal infections, water disinfection in emergencies, and as a source of iodine in thyroid disorders. Iodine can cause skin irritation and allergic reactions; large swallowed doses are corrosive.

## Remember

- Ammonium chloride assay = formol titration (HCHO releases HCl, titrate with NaOH; 1 ml 0.1 M ≡ 5.349 mg).
- Dilute HCl (about 10% w/v) for achlorhydria, taken through a straw.
- Antacids: ideal = not absorbed, no CO2, no rebound, quick and long; Al + Mg combination balances constipation and diarrhoea.
- NaHCO3: Solvay process; assay with HCl, methyl orange (1 ml 1 M ≡ 84.01 mg); drawbacks: alkalosis, CO2, sodium load, rebound.
- Saline cathartics (MgSO4, Na2HPO4) work osmotically; kaolin is an adsorbent; bentonite swells (suspending agent).
- Antimicrobial mechanisms: oxidation, halogenation, protein precipitation.
- KMnO4: from MnO2 + KOH + KClO3; antiseptic 1 in 5,000 to 1 in 10,000.
- Boric acid: titrate with NaOH only after adding glycerin or mannitol.
- H2O2: BaO2 + H2SO4; assay with KMnO4 (1 ml 0.02 M ≡ 1.701 mg); 20 volume ≈ 6% w/v.
- Chlorinated lime: Cl2 over slaked lime; assay = available chlorine by iodometry in acetic acid (1 ml 0.1 M thiosulphate ≡ 3.545 mg Cl).
- Iodine dissolves in KI as I3⁻; Lugol's = 5% I2 + 10% KI; povidone-iodine is an iodophor.
