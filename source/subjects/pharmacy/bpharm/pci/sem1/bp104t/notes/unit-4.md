# Unit 4: Miscellaneous compounds

This unit brings together inorganic compounds used as expectorants, emetics, haematinics, antidotes and astringents. Compounds marked with an asterisk in the syllabus are ammonium chloride, copper sulphate, ferrous sulphate and sodium thiosulphate; for these learn preparation, properties, assay and uses in full. Ferrous sulphate, sodium thiosulphate in cyanide poisoning, and copper sulphate assay are the commonest questions.

## 4.1 Expectorants

An **expectorant** increases the volume of bronchial secretion or makes thick sputum thinner, so that it can be coughed up more easily.

- **Sedative (reflex) expectorants** irritate the stomach lining; through a vagal reflex this increases watery bronchial secretion. Examples: ammonium chloride, potassium iodide (also acts directly), ipecacuanha in low doses.
- **Stimulant expectorants** act directly on the bronchial glands. Iodides are concentrated in bronchial secretions and thin them.

### Potassium iodide (KI, M = 166.0)

**Preparation.**

1. Iodine is added to a hot solution of potassium hydroxide until a faint brown colour remains. A mixture of iodide and iodate is formed:
   3I2 + 6KOH → 5KI + KIO3 + 3H2O
2. The solution is evaporated to dryness, mixed with **charcoal** and heated strongly to reduce the iodate to iodide:
   KIO3 + 3C → KI + 3CO
3. The residue is dissolved in water, filtered and crystallised.

Alternatively, iron filings and iodine give ferrosoferric iodide, which is decomposed by potassium carbonate. If reduction is incomplete, **iodate** remains as an impurity (hence the IP purity test for iodate in KI).

**Properties.** Colourless cubical crystals or white powder, saline bitter taste, very soluble in water, soluble in alcohol and glycerin. On exposure to moist air and light it slowly turns yellow because some iodide is oxidised to free iodine. It dissolves iodine to form the triiodide ion, which is why KI is used to make iodine solutions.

**Assay (principle): potassium iodate titration (Andrews' method).** The sample is dissolved in water, concentrated hydrochloric acid and a little chloroform are added, and it is titrated with 0.05 M potassium iodate. At first iodine is liberated and the chloroform turns violet; as titration continues the iodine is converted to **iodine monochloride**, and the end point is the **disappearance of the violet colour** from the chloroform layer.

KIO3 + 2KI + 6HCl → 3ICl + 3KCl + 3H2O
Each ml of 0.05 M KIO3 ≡ 0.0166 g KI.

The high acid concentration is needed so that the iodine monochloride is stable and does not hydrolyse.

**Uses.**

- Expectorant (in cough mixtures).
- With iodine (Lugol's solution) to prepare the hyperthyroid patient for surgery and in thyroid storm (high iodide temporarily blocks hormone release).
- Source of iodine; iodisation of salt.
- **Radiation emergencies**: blocks thyroid uptake of radioactive iodine (Unit 5).
- Antifungal in sporotrichosis.
- To solubilise iodine in solutions and as a laboratory reagent.

Long use can cause **iodism**: metallic taste, running nose, salivation, skin rashes and swelling of salivary glands.

### Ammonium chloride*

Covered fully in Unit 3 (preparation, properties, formol titration). As an expectorant it acts reflexly by mild gastric irritation. It is an ingredient of many cough syrups and is also a systemic and urinary acidifier.

## 4.2 Emetics

An **emetic** induces vomiting. Emetics were once used to empty the stomach after swallowing a poison. Today, induced vomiting is **rarely recommended** because of the risk of aspiration and because it removes little poison; gastric lavage or activated charcoal is preferred. Emetics must never be used after swallowing corrosives or kerosene, or in unconscious or convulsing patients.

- **Centrally acting** emetics stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone (apomorphine).
- **Locally (reflexly) acting** emetics irritate the stomach (copper sulphate, zinc sulphate, ipecacuanha, strong salt solution).

### Copper sulphate* (CuSO4·5H2O, M = 249.7)

**Preparation.**

1. By dissolving copper oxide or copper carbonate in dilute sulphuric acid:
   CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O
   CuCO3 + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O + CO2
2. From scrap copper heated with concentrated sulphuric acid:
   Cu + 2H2SO4 → CuSO4 + SO2 + 2H2O
   Industrially, copper scrap is treated with dilute acid while air is blown through, which avoids SO2.
The blue solution is concentrated and crystallised.

**Properties.** Blue triclinic crystals or blue crystalline powder (**blue vitriol**); efflorescent in dry air. On heating it loses water in steps: a little above 100 °C it forms a pale bluish-white lower hydrate, and at about 250 °C the **white anhydrous salt**, which turns blue again with water (a test for water). Soluble in water. With ammonia it first gives a pale blue precipitate that dissolves in excess to a **deep blue** tetrammine copper(II) complex.

**Assay (principle): iodometric titration.** In a solution made faintly acid with acetic acid, cupric ions oxidise potassium iodide; cuprous iodide precipitates and an equivalent amount of iodine is released, which is titrated with standard sodium thiosulphate using starch.

2CuSO4 + 4KI → 2CuI + I2 + 2K2SO4
I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6
Each ml of 0.1 M sodium thiosulphate ≡ 0.02497 g CuSO4·5H2O.

Near the end point potassium thiocyanate is often added; it converts cuprous iodide to cuprous thiocyanate and releases iodine adsorbed on the precipitate, giving a sharper end point.

**Uses.** Formerly an emetic (acts locally on the stomach); antidote in phosphorus poisoning (older use: coats phosphorus with copper phosphide); **fungicide and algicide** (Bordeaux mixture with lime; in swimming pools); copper supplement in deficiency; in Fehling's and Benedict's reagents. It is **toxic**: overdose causes vomiting, haemolysis, liver and kidney damage.

### Sodium potassium tartrate (Rochelle salt, KNaC4H4O6·4H2O)

**Preparation.** By neutralising a hot solution of potassium hydrogen tartrate (cream of tartar) with sodium carbonate, then crystallising:

2KHC4H4O6 + Na2CO3 → 2KNaC4H4O6 + H2O + CO2

**Properties.** Colourless crystals or white powder, cooling saline taste, freely soluble in water.

**Uses.** In practice it is a **mild saline cathartic** (it is a constituent of compound effervescent powders, Seidlitz powder); used in Fehling's solution B to keep copper in solution. Do not confuse it with **antimony potassium tartrate (tartar emetic)**, the classical emetic.

## 4.3 Haematinics

A **haematinic** is a substance that helps form haemoglobin and red blood cells; used to treat anaemia. Iron is needed for haemoglobin; deficiency causes **microcytic hypochromic (iron-deficiency) anaemia**, very common in India among women, children and pregnant women.

Key facts on iron:

- Iron is absorbed mainly in the duodenum and upper jejunum, as the **ferrous (Fe²⁺)** form.
- Vitamin C and gastric acid increase absorption; antacids, tea (tannins), phosphates, calcium and tetracyclines reduce it.
- Common side effects of oral iron: nausea, epigastric pain, constipation or diarrhoea, **black stools**.
- Iron overdose is dangerous, especially in children; **desferrioxamine** is the chelating antidote. Iron tablets must be kept out of children's reach.

### Ferrous sulphate* (FeSO4·7H2O, M = 278.0)

**Preparation.** By dissolving iron (wire or filings) in dilute sulphuric acid, keeping **iron in excess** so that no ferric salt is formed. The solution is filtered, a little sulphuric acid is added to prevent oxidation and hydrolysis, and the salt is crystallised.

Fe + H2SO4 → FeSO4 + H2

**Properties.** Pale bluish-green crystals (**green vitriol**), efflorescent in dry air. In moist air the surface oxidises and turns brownish-yellow with **basic ferric sulphate**, which is why it is stored in well-closed containers. Freely soluble in water. **Dried ferrous sulphate** (partly dehydrated) is used in tablets because it is more stable and gives a smaller tablet.

**Assay (principle): redox titration with ceric ammonium sulphate.** The sample is dissolved in a mixture of dilute sulphuric acid and freshly boiled and cooled water (to remove dissolved oxygen) and titrated with 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate using **ferroin** (1,10-phenanthroline ferrous complex) as indicator; the colour changes from red to pale blue.

Fe²⁺ + Ce⁴⁺ → Fe³⁺ + Ce³⁺
Each ml of 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate ≡ 0.02780 g FeSO4·7H2O.

Older methods titrate with potassium permanganate in sulphuric acid (self-indicating):
10FeSO4 + 2KMnO4 + 8H2SO4 → 5Fe2(SO4)3 + K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 8H2O

Ceric sulphate is preferred because it is stable, can be used in the presence of chloride, and has only one reduction step.

**Uses.** Treatment and prevention of **iron-deficiency anaemia** (pregnancy, blood loss, poor diet), usually with folic acid in pregnancy. A 200 mg tablet of ferrous sulphate (heptahydrate equivalent) gives about 65 mg of elemental iron. Hydrated ferrous sulphate contains about 20% iron.

### Ferrous gluconate ((C6H11O7)2Fe·2H2O)

**Preparation.** By double decomposition between calcium gluconate and ferrous sulphate (calcium sulphate is removed), or by reacting gluconic acid with iron or ferrous carbonate:

(C6H11O7)2Ca + FeSO4 → (C6H11O7)2Fe + CaSO4

**Properties.** Yellowish-grey or pale greenish-yellow powder or granules with a slight caramel smell; soluble in water. Contains about 12% iron.

**Assay (principle).** Like ferrous sulphate, titration of Fe²⁺ with ceric ammonium sulphate (ferroin indicator).

**Uses.** Iron-deficiency anaemia. It is **less irritating to the stomach** than ferrous sulphate (so better tolerated) but provides less iron per tablet.

| Point | Ferrous sulphate | Ferrous gluconate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron content | About 20% (hydrated) | About 12% |
| Gastric irritation | More | Less |
| Cost | Cheaper | Costlier |

## 4.4 Poisons and antidotes

A **poison** is any substance that harms the body when taken in. An **antidote** counteracts the poison or its effect.

| Type of antidote | How it works | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical (mechanical) | Adsorbs or coats the poison, preventing absorption | Activated charcoal, demulcents |
| Chemical | Reacts with the poison to form a harmless or insoluble product | Sodium thiosulphate for cyanide, chelators (EDTA, dimercaprol) for heavy metals |
| Physiological (pharmacological) | Produces the opposite effect on the body | Atropine for organophosphates, naloxone for opioids, sodium nitrite (forms methaemoglobin to trap cyanide) |

### Sodium thiosulphate* (Na2S2O3·5H2O, M = 248.2)

**Preparation.** By boiling a solution of sodium sulphite with powdered sulphur, filtering and crystallising:

Na2SO3 + S → Na2S2O3

It can also be made by passing sulphur dioxide into a suspension of sodium sulphide and sodium carbonate.

**Properties.** Large colourless transparent crystals ("hypo"), efflorescent in warm dry air, very soluble in water. Acids decompose it, giving sulphur (turbidity) and sulphur dioxide:
Na2S2O3 + 2HCl → 2NaCl + S + SO2 + H2O
It is a reducing agent: it decolourises iodine solution.

**Assay (principle): iodimetry.** The sample is dissolved in water and titrated with standard iodine solution using starch; the end point is the first permanent blue colour.

2Na2S2O3 + I2 → Na2S4O6 + 2NaI
Each ml of 0.05 M iodine ≡ 0.02482 g Na2S2O3·5H2O.

**Uses.**

- **Cyanide poisoning** (with sodium nitrite): thiosulphate provides sulphur for the enzyme **rhodanese**, which converts cyanide to the much less toxic **thiocyanate**, excreted in urine.
  CN⁻ + S2O3²⁻ → SCN⁻ + SO3²⁻
- Topical antifungal (pityriasis versicolor), removal of iodine stains, antidote for some other poisons (as a sulphur donor).
- Volumetric reagent in iodometry; photographic fixer.

### Sodium nitrite (NaNO2, M = 69.0)

**Preparation.** By heating sodium nitrate alone, or better with lead (which removes the oxygen):

2NaNO3 → 2NaNO2 + O2
NaNO3 + Pb → NaNO2 + PbO

**Properties.** White or slightly yellowish hygroscopic crystals or sticks, very soluble in water. Releases brown fumes of nitrogen oxides with acids. Oxidising and reducing properties.

**Assay (principle).** Oxidised by a measured excess of standard potassium permanganate in acid; the excess is determined by back titration.
5NaNO2 + 2KMnO4 + 3H2SO4 → 5NaNO3 + K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 3H2O

**Uses.** **Cyanide poisoning**: injected sodium nitrite converts part of the haemoglobin to **methaemoglobin**, which binds cyanide more strongly than cytochrome oxidase does, forming cyanmethaemoglobin. This frees the respiratory enzyme. Sodium thiosulphate is then given to convert the cyanide to thiocyanate. The classic adult regimen is sodium nitrite 300 mg IV (10 ml of a 3% solution) followed by sodium thiosulphate 12.5 g IV (50 ml of a 25% solution). It also relaxes smooth muscle (vasodilator) and is used as an anticorrosive and food preservative (curing meat).

Too much nitrite causes dangerous methaemoglobinaemia and low blood pressure.

```text
CYANIDE POISONING: HOW THE TWO ANTIDOTES WORK TOGETHER
Hb ──(sodium nitrite)──► Methaemoglobin
Methaemoglobin + CN⁻ ──► Cyanmethaemoglobin   (frees cytochrome oxidase)
CN⁻ (released slowly) + S2O3²⁻ ──(rhodanese)──► SCN⁻ ──► urine
```

### Activated charcoal

**Preparation.** Vegetable matter (wood, coconut shell, sawdust, peat) is carbonised by heating without air. The carbon is then **activated** by heating to high temperature (around 800–900 °C) with steam, air or carbon dioxide, or chemically with agents such as zinc chloride or phosphoric acid. Activation removes tarry matter and opens a huge network of fine pores, so the surface area becomes very large.

**Properties.** Fine, black, odourless, tasteless powder, insoluble in all solvents. Its key property is **strong adsorption** (IP tests its adsorbing power).

**Uses.**

- **Universal adsorbent antidote** in many oral poisonings and drug overdoses, best given as early as possible; repeated doses can enhance removal of some drugs.
- Flatulence and diarrhoea; decolourising and deodorising agent; in gas masks.

It is **not effective** for strong acids and alkalis, iron and lithium salts, alcohols and petroleum products, and it binds cyanide poorly. It must not be given with ipecac or to a patient who cannot protect the airway. The old "universal antidote" (activated charcoal, magnesium oxide and tannic acid) is no longer recommended.

## 4.5 Astringents

An **astringent** precipitates proteins on the surface of cells, forming a thin protective layer. This reduces secretions and permeability, causes mild contraction of tissue, stops minor bleeding (**styptic** action) and has mild antiseptic action. Astringents are salts of metals such as zinc, aluminium, iron and silver.

### Zinc sulphate (ZnSO4·7H2O, M = 287.5)

**Preparation.** By dissolving zinc, zinc oxide or zinc carbonate in dilute sulphuric acid; iron is removed by oxidation (chlorine or permanganate) and precipitation, other metals by zinc dust; then crystallised.

Zn + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2
ZnO + H2SO4 → ZnSO4 + H2O

**Properties.** Colourless transparent crystals or white powder (**white vitriol**), efflorescent, very soluble in water; astringent metallic taste.

**Assay (principle).** Complexometric titration with disodium edetate at pH about 10 (ammonia buffer) using mordant black II (each ml of 0.05 M disodium edetate ≡ 0.01438 g ZnSO4·7H2O).

**Uses.** Astringent eye drops (conjunctivitis), astringent lotions (white lotion with sulphurated potash), mouthwash; **zinc supplement**, including zinc for children with diarrhoea along with ORS and for zinc deficiency; formerly an emetic. Overdose causes vomiting.

### Potash alum (KAl(SO4)2·12H2O, M = 474.4)

**Preparation.** Aluminium sulphate (from bauxite or clay treated with sulphuric acid) is mixed with potassium sulphate in hot water in equimolar amounts; the double salt crystallises on cooling.

Al2(SO4)3 + K2SO4 + 24H2O → 2KAl(SO4)2·12H2O

**Properties.** Colourless transparent octahedral crystals or white powder; sweetish astringent taste; soluble in water (more in hot water); solution acidic (aluminium hydrolyses). On heating it melts in its water of crystallisation and then loses water to form **exsiccated (dried) alum**, a white porous mass. It is a **double salt**: in water it gives K⁺, Al³⁺ and SO4²⁻ ions separately.

**Uses.** Astringent and **styptic** (stops bleeding from small cuts, e.g. after shaving); mouthwash and gargle; exsiccated alum as a dusting powder for its drying action; **coagulant** for purifying water (aluminium hydroxide floc carries down suspended matter); mordant in dyeing; **adjuvant in vaccines** (alum-adsorbed or alum-precipitated toxoids).

## Remember

- KI: I2 + KOH gives KI + KIO3; charcoal reduces iodate; assay by KIO3 in strong HCl, chloroform end point (ICl); 1 ml 0.05 M KIO3 ≡ 16.6 mg KI.
- Expectorants act reflexly (NH4Cl) or directly (iodides).
- Emetics are now rarely used; never in corrosive or kerosene poisoning.
- CuSO4 assay: 2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI + I2; 1 ml 0.1 M thiosulphate ≡ 24.97 mg.
- Rochelle salt is a saline cathartic; tartar emetic is antimony potassium tartrate.
- FeSO4: iron kept in excess during preparation; assay by ceric ammonium sulphate with ferroin (1 ml 0.1 M ≡ 27.80 mg); about 20% iron; ferrous gluconate about 12% iron and gentler.
- Cyanide: sodium nitrite → methaemoglobin traps CN⁻; sodium thiosulphate → thiocyanate via rhodanese.
- Activated charcoal adsorbs most poisons but not acids, alkalis, iron, lithium or alcohols.
- Astringents precipitate surface proteins; ZnSO4 eye drops and zinc supplement; alum is styptic, water coagulant and vaccine adjuvant.
