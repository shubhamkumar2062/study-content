# How to use this guide

Pharmaceutical Inorganic Chemistry is mostly a memory subject, but it rewards a fixed answer pattern. Study one unit a day: read the notes, then write out every equation and assay principle from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers. The five units follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly; nothing has been split or joined.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with equations, reagent roles and a labelled diagram where it fits.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Topics that keep coming back |

| 1 Impurities | Sources and types of impurities, limit test for arsenic (with apparatus), iron, lead, heavy metals, chloride and sulphate, modified limit tests, history of the IP |

**The answer pattern for any inorganic compound**

Write these headings in this order; examiners mark each one:

1. Formula and molecular weight (and common name, if any: blue vitriol, Epsom salt, hypo).
2. **Preparation** with a balanced equation.
3. **Properties**: appearance, solubility, behaviour on heating or in air, key chemical reactions.
4. **Assay**: type of titration, principle, equation, indicator and end point, and the equivalence factor.
5. **Uses**: medicinal first, then pharmaceutical and other uses.
6. Storage and one important precaution or side effect.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Learn the **asterisked compounds** first (sodium chloride, calcium gluconate, ammonium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, hydrogen peroxide, chlorinated lime, copper sulphate, ferrous sulphate, sodium thiosulphate). Only these need a full assay in the syllabus, so they carry the most marks.
2. Group assays by type so they are easy to remember: precipitation (NaCl with AgNO3), complexometric (calcium gluconate, magnesium, zinc, aluminium), acid–base (NaHCO3, boric acid with glycerin, ammonium chloride by formol titration), redox with permanganate (H2O2, ferrous salts, sodium nitrite), ceric (ferrous sulphate), iodometry (copper sulphate, chlorinated lime, KMnO4), iodimetry (sodium thiosulphate), potassium iodate (KI).
3. In limit tests always give: principle, reaction, role of each reagent, and "test compared with standard". Draw the Gutzeit apparatus for arsenic.
4. Standard volumes and strengths in limit tests have changed between IP editions. Quote the older textbook value if you know it, but the principle is what earns the marks.
5. In isotonicity numericals write the formula, substitute with units and box the answer. Practise: W = (0.52 − a)/b, E method, and White–Vincent.
6. For radiopharmaceuticals, make one comparison table of α, β and γ, and practise one half-life calculation (fraction remaining = (1/2)ⁿ).
7. Add one line of precaution or toxicity to every use list (KCl must be diluted, boric acid not on infant skin, iron out of children's reach, I-131 not in pregnancy). It shows understanding and often earns the last mark.
8. Practical (BP110P) viva questions overlap heavily with Unit 1; reading the practical guides is also good revision for the theory paper.
