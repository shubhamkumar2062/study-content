# Unit 1: Impurities in pharmaceutical substances

A medicine must contain what its label says and nothing harmful besides. No chemical made on a large scale is perfectly pure, so the pharmacopoeia fixes how much of each common impurity can be tolerated and gives simple tests to check it. This unit covers the history of the pharmacopoeia, where impurities come from, the different kinds of impurity, and the principles of the limit tests you also perform in the BP110P practical. Questions on sources of impurities, the limit test for arsenic and the limit test for iron are among the most repeated in this subject.

## 1.1 What a pharmacopoeia is

A **pharmacopoeia** is the legally recognised book of standards for drugs. The word comes from the Greek *pharmakon* (drug) and *poiein* (to make). Each **monograph** in it gives, for one substance:

| Part of a monograph | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Name, formula, molecular weight | Identity of the substance |
| Category and dose (in older editions) | What it is used for |
| Description and solubility | Appearance, taste, odour, solvents it dissolves in |
| Identification tests | Quick chemical tests that confirm what it is |
| Tests for purity | Limit tests, loss on drying, sulphated ash, pH, heavy metals and others |
| Assay | Method to find the exact content of the active substance |
| Storage and labelling | How to keep it and what the label must state |

In India, drugs must meet the standards of the Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP) under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940. A drug that does not meet these standards is "not of standard quality".

## 1.2 History of the pharmacopoeia

**Early pharmacopoeias in the world**

- Early collections of drug formulas existed in many ancient civilisations, including the Ayurvedic texts of India.
- The **London Pharmacopoeia** (1618) was one of the first official books of its kind in Britain.
- The **United States Pharmacopeia** first appeared in 1820.
- The first **British Pharmacopoeia** was published in 1864. It replaced the separate London, Edinburgh and Dublin pharmacopoeias.

**Pharmacopoeia in India**

| Period | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 1844 | The Bengal Pharmacopoeia was published; it included many local Indian drugs |
| After 1864 | The British Pharmacopoeia became the standard used in India |
| 1946 | The Government of India issued the "Indian Pharmacopoeial List" as a stop-gap |
| 1948 | An Indian Pharmacopoeia Committee was set up to prepare a national pharmacopoeia |
| 1955 | First edition of the Indian Pharmacopoeia published (a supplement followed in 1960) |
| 1966 | Second edition |
| 1985 | Third edition |
| 1996 | Fourth edition |
| 2007 | Fifth edition, the first prepared by the Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission |
| 2010, 2014, 2018, 2022 | Sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth editions |

The **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC)** at Ghaziabad is an autonomous body under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare. It now prepares new editions, addenda and reference standards, and runs the national pharmacovigilance programme.

## 1.3 Impurity and purity

An **impurity** is any substance present in a drug that is not the drug itself and was not added on purpose. A completely pure substance is an ideal that cannot be reached in practice, and chasing absolute purity would make medicines very costly. So the pharmacopoeia sets a **limit**: the maximum amount of an impurity that is harmless and cannot be avoided by good manufacturing.

Impurities matter because they may:

- be toxic (arsenic, lead and other heavy metals) even in small amounts;
- reduce the strength of the drug, so the patient gets a lower dose;
- shorten the shelf life by speeding up decomposition (for example, traces of iron catalyse oxidation);
- change the colour, odour or taste and make the product unacceptable;
- cause incompatibility with other ingredients in a formulation;
- show that the manufacturing process was careless.

## 1.4 Sources of impurities

```text
SOURCES OF IMPURITIES
├── 1. Raw materials
├── 2. Method of manufacture
│      a. Reagents used
│      b. Solvents (including water)
│      c. Reaction vessels and plant
│      d. Intermediate products
│      e. Side reactions / incomplete reaction
├── 3. Atmospheric contamination
├── 4. Defects in manufacture (poor separation, washing, drying)
├── 5. Storage
│      a. Container (filth, leaching, reaction with container)
│      b. Chemical instability (light, heat, air, moisture)
│      c. Physical changes (efflorescence, deliquescence, caking)
└── 6. Adulteration and microbial contamination
```

**1. Raw materials.** Natural sources carry their own impurities. Rock salt contains calcium and magnesium salts; minerals used for preparing salts contain iron, lead and arsenic. Sodium chloride made from sea water may contain calcium sulphate, magnesium chloride and bromides.

**2. Method of manufacture.**

- *Reagents*: a reagent used in excess, or not fully washed out, stays behind. Calcium carbonate made from calcium chloride and sodium carbonate may keep traces of chloride. Ammonia used in a process may leave ammonium salts.
- *Solvents*: water is the commonest solvent. Ordinary tap water contains calcium, magnesium, chloride, sulphate and carbonate; that is why purified water is used.
- *Reaction vessels*: iron, copper, lead or zinc can dissolve from metal vessels and pipes, especially under acid conditions. Glass may give traces of alkali. Lead-lined vessels can add lead.
- *Intermediates*: if a reaction goes through an intermediate, some of it may stay in the final product. In making potassium iodide from iodine and potassium hydroxide, potassium iodate is formed as an intermediate; if it is not fully reduced it appears as an impurity.
- *Side reactions and incomplete reactions*: unreacted starting material or by-products remain.

**3. Atmospheric contamination.** Dust, sulphur dioxide, hydrogen sulphide, carbon dioxide and moisture from the air can contaminate a product. Sodium hydroxide absorbs carbon dioxide and becomes contaminated with sodium carbonate.

**4. Defects in manufacture.** Poor mixing, incomplete filtration, poor washing of precipitates, wrong temperature or insufficient drying all leave impurities.

**5. Storage.**

- *Filth*: dust, insects or fibres getting into poorly closed containers.
- *Container material*: alkali from soda glass, metal from tins, plasticisers from plastic containers. Strongly alkaline solutions attack glass.
- *Chemical instability*: light, heat, air and moisture cause decomposition. Ferrous salts oxidise to ferric salts; hydrogen peroxide decomposes to water and oxygen; chlorinated lime loses chlorine.
- *Physical changes*: **efflorescence** (loss of water of crystallisation, as in washing soda or crystalline sodium sulphate), **deliquescence** (absorbing moisture until dissolving, as in calcium chloride), polymorphic change and caking. These alter the composition per gram.

**6. Adulteration and microbes.** Deliberate addition of cheaper material, and growth of micro-organisms in moist or natural products.

## 1.5 Types of impurities

Impurities can be grouped by their effect:

| Type | Examples | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Toxic impurities | Arsenic, lead, mercury, other heavy metals | Harm the patient even in traces; strict limits |
| Impurities that reduce shelf life | Iron, copper (catalyse oxidation), moisture | Cause decomposition |
| Impurities that change appearance | Coloured iron salts, dust | Product looks spoiled |
| Harmless but signal poor manufacture | Chloride, sulphate | Show insufficient washing or impure water |
| Impurities that cause incompatibility | Free acid or alkali | React with other ingredients |

Chloride and sulphate are not toxic in the small amounts found; their test is a check on the quality of the process. Arsenic and lead are tested because they are poisonous and accumulate in the body.

## 1.6 Limit tests: the general idea

A **limit test** is a semi-quantitative test that shows whether an impurity is **above or below a fixed limit**. It does not measure the exact amount. The test solution (made from a weighed quantity of the substance) and a **standard** solution (made from a known small amount of the impurity) are treated in exactly the same way, side by side, and the turbidity, opalescence, colour or stain is compared.

```text
   TEST                         STANDARD
   Weighed sample               Known amount of impurity
        │                             │
   Same reagents, same volume, same time, same cylinders
        │                             │
   Turbidity / colour / stain   Turbidity / colour / stain
        └──────── compare ────────────┘
   Test ≤ Standard  → passes      Test > Standard → fails
```

Rules that apply to all limit tests:

- Use **Nessler cylinders** of the same glass, size and diameter, matched in pairs.
- Add reagents in the same order and the same quantities to both.
- View turbidity or opalescence **across** the cylinder against a black background; view colour **down** the cylinder against a white background.
- Keep the standing time the same for test and standard.
- Use reagents labelled "AsT" (arsenic-free) or "iron-free" where the test requires them.

The limit is often expressed in **parts per million (ppm)**. For example, 1 ppm means 1 µg of impurity in 1 g of substance.

## 1.7 Limit test for chloride

**Principle.** Chloride reacts with silver nitrate in the presence of dilute nitric acid to give a white precipitate of silver chloride. In the dilute solutions used, it appears as a faint **opalescence** rather than a heavy precipitate.

Cl⁻ + AgNO3 → AgCl (white) + NO3⁻

**Why nitric acid?** It makes the solution acidic so that other anions such as carbonate, phosphate and hydroxide do not precipitate with silver, and it helps produce a uniform, fine opalescence. Hydrochloric acid cannot be used because it contains chloride, and sulphuric acid would give silver sulphate.

**Procedure in brief.** The test sample is dissolved in water, dilute nitric acid is added, the volume made up in a Nessler cylinder, silver nitrate solution added, stirred and left for about 5 minutes away from bright light. A standard is prepared the same way from a known volume of standard sodium chloride solution. The older IP standard used **1 ml of 0.05845% w/v sodium chloride solution** (check the current IP).

**Result.** The opalescence of the test should not be greater than the standard. Silver chloride darkens in light, so the cylinders are not kept in direct sunlight.

## 1.8 Limit test for sulphate

**Principle.** Sulphate reacts with barium chloride in acid solution to give a white turbidity of barium sulphate.

SO4²⁻ + BaCl2 → BaSO4 (white) + 2Cl⁻

The reagent used is **barium sulphate reagent**: barium chloride with a little potassium sulphate and alcohol. The small amount of sulphate **seeds** the formation of barium sulphate, so the turbidity forms quickly and evenly; the alcohol prevents supersaturation and helps a uniform turbidity.

**Why dilute hydrochloric acid?** It prevents precipitation of other anions such as carbonate and phosphate as barium salts.

**Standard.** The older IP standard used **1 ml of 0.1089% w/v potassium sulphate solution** (check the current IP).

**Result.** Turbidity of test not more than standard.

## 1.9 Modified limit tests for chloride and sulphate

Some substances cannot be tested directly because they would spoil the comparison. The general test is then **modified** to remove the interference first.

| Problem | Example | Modification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substance is a carbonate or bicarbonate and reacts with the acid | Sodium bicarbonate, sodium carbonate | Extra acid is added and the solution boiled or allowed to stand until effervescence stops, so the carbon dioxide does not disturb the test |
| Substance is strongly coloured | Potassium permanganate | The colour is first destroyed (for example, reduced with a little alcohol and heat), and the brown manganese dioxide is filtered off; the clear filtrate is tested |
| Substance is insoluble in water | Some insoluble salts | An extract or a solution in acid is prepared and tested |
| Substance gives its own precipitate with the reagent | Salts that react with silver or barium | The interfering ion is removed or converted first |

In every case the standard is treated with the same extra reagents, so that only the impurity being tested makes a difference. Examiners usually want the reason for modification and one example with the change made.

## 1.10 Limit test for iron

**Principle.** Iron (as Fe³⁺ or Fe²⁺) reacts with **thioglycollic acid** in an ammonia-alkaline solution containing **citric acid** to give a **pale pink to deep reddish-purple** colour of ferrous thioglycollate.

```text
Fe³⁺ ──(thioglycollic acid reduces)──► Fe²⁺
Fe²⁺ + 2 HS–CH2–COOH ──(NH3, alkaline)──► Fe(S–CH2–COO)2  (purple)
```

**Role of each reagent**

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Citric acid (iron-free, 20% w/v) | Forms a soluble complex with iron so that iron hydroxide does not precipitate when the solution is made alkaline |
| Thioglycollic acid | Reduces ferric to ferrous and forms the coloured complex |
| Ammonia (iron-free) | Makes the solution alkaline; the colour develops only in alkaline medium |

**Procedure in brief.** The sample is dissolved in water, citric acid and thioglycollic acid are added, the solution is made alkaline with iron-free ammonia, diluted to volume and left for about 5 minutes. A standard is made from **standard iron solution** (ferric ammonium sulphate). The older IP standard used **2 ml of standard iron solution containing 20 µg Fe per ml** (check the current IP).

**Result.** The purple colour of the test, viewed down the cylinder, should not be deeper than the standard.

The colour fades in the presence of oxidising agents, and is pink only in alkaline solution; both are common viva points.

## 1.11 Limit test for arsenic (Gutzeit test)

Arsenic is highly toxic and accumulates in the body, so its limit is very strict. The pharmacopoeial method is based on the **Gutzeit test**.

**Principle.** Arsenic in the sample (as arsenic acid or arsenious acid) is reduced by **nascent hydrogen**, produced from zinc and hydrochloric acid, to **arsine gas (AsH3)**. The arsine passes up the tube and reacts with a strip of **mercuric chloride paper**, giving a **yellow to brown stain**. The intensity of the stain is compared with a standard stain made from a known quantity of arsenic.

**Reactions**

```text
H3AsO4 + H2SnO2 → H3AsO3 + H2SnO3          (stannous reduces As(V) to As(III))
H3AsO4 + 2HI → H3AsO3 + I2 + H2O            (iodide also reduces As(V))
H3AsO3 + 3H2 (nascent, Zn/HCl) → AsH3 + 3H2O
AsH3 + HgCl2 → yellow-brown compounds of arsenic and mercury (stain)
```

**Apparatus**

```text
           ┌──── mercuric chloride paper clamped between two
           │     glass plates/flanges at the top
     ══════╪══════
           │   narrow glass tube
           │   ░░ lead acetate cotton wool (absorbs H2S)
           │
     ┌─────┴─────┐  rubber bung
     │           │
     │  test     │  wide-mouthed bottle (about 120 ml)
     │  solution │  + stannated HCl + KI + granulated zinc
     └───────────┘  kept at about 40 °C
```

**Role of each reagent**

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Hydrochloric acid (stannated, AsT) | Reacts with zinc to give nascent hydrogen; stannous chloride in it helps reduce As(V) to As(III) and gives an even evolution of gas |
| Potassium iodide (AsT) | Reduces pentavalent arsenic to trivalent arsenic, which is converted to arsine more easily |
| Granulated zinc (AsT) | Produces hydrogen with the acid |
| Lead acetate cotton wool | Traps any hydrogen sulphide (from sulphide impurities in zinc) which would otherwise blacken the mercuric chloride paper |
| Mercuric chloride paper | Reacts with arsine to give the stain |

**Procedure in brief.** The test solution, acid, iodide and zinc are placed in the bottle, the tube with lead acetate cotton and mercuric chloride paper is fitted at once, and the reaction continues at about 40 °C for about 40 minutes. A standard stain is produced at the same time from a fixed volume of dilute arsenic standard solution (check the current IP for the exact amount).

**Result.** The stain on the test paper should not be more intense than the standard stain. The stain fades in light, so it is compared immediately.

## 1.12 Limit test for lead

Lead is a cumulative poison found in raw materials and coming from lead-lined plant and solder.

**Principle.** Lead forms a **red (cherry-red) complex, lead dithizonate**, with **dithizone** (diphenylthiocarbazone) in alkaline medium. Dithizone itself is **green** in chloroform. The lead complex is extracted into chloroform and its colour compared with a standard made from standard lead solution.

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Ammonium citrate | Keeps metals in solution in alkaline medium by complexing them |
| Potassium cyanide | Forms stable complexes with other metals (copper, zinc, nickel and others) so that they do not react with dithizone |
| Hydroxylamine hydrochloride | Reduces ferric iron to ferrous, which does not oxidise dithizone |
| Ammonia | Makes the medium alkaline (lead dithizonate forms in mild alkali) |
| Dithizone in chloroform | Forms the red lead complex, which goes into the chloroform layer |

The sample solution, after these additions, is shaken repeatedly with dithizone solution in a separating funnel; the extracts are collected and the colour compared with the standard. Potassium cyanide is very poisonous, so it is handled with great care and never pipetted by mouth.

## 1.13 Limit test for heavy metals

"Heavy metals" means metals that give a coloured precipitate with sulphide ions under the test conditions: lead, copper, mercury, bismuth, antimony, tin, cadmium, silver and others. The result is expressed as lead, in ppm.

**Principle.** In a solution buffered at **pH about 3.5** (acetate buffer), **thioacetamide** hydrolyses to give hydrogen sulphide, which forms dark brown to black metal sulphides. The colour of the test is compared with a standard prepared from standard lead solution.

```text
CH3CSNH2 + H2O → CH3CONH2 + H2S
Pb²⁺ + H2S → PbS (brown-black) + 2H⁺
```

Older editions used **hydrogen sulphide solution** directly; thioacetamide is now preferred because it is safer and gives H2S slowly and evenly. The pH matters: at pH 3.5 most heavy metals precipitate as sulphides while metals such as iron and zinc do not interfere much.

**Methods.** The IP gives more than one method depending on the substance: one for substances that give a clear, colourless solution in water; one for substances that must first be ignited (organic matter destroyed) before testing; and one for substances that dissolve in alkali. Learn the principle and the reason for having different methods; the exact volumes vary by edition.

**Result.** The colour of the test should not be darker than the standard.

## 1.15 Other purity tests in monographs (for completeness)

Monographs also include tests such as **loss on drying** (moisture), **sulphated ash** (inorganic residue in organic substances), **acidity or alkalinity** (free acid or base), **clarity and colour of solution**, **pH**, and tests for specific impurities (for example free iodine and iodate in potassium iodide, or carbonate in sodium hydroxide). These are often asked as short notes or one-mark questions.

## Remember

- Pharmacopoeia = legal book of standards; first IP in 1955, current edition prepared by the IPC, Ghaziabad.
- Six sources of impurity: raw materials, manufacturing process (reagents, solvents, vessels, intermediates), atmosphere, defects in manufacture, storage, adulteration.
- Limit tests compare test with standard under identical conditions in Nessler cylinders; they are semi-quantitative.
- Chloride: AgNO3 in HNO3, opalescence. Sulphate: barium sulphate reagent in HCl, turbidity (potassium sulphate seeds, alcohol prevents supersaturation).
- Iron: citric acid stops precipitation, thioglycollic acid reduces Fe³⁺ and gives purple ferrous thioglycollate in ammonia.
- Arsenic: Gutzeit test; Zn + HCl → nascent H → AsH3 → yellow-brown stain on HgCl2 paper; KI and SnCl2 reduce As(V); lead acetate cotton traps H2S.
- Lead: dithizone, red complex in chloroform; KCN masks other metals.
- Heavy metals: thioacetamide at pH 3.5, sulphides, expressed as ppm of lead.
- Standard volumes and strengths change between IP editions: always check the current IP.
