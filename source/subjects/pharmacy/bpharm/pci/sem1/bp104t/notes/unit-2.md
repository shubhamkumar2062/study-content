# Unit 2: Acids, bases and buffers; electrolytes; dental products

This unit has three parts. The first is about buffers and isotonic solutions, which you need for every eye drop and injection. The second covers the electrolytes of the body and the inorganic salts used to replace them, including two compounds marked with an asterisk in the syllabus (sodium chloride and calcium gluconate), for which preparation and assay are expected. The third covers dental products and the role of fluoride. Isotonicity numericals, ORS composition and calcium gluconate are frequent questions.

## Part A: Acids, bases and buffers

## 2.1 Acids and bases in brief

| Concept | Acid | Base |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Arrhenius | Gives H⁺ in water | Gives OH⁻ in water |
| Brønsted–Lowry | Proton donor | Proton acceptor |
| Lewis | Electron-pair acceptor | Electron-pair donor |

- **pH** = −log[H⁺]. Pure water at 25 °C has pH 7.
- A **weak acid** (acetic acid, boric acid, carbonic acid) ionises only partly; its strength is given by Ka, or by **pKa = −log Ka**. A lower pKa means a stronger acid.
- An acid and the species formed when it loses a proton are a **conjugate pair** (CH3COOH and CH3COO⁻).

## 2.2 Buffers

A **buffer** is a solution that resists a change in pH when a small amount of acid or alkali is added, or when it is diluted.

Buffers are usually a mixture of:

- a **weak acid and its salt with a strong base** (acetic acid + sodium acetate; boric acid + borax), or
- a **weak base and its salt with a strong acid** (ammonia + ammonium chloride), or
- two salts of a polybasic acid (sodium dihydrogen phosphate + disodium hydrogen phosphate).

**How a buffer works (acetate buffer)**

```text
Add acid  (H⁺):   CH3COO⁻ + H⁺  → CH3COOH       (salt mops up H⁺)
Add base (OH⁻):   CH3COOH + OH⁻ → CH3COO⁻ + H2O (acid mops up OH⁻)
```

The large reserve of both forms means only a small change in the ratio, and so only a small change in pH.

## 2.3 Buffer equation (Henderson–Hasselbalch)

For a weak acid buffer:

**pH = pKa + log ([salt] / [acid])**

For a weak base buffer:

**pH = pKw − pKb + log ([base] / [salt])**

Points to remember:

- When [salt] = [acid], pH = pKa. A buffer works best within about pKa ± 1.
- Dilution does not change the ratio, so the pH changes very little on moderate dilution (ionic strength effects cause small changes).
- To choose a buffer for a given pH, pick a weak acid whose pKa is close to that pH.

**Worked example.** Find the pH of a buffer containing 0.1 M acetic acid and 0.2 M sodium acetate (pKa 4.76).
pH = 4.76 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.76 + 0.30 = **5.06**.

**Worked example (preparation).** What ratio of sodium acetate to acetic acid gives pH 5.0?
5.0 = 4.76 + log(ratio) → log(ratio) = 0.24 → ratio = **1.74 : 1**.

## 2.4 Buffer capacity

**Buffer capacity (β)** is the amount of strong acid or base (in gram-equivalents per litre) needed to change the pH of 1 litre of buffer by one unit.

**β = ΔB / ΔpH**

where ΔB is the gram-equivalents of strong acid or base added per litre.

A more exact equation (Van Slyke) is:

**β = 2.303 C × Ka[H3O⁺] / (Ka + [H3O⁺])²**

where C is the total buffer concentration (acid + salt).

- β is **maximum when pH = pKa**, and then **βmax = 0.576 C**.
- β increases with total concentration; doubling the concentration doubles the capacity.
- Pharmaceutical buffers usually have a capacity of about 0.01 to 0.1.

```text
β
│        ╱╲
│       ╱  ╲
│      ╱    ╲
│     ╱      ╲
│____╱________╲______ pH
          pKa
 (buffer capacity is highest at pH = pKa)
```

**Worked example.** 0.1 M acetate buffer at pH = pKa: βmax = 0.576 × 0.1 = **0.0576**.

## 2.5 Buffers in pharmaceutical and biological systems

**Biological buffers.** Blood pH is held at 7.35–7.45 by:

- the **bicarbonate–carbonic acid** system (the main extracellular buffer; normal ratio of HCO3⁻ to H2CO3 about 20 : 1);
- the **phosphate** system (important inside cells and in urine);
- **proteins**, including haemoglobin (the main buffer of red cells).

**Pharmaceutical buffers**

| Use | Buffers commonly used |
| --- | --- |
| Eye drops | Boric acid–borax, phosphate buffers (formulas such as Sörensen's phosphate and Palitzsch's borate buffers) |
| Injections | Phosphate, citrate, acetate |
| Oral liquids | Citrate, phosphate |
| Analysis | Standard buffers to calibrate pH meters |

Reasons for buffering a formulation:

1. **Stability**: many drugs decompose fastest at certain pH values; the buffer holds the pH where the drug is most stable.
2. **Solubility**: weak acids and bases need a suitable pH to stay dissolved.
3. **Comfort**: eye and injection solutions near body pH cause less pain and irritation. The buffer capacity should be low, so that tears or blood can bring the pH back to normal quickly.
4. **Activity**: some preservatives and drugs act best at a certain pH.

## 2.6 Preparation and stability of a pharmaceutical buffer

Steps to prepare a buffer of a chosen pH:

1. Choose a weak acid (or base) with pKa near the required pH, safe for the route of use.
2. Use the buffer equation to find the ratio of salt to acid.
3. Decide the total concentration that gives the required buffer capacity (from the Van Slyke equation).
4. Weigh and dissolve; check the pH with a calibrated pH meter and adjust.
5. Adjust tonicity if the product is for the eye or for injection.
6. Sterilise if needed and store in a suitable container.

**Stability factors.** Temperature changes the pKa and therefore the pH; borate buffers and phosphate buffers can support microbial growth, so preservatives may be needed; some buffers react with the drug or with the container (phosphate with calcium salts; borate is incompatible with some drugs); and alkaline buffers absorb carbon dioxide from air.

## 2.7 Buffered isotonic solutions

Body fluids (blood, tears) have an osmotic pressure equal to that of **0.9% w/v sodium chloride**. Their freezing point is about **−0.52 °C**.

| Term | Meaning | Effect on red blood cells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Isotonic | Same osmotic pressure as the body fluid | No change |
| Hypotonic | Lower osmotic pressure | Water enters, cells swell and may burst (haemolysis) |
| Hypertonic | Higher osmotic pressure | Water leaves, cells shrink (crenation) |

**Iso-osmotic** means equal osmotic pressure measured physically; **isotonic** means the solution actually behaves as equal to the cell. A solution can be iso-osmotic but not isotonic if the solute passes through the membrane (for example, boric acid and urea pass into red cells, so their iso-osmotic solutions still cause haemolysis).

## 2.8 Measurement of tonicity

1. **Haemolytic method**: red blood cells are placed in the solution and observed for swelling, haemolysis or shrinkage, by microscope or by measuring released haemoglobin.
2. **Colligative property methods**: tonicity relates to the number of particles, so it is measured from the **freezing point depression** (Beckmann thermometer or freezing point osmometer) or from vapour pressure (vapour pressure osmometer).

## 2.9 Calculations and methods of adjusting isotonicity

Methods are divided into two classes.

```text
METHODS OF ADJUSTING TONICITY
├── Class I: add NaCl (or another substance) to the drug solution
│      ├── Cryoscopic (freezing point depression) method
│      └── Sodium chloride equivalent method
└── Class II: dissolve the drug in enough water to make it isotonic,
              then make up to volume with an isotonic vehicle
       ├── White–Vincent method
       └── Sprowls method
```

### a. Freezing point depression (cryoscopic) method

**W = (0.52 − a) / b**

- W = grams of NaCl (or adjusting substance) to add per 100 ml
- a = freezing point depression of the unadjusted drug solution
- b = freezing point depression of 1% w/v of the adjusting substance (for NaCl, b = 0.576 °C)

**Example.** Make 100 ml of 1% boric acid solution isotonic. The freezing point depression of 1% boric acid is about 0.29 °C.
W = (0.52 − 0.29) / 0.576 = **0.40 g NaCl per 100 ml**.

### b. Sodium chloride equivalent (E) method

The **sodium chloride equivalent (E)** of a drug is the weight of NaCl that has the same osmotic effect as 1 g of the drug.

**E = 17 × Liso / M** (M = molecular weight of the drug)

Typical Liso values: non-electrolytes about 1.9, weak electrolytes about 2.0, uni-univalent electrolytes (NaCl type) about 3.4, uni-divalent (Na2SO4 type) about 4.3.

Steps: (1) multiply grams of drug by E to get its NaCl equivalent; (2) find NaCl needed for isotonicity of the whole volume (0.9 g per 100 ml); (3) subtract.

**Example.** Same 100 ml of 1% boric acid; E of boric acid = 0.50.
NaCl equivalent of drug = 1 × 0.50 = 0.50 g; NaCl needed = 0.90 g; **NaCl to add = 0.40 g** (agrees with the cryoscopic method).

**Example.** Make 30 ml of 1% ephedrine sulphate isotonic (E = 0.23).
Drug = 0.3 g → 0.3 × 0.23 = 0.069 g; NaCl for 30 ml = 0.27 g; **NaCl to add = 0.201 g**.

### c. White–Vincent method

**V = w × E × 111.1**

V = ml of water in which w grams of drug gives an isotonic solution. The solution is then made up to final volume with an isotonic (and preferably buffered) vehicle.

**Example.** 0.3 g ephedrine sulphate (E = 0.23): V = 0.3 × 0.23 × 111.1 = **7.7 ml**. Dissolve the drug in 7.7 ml water and make up to 30 ml with isotonic buffered diluent.

### d. Sprowls method

A simplification of White–Vincent: tables give V, the volume of water that makes **0.3 g** of each common drug isotonic (0.3 g is the drug in 1 fluid ounce of a 1% solution). The pharmacist reads V from the table instead of calculating.

**Milliequivalents.** Electrolyte doses are often in mEq or mmol.
mEq = (mg × valency) / molecular weight. Example: 1.5 g KCl = 1500 / 74.55 ≈ 20 mmol = **20 mEq of K⁺**. Normal saline (0.9%) contains about 154 mmol/L each of Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

## Part B: Major extracellular and intracellular electrolytes

## 2.10 Distribution and functions of major ions

| Ion | Main compartment | Normal plasma level (approx.) | Main functions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium (Na⁺) | Extracellular (main cation) | 135–145 mmol/L | Controls ECF volume and osmotic pressure, nerve impulse and muscle contraction, acid–base balance |
| Potassium (K⁺) | Intracellular (main cation) | 3.5–5.0 mmol/L | Resting membrane potential, heart rhythm, muscle and nerve function, enzyme activity |
| Calcium (Ca²⁺) | Extracellular, bones and teeth | 2.2–2.6 mmol/L (about 9–10.5 mg/dl) | Bone and teeth, blood clotting, muscle contraction, nerve transmission, membrane permeability |
| Magnesium (Mg²⁺) | Intracellular, bone | About 0.7–1.0 mmol/L | Cofactor for many enzymes (ATP reactions), nerve and muscle function |
| Chloride (Cl⁻) | Extracellular (main anion) | About 96–106 mmol/L | Osmotic balance, gastric HCl, chloride shift |
| Bicarbonate (HCO3⁻) | Extracellular | About 22–28 mmol/L | Main buffer of blood |
| Phosphate (HPO4²⁻, H2PO4⁻) | Intracellular, bone | – | Bone, ATP, buffer in cells and urine |

Too little or too much of an ion causes disease: **hyponatraemia** (confusion, fits), **hypokalaemia** (muscle weakness, arrhythmia), **hyperkalaemia** (dangerous cardiac arrhythmia), **hypocalcaemia** (tetany).

## 2.11 Electrolytes used in replacement therapy

### Sodium chloride* (NaCl, M = 58.44)

**Preparation.** Obtained from sea water or rock salt (brine). Crude salt contains calcium and magnesium salts and sulphate. It is dissolved in water, and calcium and magnesium are removed by adding sodium carbonate and sodium hydroxide (they precipitate as carbonate and hydroxide); sulphate can be removed with barium chloride. The filtered solution is concentrated. Pure sodium chloride is precipitated by passing **hydrogen chloride gas** into a saturated solution (common-ion effect lowers its solubility), or by evaporation and crystallisation.

**Properties.** Colourless cubic crystals or white crystalline powder, saline taste, freely soluble in water, very slightly soluble in alcohol. Solution is neutral. Gives tests for sodium (golden yellow flame) and chloride (white precipitate with silver nitrate, soluble in ammonia).

**Assay (principle).** Precipitation titration with silver nitrate. In the classical method (Mohr's method) a neutral solution is titrated with 0.1 M silver nitrate using potassium chromate as indicator; after all chloride is precipitated, the first excess silver gives reddish-brown silver chromate.

NaCl + AgNO3 → AgCl + NaNO3
Each ml of 0.1 M AgNO3 ≡ 0.005844 g NaCl.
(Some IP editions use other end-point methods such as Volhard's back titration or a potentiometric end point; check the current IP.)

**Uses.** Fluid and sodium replacement in dehydration (0.9% normal saline, with dextrose), electrolyte replenisher, heat cramps, isotonic vehicle, nasal and eye washes, hypertonic saline in selected cases, ORS ingredient.

### Potassium chloride (KCl, M = 74.55)

**Preparation.** From natural minerals such as sylvine (KCl) and carnallite (KCl·MgCl2·6H2O) by dissolving and fractional crystallisation; also by neutralising potassium hydroxide or carbonate with hydrochloric acid: KOH + HCl → KCl + H2O.

**Properties.** White crystals or powder, saline taste, freely soluble in water.

**Uses.** Treatment and prevention of hypokalaemia (with diuretics, vomiting, diarrhoea), in ORS and in IV fluids. Intravenous potassium must always be **diluted and given slowly**; concentrated KCl injected rapidly can stop the heart. Oral KCl can irritate the stomach, so slow-release or liquid forms are used.

### Calcium gluconate* ((C6H11O7)2Ca·H2O, M = 448.4)

**Preparation.** Gluconic acid is obtained by oxidation of glucose (for example with bromine water, electrolytically, or by fermentation). It is then neutralised with calcium carbonate:

2 C6H12O7 + CaCO3 → (C6H11O7)2Ca + H2O + CO2

The solution is concentrated and the salt crystallised.

**Properties.** White crystalline granules or powder, odourless, tasteless; sparingly soluble in cold water, more soluble in boiling water. Gives tests for calcium (white precipitate with ammonium oxalate, brick-red flame) and for gluconate.

**Assay (principle).** **Complexometric titration** with disodium edetate (EDTA). Calcium forms a stable 1 : 1 complex with EDTA. The usual method adds a measured amount of magnesium sulphate solution and an ammonia–ammonium chloride buffer (pH about 10), then titrates with 0.05 M disodium edetate using **mordant black II** (eriochrome black T) as indicator, the colour changing from wine-red to blue. The volume used by the added magnesium is subtracted. Magnesium is added because the indicator gives a sharper end point with magnesium than with calcium.

Each ml of 0.05 M disodium edetate ≡ 0.02242 g of calcium gluconate (monohydrate).

**Uses.** Hypocalcaemia and tetany, calcium supplement, to protect the heart in severe hyperkalaemia, antidote in magnesium sulphate overdose, and in calcium deficiency states. Given slowly by IV injection (10% solution) or orally. It is preferred to calcium chloride for injection because it is less irritant to tissues.

### Oral rehydration salt (ORS)

ORS replaces water and salts lost in diarrhoea. Glucose helps sodium absorption from the intestine (**sodium–glucose cotransport**), and water follows the absorbed solutes, so ORS works even when the gut is secreting fluid.

**WHO low-osmolarity ORS (per litre of water)**

| Ingredient | g per litre |
| --- | --- |
| Sodium chloride | 2.6 |
| Glucose, anhydrous | 13.5 |
| Potassium chloride | 1.5 |
| Trisodium citrate dihydrate | 2.9 |

This gives sodium 75, chloride 65, glucose 75, potassium 20 and citrate 10 mmol/L; total osmolarity **245 mOsm/L**. The older standard WHO formula had 3.5 g NaCl and 20 g glucose (osmolarity 311 mOsm/L); the low-osmolarity formula reduces stool output and vomiting. Citrate (or bicarbonate in older formulas) corrects acidosis; potassium replaces potassium loss. Zinc is given alongside ORS to children with diarrhoea.

## 2.12 Physiological acid–base balance

Normal arterial blood pH is **7.35–7.45**. It is controlled by three lines of defence:

1. **Chemical buffers** (seconds): bicarbonate, phosphate, protein and haemoglobin buffers.
2. **Lungs** (minutes): breathing faster removes CO2 and raises pH; slower breathing retains CO2.
3. **Kidneys** (hours to days): excrete H⁺ (as ammonium and phosphate) and reabsorb or generate bicarbonate.

pH = 6.1 + log ([HCO3⁻] / (0.03 × pCO2))

| Disorder | Primary change | Common causes | Compensation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | ↓ HCO3⁻ | Diabetic ketoacidosis, renal failure, severe diarrhoea | Hyperventilation |
| Metabolic alkalosis | ↑ HCO3⁻ | Vomiting, excess antacid (bicarbonate) | Slow breathing |
| Respiratory acidosis | ↑ pCO2 | Lung disease, respiratory depression | Kidneys retain HCO3⁻ |
| Respiratory alkalosis | ↓ pCO2 | Hyperventilation, anxiety, high altitude | Kidneys excrete HCO3⁻ |

Sodium bicarbonate, sodium lactate and sodium citrate are used to treat acidosis; ammonium chloride was used for alkalosis (Unit 3).

## Part C: Dental products

## 2.13 Dentifrices

A **dentifrice** (toothpaste or tooth powder) is used with a brush to clean the teeth, remove plaque and food debris, and freshen the mouth.

| Component | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Abrasive / polishing agent | Removes stains and plaque | Calcium carbonate, dicalcium phosphate, hydrated silica |
| Humectant | Stops the paste drying | Glycerin, sorbitol, propylene glycol |
| Detergent (foaming agent) | Cleans and foams | Sodium lauryl sulphate |
| Binder / thickener | Holds the paste together | Sodium CMC, carrageenan |
| Anticaries agent | Prevents decay | Sodium fluoride, sodium monofluorophosphate, stannous fluoride |
| Desensitising agent | Reduces sensitivity | Potassium nitrate, strontium chloride |
| Sweetener, flavour, colour, preservative | Acceptability | Saccharin, peppermint oil, parabens |

The abrasive should remove stains without scratching enamel; very hard abrasives damage teeth.

## 2.14 Role of fluoride in dental caries

**Dental caries** is decay of the tooth caused by acids produced by plaque bacteria (mainly *Streptococcus mutans*) fermenting dietary sugars. The acid dissolves the enamel mineral, hydroxyapatite.

Fluoride protects the teeth in three ways:

1. **Fluorapatite formation**: fluoride replaces hydroxyl ions in hydroxyapatite.
   Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2 + 2F⁻ → Ca10(PO4)6F2 + 2OH⁻
   Fluorapatite is harder and less soluble in acid.
2. **Remineralisation**: fluoride in saliva helps redeposit mineral in early lesions.
3. **Antibacterial action**: fluoride inhibits bacterial enzymes (such as enolase), reducing acid production.

Fluoride is supplied through fluoridated water (about 1 ppm), toothpaste, mouth rinses, gels and varnishes. **Excess fluoride** causes **dental fluorosis** (mottled, stained enamel) and, with long exposure to high levels, skeletal fluorosis, a known problem in some parts of India where ground water is rich in fluoride.

## 2.15 Desensitising agents

**Dentine hypersensitivity** is a sharp pain on contact with cold, heat or sweet food when dentine is exposed. Desensitising agents work by:

- **blocking the open dentinal tubules** (strontium chloride, stannous fluoride, sodium fluoride, calcium compounds), or
- **calming the nerve** (potassium nitrate reduces nerve excitability).

Other agents listed in textbooks include zinc chloride and formaldehyde (older use).

## 2.16 Calcium carbonate (CaCO3, M = 100.1)

**Preparation.** Precipitated calcium carbonate is made by adding sodium carbonate to a solution of calcium chloride, or by passing carbon dioxide into milk of lime:

CaCl2 + Na2CO3 → CaCO3 + 2NaCl
Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3 + H2O

The precipitate is washed free of chloride and dried.

**Properties.** Fine white odourless powder, practically insoluble in water, dissolves with effervescence in dilute acids.

**Assay (principle).** Dissolved in dilute hydrochloric acid and titrated complexometrically with disodium edetate at alkaline pH.

**Uses.** Abrasive in dentifrices, antacid (non-systemic; can cause constipation and acid rebound), calcium supplement, phosphate binder.

## 2.17 Sodium fluoride (NaF, M = 41.99)

**Preparation.** By neutralising hydrofluoric acid with sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide:

Na2CO3 + 2HF → 2NaF + H2O + CO2

**Properties.** White odourless powder, soluble in water, solution slightly alkaline. Toxic if swallowed in large amounts.

**Uses.** Anticaries agent in toothpastes, mouth rinses and topical gels; desensitising agent. Toothpaste must be kept away from young children who swallow it. Fluoride compounds are poisonous in large doses; fluoride toxicity causes vomiting and low calcium.

## 2.18 Zinc eugenol cement (zinc oxide–eugenol)

A dental cement made by mixing **zinc oxide powder** with **eugenol** (clove oil) liquid. They set by forming **zinc eugenolate**, a chelate, in a matrix of unreacted zinc oxide.

```text
ZnO (powder) + 2 eugenol (liquid) → zinc eugenolate (hard mass) + H2O
```

The powder may contain rosin and zinc acetate (an accelerator); the liquid may contain olive oil. Moisture speeds setting.

**Uses.** Temporary fillings, sedative (soothing) dressings in deep cavities, root canal sealing, impression pastes and cementing temporary crowns. Eugenol has a mild analgesic and antiseptic effect on the pulp. It is not used under resin fillings because eugenol interferes with their setting.

## Remember

- Buffer equation: pH = pKa + log(salt/acid); best range pKa ± 1; βmax = 0.576 C at pH = pKa.
- Body fluids ≡ 0.9% NaCl, freezing point −0.52 °C. W = (0.52 − a)/b with b = 0.576 for NaCl; E = 17 Liso/M; V = w × E × 111.1.
- Class I methods add NaCl (cryoscopic, NaCl equivalent); Class II make an isotonic solution of the drug and dilute with isotonic vehicle (White–Vincent, Sprowls).
- Na⁺ main ECF cation, K⁺ main ICF cation; Cl⁻ and HCO3⁻ main ECF anions.
- NaCl assay: AgNO3 titration (1 ml 0.1 M ≡ 5.844 mg); calcium gluconate assay: EDTA with mordant black II (1 ml 0.05 M ≡ 22.42 mg).
- WHO low-osmolarity ORS: NaCl 2.6, glucose 13.5, KCl 1.5, trisodium citrate 2.9 g/L; 245 mOsm/L.
- Blood pH 7.35–7.45; HCO3⁻ : H2CO3 = 20 : 1.
- Fluoride forms acid-resistant fluorapatite; excess causes fluorosis.
- Zinc oxide + eugenol → zinc eugenolate: temporary filling and sedative dressing.
