# Unit 1: History of pharmacy, pharmacopoeias, dosage forms, prescription and posology

This first unit sets the base for the whole subject. It explains how the profession grew in India, which books set the official standards for medicines, what a dosage form is, how a prescription is written and handled, and how a dose is worked out for a child. Posology numericals (Young's, Clark's, BSA) and the parts of a prescription are asked in almost every paper.

## 1.1 Historical background of pharmacy in India

### Ancient and medieval period

- Indian medicine has a long written tradition. **Ayurveda** texts such as the **Charaka Samhita** (medicine) and the **Sushruta Samhita** (surgery) describe the collection, processing and mixing of plant, animal and mineral drugs.
- In these systems the physician (vaidya) himself prepared and gave medicines. Pharmacy was not a separate profession.
- The **Unani** system came with Arab and Persian physicians (hakims), and **Siddha** developed in South India. Both had their own methods of making medicines.

### British period and the start of modern pharmacy

- Allopathic (modern) medicine came with the British. Medicines were imported, and the first chemist and druggist shops were opened by British firms in the port cities (for example in Calcutta in the early nineteenth century).
- Hospitals employed "compounders" who learnt by working under doctors, with no formal training or registration.
- There was no law controlling the quality of drugs, so adulterated and substandard medicines were common.

### Growth of pharmacy education

| Milestone | What it meant |
| --- | --- |
| Compounder training in hospitals | First informal training, learnt on the job |
| **Prof. M. L. Schroff** at Banaras Hindu University | Started university-level pharmacy teaching; BHU began a degree course in pharmacy in 1937. Schroff is called the **father of pharmacy education in India** |
| Indian Pharmaceutical Association (1939) | First national body of pharmacists |
| **Pharmacy Act, 1948** | Created the Pharmacy Council of India and registration of pharmacists |
| **Pharmacy Council of India (PCI), 1949** | Regulates pharmacy education (minimum standards for D.Pharm, B.Pharm, Pharm.D) and keeps registers through State Pharmacy Councils |
| Later growth | M.Pharm and Ph.D. programmes, Pharm.D (2008), B.Pharm syllabus revised by PCI (the present credit-based syllabus) |

### Growth of the industry and of drug control

- **Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works** was founded in Calcutta by **Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray**; it is regarded as the first Indian pharmaceutical company (registered as a company in 1901).
- The **Drugs Enquiry Committee** (Chopra Committee, 1930–31), headed by Col. R. N. Chopra, reported on poor-quality drugs and recommended a drug law and a pharmacopoeia for India.
- This led to the **Drugs Act, 1940** (now the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**) and its Rules (1945), which control import, manufacture, sale and distribution of drugs.
- After independence, public sector units (for example Hindustan Antibiotics Ltd and IDPL) and many private firms grew. The Patents Act of 1970 allowed Indian firms to make generic versions of drugs, and India became a large exporter of generic medicines, often called the "pharmacy of the world".

### Organisations connected with pharmacy

| Organisation | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacy Council of India | Education standards and registration (Pharmacy Act, 1948) |
| Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO), headed by the Drugs Controller General of India | Approval of new drugs, import licences, regulation under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act |
| Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission (IPC), Ghaziabad | Publishes the Indian Pharmacopoeia and runs the pharmacovigilance programme |
| Indian Pharmaceutical Association (IPA) | Professional body of pharmacists |
| Indian Pharmaceutical Congress Association | Holds the yearly Indian Pharmaceutical Congress |
| All India Council for Technical Education (AICTE) | Approves technical institutions |

## 1.2 Pharmacy as a career

A registered pharmacist can work in many fields:

| Field | Typical roles |
| --- | --- |
| Community pharmacy | Retail and wholesale pharmacist, dispensing, patient counselling |
| Hospital and clinical pharmacy | Dispensing in hospitals, drug information, clinical pharmacist, therapeutic drug monitoring |
| Pharmaceutical industry | Formulation research and development, production, quality control, quality assurance, regulatory affairs, packaging, marketing and sales |
| Government service | Drug Inspector, Government Analyst, pharmacist in government hospitals, armed forces, railways |
| Teaching and research | Lecturer and professor in pharmacy colleges, scientist in research institutes |
| Clinical research and pharmacovigilance | Clinical research associate, drug safety associate, medical writing |
| Other | Entrepreneur (own pharmacy or manufacturing unit), medical coding, data analysis, cosmetics and nutraceuticals |

Only a person registered with a State Pharmacy Council may dispense medicines on a prescription in a pharmacy.

## 1.3 Pharmacopoeias

**Pharmacopoeia** (Greek *pharmakon* = drug, *poiein* = to make): an official book, published under the authority of a government, that lays down the **standards of identity, purity and strength** of drugs and their formulations, with methods of testing. Drugs sold in a country must meet the standards of its official pharmacopoeia.

A **monograph** is the entry for one substance or preparation. It usually gives the name, chemical formula and molecular weight, definition and content limits, description, solubility, identification tests, tests for impurities, assay, storage and labelling.

### Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)

- First edition published in **1955** (supplement 1960), following the Chopra Committee's advice. Later editions: 1966, 1985, 1996, 2007, 2010, 2014, 2018 and **2022** (current).
- Now published by the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission**, an autonomous body under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, at **Ghaziabad**.
- Under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act, the IP is the official book of standards for drugs in India.
- Recent editions are in several volumes and include general notices, appendices (test methods, reagents), monographs on drug substances, dosage forms, vaccines, blood products, herbal products and veterinary products.

### British Pharmacopoeia (BP)

- First published in **1864** by the General Medical Council; now prepared by the British Pharmacopoeia Commission and published every year.
- Official in the United Kingdom and used in many Commonwealth countries; includes the European Pharmacopoeia monographs.
- The **British Pharmaceutical Codex (BPC)** was a companion book of formulas for preparations not in the BP; many classical formulations you make in the laboratory (for example BPC 1968 preparations) come from it.

### United States Pharmacopeia (USP)

- First published in **1820**. Published by the United States Pharmacopeial Convention, a non-government body, and legally recognised in the USA.
- Now issued together with the **National Formulary (NF)** as the single book **USP–NF**: the USP covers drug substances and dosage forms, the NF covers excipients.

### Extra Pharmacopoeia (Martindale)

- First compiled by **William Martindale** in 1883. Now called **Martindale: The Complete Drug Reference**.
- It is **not an official standard**; it is a reference book giving information on drugs in use worldwide: actions, uses, adverse effects, interactions, doses and proprietary names.

### Other compendia

International Pharmacopoeia (WHO), European Pharmacopoeia, Japanese Pharmacopoeia, National Formulary of India (NFI, a guide to rational prescribing), Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India.

| Book | Official? | Main use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IP | Yes, in India | Legal standards in India |
| BP | Yes, in UK | Standards, yearly editions |
| USP–NF | Yes, in USA | Drug and excipient standards |
| Martindale | No | Drug information reference |

## 1.4 Dosage forms

**Drug:** a substance used for diagnosis, prevention, treatment or relief of disease.

**Dosage form:** the physical form in which a drug is given to the patient, made by combining the drug with suitable non-medicinal substances (**excipients**). Examples: tablet, capsule, syrup, ointment, injection.

### Why dosage forms are needed

1. To give an **accurate dose**, especially of very potent drugs in milligram or microgram amounts.
2. To **protect the drug** from air, moisture, light and stomach acid (coated tablets, amber bottles).
3. To **mask bitter taste or odour** (sugar coating, capsules, flavoured syrups).
4. To give **sustained or controlled release** (sustained-release tablets, patches).
5. To deliver drug to a **particular site** (eye drops, inhalers, suppositories, topical creams).
6. To allow **easy use** by children, the elderly and unconscious patients (liquids, suppositories, injections).
7. To give **rapid action** (injections, sublingual tablets).

### Classification

**A. By physical state**

| Class | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Solid | Powders, granules, tablets, capsules, lozenges, pills, suppositories, pessaries |
| Semisolid | Ointments, creams, pastes, gels, jellies, poultices |
| Liquid | Monophasic: solutions, syrups, elixirs, linctuses, drops, gargles, mouthwashes, liniments, lotions; biphasic: suspensions, emulsions; parenterals |
| Gaseous | Aerosols, inhalations, sprays, fumigants |

**B. By route of administration**

| Route | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Oral | Tablets, capsules, syrups, suspensions |
| Parenteral | Injections, infusions, implants |
| Topical (skin) | Ointments, creams, lotions, dusting powders |
| Rectal | Suppositories, enemas |
| Vaginal | Pessaries, vaginal creams |
| Ophthalmic | Eye drops, eye ointments |
| Otic | Ear drops |
| Nasal | Nasal drops, nasal sprays |
| Inhalation | Metered-dose inhalers, dry powder inhalers, nebuliser solutions |
| Buccal / sublingual | Sublingual tablets, lozenges |

**C. By use:** internal (taken into the body) and external (applied to the body surface).

### Short definitions

- **Tablet:** solid unit dosage form made by compressing drug with excipients.
- **Capsule:** drug enclosed in a hard or soft shell, usually of gelatin.
- **Syrup:** concentrated aqueous solution of sugar, with or without medicinal or flavouring agents.
- **Elixir:** clear, sweetened, hydroalcoholic liquid for oral use.
- **Suspension:** fine insoluble solid particles dispersed in a liquid.
- **Emulsion:** two immiscible liquids, one dispersed as globules in the other, stabilised by an emulsifying agent.
- **Ointment:** soft greasy semisolid for external application.
- **Suppository:** solid dosage form for insertion into the rectum, which melts or dissolves at body temperature.

## 1.5 Prescription

**Prescription:** a written order from a registered medical practitioner (or dentist or veterinarian) to a pharmacist to prepare and dispense a specified medicine for a named patient, with directions for its use.

### Parts of a prescription

```text
 Dr. A. Kumar, MBBS, MD          Reg. No. 12345       <- prescriber details
 Clinic address, phone
 Date: 10-07-2025                                     <- 1. date
 Name: Ravi Sharma   Age: 8 y   Sex: M   Wt: 24 kg    <- 2. patient details
 Address: ...
 Rx                                                    <- 3. superscription
   Paracetamol oral suspension 250 mg/5 ml   60 ml    <- 4. inscription
   Send 60 ml                                          <- 5. subscription
   Sig: 5 ml three times a day after food x 3 days    <- 6. signatura
 (Refill: nil)                                         <- 7. renewal
                                      Sd/- Dr. A. Kumar <- 8. signature
```

| Part | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Date | Date of writing; helps check the age of the prescription, especially for scheduled drugs |
| 2. Patient details | Name, age, sex, weight, address. Age and weight are essential for children's doses |
| 3. Superscription | The symbol **Rx** (from Latin *recipe*, "take thou") |
| 4. Inscription | The main part: names and quantities of the ingredients |
| 5. Subscription | Directions to the pharmacist: dosage form to make and the quantity to supply (for example "make 10 such powders") |
| 6. Signatura (Sig.) | Directions to the patient (dose, how often, how long); transferred to the label |
| 7. Renewal / refill | Whether the prescription may be dispensed again |
| 8. Prescriber's signature and registration number | Makes the prescription legally valid |

### Common Latin abbreviations

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| a.c. / p.c. | before food / after food |
| b.d. (b.i.d.) | twice a day |
| t.d.s. (t.i.d.) | three times a day |
| q.i.d. (q.d.s.) | four times a day |
| h.s. | at bedtime |
| stat | immediately |
| p.r.n. / s.o.s. | when required / if necessary |
| o.d. | once a day (also used for "right eye", so write it out in full) |
| q.s. | quantity sufficient |
| ad | up to |
| mist. | mixture |
| ung. | ointment |
| caps. / tab. | capsule / tablet |

### Handling of a prescription

1. **Receiving:** receive it yourself; do not keep the patient waiting without acknowledgement; do not show surprise or comment on the medicines.
2. **Reading and checking:** read it fully; check that it is genuine, dated, signed, and legible; check doses against standard references, look for incompatibilities and interactions; clear doubts with the prescriber, never with the patient.
3. **Collecting and weighing:** collect all ingredients, check each label three times (when taking the container, when weighing, when returning it); weigh and measure accurately.
4. **Compounding:** prepare by the correct method in clean equipment; only one prescription at a time.
5. **Finishing:** fill into a suitable, clean container; close it properly.
6. **Labelling:** name of the patient, name and strength of the medicine, directions, date, name and address of the pharmacy, auxiliary labels such as "Shake well before use" or "For external use only".
7. **Pricing and record:** enter the prescription in the prescription register (essential for scheduled drugs) and keep a copy when required.
8. **Delivering and counselling:** hand over to the patient, explain dose, timing, storage and possible side effects.

### Errors in prescriptions

| Type of error | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Illegible handwriting | Similar-looking drug names misread |
| Look-alike or sound-alike names | Mix-ups between drugs with similar names |
| Wrong dose or strength | Decimal errors: 0.5 mg written ".5 mg" and read as 5 mg; a trailing zero "5.0 mg" read as 50 mg |
| Unclear abbreviations | "U" for units read as "0"; "o.d." meaning once daily or right eye |
| Wrong route, frequency or duration | Missing duration; oral drug marked for injection |
| Omission | Patient's age or weight missing for a child; strength missing |
| Incompatibility or interaction | Two drugs that react or cancel each other |
| Missing legal details | No signature, date or registration number |

The pharmacist must never guess. Any doubt is cleared with the prescriber.

## 1.6 Posology

**Posology** (Greek *posos* = how much, *logos* = study): the branch of pharmacy that deals with **doses** of drugs.

**Dose:** the quantity of a drug given at one time to produce the desired effect. Related terms: **therapeutic dose** (usual effective dose), **minimum dose**, **maximum dose** (largest safe dose), **toxic dose**, **lethal dose**, **loading dose** (larger first dose to reach effective level quickly) and **maintenance dose**.

### Factors affecting dose

| Factor | Effect on dose |
| --- | --- |
| **Age** | Children (immature liver and kidney) and the elderly (reduced organ function) need smaller doses |
| **Body weight / surface area** | Dose often worked out per kg or per m² |
| **Sex** | Women may need smaller doses of some drugs; care in pregnancy and lactation (drugs reach the fetus or milk) |
| **Route of administration** | Intravenous dose is usually smaller than the oral dose of the same drug |
| **Time of administration** | Drugs act faster on an empty stomach; irritant drugs are given after food |
| **Disease state** | Kidney or liver disease reduces elimination, so doses are reduced |
| **Tolerance** | Larger doses needed after repeated use (for example opioids, alcohol) |
| **Idiosyncrasy / genetic factors** | Unusual response in some individuals |
| **Cumulation** | Slowly eliminated drugs (for example digoxin) build up, so doses are adjusted |
| **Drug interactions** | One drug can raise or lower the effect of another |
| **Emotional and environmental factors** | Placebo response, temperature, altitude |
| **Rate of metabolism and excretion** | Fast metabolisers may need larger doses |

## 1.7 Paediatric dose calculations

Most rules give the child dose as a **fraction of the adult dose**. Learn the formula, then substitute.

### Based on age

| Rule | Formula | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Young's rule** | Child dose = Age (years) ÷ (Age + 12) × Adult dose | Children 1–12 years |
| **Dilling's rule** | Child dose = Age (years) ÷ 20 × Adult dose | Children (up to about 20 years) |
| **Cowling's rule** | Child dose = Age at next birthday (years) ÷ 24 × Adult dose | Children |
| **Fried's rule** | Infant dose = Age (months) ÷ 150 × Adult dose | Infants below 1–2 years |

### Based on body weight

**Clark's rule:** Child dose = Weight (lb) ÷ 150 × Adult dose, or Weight (kg) ÷ 70 × Adult dose.

When a dose per kg is known, simply multiply: dose = mg/kg × body weight.

### Based on body surface area (most accurate)

Body surface area (BSA) is closely related to blood volume, kidney function and metabolic rate, so it gives the best estimate, and it is used for anticancer drugs.

Child dose = **BSA of child (m²) ÷ 1.73 × Adult dose** (1.73 m² is the average adult BSA).

BSA is read from a nomogram, or calculated. **Mosteller formula:** BSA (m²) = √[height (cm) × weight (kg) ÷ 3600]. (The Du Bois formula, BSA = 0.007184 × W^0.425 × H^0.725, is an older alternative.)

### Worked examples (adult dose 500 mg)

| Problem | Working | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Young's rule, child of 6 years | 6 ÷ (6 + 12) × 500 = 6/18 × 500 | **166.7 mg** |
| Dilling's rule, child of 10 years | 10 ÷ 20 × 500 | **250 mg** |
| Cowling's rule, child aged 5 (next birthday 6) | 6 ÷ 24 × 500 | **125 mg** |
| Fried's rule, infant of 6 months | 6 ÷ 150 × 500 | **20 mg** |
| Clark's rule, child of 30 lb | 30 ÷ 150 × 500 | **100 mg** |
| Clark's rule, child of 20 kg | 20 ÷ 70 × 500 = 0.2857 × 500 | **142.9 mg** |
| BSA, child 0.8 m² | 0.8 ÷ 1.73 × 500 = 0.4624 × 500 | **231.2 mg** |

**BSA by Mosteller, then dose.** A child is 120 cm tall and weighs 25 kg; adult dose 500 mg.
BSA = √(120 × 25 ÷ 3600) = √(3000 ÷ 3600) = √0.8333 = **0.913 m²** (0.9129).
Child dose = 0.9129 ÷ 1.73 × 500 = 0.5277 × 500 = **263.8 mg** (about 264 mg).

**Dose by mg/kg.** Paracetamol is prescribed at 15 mg/kg per dose for a 24 kg child, using a suspension of 250 mg in 5 ml.
Dose = 15 × 24 = 360 mg. Volume = 360 ÷ 250 × 5 = **7.2 ml** per dose.

Notes for answers: age rules ignore the size of the child, so weight or BSA rules are preferred; always check the result against the maximum dose; round to a measurable volume.

## Remember

- PCI was set up under the **Pharmacy Act, 1948** (Council formed 1949); drug quality is controlled by the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**.
- Prof. M. L. Schroff is the father of pharmacy education in India; the Chopra Committee (1930–31) led to drug laws and the IP.
- IP first edition **1955**, current edition **2022**, published by the IPC, Ghaziabad. BP first 1864, USP first 1820. Martindale is a reference, not an official standard.
- Parts of a prescription: date, patient details, superscription (Rx), inscription, subscription, signatura, renewal, signature.
- Young's: Age/(Age + 12); Dilling's: Age/20; Cowling's: next birthday/24; Fried's: months/150; Clark's: lb/150 or kg/70; BSA: m²/1.73.
