# Unit 5: Semisolid dosage forms

Semisolids are applied to the skin or mucous membranes for protection, moisturising and treatment of skin disease, and sometimes for drug action deeper in the tissues. This unit covers the types of semisolids, how drugs pass through the skin, ointment bases, methods of preparing ointments, pastes, creams and gels, the excipients used and how the products are evaluated. Classification of ointment bases and factors affecting skin penetration are the most frequently asked topics.

## 5.1 Definitions and classification

**Semisolid dosage forms:** preparations of soft consistency, intended for application to the skin or certain mucous membranes (eye, nose, rectum, vagina), that keep their shape at rest but spread easily under pressure.

| Dosage form | Definition | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ointment** | Soft, greasy (or water-soluble) semisolid with drug dissolved or dispersed in a single-phase base | Sulphur ointment, simple ointment, Whitfield's ointment |
| **Cream** | Semisolid **emulsion** (O/W or W/O), less greasy and more cosmetically acceptable | Cold cream (W/O), vanishing cream (O/W), cetrimide cream |
| **Paste** | Ointment with a **high proportion of finely powdered solids** (often 20–50% or more), stiff and less greasy | Zinc oxide paste, compound zinc paste, Lassar's paste |
| **Gel (jelly)** | Semisolid made of a liquid held in a three-dimensional network of a gelling agent; usually transparent | Carbopol gel, diclofenac gel, lignocaine gel, sodium alginate gel |
| **Poultice (cataplasm)** | Soft paste applied hot to reduce inflammation | Kaolin poultice |
| **Plasters** | Adhesive material spread on a backing | Medicated plasters |

**Classification by site of use:** dermatological (skin), ophthalmic (eye ointments, sterile), nasal, rectal, vaginal.

**Classification by depth of action:**

- **Epidermic (surface) action:** protective, emollient, antiseptic (zinc oxide, calamine).
- **Endodermic action:** drug penetrates the skin layers (corticosteroids, antifungals, local anaesthetics).
- **Diadermic action:** drug passes through the skin into the blood for systemic action (nitroglycerin ointment).

## 5.2 Structure of skin and dermal penetration of drugs

```text
   SKIN (cross-section)
   ---------------------------------------------- surface
   | Stratum corneum  (10-20 µm, dead keratinised  |  <- main barrier
   |                   cells + lipid "mortar")     |
   | Viable epidermis (living cells, no blood)     |
   |-----------------------------------------------|
   | Dermis (blood vessels, nerves, hair follicles,|
   |         sweat glands, sebaceous glands)       |
   |-----------------------------------------------|
   | Subcutaneous fat (hypodermis)                 |
   ----------------------------------------------
```

The **stratum corneum** is the main barrier. It is often described as "bricks and mortar": dead keratin-filled cells (bricks) in a matrix of lipids (mortar).

### Mechanisms (routes) of penetration

1. **Transepidermal route** (main route)
   - **Intercellular**: drug diffuses through the lipid channels between the cells — the main path for most drugs.
   - **Transcellular (intracellular)**: drug passes through the cells and the lipids in turn; favours hydrophilic drugs to a small extent.
2. **Transappendageal (shunt) route** — through **hair follicles and sebaceous glands** (transfollicular) and **sweat ducts**. These make up only a small fraction of the skin surface, but they matter early after application and for large or ionised molecules.

Passive diffusion follows **Fick's law**:

**J = D × K × ΔC ÷ h**

J = flux (amount crossing per unit area per unit time), D = diffusion coefficient in the membrane, K = partition coefficient between membrane and vehicle, ΔC = concentration difference across the membrane, h = thickness of the membrane.

### Factors influencing dermal penetration

**A. Drug (physicochemical) factors**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Molecular size | Small molecules (molecular weight below about 500) penetrate better |
| Partition coefficient | A balance of oil and water solubility is best; moderately lipophilic drugs (log P about 1–3) cross the skin well |
| Solubility and concentration | Higher concentration in the vehicle (up to saturation) gives a higher driving force |
| Ionisation (pKa) | Un-ionised molecules cross more easily |
| Melting point | Low-melting drugs are more soluble in the skin lipids |

**B. Vehicle (base) factors**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Release of drug from base | The drug must leave the base; if it is very soluble in the base, it stays there |
| **Occlusion** | Greasy bases and occlusive dressings stop water loss, so the stratum corneum hydrates and swells, increasing penetration |
| Penetration enhancers | Propylene glycol, alcohols, DMSO, oleic acid, urea, surfactants loosen the lipid barrier |
| pH of the vehicle | Controls ionisation of the drug |

**C. Skin (biological) factors**

| Factor | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| **Hydration** | Hydrated skin is more permeable |
| Site of application | Thin skin (scrotum, face, eyelids) is more permeable than thick skin (palms, soles) |
| Condition of skin | Damaged, diseased or burnt skin allows much more absorption |
| Age | Infants (larger surface area per kg) and the elderly absorb differently; infants are at risk of toxicity |
| Blood flow | Higher blood flow removes drug faster and keeps the gradient |
| Temperature | Warm skin is more permeable |
| Skin metabolism | Enzymes in skin may break down some drugs |

**D. Method of application:** amount applied, area, rubbing and time of contact.

## 5.3 Ointment bases

The base is the vehicle in which the drug is incorporated. It controls release, occlusion, ease of application and stability.

### Ideal properties of an ointment base

- Non-irritant, non-sensitising, inert and compatible with drugs.
- Stable; does not turn rancid.
- Releases the drug easily.
- Easy to apply and remove; does not stain.
- Smooth, of suitable consistency, not affected much by temperature.
- Free from microbial contamination.

### Classification of ointment bases

| Class | Properties | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Oleaginous (hydrocarbon) bases** | Greasy, anhydrous, occlusive, emollient, do not absorb water, hard to wash off, do not dry out, cheap and stable | White and yellow soft paraffin (petrolatum), hard paraffin, liquid paraffin, vegetable oils, animal fats, beeswax |
| **2. Absorption bases** | Can absorb water to form W/O emulsions. (a) **Anhydrous** types that take up water; (b) **W/O emulsion** types that already contain water but can take more. Greasy, not easily washed | (a) Wool fat (anhydrous lanolin), wool alcohols ointment, hydrophilic petrolatum; (b) hydrous wool fat, cold cream |
| **3. Water-removable (emulsion O/W) bases** | O/W emulsions; washable with water, non-greasy, can be diluted with water, absorb exudates. Less occlusive | Hydrophilic ointment, emulsifying ointment (when made into a cream), vanishing cream |
| **4. Water-soluble bases** | Contain no fat; completely water-soluble and washable; non-occlusive; do not stain | Polyethylene glycol (macrogol) ointment |

```text
   Increasing water-washability ------------------------>
   Hydrocarbon  -->  Absorption  -->  O/W removable  -->  Water-soluble
   Increasing occlusion and greasiness <------------------
```

### Choice of base

Depends on: desired action (surface protection needs an occlusive base; deep penetration needs a base that releases the drug and hydrates skin), nature of the skin condition (dry scaly lesions → greasy base; wet oozing lesions → washable or drying base or paste), stability and compatibility of the drug, ease of application and patient acceptance.

## 5.4 Preparation of ointments

| Method | Used when | Procedure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Trituration (incorporation)** | Base is soft and drug is a solid or small liquid quantity | Finely powder the drug, levigate it with a little base (or liquid paraffin) on a tile with a spatula until smooth, then add the rest of the base in portions (geometric dilution). Soluble drugs may be dissolved in a little water or solvent first |
| **Fusion** | Base contains hard ingredients (waxes, hard paraffin, wool fat) or the drug is soluble in the melted base | Melt the ingredients in order of decreasing melting point (highest first) on a water bath, add soluble drugs, stir continuously while cooling until it sets (to avoid lumps and graininess). Do not over-heat |
| **Chemical reaction** | A reaction forms the active ingredient | Example: iodine ointments in which iodine reacts with an oil or fatty acid, giving a non-staining product |
| **Emulsification** | Creams and emulsion ointments | Heat oil phase and aqueous phase separately to about the same temperature (about 70–75 °C), add one to the other with stirring, stir until cool |

**Example formula: simple ointment** (an oleaginous base used in fusion practice) contains wool fat, hard paraffin and cetostearyl alcohol in white or yellow soft paraffin.

**Example: Sulphur ointment** — precipitated sulphur (10%) incorporated in simple ointment by trituration; used for scabies and fungal skin infections.

## 5.5 Preparation of pastes

- Pastes are stiff because of a high solid content. They stay in place, absorb exudates and protect the skin. They are less occlusive than ointments because the powder forms channels.
- **Bases:** hydrocarbon (soft paraffin) for fatty pastes; glycerin or a gel for non-greasy pastes.
- **Method:** finely sift the powders, triturate them with a portion of the base (often warmed) or with liquid paraffin to a smooth paste, add the remaining base, or use fusion when the base needs melting.
- Examples: **Compound zinc paste** (zinc oxide and starch in white soft paraffin), **Zinc and salicylic acid paste (Lassar's paste)** (zinc oxide, starch and 2% salicylic acid in white soft paraffin), **Coal tar paste**.

## 5.6 Preparation of creams

- Creams are emulsions: **O/W (vanishing)** creams are non-greasy and washable; **W/O (cold)** creams are emollient and cooling as the water evaporates.
- **Method:** prepare oil phase (oils, waxes, oil-soluble emulsifier and drug) and aqueous phase (water, water-soluble emulsifier, humectant, preservative) separately; heat both to about 70–75 °C; add the internal phase to the external phase with stirring; stir until cool; add volatile ingredients and perfume below about 40 °C; homogenise if needed.
- **Cold cream:** beeswax and liquid paraffin with borax and water; borax reacts with free fatty acids in beeswax to form sodium soap at the interface.
- **Vanishing cream:** stearic acid partly neutralised with an alkali (potassium hydroxide or triethanolamine) to form soap; leaves a thin non-greasy film.

## 5.7 Preparation of gels

- **Gels** contain a gelling agent that forms a network trapping the liquid.
- **Gelling agents:** natural (tragacanth, sodium alginate, pectin, gelatin, xanthan), semi-synthetic cellulose derivatives (methylcellulose, HPMC, sodium CMC), synthetic polymers (**carbomers / carbopol**, poloxamers), inorganic (bentonite, colloidal silica).
- **Carbopol gel method:** sprinkle the carbomer powder slowly on water with constant stirring (avoid lumps), allow it to swell, then **neutralise with triethanolamine or sodium hydroxide** to a pH of about 6–7. The acidic polymer chains ionise, uncoil by charge repulsion and the dispersion thickens suddenly into a clear gel.
- **Single-phase gels** (polymer uniformly spread, clear) and **two-phase gels** (small discrete particles such as aluminium hydroxide gel).
- Problems: **syneresis** (gel contracts and squeezes out liquid), swelling, microbial growth (add preservative).

## 5.8 Excipients used in semisolid dosage forms

| Excipient | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bases | Vehicle | Soft paraffin, wool fat, PEGs, emulsifying wax |
| Emulsifying agents | Form creams | Emulsifying wax, cetostearyl alcohol with sodium lauryl sulphate, polysorbates, sorbitan esters, wool alcohols |
| Stiffening agents | Increase consistency | Hard paraffin, beeswax, cetyl and stearyl alcohol |
| Gelling agents | Form gels | Carbomer, HPMC, sodium CMC, tragacanth |
| Neutralising agents | Thicken carbomer gels | Triethanolamine, sodium hydroxide |
| Humectants | Prevent drying, keep skin moist | Glycerin, propylene glycol, sorbitol |
| Preservatives | Prevent microbial growth (water-containing products) | Methyl and propyl parabens, chlorocresol, benzalkonium chloride, phenoxyethanol |
| Antioxidants | Prevent rancidity of fats and oils | BHA, BHT, tocopherol, propyl gallate |
| Penetration enhancers | Increase drug passage through skin | Propylene glycol, DMSO, oleic acid, urea, ethanol, isopropyl myristate |
| Buffers | Maintain pH | Citrate and phosphate buffers |
| Chelating agents | Bind metal ions | Disodium edetate |
| Perfumes and colours | Acceptability | Rose oil, lavender oil |

## 5.9 Evaluation of semisolid dosage forms

| Test | Purpose and method |
| --- | --- |
| **Appearance and homogeneity** | Colour, odour, texture; smear on a glass plate to check for grittiness or lumps |
| **Drug content uniformity** | Assay samples from different parts of the container |
| **pH** | pH meter on a dispersion; skin-compatible pH |
| **Viscosity / rheology** | Brookfield (rotational) viscometer; semisolids show non-Newtonian (plastic or pseudoplastic, thixotropic) flow |
| **Consistency (penetration test)** | **Cone penetrometer**: depth a standard cone sinks into the sample in a fixed time |
| **Spreadability** | Parallel-plate method: time for a top slide to move a fixed distance under a weight. **S = M × L ÷ T** (M = weight tied to the upper slide, L = length moved, T = time); shorter time means better spreading |
| **Extrudability** | Weight or pressure needed to squeeze a ribbon of product from a collapsible tube |
| **Drug release (in vitro diffusion)** | **Franz diffusion cell**: product placed on a membrane (synthetic or skin) over a receptor fluid at 32–37 °C; samples analysed over time |
| **Skin irritation / sensitisation** | Patch tests on animals or volunteers |
| **Microbial limit test / sterility** | Absence of specified organisms; ophthalmic ointments must be sterile and checked for metal particles |
| **Minimum fill** | Contents of the container not below the labelled amount |
| **Stability** | Phase separation, colour, odour, pH, viscosity, drug content at accelerated and normal storage; freeze–thaw cycling for creams |
| **Occlusivity / water absorption** | Ability to prevent water loss; water number of a base (amount of water a base can hold) |

**Spreadability example.** A 50 g weight pulls the upper slide 7.5 cm in 10 s.
S = 50 × 7.5 ÷ 10 = **37.5 g·cm/s**.

## Remember

- Ointment = single-phase greasy base; cream = emulsion; paste = high solid content; gel = liquid in a polymer network.
- Stratum corneum is the main barrier; main route is intercellular (transepidermal); appendages give a minor shunt route. Fick's law J = DKΔC/h.
- Penetration rises with small size, balanced log P, high concentration, occlusion, hydration, damaged or thin skin.
- Ointment bases: hydrocarbon, absorption (anhydrous or W/O), water-removable (O/W), water-soluble (PEG).
- Methods: trituration, fusion (melt highest melting point first), chemical reaction, emulsification (both phases at 70–75 °C).
- Carbopol gels form when the polymer is neutralised (triethanolamine) to about pH 6–7.
- Evaluation: content, pH, viscosity, penetrometer, spreadability S = ML/T, extrudability, Franz cell release, irritation, microbial limits.
