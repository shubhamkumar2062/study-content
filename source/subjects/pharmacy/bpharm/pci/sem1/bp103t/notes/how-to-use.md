# How to use this guide

Pharmaceutics I is the first "making medicines" subject of the B.Pharm course. It mixes descriptive theory (history, dosage forms, suppositories, semisolids) with numericals (posology, percentage, alligation, proof spirit, isotonicity and displacement value). Study one unit a day: read the notes, redo every worked example with a calculator without looking, draw the diagrams and tables once from memory, then answer the question bank. Each unit has very short (2-mark), short answer (5-mark) and long essay (10-mark) questions, rated by how often that kind of question appears in PCI-syllabus pharmacy papers.

The notes follow the five units of the PCI syllabus exactly. Unit 2 is large (calculations, powders and liquid dosage forms), so it has the longest notes; no unit has been split or joined. The practical guides cover the BP109P list: syrups, elixirs, linctus and paint, solutions, suspensions, emulsions, powders and granules, suppositories, semisolids, and gargles and mouthwashes.

**Typical PCI end-semester paper (75 marks, 3 hours).** Confirm the exact split with your college, since universities sometimes vary it.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Objective / MCQ or very short answers | 20 × 1 = 20 |
| B | Long essays — attempt 2 of 3 | 2 × 10 = 20 |
| C | Short answers — attempt 7 of 9 | 7 × 5 = 35 |

**Probability ratings**

- **High** — asked in most years; prepare the full answer with tables, diagrams and a worked numerical.
- **Medium** — asked every few years or as part of a bigger question.
- **Low** — rare, but useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from common patterns and syllabus weight, not a promise of what will be asked.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Start every definition-type answer with a one-line definition and one example; examiners give marks for both.
2. Numericals: write the formula, substitute with units, and box the final answer with its unit (mg, ml, g of NaCl per 100 ml, degree proof). Method marks are given even if the arithmetic slips, so never leave a numerical blank.
3. Learn these numbers cold: proof spirit 57.1% v/v and factor 1.753; blood freezing point −0.52 °C; FPD of 1% NaCl 0.576 °C; 0.9% NaCl isotonic; adult BSA 1.73 m²; Simple Syrup IP 66.7% w/w; dry gum ratios 4:2:1, 3:2:1, 2:2:1; effervescent ratio 1:2:3.44.
4. Use tables for comparisons (flocculated vs deflocculated, O/W vs W/O, liniment vs lotion, types of ointment bases, physical vs chemical vs therapeutic incompatibility). A neat table often earns full marks for a 5-mark question.
5. Draw the prescription layout, the alligation cross, the flocculated and deflocculated sediment, the suppository mould and the skin layers. Simple labelled diagrams are enough.
6. In long essays on dosage forms, follow the same order every time: definition → classification → advantages and disadvantages → method of preparation → stability problems → evaluation → examples.
7. In practical exams, write the formula, the role of each ingredient, the label and storage; these carry marks in the viva and the record.
