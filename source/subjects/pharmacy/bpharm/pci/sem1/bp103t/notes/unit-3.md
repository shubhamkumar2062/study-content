# Unit 3: Monophasic liquids, suspensions and emulsions

Liquid preparations are the most common dosage forms prepared in a dispensing laboratory. Monophasic liquids are true solutions with one phase. Biphasic liquids have two phases: a solid dispersed in a liquid (suspension) or a liquid dispersed in another liquid (emulsion). Long questions on flocculated versus deflocculated suspensions, emulsifying agents, identification of emulsion type and stability problems appear very often.

## 3.1 Monophasic liquids

Monophasic liquids may be for **internal use** (syrups, elixirs, linctuses, drops), for use in **body cavities** (gargles, mouthwashes, throat paints, ear drops, nasal drops, enemas) or for **external use** (liniments, lotions).

### Gargles

- **Definition:** aqueous solutions used to treat or soothe the throat and pharynx. The liquid is held in the throat, agitated with air, and **spat out, not swallowed**.
- Usually dispensed as concentrated solutions, diluted with warm water before use.
- Contain antiseptics, astringents or mild analgesics.
- Examples: phenol gargle, potassium chlorate and phenol gargle, iodine gargle, povidone-iodine gargle.
- **Preparation:** dissolve the ingredients in purified water, filter if needed, label "Not to be swallowed" and give dilution directions.

### Mouthwashes

- **Definition:** aqueous solutions used to **clean, deodorise and freshen the mouth**, and for antiseptic or anti-plaque action. Used with or without dilution, swished in the mouth and spat out.
- Examples: **compound sodium chloride mouthwash** (sodium chloride, sodium bicarbonate, peppermint), **chlorhexidine gluconate mouthwash 0.2% w/v** (anti-plaque), hydrogen peroxide mouthwash.
- Ingredients: antiseptic, astringent, flavour, colour, sometimes a little alcohol.

### Throat paints

- **Definition:** **viscous** liquid preparations applied to the mouth or throat with a brush or swab.
- **Glycerin** is the usual base: it is viscous so it clings to the mucosa, it is sweet, and it has a mild antiseptic action.
- Example: **Compound iodine paint (Mandl's paint)** — iodine dissolved in potassium iodide solution, with peppermint oil and glycerin. Potassium iodide forms the soluble triiodide complex (I2 + I− ⇌ I3−).
- Others: borax glycerin, tannic acid glycerin.

### Ear drops

- **Definition:** solutions, suspensions or emulsions of drugs instilled into the ear with a dropper.
- **Uses:** to clean the ear, **soften and remove wax** (sodium bicarbonate ear drops, olive oil, hydrogen peroxide), and to treat infection or inflammation (antibiotic and corticosteroid drops).
- **Vehicles:** water, glycerin, propylene glycol, alcohol. Glycerin and propylene glycol are viscous and hygroscopic, so they stay in the ear and absorb moisture.
- Warm the bottle in the hand before use; do not use if the eardrum is perforated unless prescribed.

### Nasal drops

- **Definition:** solutions (or suspensions) instilled into the nostrils for local action (decongestant, antiseptic) or systemic effect.
- Should be **aqueous, nearly isotonic** and of pH close to that of nasal secretions, so that the cilia are not damaged.
- **Oily nasal drops are avoided**, because oil can be inhaled into the lungs and cause lipoid pneumonia; oil also slows ciliary movement.
- Examples: ephedrine nasal drops, xylometazoline nasal drops, saline nasal drops.

### Enemas

- **Definition:** aqueous or oily liquids introduced into the **rectum** for evacuation, local action, diagnosis or systemic effect.
- **Evacuant enemas** (cleansing, larger volume, expelled): soap enema, glycerin enema, phosphate enema, liquid paraffin (oily) enema.
- **Retention enemas** (small volume, kept in the rectum for local or systemic action): prednisolone and mesalazine enemas for ulcerative colitis; contrast enemas for X-ray.
- Warm to body temperature before use. Packed in disposable plastic packs with a nozzle.

### Syrups

- **Definition:** concentrated, viscous aqueous solutions of sugar (sucrose), with or without medicinal and flavouring agents.
- **Simple Syrup IP** contains **66.7% w/w sucrose** in purified water. At this concentration it is nearly saturated and resists microbial growth because the high osmotic pressure draws water out of microbes.
- **Types:** simple syrup (no drug), flavoured syrups (orange syrup, vehicle), medicated syrups (for example compound syrup of ferrous phosphate, paracetamol syrup), sugar-free syrups (sorbitol, saccharin, aspartame; for diabetics and dental care).

**Methods of preparation**

| Method | When used |
| --- | --- |
| Solution with heat | Quick; for heat-stable ingredients. Heat only enough to dissolve sugar |
| Agitation without heat | Heat-sensitive or volatile ingredients; slow |
| Percolation | Water passed through a column of sucrose |
| Addition of a medicated liquid to syrup | Tinctures, extracts or drug solutions mixed into syrup |

**Problems with syrups**

- **Inversion:** prolonged heating, especially in acid, hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose (invert sugar), which is more prone to fermentation and darkening (caramelisation).
- **Crystallisation:** sugar crystallises at the neck of the bottle (cap locking) if the syrup is supersaturated or evaporates.
- **Fermentation and mould** in dilute syrups: add preservative or keep the concentration high.

### Elixirs

- **Definition:** **clear, sweetened, flavoured, hydroalcoholic** liquids for oral use. Less sweet and less viscous than syrups.
- Alcohol keeps both water-soluble and alcohol-soluble ingredients in solution and acts as a preservative.
- **Types:** non-medicated (aromatic elixir, used as a vehicle) and medicated (paracetamol paediatric elixir, piperazine citrate elixir).
- Other ingredients: glycerin, propylene glycol, sorbitol, syrup, flavour, colour.
- **Preparation:** dissolve water-soluble ingredients in water and alcohol-soluble ones in alcohol; add the aqueous part to the alcoholic part slowly with stirring (to avoid cloudiness), make up to volume, filter (with talc if needed) and pack in tight amber bottles.

### Linctuses

Viscous, sweet liquids used for **cough**, sipped slowly and swallowed without water so they coat the throat. They usually contain a demulcent, expectorant or cough suppressant in syrup base (example: simple linctus, codeine linctus, terpin hydrate linctus).

### Liniments

- **Definition:** liquid or semi-liquid preparations for application to **intact skin with friction (rubbing)**.
- **Vehicles:** alcoholic (aid penetration, counter-irritant and rubefacient effect) or oily (milder, lubricant, good for massage).
- Contain counter-irritants, rubefacients or analgesics: turpentine oil, methyl salicylate, camphor.
- **Never apply to broken skin** (pain and toxic absorption). Label "For external use only".
- Examples: **turpentine liniment** (an emulsion made with soft soap), methyl salicylate liniment, camphor liniment.

### Lotions

- **Definition:** liquid preparations applied to the skin **without friction**, by dabbing with cotton or a soft cloth.
- Uses: cooling, soothing, protective, antiseptic, astringent or antipruritic.
- May be solutions or suspensions. Alcohol in a lotion gives a cooling effect by evaporation; glycerin keeps the skin moist.
- Example: **calamine lotion** (soothing for itching and sunburn), salicylic acid lotion.
- Label "Shake well" (suspensions) and "For external use only".

| Feature | Liniment | Lotion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Applied | With friction (rubbing) | Without friction (dabbing) |
| Purpose | Rubefacient, counter-irritant, analgesic | Cooling, soothing, protective |
| On broken skin | Never | Some can be used |

## 3.2 Suspensions

**Suspension:** a biphasic liquid in which **fine insoluble solid particles** (dispersed phase, usually 0.5–5 µm for oral use) are distributed in a liquid (continuous phase), usually with a suspending agent.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Give insoluble drugs in liquid form | Physically unstable: particles settle |
| Mask bitter taste (insoluble drug is not tasted) | Caking may make redispersion impossible |
| Drug more stable than in solution (less hydrolysis) | Dose uniformity depends on shaking |
| Can give sustained release (depot injections) | Bulky; must be handled carefully |
| Suitable for patients who cannot swallow tablets | Need a preservative and good formulation skill |

### Classification

- **By route:** oral (antacid suspensions, paracetamol suspension), topical (calamine lotion), parenteral (procaine penicillin, depot steroids), ophthalmic, otic, rectal.
- **By particle size:** colloidal (below 1 µm), coarse (above 1 µm), nanosuspensions.
- **By the state of the particles:** **flocculated** and **deflocculated**.
- **By nature of the solid:** **diffusible** solids (light kaolin, light magnesium carbonate, magnesium trisilicate, bismuth carbonate), which stay dispersed long enough after shaking to give a dose; **indiffusible** solids (calamine, zinc oxide, sulphur, hydrocortisone), which settle quickly and need a suspending agent.

### Properties of a good suspension

- Particles settle slowly and redisperse easily on gentle shaking.
- No cake forms; uniform dose is withdrawn after shaking.
- Pours easily from the bottle (not too viscous).
- Fine, uniform particles; pleasant appearance, taste and smell.
- Physically, chemically and microbially stable.

### Stokes' law

The rate of settling of a spherical particle:

**v = d² (ρs − ρo) g ÷ 18 η**

v = settling velocity, d = particle diameter, ρs and ρo = density of solid and liquid, g = gravity, η = viscosity of the medium.

So settling is slowed by **smaller particles**, **smaller density difference** and **higher viscosity**. Because d is squared, halving the particle size cuts the settling rate to one quarter.

### Preparation of suspensions

**1. Dispersion method (most common in dispensing)**

1. Reduce the solid to a fine powder.
2. **Wet** the hydrophobic powder with a wetting agent (glycerin, alcohol, a surfactant such as polysorbate) to drive off air.
3. Triturate with the **suspending agent** (tragacanth, acacia, methylcellulose, sodium CMC, bentonite) and a little vehicle to form a smooth paste.
4. Add the rest of the vehicle in portions with stirring; add preservative, flavour and colour.
5. Make up to volume and pack in a wide-mouthed bottle with space for shaking.

**2. Precipitation (condensation) method**

- **Organic solvent precipitation:** the drug dissolved in an organic solvent is poured into water (for example precipitating a water-insoluble substance from an alcoholic solution).
- **pH change:** the drug is soluble at one pH and precipitates at another.
- **Double decomposition:** two soluble reactants form an insoluble product, for example magnesium hydroxide mixture (MgSO4 + 2NaOH → Mg(OH)2 + Na2SO4) and aluminium hydroxide gel.

### Flocculated and deflocculated suspensions

Particles carry a surface charge (zeta potential). If repulsion is strong, they stay separate (**deflocculated**). If repulsion is reduced, they form loose clusters called **flocs** (**flocculated**).

| Property | Flocculated | Deflocculated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Particles | Loose aggregates (flocs) | Separate individual particles |
| Rate of sedimentation | Fast | Slow |
| Sediment | Loose, high volume, easily redispersed | Compact, small volume, hard **cake** that is difficult to redisperse |
| Supernatant | Clear quickly | Stays cloudy for a long time |
| Appearance | Less elegant (clear layer forms quickly) | Pleasing, uniform for a long time |
| Zeta potential | Low | High |

```text
   Deflocculated                     Flocculated
   (after storage)                   (after storage)
   |  cloudy liquid  |               |  clear liquid   |
   |  cloudy liquid  |               |                 |
   |  cloudy liquid  |               |.:.:.:.:.:.:.:.:.|
   |#################|  <- hard cake |.:.:.:.:.:.:.:.:.| <- loose floc bed
```

**Controlled flocculation** gives the best of both: flocs that settle into a loose, easily redispersed sediment, combined with a suspending agent (structured vehicle) to slow settling. Flocculating agents:

- **Electrolytes** (for example potassium dihydrogen phosphate, aluminium chloride) lower the zeta potential and form bridges.
- **Surfactants** (ionic or non-ionic) adsorb and neutralise charge.
- **Polymers** (hydrophilic colloids) form bridges between particles.

**Measuring suspension quality**

- **Sedimentation volume, F = Vu ÷ Vo**, where Vu = ultimate volume of sediment and Vo = original volume of suspension. F nearer 1 is better. Example: 50 ml suspension gives a 30 ml sediment → F = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.6.
- **Degree of flocculation, β = F ÷ F∞**, where F∞ is the sedimentation volume of the deflocculated suspension. Example: F = 0.6 and F∞ = 0.15 → β = 4.
- Ease of redispersion, particle size, zeta potential, viscosity (rheology).

### Stability problems and how to overcome them

| Problem | Cause | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rapid sedimentation | Large particles, low viscosity, high density difference | Reduce particle size, add suspending agent, adjust density (sorbitol, glycerin) |
| **Caking** | Deflocculated particles pack tightly | Controlled flocculation, structured vehicle |
| **Crystal growth (Ostwald ripening)** | Small particles dissolve and redeposit on large ones, especially with temperature changes | Narrow particle size range, protective colloids, steady storage temperature |
| Polymorphic change | Metastable form converts to stable form | Use the stable form |
| Microbial growth | Suspending agents of natural origin support microbes | Preservatives such as parabens |
| Chemical breakdown | Hydrolysis, oxidation | Buffers, antioxidants, correct pH |

## 3.3 Emulsions

**Emulsion:** a biphasic system of two immiscible liquids in which one liquid (**dispersed / internal phase**) is distributed as fine globules in the other (**continuous / external phase**), stabilised by an **emulsifying agent**.

### Classification

| Type | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oil-in-water (O/W)** | Oil globules in water | Liquid paraffin emulsion, castor oil emulsion, vanishing creams; most oral emulsions |
| **Water-in-oil (W/O)** | Water droplets in oil | Cold creams, oily calamine lotion, butter |
| **Multiple emulsions** | W/O/W or O/W/O | Used for prolonged release, taste masking |
| **Microemulsions** | Transparent, thermodynamically stable, globules below about 100 nm | Used for poorly soluble drugs |

**By use:** oral (O/W, oil taste masked), external (O/W washable, W/O emollient), parenteral (intravenous fat emulsions are O/W).

### Emulsifying agents

They lower interfacial tension and form a protective film round the globules.

| Group | Examples | Type formed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Natural** (hydrophilic colloids) | Acacia, tragacanth, gelatin, egg yolk, pectin, methylcellulose | O/W |
| **Natural** (sterol-containing) | Wool fat, wool alcohols, cholesterol, beeswax | W/O |
| **Synthetic surfactants: anionic** | Sodium lauryl sulphate; monovalent soaps (sodium or potassium stearate) | O/W |
| | Divalent soaps (calcium oleate) | W/O |
| **Cationic** | Cetrimide, benzalkonium chloride | O/W (mainly in external preparations) |
| **Non-ionic** | Polysorbates (Tweens) | O/W |
| | Sorbitan esters (Spans) | W/O |
| **Finely divided solids** | Bentonite, magnesium hydroxide, aluminium hydroxide (wetted mainly by water) | O/W |
| | Carbon black, talc (wetted by oil) | W/O |

**Bancroft's rule:** the phase in which the emulsifier is more soluble becomes the continuous phase.

### HLB system

The **hydrophile–lipophile balance** (HLB) number (0–20) shows the balance between water-loving and oil-loving parts of a surfactant.

| HLB range | Use |
| --- | --- |
| 1–3 | Antifoaming agents |
| 3–6 | W/O emulsifiers |
| 7–9 | Wetting agents |
| 8–18 | O/W emulsifiers |
| 13–16 | Detergents |
| 15–18 | Solubilisers |

**Blending example.** An oil needs a required HLB of 12. Use Span 80 (HLB 4.3) and Tween 80 (HLB 15.0). Let x = fraction of Tween 80.
15.0x + 4.3(1 − x) = 12 → 10.7x = 7.7 → x = 0.72.
So use **72% Tween 80 and 28% Span 80** (for 5 g of emulsifier: 3.6 g Tween 80 + 1.4 g Span 80).

### Tests to identify the type of emulsion

| Test | Procedure | O/W result | W/O result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Dilution test** | Add the emulsion to water | Mixes easily (water is continuous) | Does not mix; stays as lumps |
| **Dye solubility test** | Add a water-soluble dye (amaranth or methylene blue) and look under the microscope | Continuous phase coloured, globules colourless | Globules coloured, background colourless |
| | Add an oil-soluble dye (Sudan III) | Globules red | Continuous phase red |
| **Conductivity test** | Dip two electrodes with a bulb in circuit | Bulb glows (water conducts) | Bulb does not glow |
| **Fluorescence test** | View under UV light (many oils fluoresce) | Only dots fluoresce | Whole field fluoresces |
| **Cobalt chloride paper** | Dip filter paper soaked in CoCl2 and dried (blue) | Turns pink (water continuous) | Stays blue |

### Methods of preparation

| Method | How | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Dry gum (continental) method** | Triturate oil with dry gum (acacia), then add all the water at once and triturate rapidly until a clicking primary emulsion forms; dilute | Ratio oil : water : gum = **4 : 2 : 1** for fixed oils, **3 : 2 : 1** for mineral oil, **2 : 2 : 1** for volatile oils |
| **Wet gum (English) method** | Make a mucilage of gum with water, then add oil in small portions with trituration | Same ratios; slower but suits larger batches |
| **Bottle (Forbes bottle) method** | Shake oil and powdered acacia in a dry bottle, add water, shake vigorously | For **volatile oils** and low-viscosity oils |
| **Nascent soap (in situ soap) method** | A fatty acid in the oil reacts with an alkali (for example oleic acid in a vegetable oil with lime water) to form soap at the interface | Calcium soaps give W/O (oily calamine lotion, lime liniment) |
| **Mechanical methods** | Mixers, homogenisers, colloid mills, ultrasonifiers | Industrial scale, fine and uniform globules |

The **primary emulsion** is the thick, white, clicking emulsion formed first; it is then diluted with the rest of the water.

### Stability problems and how to overcome them

| Problem | What happens | Reversible? | Remedy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Creaming** (upward) or **sedimentation** (downward) | Globules collect at the top or bottom without joining; obeys Stokes' law | Yes, by shaking | Reduce globule size (homogenise), increase viscosity of continuous phase, reduce density difference |
| **Flocculation** | Globules cluster loosely | Yes | Adequate emulsifier film, suitable charge |
| **Cracking (coalescence / breaking)** | Globules merge and the phases separate permanently | No | Enough emulsifier, avoid incompatible additives (electrolytes, alcohol, opposite-charge surfactants), avoid heat, freezing and microbial growth |
| **Phase inversion** | O/W changes to W/O or the reverse | Sometimes | Keep the phase volume ratio suitable (internal phase usually below about 60–70%), avoid adding substances that change the emulsifier (for example calcium salts with sodium soaps) |
| **Microbial contamination** | Water phase and natural gums support growth | No | Preservatives effective in the water phase (parabens), clean manufacture |
| **Oxidation (rancidity)** | Oils turn rancid | No | Antioxidants (BHA, BHT, tocopherol), air-tight full containers |

## Remember

- Simple Syrup IP = 66.7% w/w sucrose, self-preserving. Elixir = clear, sweet, hydroalcoholic. Throat paints use glycerin. Nasal drops: aqueous, isotonic, never oily.
- Liniment = with friction, never on broken skin; lotion = without friction.
- Stokes' law: v = d²(ρs − ρo)g/18η — smaller particles, higher viscosity, smaller density difference slow settling.
- Flocculated: fast settling, loose easily redispersed sediment; deflocculated: slow settling but hard cake. Controlled flocculation is the aim. F = Vu/Vo, β = F/F∞.
- Emulsion tests: dilution, dye, conductivity, fluorescence, cobalt chloride paper.
- Dry gum ratios 4:2:1 (fixed oil), 3:2:1 (mineral oil), 2:2:1 (volatile oil).
- Creaming is reversible; cracking is not. HLB 3–6 W/O, 8–18 O/W.
