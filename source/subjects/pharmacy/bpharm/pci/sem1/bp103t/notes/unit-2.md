# Unit 2: Pharmaceutical calculations, powders and liquid dosage forms

This unit has three parts. Pharmaceutical calculations (percentage, alligation, proof spirit, isotonicity) carry sure marks if you practise them. Powders cover simple and compound powders, dusting and effervescent powders and the special problems of eutectic, efflorescent and hygroscopic substances. The last part introduces liquid dosage forms, their excipients and ways of improving solubility.

## 2.1 Weights and measures

### Metric system

Based on multiples of 10. Base units: **gram (g)** for mass, **litre (L)** for volume, **metre (m)** for length.

| Mass | Volume |
| --- | --- |
| 1 kg = 1000 g | 1 L = 1000 ml |
| 1 g = 1000 mg | 1 ml = 1000 µl |
| 1 mg = 1000 µg | 1 ml of water weighs about 1 g at 4 °C |

### Imperial and apothecaries' systems

These older British systems still appear in old formulas and in some exam questions.

**Weight (avoirdupois and apothecaries')**

| Unit | Equals | Metric value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 grain (gr) | basic unit in both systems | 64.8 mg |
| 1 scruple (apoth.) | 20 grains | 1.296 g |
| 1 drachm (apoth.) | 3 scruples = 60 grains | 3.888 g |
| 1 ounce (apoth.) | 8 drachms = 480 grains | 31.1 g |
| 1 ounce (avoirdupois) | 437.5 grains | 28.35 g |
| 1 pound (avoirdupois) | 16 ounces = 7000 grains | 453.6 g |

**Volume (imperial)**

| Unit | Equals | Metric value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 minim | basic unit | 0.0592 ml |
| 1 fluid drachm | 60 minims | 3.55 ml |
| 1 fluid ounce | 8 fluid drachms = 480 minims | 28.4 ml |
| 1 pint | 20 fluid ounces | 568 ml |
| 1 gallon | 8 pints | 4.546 L |

**Domestic measures:** 1 teaspoonful = 5 ml; 1 tablespoonful = 15 ml. Prescribers should use a measuring spoon or oral syringe, because household spoons vary.

**Useful conversions:** 1 kg = 2.2 lb; 1 lb = 453.6 g.

**Examples**

- 5 grains in mg = 5 × 64.8 = **324 mg**.
- 2 fluid ounces in ml = 2 × 28.4 = **56.8 ml**.
- A child weighs 44 lb: 44 ÷ 2.2 = **20 kg**.

## 2.2 Percentage calculations

| Expression | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| % w/w | grams of solute in 100 g of product |
| % w/v | grams of solute in 100 ml of product |
| % v/v | millilitres of solute in 100 ml of product |
| % v/w | millilitres of liquid in 100 g of product |
| Ratio strength 1 in 1000 | 1 g (or ml) in 1000 ml (or g) = 0.1% |

If the type is not stated: solids in liquids are taken as **w/v**, liquids in liquids as **v/v**, solids in solids as **w/w**.

### Worked examples

1. **Amount needed.** How much boric acid is needed for 500 ml of a 2% w/v solution?
   2 g in 100 ml, so 2 × 500 ÷ 100 = **10 g**.

2. **Ratio strength.** How much potassium permanganate is needed for 1 L of a 1 in 5000 solution?
   1000 ÷ 5000 = **0.2 g** (200 mg).

3. **Percentage of a product.** 15 g of drug is in 300 ml of solution. Strength = 15 × 100 ÷ 300 = **5% w/v**.

4. **Dilution (C1V1 = C2V2).** Make 100 ml of a 0.5% w/v solution from a 5% w/v stock.
   V1 = (0.5 × 100) ÷ 5 = **10 ml** of stock, made up to 100 ml with water.

5. **Stock solution in ratio strength.** How many ml of a 1 in 400 solution are needed to make 2 L of a 1 in 10,000 solution?
   (1/400) × V1 = (1/10,000) × 2000 → V1 = 2000 × 400 ÷ 10,000 = **80 ml**.

6. **w/w.** Make 50 g of a 3% w/w salicylic acid ointment: 3 × 50 ÷ 100 = **1.5 g** salicylic acid + 48.5 g base.

## 2.3 Alligation

Alligation is a quick way to solve problems of **mixing preparations of different strengths**.

### Alligation medial

Finds the **strength of a mixture** when the quantities and strengths of the parts are known (a weighted average).

Strength of mixture = Σ(quantity × strength) ÷ Σ(quantity)

**Example.** Mix 50 ml of 95% alcohol, 30 ml of 70% alcohol and 20 ml of 40% alcohol.
(50 × 95) + (30 × 70) + (20 × 40) = 4750 + 2100 + 800 = 7650
Strength = 7650 ÷ 100 = **76.5% v/v** (ignoring the small volume contraction on mixing alcohol and water).

### Alligation alternate

Finds the **proportions** in which two strengths must be mixed to give a required strength.

```text
   Higher strength  H ----------  (D - L) parts of H
                         \     /
                          D       (desired strength)
                         /     \
   Lower strength   L ----------  (H - D) parts of L
```

Subtract along the diagonals and ignore signs. A diluent such as water or a plain base has strength 0.

**Example 1.** Mix 95% and 40% alcohol to make 70% alcohol.
95% → 70 − 40 = **30 parts**; 40% → 95 − 70 = **25 parts**; ratio 30 : 25 = 6 : 5.
For 1100 ml: 600 ml of 95% + 500 ml of 40%.
Check: (600 × 95 + 500 × 40) ÷ 1100 = (57,000 + 20,000) ÷ 1100 = 70%.

**Example 2.** Dilute 95% alcohol with water to make 950 ml of 70% alcohol.
95% → 70 parts; water (0%) → 25 parts. Volume of 95% alcohol = 950 × 70 ÷ 95 = **700 ml**, made up to 950 ml with water (about 250 ml; in practice make up to volume because of contraction).

**Example 3.** Mix a 10% and a 2% ointment to make 80 g of a 5% ointment.
10% → 5 − 2 = 3 parts; 2% → 10 − 5 = 5 parts; total 8 parts = 80 g, so **30 g of 10%** + **50 g of 2%**.
Check: (30 × 10 + 50 × 2) ÷ 80 = 400 ÷ 80 = 5%.

## 2.4 Proof spirit

**Proof spirit** is the British standard strength of alcohol used for excise purposes. It contains about **57.1% v/v** ethyl alcohol at 51 °F. Historically it was the weakest spirit that would still let gunpowder soaked in it ignite.

- A spirit stronger than proof spirit is **over proof (o.p.)**; weaker is **under proof (u.p.)**.
- Proof spirit = **100° proof**. Absolute alcohol (100% v/v) = 175.3° proof = 75.3 o.p.
- Conversion: **degree proof = % v/v × 1.753** (because 100 ÷ 57.06 = 1.7525), and **% v/v = degree proof ÷ 1.753**.
- Over proof = degree proof − 100; under proof = 100 − degree proof.
- In the USA, proof is simply **twice the % v/v** (50% v/v = 100 US proof).

### Worked examples

1. **90% v/v alcohol:** 90 × 1.753 = 157.8° proof → **57.8 o.p.**
2. **40% v/v alcohol:** 40 × 1.753 = 70.1° proof → **29.9 u.p.**
3. **60 o.p. to % v/v:** 60 o.p. = 160° proof; 160 ÷ 1.753 = **91.3% v/v**.
4. **25 u.p. to % v/v:** 25 u.p. = 75° proof; 75 ÷ 1.753 = **42.8% v/v**.
5. **Dilution in proof terms.** How much water is added to 1000 ml of 60 o.p. spirit to make it 30 u.p.?
   60 o.p. = 160° proof; 30 u.p. = 70° proof.
   Final volume = 1000 × 160 ÷ 70 = 2285.7 ml, so water added = 2285.7 − 1000 = **1285.7 ml** (ignoring contraction).
6. **Proof gallons.** The "proof volume" = actual volume × degree proof ÷ 100. 10 L of 157.8° proof spirit = 10 × 1.578 = **15.78 L of proof spirit**, which is the basis of duty.

## 2.5 Isotonic solutions

**Isotonic** solutions have the same osmotic pressure as body fluids (blood, tears). Body fluids freeze at **−0.52 °C**, which is the same as a **0.9% w/v NaCl** solution. Hypotonic solutions make red cells swell and burst (haemolysis); hypertonic solutions make them shrink (crenation). Eye drops, nasal drops and injections should be close to isotonic.

### Method 1: Freezing point depression

Amount of adjusting substance (g per 100 ml):

**W = (0.52 − a) ÷ b**

- a = freezing point depression of the unadjusted drug solution (°C) = FPD of 1% drug × its % strength
- b = freezing point depression of a 1% w/v solution of the adjusting substance (for NaCl, b = **0.576 °C**)

**Example.** Make 100 ml of a 2% w/v drug solution isotonic with NaCl. FPD of a 1% solution of the drug = 0.10 °C.
a = 2 × 0.10 = 0.20 °C
W = (0.52 − 0.20) ÷ 0.576 = 0.32 ÷ 0.576 = **0.556 g NaCl per 100 ml**.

If a is already 0.52 or more, the solution is isotonic or hypertonic and no NaCl is added.

### Method 2: Molecular weight (sodium chloride equivalent) method

The **sodium chloride equivalent (E)** of a drug is the weight of NaCl that has the same osmotic effect as 1 g of the drug. It is calculated from the molecular weight:

**E = 17 × L_iso ÷ M**

where M is the molecular weight and L_iso depends on the type of molecule: **1.9** for non-electrolytes, about **2.0** for weak electrolytes, **3.4** for 1:1 electrolytes (like NaCl), and higher for salts that give more ions.

- NaCl: E = 17 × 3.4 ÷ 58.5 = 0.99 (about 1, as expected).
- Dextrose (M 180, non-electrolyte): E = 17 × 1.9 ÷ 180 = **0.18**.

**NaCl to add = 0.9 − (% strength of drug × E)** g per 100 ml.

**Example.** Make 100 ml of a 1% w/v ephedrine hydrochloride solution isotonic (E = 0.30).
NaCl represented by drug = 1 × 0.30 = 0.30 g. NaCl to add = 0.9 − 0.30 = **0.60 g per 100 ml**.

**Isotonic strength of a drug alone:** % = 0.9 ÷ E. For dextrose: 0.9 ÷ 0.18 = **5% w/v**, which is why 5% dextrose is used for infusion.

**Using the freezing point equation with molecular weight.** ΔTf = i × Kf × m, with Kf for water 1.86 °C kg/mol. For ΔTf = 0.52 °C, grams per litre = 0.52 × M ÷ (1.86 × i). For dextrose (i = 1): 0.52 × 180 ÷ 1.86 = 50.3 g/L, about 5% w/v. For NaCl (i about 1.8): 0.52 × 58.5 ÷ (1.86 × 1.8) = 9.1 g/L, about 0.9% w/v.

**White–Vincent method.** Volume of water that makes w g of drug isotonic: **V = w × E × 111.1 ml**; then make up to the final volume with an isotonic vehicle. For 0.3 g of ephedrine HCl (E 0.30): V = 0.3 × 0.30 × 111.1 = **10.0 ml**.

## 2.6 Powders

**Powder:** a mixture of finely divided, dry drugs and/or chemicals for internal or external use.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Faster dissolution and absorption than tablets | Bitter or unpleasant taste is not masked |
| More stable than liquids | Not suitable for hygroscopic, deliquescent or volatile drugs |
| Flexible dose; easy to mix several drugs | Bulk powders give inaccurate doses |
| Easy for children who cannot swallow tablets | Time-consuming to prepare divided powders |
| Large doses possible (for example antacids) | Not convenient to carry |

### Classification

```text
                         POWDERS
          ________________|________________
         |                                  |
   Bulk powders                      Divided powders
   (patient measures dose)           (each dose wrapped separately)
     |                                  |
   Internal: antacid powders,        Simple powders (one drug)
   effervescent powders              Compound powders (two or more drugs)
   External: dusting powders,        Packed in paper (chartulae),
   insufflations, snuffs,            cachets, sachets
   dentifrices
```

- **Simple powder:** contains one active ingredient, with or without diluent.
- **Compound powder:** two or more active ingredients. Official examples include oral rehydration salts and compound magnesium trisilicate-type antacid powders.

### Dusting powders

Fine powders applied to the skin for lubricant, absorbent, antiseptic, antipruritic or antifungal effect.

- Must be very fine (passed through a fine sieve), free from grittiness, and sterile if meant for wounds or broken skin.
- Common bases: talc, starch, zinc oxide, kaolin, magnesium stearate. Talc must be sterilised because it can carry spores of tetanus and gas gangrene organisms.
- Example: **Zinc, Starch and Talc Dusting Powder** (zinc oxide 25 g, starch 25 g, purified talc 50 g).
- Dispensed in sifter-top containers; labelled "For external use only".

**Other bulk powders for external use:** insufflations (blown into ear, nose or throat), snuffs (inhaled through the nose) and dentifrices (tooth powders).

### Effervescent powders and granules

They contain a drug with an acid (citric and/or tartaric acid) and sodium bicarbonate. On adding water, CO2 is released, giving a pleasant, sparkling drink that masks taste and helps dissolution.

- Citric acid alone gives a sticky mass; tartaric acid alone gives crumbly granules that lose firmness. A **mixture of both** gives good granules.
- **Reactions:** C6H8O7·H2O + 3NaHCO3 → Na3C6H5O7 + 4H2O + 3CO2; C4H6O6 + 2NaHCO3 → Na2C4H4O6 + 2H2O + 2CO2.
- **Amount of bicarbonate:** 1 g citric acid monohydrate (M 210) needs 3 × 84 ÷ 210 = 1.2 g NaHCO3; 1 g tartaric acid (M 150) needs 2 × 84 ÷ 150 = 1.12 g NaHCO3. With citric : tartaric = 1 : 2, bicarbonate = 1.2 + 2.24 = **3.44 parts**, giving the usual ratio **1 : 2 : 3.44**.
- **Granulation:** dry (fusion) method, where the water of crystallisation of citric acid is released on gentle heating and binds the powder, or wet method with non-aqueous liquid (alcohol).
- Store in tightly closed containers, away from moisture.

### Efflorescent powders

Crystalline substances that **lose water of crystallisation** on exposure to dry air and become powdery, so the dose becomes stronger and the powder may become damp when water is released during mixing.
Examples: sodium carbonate decahydrate, sodium sulphate decahydrate (Glauber's salt), alum, caffeine hydrate, citric acid monohydrate.
Remedy: use the **anhydrous or dried form** in an equivalent amount, and pack in airtight containers.

### Hygroscopic and deliquescent powders

**Hygroscopic** substances absorb moisture from the air; **deliquescent** ones absorb so much that they dissolve in it.
Examples: calcium chloride, potassium carbonate, zinc chloride, potassium citrate, many dry plant extracts.
Remedy: dispense in airtight containers (waxed or aluminium-lined paper), add an inert absorbent, prepare in a dry atmosphere and do not use too fine a powder.

### Eutectic mixtures

Some substances, when mixed, form a mixture whose melting point is **below room temperature**, so the powder liquefies or becomes damp.
Examples: camphor, menthol, phenol, thymol, chloral hydrate, phenyl salicylate (salol), aspirin with phenazone.

Remedies:
1. Dispense the ingredients **separately** if possible.
2. Mix each with an **inert absorbent powder** such as light magnesium oxide, light magnesium carbonate or kaolin, then combine lightly.
3. Allow the mixture to liquefy completely, then absorb the liquid on an inert powder.

### Geometric dilution

Used to mix a **small amount of potent drug** uniformly with a large amount of diluent.

1. Place the potent drug in the mortar with an **equal amount** of diluent and triturate.
2. Add diluent **equal to the amount of mixture** in the mortar and mix again.
3. Repeat, doubling each time, until all the diluent is added.

**Trituration example.** Ten powders each containing 5 mg of a potent drug are needed; the balance cannot weigh less than 100 mg accurately.
Make a **1 in 10 trituration**: weigh 100 mg drug + 900 mg lactose = 1 g; every 100 mg of trituration has 10 mg drug.
Drug needed = 10 × 5 mg = 50 mg = **500 mg of trituration**. If each powder weighs 200 mg, total = 2 g, so add **1.5 g lactose** to the 500 mg trituration by geometric dilution and divide into 10 powders.

## 2.7 Liquid dosage forms

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Easy to swallow, suitable for children and the elderly | Bulky, costly to transport, containers can break |
| Faster absorption (drug already in solution) | Less stable; hydrolysis and microbial growth more likely |
| Flexible dosing | Accurate dosing depends on the patient's measure |
| Irritant drugs such as bromides are diluted | Unpleasant taste is harder to mask |
| Uniform dose in solutions | Suspensions and emulsions need shaking |

Liquid dosage forms are **monophasic** (true solutions: syrups, elixirs, linctuses, drops, gargles, mouthwashes) or **biphasic** (suspensions and emulsions). Unit 3 covers them in detail.

### Excipients used in liquid dosage forms

| Excipient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vehicles / solvents | Dissolve or carry the drug | Purified water, ethanol, glycerin, propylene glycol, PEG 400, fixed oils |
| Cosolvents | Raise solubility | Ethanol, propylene glycol, glycerin, sorbitol |
| Buffers | Hold pH for stability and comfort | Citrate, phosphate, acetate buffers |
| Preservatives | Prevent microbial growth | Methyl and propyl parabens, benzoic acid, sodium benzoate, chlorocresol, benzalkonium chloride |
| Antioxidants | Prevent oxidation | Sodium metabisulphite, ascorbic acid, BHA, BHT |
| Chelating agents | Bind trace metals that catalyse breakdown | Disodium edetate |
| Sweeteners | Taste | Sucrose, sorbitol, glycerin, saccharin sodium, aspartame |
| Flavours | Mask taste | Orange, lemon, peppermint, raspberry, vanilla |
| Colours | Appearance and identification | Amaranth, tartrazine, sunset yellow (permitted food colours) |
| Viscosity enhancers / suspending agents | Slow settling, give body | Methylcellulose, sodium CMC, tragacanth, acacia, bentonite, xanthan gum |
| Wetting agents / surfactants | Help wet hydrophobic powders | Polysorbates, sodium lauryl sulphate |
| Emulsifying agents | Stabilise emulsions | Acacia, polysorbates, sorbitan esters, wool alcohols |

### Solubility enhancement techniques

| Technique | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **pH adjustment** | Weak acids dissolve better at higher pH, weak bases at lower pH | Injections adjusted to suitable pH |
| **Salt formation** | Ionised salts are more soluble than the free acid or base | Sodium phenobarbitone, atropine sulphate |
| **Cosolvency** | A water-miscible solvent lowers the polarity of the vehicle | Ethanol and propylene glycol in elixirs and injections |
| **Complexation** | A soluble complex forms | Iodine with potassium iodide; drugs with cyclodextrins; caffeine with sodium benzoate |
| **Micellar solubilisation** | Surfactant micelles hold the drug inside | Polysorbate 80 with vitamins and steroids |
| **Hydrotropy** | High concentrations of certain salts raise solubility | Sodium benzoate, sodium salicylate, urea |
| **Particle size reduction** | Larger surface area increases dissolution rate (micronisation, nanosuspensions) | Griseofulvin |
| **Solid dispersion** | Drug dispersed in a water-soluble carrier | Drugs in PEG or PVP |
| **Prodrug / chemical modification** | A soluble derivative is made | Phosphate esters of steroids |
| **Use of polymorph or amorphous form** | Metastable forms dissolve more | Amorphous forms of poorly soluble drugs |

## Remember

- 1 grain = 64.8 mg; 1 fl oz = 28.4 ml; 1 pint = 568 ml; 1 kg = 2.2 lb.
- Alligation medial gives the strength of a mixture; alligation alternate gives the ratio (subtract diagonally).
- Proof spirit = 57.1% v/v ethanol; degree proof = % v/v × 1.753; o.p. = proof − 100; u.p. = 100 − proof.
- Isotonic = FPD 0.52 °C = 0.9% NaCl. W = (0.52 − a)/b with b = 0.576 for NaCl; E = 17 L_iso/M; NaCl to add = 0.9 − (C × E).
- Effervescent ratio citric : tartaric : bicarbonate = 1 : 2 : 3.44.
- Eutectic (camphor, menthol, phenol): dispense separately or absorb on light MgO/kaolin. Efflorescent: use dried form. Hygroscopic: airtight packing.
- Geometric dilution: start with equal parts, keep doubling.
