# Unit 4: Suppositories and pharmaceutical incompatibilities

The first half of this unit deals with suppositories: their types, bases, methods of making them and the displacement value numerical, which is asked almost every year. The second half covers incompatibilities, the undesirable changes that happen when ingredients of a prescription react with each other or with the body.

## 4.1 Suppositories

**Suppository:** a solid unit dosage form of suitable shape and size for insertion into a body cavity (rectum, vagina or urethra), where it **melts, softens or dissolves** to release the drug for local or systemic action.

### Types

| Type | Site | Usual weight / shape | Example use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Rectal suppositories** | Rectum | About 1 g for children and 2 g for adults; torpedo or bullet shaped (tapered at one or both ends) | Glycerol (laxative), paracetamol, diclofenac, antiemetics, haemorrhoid preparations |
| **Vaginal suppositories (pessaries)** | Vagina | Larger, 3–8 g; oval, conical or globular | Antifungals (clotrimazole), antiseptics, contraceptives |
| **Urethral suppositories (bougies)** | Urethra | Thin pencil shape | Antiseptics, local anaesthetics (rare now) |
| **Nasal / ear cones** | Nose, ear | Small cones | Rarely used |

### Advantages

1. Useful when the **oral route is not possible**: vomiting, unconsciousness, young children, before and after surgery, difficulty in swallowing.
2. Drugs that **irritate the stomach** or are **destroyed by stomach acid or enzymes** can be given.
3. Drug absorbed from the lower rectum partly **avoids first-pass metabolism** in the liver (veins of the lower rectum drain into the general circulation).
4. **Local action** in haemorrhoids, constipation and vaginal infections.
5. Bitter taste and smell are avoided.

### Disadvantages

1. Not acceptable to many patients; embarrassing to use.
2. Absorption can be slow, irregular and incomplete; affected by faeces in the rectum.
3. Can cause local irritation (proctitis).
4. Bases may melt in hot weather; need cool storage.
5. Making them is more difficult and costly than tablets.

### Ideal properties of a suppository base

- Melts at **body temperature** (just below 37 °C) or dissolves in body fluids.
- Non-toxic, non-irritant to mucous membranes.
- Compatible with a wide range of drugs; releases the drug readily.
- Stable on storage: does not turn rancid or change shape; keeps firm at room temperature.
- **Contracts on cooling** so the suppository leaves the mould easily.
- Has a narrow gap between melting and solidifying points; can be moulded by hand, by pouring or by compression.

### Types of suppository bases

**A. Fatty (oleaginous) bases** — melt at body temperature.

| Base | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Theobroma oil (cocoa butter)** | Fat from roasted cocoa seeds; melts at about 30–35 °C. Pleasant, bland, non-irritant. **Polymorphic**: if heated much above 36 °C and cooled fast, it forms unstable crystal forms of low melting point, so the suppositories do not set or melt too easily. Melt it on a water bath at low temperature (creamy melt) to keep the stable form. Other drawbacks: becomes rancid, does not contract much on cooling (mould must be lubricated), softens in hot climates, melting point lowered by some drugs (chloral hydrate) |
| **Synthetic and semi-synthetic fats** | Hydrogenated vegetable oils and fractionated fats (hard fat bases sold under trade names). Fewer polymorphism problems, do not turn rancid easily, contract well, need no lubricant |
| **Emulsified bases** | Fats with emulsifiers, which help take up water |

**B. Water-soluble and water-miscible bases** — dissolve or disperse in body fluids.

| Base | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Glycero-gelatin base** | Gelatin, glycerin and water (glycerol suppositories contain about 14% gelatin and 70% glycerol). Dissolves slowly in rectal fluid; good for vaginal pessaries. Drawbacks: hygroscopic (draws water from mucosa, causing irritation), supports microbial growth (needs preservative), takes longer to make, must be stored in airtight containers |
| **Polyethylene glycols (PEG, macrogols)** | Blends of high and low molecular weight PEGs give the required hardness. Dissolve in body fluids; stable; no refrigeration needed; do not support microbes. Drawbacks: can irritate the mucosa (moisten the suppository before insertion), incompatible with some drugs (for example phenols, benzocaine, some antibiotics) |
| **Soap glycerin** | Glycerin hardened with sodium stearate; used for laxative suppositories |

**C. Other bases:** surfactant bases (polyoxyethylene derivatives), which spread and disperse.

### Methods of preparation

**1. Hand rolling (manual moulding)** — the oldest method. The drug is mixed with grated base in a mortar into a plastic mass, rolled into a cylinder on a tile and cut and shaped by hand. Only for small numbers; mainly with theobroma oil.

**2. Compression moulding** — grated base and drug are mixed and pressed into a mould in a cylinder by a screw or wheel. No heat, so suitable for **heat-labile drugs** and insoluble substances. Cold compression avoids sedimentation of drug.

**3. Fusion (pour moulding)** — the most common method, used for all base types.

1. **Calibrate the mould** with the chosen base (fill with plain base, weigh the suppositories).
2. **Lubricate** the mould if needed: for theobroma oil use soap spirit or a soap–glycerin–alcohol solution; for glycero-gelatin use liquid paraffin or an oil. Synthetic fats need no lubricant.
3. **Melt the base** on a water bath at the lowest temperature possible.
4. **Incorporate the drug**: dissolve soluble drugs in the melted base; finely powder insoluble drugs and levigate with a little melted base; calculate the base needed using the **displacement value**.
5. **Pour** into the mould when the mixture is near its setting point (creamy), with slight overfilling to allow for contraction.
6. **Cool** (in a refrigerator if needed), trim the excess with a warm spatula, open the mould and remove the suppositories.
7. Pack individually in foil or plastic, label and store in a cool place.

### Calibration of the mould

The nominal size of a mould (for example "1 g") refers to theobroma oil. Each mould must be calibrated with the actual base: prepare a set of plain suppositories, weigh them and find the average weight. A glycero-gelatin base is denser, so the same mould holds roughly 1.2 times the weight of theobroma oil.

## 4.2 Displacement value

When a drug is added, it **takes up space** in the mould and pushes out (displaces) some base. Because the drug and base have different densities, the weight of base displaced is not equal to the weight of drug.

**Displacement value (DV):** the number of parts by weight of a drug that displaces **one part by weight of the base** (usually theobroma oil).

DV = weight of drug ÷ weight of base displaced by it

A drug with DV 5 (for example zinc oxide is often quoted as 5) is dense: 5 g of it occupies the space of only 1 g of base.

### Calculating the amount of base needed

**Base needed = (number of suppositories × mould capacity) − (total weight of drug ÷ DV)**

**Example 1.** Prepare 10 suppositories each containing 300 mg zinc oxide in a 1 g mould (DV of zinc oxide = 5, given).
Total drug = 10 × 0.3 = 3.0 g. Base displaced = 3.0 ÷ 5 = 0.6 g.
Base needed = 10 × 1 − 0.6 = **9.4 g theobroma oil**.
In practice prepare for 2 extra (12 suppositories): drug 3.6 g; base = 12 − 3.6 ÷ 5 = 12 − 0.72 = **11.28 g**.

**Example 2.** Six suppositories each containing 200 mg of a drug, DV = 2, 1 g mould.
Drug = 6 × 0.2 = 1.2 g. Base displaced = 1.2 ÷ 2 = 0.6 g. Base = 6 − 0.6 = **5.4 g**.

**Example 3 (two drugs).** Ten suppositories (1 g mould), each with 100 mg of drug A (DV 1.5) and 250 mg of drug B (DV 2.5).
A = 1.0 g → displaces 1.0 ÷ 1.5 = 0.667 g. B = 2.5 g → displaces 2.5 ÷ 2.5 = 1.0 g.
Base = 10 − (0.667 + 1.0) = **8.33 g**.

### Determination of displacement value

1. Prepare 6 plain (unmedicated) suppositories and weigh them: weight = **a** g.
2. Prepare 6 suppositories containing a known percentage (x%) of drug in the same mould and weigh them: weight = **b** g.
3. Drug in medicated suppositories = b × x ÷ 100 = **c** g.
4. Base in medicated suppositories = b − c.
5. Base displaced by drug = a − (b − c).
6. **DV = c ÷ [a − (b − c)]**.

**Worked example.** 6 plain suppositories weigh 6.0 g. 6 suppositories with 40% drug weigh 8.5 g.
Drug = 8.5 × 40 ÷ 100 = 3.4 g. Base = 8.5 − 3.4 = 5.1 g. Base displaced = 6.0 − 5.1 = 0.9 g.
DV = 3.4 ÷ 0.9 = **3.78**.

## 4.3 Evaluation of suppositories

| Test | What it checks |
| --- | --- |
| **Visual examination** | Uniform colour and shape, no cracks, fissures, pits, fat bloom or sedimentation of drug (cut lengthwise to see drug distribution) |
| **Uniformity of weight** | Weigh 20 suppositories individually; compare with the average weight within pharmacopoeial limits |
| **Content uniformity / assay** | Drug content of individual suppositories |
| **Melting range / macro melting range test** | Temperature at which the suppository melts (for fatty bases) |
| **Liquefaction (softening) time test** | Time taken to soften and lose shape at 37 °C under a weight (for example U-tube or rectal-simulation apparatus) |
| **Breaking (hardness / mechanical strength) test** | Weight needed to break the suppository; checks it will withstand handling |
| **Disintegration test** | Time to melt, soften or dissolve at 37 °C in water (pharmacopoeial apparatus); fatty-base suppositories should disintegrate within about 30 minutes and water-soluble ones within about 60 minutes |
| **Dissolution / drug release** | Release of drug into a medium, often using a basket or membrane |
| **Stability** | Changes on storage: hardness, melting point, appearance |

## 4.4 Pharmaceutical incompatibilities

**Incompatibility:** an undesirable change that takes place when two or more substances are mixed, or when a drug is given with another drug, so that the product becomes unacceptable in **appearance, stability, safety or therapeutic effect**.

Incompatibilities may be **intentional** (the prescriber means the reaction to happen, for example effervescence in effervescent preparations or forming a soap in situ) or **unintentional** (an error that the pharmacist must spot and correct).

### Classification

```text
                      INCOMPATIBILITIES
         ____________________|____________________
        |                    |                    |
    Physical             Chemical            Therapeutic
   (visible change,     (chemical reaction   (undesired effect
    no new compound)     new substance)       in the body)
```

### A. Physical incompatibility

A visible physical change without a new chemical substance. Usually corrected by a change in the order of mixing, a solvent, an emulsifier or a suspending agent.

| Type | Example | Correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Insolubility** | Resinous tinctures (for example compound benzoin tincture) precipitate resin when added to water | Add slowly with a suspending agent (tragacanth) |
| **Immiscibility** | Oils with water | Emulsify with a suitable emulsifying agent |
| **Liquefaction (eutectic)** | Camphor, menthol, phenol, thymol together in powders | Dispense separately or use an absorbent (light MgO, kaolin) |
| **Precipitation by change of solvent** | Alcoholic solution diluted with water | Use a cosolvent, add slowly with stirring |
| **Salting out** | High salt concentration throws a dissolved substance out of solution | Dilute or reduce salt |
| **Adsorption** | Drugs adsorbed on kaolin, charcoal or containers | Change the formulation or separate dosing |
| **Hygroscopic or efflorescent changes** | Powders become damp | Use dried forms, airtight packing |

### B. Chemical incompatibility

A chemical reaction forms a new substance. It may be visible (precipitate, colour, gas, heat) or invisible (loss of potency).

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Precipitation** | Alkaloidal salts (for example quinine) with alkalis precipitate the free base; tannic acid precipitates alkaloids and proteins; sodium salts of barbiturates (sodium phenobarbitone) precipitate the free acid in acid solution |
| **Effervescence / gas evolution** | Sodium bicarbonate with acids or acid salts gives CO2 (bottle may burst); carbonates with acids |
| **Oxidation–reduction** | Adrenaline, phenols and ascorbic acid oxidise and change colour in light or alkali |
| **Hydrolysis** | Aspirin and esters such as procaine break down in moist or alkaline conditions |
| **Colour change** | Iron salts give dark colours with tannins and salicylates |
| **Explosive or fire-risk combinations** | Strong oxidising agents (potassium permanganate, potassium chlorate) with reducing agents or organic matter such as glycerin, sugar, sulphur, tannic acid |
| **Complexation / chelation** | Tetracyclines bind calcium, magnesium, aluminium and iron ions |
| **Cementation** | Some insoluble powders set into a hard mass in liquids |

**Ways of handling chemical incompatibility:** change the order of mixing, dilute the reacting ingredients, add a suspending agent to make an unavoidable precipitate diffusible, adjust pH, use an alternative salt or drug (with the prescriber's consent), dispense separately, protect from light and air, or for effervescent mixtures let the gas escape before closing the bottle.

### C. Therapeutic incompatibility

The combination gives an effect in the patient different from the one intended.

| Type | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Antagonism** | A beta-blocker with a beta-agonist bronchodilator; a cholinergic drug with an anticholinergic drug |
| **Synergism leading to toxicity** | Two central nervous system depressants (sedative with alcohol); two drugs that lower potassium |
| **Interference with absorption** | Tetracycline with antacids, milk or iron salts (chelation) |
| **Contraindication** | Aspirin in a patient with a peptic ulcer; certain drugs in pregnancy |
| **Overdose or wrong dose** | Dose above the maximum, wrong frequency |
| **Wrong drug or wrong combination** | Sound-alike names, duplicate drugs of the same class |

The pharmacist must contact the prescriber about any therapeutic incompatibility. It must never be "corrected" without the prescriber's agreement.

## Remember

- Rectal suppositories about 1 g (child) and 2 g (adult); pessaries 3–8 g.
- Theobroma oil melts at about 30–35 °C and is polymorphic: melt gently (creamy melt). Glycero-gelatin is hygroscopic and needs a preservative; PEG bases dissolve rather than melt.
- Methods: hand rolling, compression moulding (heat-sensitive drugs), fusion / pour moulding (most common). Calibrate and lubricate the mould.
- DV = drug ÷ base displaced. Base needed = N × mould capacity − (total drug ÷ DV). DV from data = c ÷ [a − (b − c)].
- Evaluation: appearance, weight uniformity, content, melting range, liquefaction time, breaking test, disintegration, dissolution.
- Incompatibility: physical (no new substance), chemical (new substance), therapeutic (unwanted effect in the body); never change a therapeutic incompatibility without the prescriber.
