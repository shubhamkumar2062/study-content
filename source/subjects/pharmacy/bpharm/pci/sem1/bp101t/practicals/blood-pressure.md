# Recording of blood pressure

**Aim:** To record the arterial blood pressure of the given subject by the palpatory and auscultatory methods using a sphygmomanometer.

## Principle

A cuff wrapped around the arm is inflated until it compresses the brachial artery completely. As the pressure in the cuff is slowly released, blood begins to spurt through the partly opened artery when cuff pressure falls just below **systolic pressure**, producing tapping sounds (**Korotkoff sounds**) heard with a stethoscope below the cuff. The sounds disappear when cuff pressure falls below **diastolic pressure** and flow becomes smooth again.

**Korotkoff phases:** I — first clear tapping (systolic pressure); II — murmuring; III — louder, crisper sounds; IV — sudden muffling; V — disappearance (diastolic pressure in adults).

## Requirements

Sphygmomanometer (mercury, aneroid or digital with a cuff of suitable size), stethoscope, couch or chair.

## Procedure

1. Let the subject rest for at least 5 minutes, seated with the arm supported at heart level, palm up. No smoking, coffee or exercise in the previous 30 minutes.
2. Wrap the deflated cuff evenly around the bare upper arm, its lower edge about 2–3 cm above the elbow crease, with the centre of the bladder over the brachial artery.
3. **Palpatory method:** feel the radial pulse; close the valve and inflate until the pulse disappears; inflate about 30 mmHg more; deflate slowly (2–3 mmHg per second) and note the reading when the pulse reappears — the palpatory systolic pressure.
4. Deflate completely and wait about a minute.
5. **Auscultatory method:** place the stethoscope diaphragm over the brachial artery in the cubital fossa (medial to the biceps tendon), not under the cuff. Inflate to about 30 mmHg above the palpatory systolic value.
6. Deflate at 2–3 mmHg per second. Note the pressure at the **first tapping sound** (systolic) and when the sounds **disappear** (diastolic).
7. Deflate fully, remove the cuff, repeat after 1–2 minutes and record the average. Optionally record lying, standing and after exercise.

**Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic.** **Mean arterial pressure (MAP) = diastolic + (pulse pressure ÷ 3).**

**Worked example:** BP = 120/80 mmHg → pulse pressure = 40 mmHg; MAP = 80 + 40/3 ≈ **93 mmHg**.

**Normal adult value:** about 120/80 mmHg. A persistent reading at or above about 140/90 mmHg suggests hypertension (it must be confirmed on repeated readings); low readings with symptoms suggest hypotension.

## Precautions

1. Use a cuff of the correct size; a narrow cuff gives falsely high values.
2. Keep the arm and the manometer at heart level; read mercury at eye level.
3. Do not inflate unnecessarily high or keep the cuff inflated for long.
4. Deflate at a steady 2–3 mmHg per second.
5. Always do the palpatory method first to avoid missing the auscultatory gap.

## Viva questions

1. **What are Korotkoff sounds?** Sounds produced by turbulent flow through a partly compressed artery as cuff pressure is released.
2. **Why is the palpatory method done first?** To find the approximate systolic pressure and avoid errors from the auscultatory gap (a silent interval in some people).
3. **What is pulse pressure?** Systolic minus diastolic pressure (normally about 40 mmHg).
4. **Name the receptors that regulate BP quickly.** Baroreceptors of the carotid sinus and aortic arch.
5. **Why does systolic pressure rise on exercise?** Cardiac output increases.
6. **What happens to BP on standing up suddenly?** It falls slightly, but the baroreceptor reflex quickly restores it.
