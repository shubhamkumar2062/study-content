# Unit 3: Body fluids, blood and the lymphatic system

Blood carries every injected drug, and most oral drugs reach their target through it; the lymphatic system returns leaked fluid and fat to the blood and houses much of the immune system. This unit is high-scoring: coagulation, haemopoiesis, blood groups, anaemia and the lymph node appear in almost every paper.

## 3.1 Body fluids

Water makes up about **60% of body weight** in an adult man (less in women and the elderly because they have more fat; about 75% in a newborn). In a 70 kg man this is roughly 42 litres.

```text
TOTAL BODY WATER (about 60% of body weight, ~42 L)
 |
 |-- Intracellular fluid (ICF): about 2/3  (~28 L)  inside cells
 |
 |-- Extracellular fluid (ECF): about 1/3  (~14 L)
        |-- Interstitial fluid (incl. lymph): about 3/4 of ECF (~10-11 L)
        |-- Plasma: about 1/4 of ECF (~3 L)
        |-- Transcellular fluid (small): CSF, synovial, pleural,
            pericardial, peritoneal, aqueous humour, digestive juices
```

| Ion | ECF | ICF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main cation | Na+ (high) | K+ (high) |
| Main anions | Cl−, HCO3− | Phosphates, proteins |
| Others | Ca2+ higher | Mg2+ higher |

The difference is kept by the Na+/K+ ATPase pump. Water moves between compartments by osmosis; between plasma and interstitial fluid it moves by the balance of capillary hydrostatic pressure (pushes fluid out) and plasma protein (oncotic) pressure (pulls fluid in), described by **Starling forces**. Too much fluid in the tissues is **oedema** (low plasma proteins, raised venous pressure, blocked lymphatics).

## 3.2 Blood: composition

Blood is a fluid connective tissue. Adult blood volume is about **5–6 litres** (about 7–8% of body weight). It is slightly alkaline (pH 7.35–7.45), with specific gravity about 1.05–1.06 and viscosity several times that of water.

```text
Centrifuged blood (haematocrit tube)
 _________
|  PLASMA |  ~55%  straw-coloured liquid
|_________|
|buffy coat| <1%  white cells + platelets
|_________|
|   RBCs  |  ~45%  (haematocrit / packed cell volume)
|_________|
```

**Plasma (about 55%)**

| Component | Approximate share / details |
| --- | --- |
| Water | 91–92% |
| Plasma proteins (about 6–8 g/dL) | Albumin (largest share; maintains oncotic pressure, carries drugs and bilirubin), globulins (antibodies, carriers), fibrinogen (clotting) |
| Others | Glucose, amino acids, lipids, electrolytes, urea, creatinine, hormones, enzymes, dissolved gases |

Serum is plasma without fibrinogen and other clotting factors (the fluid left after blood clots).

**Formed elements (about 45%)**

| Cell | Normal count (adult) | Size and shape | Life span | Main function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Red blood cells (erythrocytes) | About 4.5–5.5 million/mm³ (men), 4.0–5.0 million/mm³ (women) | Biconcave discs about 7.5 µm across, no nucleus | About 120 days | Carry O2 and CO2 using haemoglobin |
| White blood cells (leucocytes) | 4,000–11,000/mm³ | Nucleated, larger than RBCs | Hours to years | Defence |
| Platelets (thrombocytes) | 1.5–4 lakh/mm³ | Fragments of megakaryocytes, 2–4 µm, no nucleus | About 7–10 days | Haemostasis |

**White cells and differential count**

| Type | Percentage | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Neutrophils | 50–70% | Multilobed nucleus (2–5 lobes), fine granules | First phagocytes at bacterial infection; raised in acute infection |
| Eosinophils | 1–4% | Bilobed nucleus, red-orange granules | Parasitic infections, allergy |
| Basophils | 0–1% | Blue granules hide the nucleus | Release histamine and heparin |
| Lymphocytes | 20–40% | Large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm | Immunity: B cells (antibodies), T cells (cell-mediated) |
| Monocytes | 2–8% | Largest; kidney-shaped nucleus | Become macrophages in tissues |

## 3.3 Functions of blood

1. **Transport** — O2 from lungs to tissues and CO2 back; nutrients from the gut; wastes to kidneys; hormones; drugs.
2. **Regulation** — body temperature (distributes heat), pH (buffers such as bicarbonate, proteins, haemoglobin), water balance (osmotic pressure of proteins).
3. **Protection** — white cells and antibodies fight infection; clotting prevents blood loss.

## 3.4 Haemopoiesis

**Haemopoiesis** is the formation of blood cells.

| Stage of life | Main site |
| --- | --- |
| Early embryo | Yolk sac |
| Middle of fetal life | Liver and spleen |
| Later fetal life onwards | Red bone marrow |
| Adult | Red marrow of flat bones and ends of long bones (skull, sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, upper ends of femur and humerus) |

All blood cells come from **pluripotent haemopoietic stem cells**, which divide into two lines:

```text
               Pluripotent stem cell
                /                 \
     Myeloid stem cell        Lymphoid stem cell
     /      |      \     \           |
 RBCs  Platelets  Granulocytes  Monocytes   Lymphocytes
 (via  (via mega-  (neutrophil,  (->macro-  (B and T cells;
 erythro karyocytes) eosinophil,  phages)    T cells mature
 blasts)            basophil)                in the thymus)
```

**Erythropoiesis** (RBC formation) takes about 7 days:

Pluripotent stem cell → proerythroblast → early normoblast → intermediate normoblast (haemoglobin appears) → late normoblast (nucleus shrinks and is lost) → **reticulocyte** (network of remaining RNA; enters blood) → mature erythrocyte (in 1–2 days).

**Factors needed for erythropoiesis**

| Factor | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Erythropoietin | Hormone made mainly by the kidneys when tissue O2 is low (hypoxia, high altitude); stimulates the marrow |
| Vitamin B12 and folic acid | Needed for DNA synthesis; lack causes large immature cells (megaloblastic anaemia). B12 absorption needs intrinsic factor from the stomach |
| Iron | Part of haem |
| Proteins (amino acids) | Globin chains |
| Vitamin C, copper, cobalt, vitamin B6 | Help iron absorption and haemoglobin synthesis |
| Hormones | Thyroxine, androgens and others stimulate |

Other lines: leucopoiesis (white cells; stimulated by colony-stimulating factors and interleukins) and thrombopoiesis (platelets from megakaryocytes; stimulated by thrombopoietin from the liver).

## 3.5 Haemoglobin and its formation

**Haemoglobin (Hb)** is the red O2-carrying protein of RBCs. Normal values: about 14–18 g/dL in men and 12–16 g/dL in women (higher in newborns). Each Hb molecule has **four globin chains** (adult HbA = α2β2; fetal HbF = α2γ2) and **four haem groups**; each haem has one ferrous iron (Fe2+) that binds one O2 molecule, so one Hb carries four O2. One gram of Hb can carry about 1.34 ml of O2.

**Synthesis**

```text
Succinyl-CoA + glycine  (in mitochondria of developing RBCs)
        |  ALA synthase (needs vitamin B6)
 delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA)
        |
 Porphobilinogen (pyrrole ring)
        |  4 pyrroles join
 Protoporphyrin IX
        |  + Fe2+  (ferrochelatase)
      HAEM
        |  + globin chain (made on ribosomes)
 Haemoglobin subunit;  4 subunits -> haemoglobin
```

**Forms:** oxyhaemoglobin (with O2), deoxyhaemoglobin, carbaminohaemoglobin (with CO2), carboxyhaemoglobin (with carbon monoxide, which binds far more strongly than O2), methaemoglobin (iron oxidised to Fe3+, cannot carry O2).

**Breakdown:** old RBCs are destroyed by macrophages in the spleen and liver. Globin → amino acids; iron → stored as ferritin or carried by transferrin for reuse; haem → biliverdin → **bilirubin** (unconjugated, carried by albumin) → conjugated in the liver → excreted in bile → urobilinogen and stercobilin. Excess bilirubin causes **jaundice**.

## 3.6 Anaemia

**Anaemia** is a fall in the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood because of fewer RBCs or less haemoglobin. Signs: tiredness, pallor, breathlessness, palpitations, fast pulse.

| Type (by cause) | Cause | Blood picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemorrhagic | Acute or chronic blood loss (injury, peptic ulcer, heavy periods) | Acute: normocytic; chronic: becomes iron deficient |
| Iron-deficiency | Poor intake, poor absorption, chronic blood loss, pregnancy; the most common anaemia in India | Microcytic, hypochromic |
| Megaloblastic / pernicious | Lack of vitamin B12 or folic acid; pernicious anaemia = no intrinsic factor (autoimmune) | Macrocytic |
| Haemolytic | RBCs destroyed early: sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, G6PD deficiency, incompatible transfusion, Rh disease of newborn, malaria | Raised bilirubin, raised reticulocytes |
| Aplastic | Marrow failure (drugs such as chloramphenicol, radiation, toxins) | All cell lines low (pancytopenia) |
| Anaemia of chronic disease | Kidney failure (low erythropoietin), chronic infections | Normocytic |

By morphology: **normocytic normochromic**, **microcytic hypochromic**, **macrocytic**.

## 3.7 Haemostasis and mechanism of coagulation

**Haemostasis** = stopping of bleeding. Steps:

1. **Vascular spasm** — injured vessel constricts (reflex and serotonin, thromboxane A2).
2. **Platelet plug formation** — platelets **adhere** to exposed collagen (helped by von Willebrand factor), become activated, release ADP and thromboxane A2, and **aggregate** into a loose plug. Aspirin reduces thromboxane formation and so platelet aggregation.
3. **Coagulation** — fibrin threads strengthen the plug into a clot.
4. **Clot retraction** — the clot shrinks and pulls the edges together; serum is squeezed out.
5. **Fibrinolysis** — plasminogen is converted to **plasmin**, which dissolves fibrin once the vessel heals.

**Clotting factors**

| Number | Name |
| --- | --- |
| I | Fibrinogen |
| II | Prothrombin |
| III | Tissue factor (thromboplastin) |
| IV | Calcium |
| V | Labile factor (proaccelerin) |
| VII | Stable factor |
| VIII | Antihaemophilic factor |
| IX | Christmas factor |
| X | Stuart–Prower factor |
| XI | Plasma thromboplastin antecedent |
| XII | Hageman factor |
| XIII | Fibrin-stabilising factor |

There is no factor VI. Factors **II, VII, IX and X** need **vitamin K** for their synthesis in the liver (warfarin blocks this).

**The cascade**

```text
 INTRINSIC PATHWAY                       EXTRINSIC PATHWAY
 (blood contacts collagen / glass)       (tissue injury releases tissue factor, III)
   XII -> XIIa                                 III + VII -> VIIa
   XI  -> XIa                                       |
   IX  -> IXa  (+ VIII, Ca2+, PF3)                  |
             \                                     /
              \------------> X -> Xa <------------/
                                |   (+ V, Ca2+, platelet phospholipid)
                    PROTHROMBIN ACTIVATOR
                                |
          Prothrombin (II) ---> THROMBIN (IIa)
                                |
          Fibrinogen (I) -----> FIBRIN (loose threads)
                                |  XIIIa + Ca2+
                       STABLE FIBRIN MESH (clot traps RBCs)
```

- Extrinsic pathway is fast (seconds); intrinsic is slower (minutes). Both meet at factor X — the **common pathway**.
- Thrombin also speeds its own formation (positive feedback) by activating V, VIII and platelets.

**Anticoagulants**

| Agent | Action | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heparin | Activates antithrombin III → inhibits thrombin and Xa | In the body (injection) and in blood samples |
| Warfarin, coumarins | Vitamin K antagonists → less II, VII, IX, X | Oral, long-term |
| Sodium citrate, oxalates, EDTA | Remove Ca2+ | Only outside the body: blood banks (citrate), lab tubes |

**Bleeding disorders:** haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency, X-linked, mostly males), haemophilia B (factor IX), thrombocytopenic purpura (low platelets, skin bleeds), vitamin K deficiency, von Willebrand disease. **Thrombosis** is an unwanted clot inside a vessel; a piece that travels is an **embolus**.

## 3.8 Blood grouping and Rh factor

**ABO system** (Landsteiner). RBCs carry antigens (agglutinogens); plasma carries antibodies (agglutinins) against the antigen it lacks.

| Group | Antigen on RBC | Antibody in plasma | Can receive from | Can donate to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O | A, AB |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O | B, AB |
| AB | A and B | None | All (universal recipient) | AB |
| O | None | Anti-A and anti-B | O only | All (universal donor) |

If incompatible blood is given, donor RBCs are **agglutinated** and haemolysed by the recipient's antibodies.

**Rh system:** the important antigen is **D**. People with D are Rh positive (the majority); those without are Rh negative. Unlike ABO, anti-D antibodies are **not naturally present**; they form only after exposure to Rh-positive blood (transfusion or pregnancy).

**Erythroblastosis fetalis (haemolytic disease of the newborn):** an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive baby is sensitised at the first delivery when fetal cells enter her blood. In the next Rh-positive pregnancy her anti-D (IgG) crosses the placenta and destroys fetal RBCs → anaemia, jaundice, swelling. Prevention: **anti-D immunoglobulin** given to the Rh-negative mother within 72 hours of delivery of an Rh-positive baby (and in other sensitising events).

## 3.9 Blood transfusion and its significance

**Uses:** severe haemorrhage, severe anaemia, surgery, burns (plasma), clotting disorders (factor concentrates, fresh frozen plasma), exchange transfusion in the newborn. Components can be given separately: packed red cells, platelets, plasma, cryoprecipitate.

**Precautions:** blood grouping (ABO and Rh) of donor and recipient; **cross-matching** (donor cells mixed with recipient serum — major cross-match); screening for HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis and malaria; correct storage (2–6 °C for whole blood with citrate-based anticoagulant); give slowly and watch the patient.

**Transfusion reactions and hazards:** haemolytic reaction from mismatch (fever, chills, back pain, haemoglobin in urine, kidney failure, shock); febrile and allergic reactions; circulatory overload; transmission of infection; iron overload after repeated transfusions; citrate toxicity with large volumes.

## 3.10 Disorders of blood (summary)

| Disorder | Key point |
| --- | --- |
| Anaemias | See 3.6 |
| Polycythaemia | Too many RBCs (high altitude, chronic lung disease, polycythaemia vera); thick blood |
| Leucocytosis / leucopenia | High / low WBC count (infection / typhoid, marrow failure) |
| Leukaemia | Cancer of white cell precursors; many abnormal immature WBCs, anaemia, bleeding, infections |
| Thalassaemia | Inherited defect in globin chain production; severe forms need regular transfusion |
| Sickle cell disease | Abnormal HbS; RBCs sickle at low O2, block small vessels |
| Haemophilia | Clotting factor deficiency; prolonged clotting time |
| Thrombocytopenia | Low platelets; prolonged bleeding time, purpura |
| Agranulocytosis | Very low neutrophils, often drug induced |

## 3.11 Reticulo-endothelial system (mononuclear phagocyte system)

A widespread group of phagocytic cells derived from monocytes, found in the lining of blood and lymph channels and in tissues.

| Location | Cells |
| --- | --- |
| Blood | Monocytes |
| Liver | Kupffer cells |
| Lungs | Alveolar macrophages (dust cells) |
| Brain | Microglia |
| Skin | Langerhans cells (dendritic cells) |
| Connective tissue | Histiocytes (tissue macrophages) |
| Spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow | Fixed macrophages lining sinuses |
| Bone | Osteoclasts (same origin) |
| Kidney | Mesangial cells |

**Functions:** phagocytosis of bacteria, dead cells and foreign particles; destruction of old RBCs and haemoglobin breakdown (bilirubin formation); storage of iron; presenting antigens to lymphocytes to start immune responses; secretion of cytokines.

## 3.12 Lymphatic system

The lymphatic system consists of **lymph**, **lymphatic vessels** and **lymphatic organs and tissues**.

**Lymph** is interstitial fluid that has entered lymphatic vessels: a clear fluid like plasma but with less protein, containing lymphocytes; lymph from the intestine is milky (**chyle**) because of absorbed fat.

### Lymphatic vessels and lymph circulation

```text
Blood capillary -> fluid filters into tissue spaces (interstitial fluid)
          |
  LYMPH CAPILLARIES (blind-ended; overlapping endothelial flaps act as
          |          one-way valves; lacteals in intestinal villi)
  Lymphatic vessels (valves; pass through LYMPH NODES)
          |
  Lymph trunks (lumbar, intestinal, bronchomediastinal, subclavian, jugular)
          |
  Lymph ducts
   |-- THORACIC DUCT: starts at cisterna chyli (abdomen); drains the lower body
   |   and left side of the upper body -> left subclavian / internal jugular junction
   |-- RIGHT LYMPHATIC DUCT: drains right arm, right side of head and thorax
       -> right subclavian / internal jugular junction
          |
      Venous blood (about 3 litres of fluid returned each day)
```

**What moves lymph** (there is no pump): contraction of skeletal muscles squeezing the vessels, pressure changes during breathing, contraction of smooth muscle in lymphatic walls, and valves that prevent backflow. Blocked lymphatics cause **lymphoedema** (for example, filariasis causes elephantiasis).

### Lymphatic organs and tissues

| Type | Organs | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary (central) | Red bone marrow, thymus | Lymphocytes are produced and become mature (B cells in marrow, T cells in thymus) |
| Secondary (peripheral) | Lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) such as Peyer's patches of the ileum and the appendix | Immune responses take place |

**Thymus** — in the upper mediastinum behind the sternum; large in children, shrinks after puberty; makes thymic hormones (thymosin) that help T cells mature.

**Lymph node** — bean-shaped, about 1–25 mm, along lymph vessels in groups (cervical, axillary, inguinal).

```text
            afferent lymph vessels (several, enter convex side)
               \     |     /
         _______v____v____v_______
        /  capsule (with trabeculae)\
       |  CORTEX: lymphoid follicles  |
       |  (B cells, germinal centres) |
       |  PARACORTEX: T cells         |
       |  MEDULLA: medullary cords    |
       |  and sinuses (macrophages)   |
        \___________ HILUM __________/
                     |  efferent lymph vessel (one or two) leaves
                     |  artery enters, vein leaves at hilum
```

Functions of lymph nodes: filter lymph (macrophages remove bacteria and debris); house lymphocytes that respond to antigens and produce antibodies; nodes swell and become tender in infection; cancer cells spread through them.

**Spleen** — largest lymphatic organ, in the left hypochondrium under ribs 9–11. **White pulp** (lymphocytes around arteries) gives immune responses; **red pulp** (blood-filled sinuses with macrophages) removes old RBCs and platelets, stores platelets and some blood, and can form blood cells in the fetus.

**Tonsils** — palatine, pharyngeal (adenoids) and lingual; guard the entrance of the throat.

### Functions of the lymphatic system

1. **Returns excess interstitial fluid and leaked plasma proteins** to the blood, keeping blood volume and preventing oedema.
2. **Absorbs fats** and fat-soluble vitamins from the intestine through lacteals (chylomicrons), carrying them to the blood.
3. **Immunity** — lymphocytes and macrophages in lymphatic organs defend against microbes and cancer cells.
4. Carries some large molecules (including some proteins and drugs given subcutaneously) to the blood.

## Remember

- Total body water about 60% of body weight: ICF 2/3, ECF 1/3 (interstitial 3/4, plasma 1/4).
- Blood 5–6 L; plasma 55%, cells 45%; pH 7.35–7.45.
- RBC 4.5–5.5 million/mm³ (men), life 120 days; WBC 4,000–11,000/mm³; platelets 1.5–4 lakh/mm³.
- Neutrophils are the commonest WBC; monocytes become macrophages.
- Erythropoietin from the kidney; B12, folic acid and iron are essential; reticulocyte is the last stage before the mature RBC.
- Hb = 4 globins + 4 haems; HbA α2β2; breakdown gives bilirubin.
- Vitamin K factors: II, VII, IX, X; no factor VI; Ca2+ is factor IV.
- O = universal donor, AB = universal recipient; anti-D given within 72 hours protects the next Rh-positive baby.
- Thoracic duct drains most of the body into the left subclavian vein; spleen red pulp destroys old RBCs.
