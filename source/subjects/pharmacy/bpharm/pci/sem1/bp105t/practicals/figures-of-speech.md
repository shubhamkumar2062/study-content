# Figures of speech

**Aim:** To identify common figures of speech, explain how each works, and use them in your own sentences and short talks.

> Language lab activities vary between colleges. Follow your lab teacher and your lab manual where they differ from this guide.

## Principle

A **figure of speech** uses words in a special way, different from their plain meaning, to make language vivid, memorable or persuasive. They are common in speeches, advertisements, health-awareness slogans and everyday talk. In professional and scientific writing they are used sparingly, but recognising them helps you understand what people really mean.

**Common figures of speech**

| Figure | How it works | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simile | Compares two unlike things using "like" or "as" | The wound was as red as a tomato. |
| Metaphor | Compares by saying one thing *is* another | The liver is the body's chemical factory. |
| Personification | Gives human qualities to a non-living thing | The old machine groaned and refused to start. |
| Hyperbole | Deliberate exaggeration | I have told you a thousand times to finish the course. |
| Alliteration | Same first consonant sound in nearby words | Pills, powders and pastes |
| Onomatopoeia | Word that imitates a sound | The tablet fizzed in the water; the cough went "hack". |
| Oxymoron | Two opposite words placed together | Bitter-sweet memory; open secret |
| Irony | Saying the opposite of what is meant, or an outcome opposite to what is expected | The fire station burnt down. |
| Metonymy | Using something closely linked to stand for the thing | New Delhi announced a new health scheme (the city stands for the government). |
| Euphemism | A mild word in place of a harsh or unpleasant one | "He passed away" for "he died". |
| Antithesis | Opposite ideas placed in balanced phrases | Prevention is easy; cure is hard. |
| Pun | Plays on two meanings of a word | The new pharmacist was very patient with his patients. |
| Apostrophe | Addressing an absent person or a thing directly | O sleep, why do you leave me the night before exams? |
| Rhetorical question | A question asked for effect, not for an answer | Who does not want good health? |

**Simile vs metaphor:** a simile says "like/as" (as busy as a bee); a metaphor drops it (he is a busy bee).

**Euphemism in healthcare:** used kindly ("passed away", "visually impaired"), but in instructions and records plain words are safer, so the meaning is never unclear.

## Requirements

Language lab with headsets and the figures of speech module, worksheet of sentences, health-awareness posters or advertisement slogans, answer key.

## Procedure

1. Listen to the module's explanation and examples of each figure.
2. Identify the figure of speech in twenty worksheet sentences and underline the key words.
3. Write one original sentence for each of eight figures.
4. **Group activity:** collect five health-awareness slogans (for example on hand washing, vaccination, anti-tobacco) and identify the figures used.
5. **Creative task:** in pairs, write a three-line poster slogan on the safe use of antibiotics using at least two figures of speech; read it aloud to the class.
6. Mark the worksheet with the answer key and correct errors.

## Precautions

1. Look for the comparison word (like/as) to separate simile from metaphor.
2. Do not confuse hyperbole (exaggeration) with a plain factual statement.
3. Avoid figures of speech in prescriptions, labels and reports, where meaning must be exact.
4. Use original examples; do not copy slogans as your own.

## Viva questions

1. **What is a figure of speech?** A use of words in a non-literal way to create a special effect or emphasis.
2. **Difference between simile and metaphor?** A simile compares using "like" or "as"; a metaphor says one thing is another.
3. **Give an example of oxymoron.** Open secret (or bitter-sweet).
4. **What is a euphemism?** A mild word used in place of an unpleasant one, such as "passed away".
5. **Identify: "The tablet fizzed."** Onomatopoeia.
6. **Why avoid figures of speech in patient instructions?** They can be misunderstood; instructions must be literal and exact.
