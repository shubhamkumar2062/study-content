# Unit 1: Communication process, barriers and perspectives

A pharmacist talks all day: to patients at the counter, to doctors about a prescription, to nurses on the ward, to suppliers, inspectors and colleagues. A correct medicine with a wrong or unclear instruction can still harm a patient. This unit explains what communication is, how a message travels from one person to another, what stops it from getting through, and why two people can see the same thing differently.

## 1.1 Introduction and definition

The word *communication* comes from the Latin *communis*, meaning "common". To communicate is to make an idea, fact or feeling **common** to two or more people, so that the receiver understands it the way the sender meant it.

**Definition.** Communication is the process of sending and receiving messages (ideas, information, feelings) between two or more people through words, signs, writing or behaviour, so that a shared understanding is reached and a response is produced.

Key points hidden in this definition:

- It is a **process**, not a single act: it has steps and it goes on continuously.
- It is **two-way**: a message that is sent but not understood is not communication.
- It uses **verbal** (spoken and written words) and **non-verbal** (gestures, face, tone, posture) means.
- Its aim is **understanding and response**, not just speaking.

**Types of communication at a glance**

| Basis | Types | Pharmacy example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mode | Verbal (oral, written), non-verbal | Counselling a patient aloud; a printed label; a reassuring nod |
| Number of people | Intrapersonal (with oneself), interpersonal (two people), group, mass | Planning what to say; pharmacist–patient talk; ward meeting; health awareness poster |
| Formality | Formal, informal | Written order to a supplier; chat in the staff room |
| Direction (in an organisation) | Downward, upward, horizontal (lateral), diagonal | Chief pharmacist to staff; staff report to manager; pharmacist to fellow pharmacist; pharmacist to a ward doctor |

## 1.2 The importance of communication

Communication matters for a pharmacist and for any organisation because:

1. **Patient safety.** Correct dose, timing, storage and warnings reach the patient only through clear counselling and labelling. Most medication errors involve a breakdown somewhere in the chain of messages.
2. **Teamwork in healthcare.** Doctors, nurses, dentists, physiotherapists and pharmacists work as one team. Clear messages avoid duplicate drugs, interactions and missed doses.
3. **Building trust.** A patient follows advice (adherence) when he feels heard and understands why a medicine matters.
4. **Smooth running of an organisation.** Planning, giving instructions, coordinating departments, motivating staff and solving problems all depend on communication.
5. **Decision making.** Managers decide on the basis of information that is reported upward accurately and on time.
6. **Business and marketing.** Medical representatives, sales staff and pharmacy owners must explain products, negotiate and keep customers.
7. **Personal growth and career.** Interviews, presentations, group discussions and promotions reward good communicators.
8. **Conflict prevention.** Many disputes start from a misunderstood message, not from a real disagreement.

## 1.3 The communication process

The communication process is the chain of steps through which a message passes from sender to receiver and back. It is usually shown as a cycle.

```text
          CONTEXT (setting, culture, relationship, time)
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │                                                              │
 │  SOURCE ──► ENCODING ──► MESSAGE ──► CHANNEL ──► DECODING ──► RECEIVER
 │  (idea)    (into words,            (spoken, written,  (meaning     │
 │             signs)                  phone, e-mail)     taken out)  │
 │     ▲                                                          │   │
 │     └──────────────────── FEEDBACK ◄──────────────────────────┘   │
 │                   (reply, question, nod, action)                   │
 │            NOISE / BARRIERS can act at any step                    │
 └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The eight elements named in the syllabus:

| Element | Meaning | Pharmacy example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Source (sender)** | The person who has an idea and starts the communication. Credibility, knowledge and attitude of the source affect how the message is received. | The pharmacist who wants to explain how to use an inhaler |
| **Message** | The idea, information or feeling that is to be shared; the content. | "Shake the inhaler, breathe out fully, press and breathe in slowly." |
| **Encoding** | Turning the idea into a form others can understand: words, symbols, pictures, gestures. Good encoding chooses simple words that suit the receiver. | Using everyday words instead of "metered-dose aerosol actuation" |
| **Channel (medium)** | The route by which the message travels: face to face, telephone, letter, e-mail, label, poster, video. | Showing the steps with a demonstration device |
| **Decoding** | The receiver interpreting the symbols and drawing a meaning from them. | The patient understanding what "breathe in slowly" means |
| **Receiver** | The person or group for whom the message is meant. His language, education, mood and needs affect decoding. | The asthma patient (or a caregiver) |
| **Feedback** | The receiver's response that tells the sender whether the message was understood. It closes the loop and makes communication two-way. | Patient repeats the steps and demonstrates them (teach-back) |
| **Context** | The setting in which communication happens: physical place, time, social relationship, culture and the situation. The same words mean different things in different contexts. | A busy counter with a queue versus a quiet counselling room |

**Noise** is anything that disturbs the message at any stage (traffic sound, poor handwriting, worry, a wrong word). Noise is the general name for the barriers in section 1.4.

**Why feedback is the most important step for a pharmacist.** Without feedback the sender only *assumes* understanding. Asking the patient to repeat back the dose ("So, how will you take this tablet?") is the simplest way to check.

**Models of communication** (useful for a long answer):

- **Linear (one-way) model** — sender → message → receiver, no feedback (for example a notice board). Simple but no check of understanding.
- **Interactive model** — adds feedback and the sender's and receiver's fields of experience.
- **Transactional model** — both people send and receive at the same time (while you talk you also watch the listener's face); communication is shaped by context and relationship.

## 1.4 Barriers to communication

A barrier is anything that blocks, distorts or delays a message so that the receiver does not get the meaning intended. The syllabus lists eight kinds.

### 1. Physiological barriers

Arise from the body or health of the sender or receiver.

- Hearing loss, poor eyesight, speech defects (stammering), memory loss in the elderly, pain, fatigue, illness, effect of sedating drugs.
- Example: an elderly patient with poor eyesight cannot read small print on a label; a patient in pain cannot concentrate on counselling.
- Overcoming: large-print labels, speaking clearly facing the patient, pictograms, involving a caregiver, short sessions.

### 2. Physical barriers

Arise from the environment and the medium.

- Noise, distance, closed doors and cabins, poor lighting, crowded counters, bad telephone line, faulty equipment, poor handwriting on prescriptions, time zones.
- Example: counselling across a noisy counter with a long queue.
- Overcoming: a quiet counselling area, good lighting, checking equipment, typed prescriptions, choosing the right time.

### 3. Cultural barriers

Arise when people come from different cultures, regions, religions or social backgrounds with different beliefs, values, customs and meanings of gestures.

- Gestures (a nod means "yes" in most places but "no" in Bulgaria; the Indian side-to-side head tilt often means "OK" and confuses visitors), eye contact, personal space, food habits and fasting customs, beliefs about illness and medicines.
- Example: a patient fasting during a religious festival may skip a twice-daily medicine unless timing is discussed.
- Overcoming: respect, cultural awareness, avoiding stereotypes, asking rather than assuming.

### 4. Language barriers

Arise from differences in language or in the way words are used.

- Different mother tongues, regional accents, technical jargon, words with more than one meaning, poor translation, slang and abbreviations.
- Example: telling a patient "take this PRN" or "the drug is contraindicated"; "take one tablet twice a day" misunderstood as two tablets.
- Overcoming: simple local-language words, avoiding jargon, pictures, written instructions, interpreters, confirming understanding.

### 5. Gender barriers

Arise from differences in how men and women are brought up to communicate and from gender stereotypes.

- Different styles (one may focus on facts and solutions, the other on feelings and relationships), discomfort in discussing private health issues with someone of the other gender, prejudice about who is "in charge".
- Example: a woman patient may hesitate to discuss a contraceptive or a gynaecological problem with a male pharmacist.
- Overcoming: sensitivity, privacy, offering a same-gender colleague, avoiding stereotyped remarks.

### 6. Interpersonal barriers

Arise from the relationship between the two people.

- Lack of trust, ego clashes, status and hierarchy (a junior afraid to question a senior doctor), unwillingness to share information, poor rapport, withdrawal.
- Example: a junior pharmacist does not query an unusually high dose because he is afraid of the prescriber.
- Overcoming: mutual respect, open-door culture, a professional and polite way to raise concerns, building rapport.

### 7. Psychological barriers

Arise from the state of mind and attitudes of the people.

- Pre-conceived ideas, closed mind, poor attention, low self-esteem, stress, anxiety, distrust, selective perception (hearing only what one wants to hear).
- Example: a patient who believes "allopathic medicines damage the kidneys" does not really listen to advice.
- Overcoming: patience, listening first, building confidence, clearing misconceptions with evidence.

### 8. Emotional barriers

Arise from strong feelings at the moment of communication.

- Anger, fear, grief, excitement, embarrassment, shame. Strong emotion narrows attention and distorts meaning.
- Example: a patient just told he has diabetes is too shocked to absorb instructions about insulin.
- Overcoming: acknowledge the feeling, give simple key points, provide written material, follow up later.

**Summary table**

| Barrier | Root | Quick example | One remedy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Physiological | Body/health | Hearing loss | Speak clearly, face the person |
| Physical | Environment | Noisy counter | Quiet counselling area |
| Cultural | Beliefs/customs | Fasting habits | Respect, ask, adapt |
| Language | Words | Jargon | Simple words, pictograms |
| Gender | Gender roles/styles | Embarrassment | Privacy, sensitivity |
| Interpersonal | Relationship | Fear of a senior | Mutual respect |
| Psychological | Mind-set | Prejudice against medicines | Patience, evidence |
| Emotional | Feelings | Shock, anger | Acknowledge, follow up |

**General ways to overcome barriers (the "7 Cs")** — a message should be: **Clear, Concise, Concrete, Correct, Coherent, Complete and Courteous**. Add active listening, feedback, choosing the right channel and the right time.

## 1.5 Perspectives in communication

### Introduction

A **perspective** is the point of view from which a person sees and understands something. Two people can receive exactly the same message and still take different meanings, because each decodes it through his own perspective. Understanding perspective helps us predict misunderstandings and adjust our message.

### Visual perception

**Perception** is the process by which the brain selects, organises and interprets what the senses receive. What we "see" is not just what is in front of the eyes; the brain fills gaps and adds meaning from past learning.

- Optical illusions show this: two equal lines look unequal because of the arrows at their ends; one picture can look like a vase or two faces.
- People notice what interests them or what they expect (selective perception).
- First impressions are formed within seconds from appearance, dress, posture and facial expression, and they colour everything that follows.

```text
Same picture, two viewers:
 Viewer A (expects a vase)  ──►  "It is a vase"
 Viewer B (expects people)  ──►  "Two faces looking at each other"
 Lesson: check what the other person actually perceived.
```

Pharmacy relevance: look-alike packs and sound-alike names (for example different strengths in similar cartons) can be misread because the eye sees what it expects. Double-checking and reading aloud reduce this.

### Language

Words do not carry meaning by themselves; people give meaning to words.

- **Denotation** is the dictionary meaning; **connotation** is the feeling or idea attached to a word. "Cheap" and "economical" denote the same thing but feel different.
- The same word can mean different things to different groups ("positive" report: good news to a layman, but a positive test may be bad news).
- The language we think in shapes how we describe and remember events.
- Choosing words that match the listener's vocabulary changes how a message is received.

### Other factors affecting our perspective

| Factor | How it shapes perspective | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Past experiences** | We interpret new messages using what happened before. A good or bad experience colours expectations. | A patient who had a side effect from a painkiller refuses all painkillers. |
| **Prejudices** | Fixed opinions formed without full information, often about groups of people. They make us judge the message by the messenger. | Dismissing advice because the pharmacist "looks too young". |
| **Feelings** | Mood at the moment changes interpretation; a happy person takes a neutral remark kindly, an upset person takes it as criticism. | "Please wait" heard as rude by an anxious relative. |
| **Environment** | Surroundings, culture, upbringing, workplace and even weather affect how we see things. | A message in a calm clinic is received better than the same one in a crowded emergency room. |

**How to communicate across different perspectives**

1. Remember that your view is one view, not the only one.
2. Ask questions to understand the other person's view before replying.
3. Avoid judging by appearance or first impression.
4. Check understanding through feedback.
5. Separate the person from the problem.

## Remember

- Communication = sharing meaning so that it is understood and gets a response; it is a two-way process.
- Process elements: source, message, encoding, channel, decoding, receiver, feedback, context (noise can enter anywhere).
- Feedback closes the loop; teach-back is the pharmacist's best check.
- Eight barriers: physiological, physical, cultural, language, gender, interpersonal, psychological, emotional — learn one example and one remedy for each.
- 7 Cs: clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, courteous.
- Perspective is shaped by visual perception, language, past experiences, prejudices, feelings and environment.
