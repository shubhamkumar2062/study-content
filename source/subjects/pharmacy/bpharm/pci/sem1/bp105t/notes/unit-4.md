# Unit 4: Interview skills and giving presentations

Every pharmacy graduate faces interviews: for hospital and community pharmacy posts, industry jobs, government recruitment, higher studies and scholarships. Presentations come even earlier — seminars, journal clubs, project defences, and later product presentations, training sessions and patient-education talks. Both are skills that improve with preparation and practice.

## 4.1 Interview skills: what is an interview?

**Definition.** An interview is a planned, purposeful, face-to-face (or online) conversation in which one party (the interviewer or a panel) asks questions to obtain information and assess the other party (the candidate), and the candidate also learns about the organisation.

An interview is two-way: the employer assesses you, and you assess whether the job and organisation suit you.

**Types of interviews**

| Type | Feature | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job (selection) interview | To choose the best candidate | Hospital pharmacist post |
| Panel interview | Several interviewers together | Government recruitment board |
| Telephone / video interview | First screening at a distance | Online round for an industry job |
| Structured | Fixed set of questions for all | Campus placement |
| Unstructured | Free conversation | Small pharmacy owner hiring |
| Stress interview | Deliberate pressure to test calmness | Sales or marketing roles |
| Group interview | Several candidates at once | Walk-in recruitment |
| Technical interview | Tests subject knowledge | Quality control post |
| HR interview | Attitude, salary, fit | Final round |
| Appraisal, exit, counselling interviews | Within employment | Annual review, leaving the job |

## 4.2 Purpose of an interview

**For the employer (interviewer)**

1. To verify the information in the CV/résumé.
2. To assess knowledge, skills and experience for the job.
3. To judge communication, confidence, attitude, personality and teamwork.
4. To check how the candidate thinks and solves problems.
5. To see whether the candidate fits the organisation's culture and values.
6. To give information about the job, salary and conditions.

**For the candidate**

1. To present himself and his abilities convincingly.
2. To learn about the job, the work environment and growth prospects.
3. To decide whether to accept an offer.
4. To negotiate terms where possible.

## 4.3 Preparing for an interview

```text
BEFORE                   DURING                       AFTER
- Know yourself          - Arrive early               - Thank-you e-mail
- Research the employer  - Greet, sit when asked      - Note questions asked
- Revise subject basics  - Listen, answer clearly     - Follow up politely
- Prepare CV & documents - Positive body language     - Learn from the result
- Practise mock answers  - Ask 1–2 good questions
- Plan dress and travel  - Close with thanks
```

**Common interview questions to prepare:** Tell us about yourself. Why pharmacy? Why do you want to join us? Your strengths and weaknesses. Explain your project. A difficult situation you handled. Where do you see yourself in five years? Subject questions (for example: schedules of drugs, storage of vaccines, dose calculations, generic and brand names, good manufacturing practice).

**A good structure for answering "Tell us about yourself":** present (who you are now, qualification) → past (relevant training, project, achievements) → future (why this job fits your goal). Keep it to about one to two minutes.

**Answering behavioural questions — the STAR method**

- **S**ituation — set the scene briefly.
- **T**ask — what you had to do.
- **A**ction — what *you* did.
- **R**esult — the outcome and what you learned.

## 4.4 Do's and don'ts of an interview

| Do's | Don'ts |
| --- | --- |
| Research the organisation and the job | Go without knowing what the company does |
| Dress formally, neat and clean; groomed hair, polished shoes | Wear loud colours, heavy perfume or casual clothes |
| Arrive 10–15 minutes early | Arrive late or rushed |
| Carry organised documents (CV copies, certificates, photos) in a file | Search for papers in front of the panel |
| Knock, greet ("Good morning"), wait to be offered a seat | Walk in and sit without permission |
| Firm handshake if offered, smile, steady eye contact | Weak handshake, avoid eye contact, stare |
| Sit upright, hands relaxed | Slouch, fidget, cross arms, play with pen or phone |
| Listen fully; ask to repeat if unclear | Interrupt or answer before the question ends |
| Answer honestly, clearly and to the point | Lie, bluff, or give one-word or very long answers |
| Say "I don't know, but I would find out by…" when unsure | Guess wildly about facts, especially drug facts |
| Show enthusiasm and positive attitude | Criticise former teachers, college or employer |
| Give examples from projects, training and internships | Speak in slang or overly casual language |
| Ask one or two thoughtful questions at the end | Ask first about salary and leave |
| Thank the panel and leave politely | Argue with the panel or show frustration |
| Switch off your mobile phone | Let the phone ring or check messages |
| Send a short thank-you note afterwards | Forget to follow up |

**For online interviews:** test the internet, camera and microphone; use a quiet room with a plain background and good light in front of you; look at the camera when speaking; dress fully formally; keep documents ready on screen.

**Curriculum vitae (CV) tips** (often asked with interview skills): one to two pages; contact details; career objective; education in reverse order; training/internship; projects and publications; skills (technical, computer, language); achievements and activities; references. No spelling errors; honest; tailored to the job.

## 4.5 Giving presentations: introduction

A **presentation** is a planned talk given to an audience to inform, persuade, train or entertain, usually supported by visual aids such as slides, charts or demonstrations.

**Purposes:** to inform (a seminar on a new drug), to instruct or train (staff training on cold-chain storage), to persuade (a product or proposal), to report (project results), to inspire (a health awareness talk).

## 4.6 Dealing with fears

Fear of public speaking (stage fright, glossophobia) is very common. Symptoms: fast heartbeat, dry mouth, shaky voice and hands, sweating, mind going blank. Fear comes from fear of judgement, of forgetting, of failing, or from lack of preparation and practice.

**Ways to manage fear**

1. **Prepare thoroughly** — knowing the content is the strongest cure for fear.
2. **Practise aloud** several times, in front of a mirror, friends or a recorder; time yourself.
3. **Know the venue and equipment** — arrive early, test the projector, microphone and slides.
4. **Breathing** — slow deep breaths before starting calm the body.
5. **Positive self-talk and visualisation** — imagine the talk going well; replace "I will fail" with "I am prepared".
6. **Memorise the opening** — the first minute is the most stressful; a confident start carries you.
7. **Make eye contact with friendly faces** first.
8. **Accept nervousness** — some nervousness gives energy; the audience notices it much less than you think.
9. **Keep water** for a dry mouth.
10. **Focus on the message and the audience**, not on yourself.

## 4.7 Planning your presentation

Answer these before making a single slide:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Purpose** — what should the audience know, feel or do at the end? | Keeps the content focused |
| **Audience** — who are they, how many, what do they know, what do they need? | Decides language, depth and examples |
| **Time** — how long is allowed? | Decides how much content; leave time for questions |
| **Venue and equipment** — room size, projector, microphone, seating | Avoids last-minute problems |
| **Content** — main points, supporting facts, examples, data | Research from reliable sources and note references |
| **Visual aids** — slides, charts, models, handouts | Choose what helps understanding, not decoration |

**Rule of three:** most audiences remember about three main points well; build the talk around three key messages.

## 4.8 Structuring your presentation

A classic structure: **"Tell them what you will tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told them."**

```text
INTRODUCTION (about 10–15% of time)
 - Greet, introduce yourself and the topic
 - Hook: a question, a fact, a short story, a case
 - Purpose and outline (roadmap) of the talk
BODY (about 70–80%)
 - Main point 1 → evidence / example
 - Main point 2 → evidence / example
 - Main point 3 → evidence / example
 - Smooth transitions ("Now that we have seen…, let us look at…")
CONCLUSION (about 10%)
 - Summary of key points
 - Take-home message / call to action
 - Thank the audience; invite questions
```

Ways to arrange the body: chronological (history of a drug), problem–solution, cause–effect, compare–contrast, topical (classification, mechanism, uses, side effects).

**Good slide design**

- One idea per slide; a clear title.
- About six lines of short phrases at most; not full paragraphs.
- Readable font size (large enough for the back row) and high contrast.
- Simple charts, labelled diagrams and images where they help.
- Consistent design; avoid distracting animations and sounds.
- Do not read the slides; they support you, they do not replace you.
- Cite sources on the slide or at the end.

## 4.9 Delivering your presentation

1. **Start confidently** — stand straight, smile, greet, pause, then begin.
2. **Voice** — audible to the last row, varied pitch, moderate pace, pauses after key points, clear pronunciation of drug names.
3. **Eye contact** — move it across all sections of the audience, a few seconds per person.
4. **Body language** — open posture, natural gestures, move with purpose; do not turn your back to read the screen; avoid hands in pockets.
5. **Engage the audience** — ask questions, use examples close to their experience, a short quiz or demonstration.
6. **Watch the time** — keep to the time allowed; skip less important points if needed.
7. **Handle questions** — listen fully, repeat the question for everyone, answer briefly and honestly; if you do not know, say so and offer to find out; stay calm with hostile questions.
8. **End strongly** — summary and a clear final message, then thank the audience.

## 4.10 Techniques of delivery

| Technique | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Extempore (extemporaneous)** | Well prepared and practised, delivered from brief notes or key-word cards, words chosen naturally at the time | Natural, flexible, good eye contact, most recommended | Needs preparation and practice |
| **Manuscript (reading)** | Read word for word from a written text | Exact wording, useful for official statements and technical detail | Dull, little eye contact, sounds unnatural |
| **Memorised** | Text learnt by heart and recited | Fluent, good eye contact | Risk of forgetting, sounds mechanical, inflexible |
| **Impromptu** | Speaking with little or no preparation | Spontaneous | Disorganised, nervous, may miss points |

Other useful delivery techniques: using cue cards; storytelling and case examples; signposting phrases ("first", "next", "finally"); demonstrations; asking rhetorical questions; summarising after each section; using a pointer sparingly; rehearsing with the actual slides.

## Remember

- Interview = planned two-way conversation for selection and information exchange.
- Purposes: verify CV, assess knowledge, attitude, communication and fit; candidate learns about the job.
- Prepare: research, CV, documents, mock answers (STAR), dress, arrive early.
- Learn at least eight do's and eight don'ts in table form.
- Presentation steps: deal with fear → plan (purpose, audience, time, venue) → structure (introduction, body, conclusion) → deliver (voice, eyes, body, time, questions).
- Delivery techniques: extempore (best), manuscript, memorised, impromptu.
