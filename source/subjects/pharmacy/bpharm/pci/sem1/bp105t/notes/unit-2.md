# Unit 2: Elements of communication and communication styles

When we talk face to face, the words are only part of the message. The tone in which they are said, the face and hands that go with them, and even a handshake or a pat on the shoulder carry meaning. This unit covers the elements of face-to-face communication and then four common communication styles, so that you can recognise your own style and adjust to other people's.

## 2.1 Introduction to the elements of communication

Every face-to-face message has three channels working together:

```text
               FACE-TO-FACE MESSAGE
      ┌──────────────┼───────────────────┐
   WORDS         TONE OF VOICE       BODY LANGUAGE
 (verbal)       (vocal / para-       (non-verbal: face,
                 language)            eyes, gestures,
                                      posture, touch, space)
```

When words and tone or body language disagree, listeners usually believe the tone and body language. A pharmacist who says "Take your time, ask me anything" while looking at the clock and tapping the counter sends the opposite message.

A widely quoted rule (from experiments by Albert Mehrabian) says that when people talk about **feelings and attitudes** and the channels conflict, the words count for about 7%, tone of voice about 38% and facial expression/body language about 55%. Remember the limit: this applies to messages about liking and feelings, not to every kind of communication. Technical information (a dose, a time) is carried mainly by words, which is why written instructions matter.

## 2.2 Face-to-face communication

Face-to-face communication is the richest channel because the sender and receiver can see and hear each other and get instant feedback. It allows questions, demonstration (for example how to use an inhaler or an insulin pen) and emotional support. Its limits: it needs both people present, leaves no record, and can be affected by nervousness.

### 2.2.1 Tone of voice (paralanguage)

**Paralanguage** is *how* something is said rather than *what* is said. Its parts:

| Feature | Meaning | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pitch** | Highness or lowness of the voice | High pitch may sound nervous or excited; a lower, steady pitch sounds calm and confident |
| **Volume** | Loudness | Too loud sounds aggressive; too soft sounds unsure; adjust to the room and to hearing-impaired patients |
| **Pace (rate)** | Speed of speaking | Fast speech loses the listener; slow down for instructions and numbers |
| **Pause** | Silence between words | Pauses let important points sink in and invite questions |
| **Intonation** | Rise and fall of the voice | Rising tone for questions; flat tone sounds bored |
| **Stress (emphasis)** | Weight put on a word | "Take *one* tablet" versus "take one *tablet*" changes the focus |
| **Clarity (articulation)** | Pronouncing words clearly | Needed for drug names that sound alike |

The same sentence can carry different meanings: "I did not say he stole the medicine" changes meaning depending on which word is stressed.

### 2.2.2 Body language (non-verbal communication)

**Non-verbal communication** is communication without words. Its main forms:

| Form | What it includes | Positive signal | Negative signal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Facial expression** | Smile, frown, raised eyebrows | Warm smile shows friendliness | Frown or rolling eyes shows annoyance |
| **Eye contact (oculesics)** | Looking at the listener | Steady, comfortable eye contact shows honesty and interest | Avoiding eyes suggests nervousness or hiding; staring feels aggressive |
| **Gestures** | Movement of hands and head | Open palms, nodding | Pointing a finger, crossed arms, fidgeting |
| **Posture** | How one sits or stands | Upright, leaning slightly forward = interest | Slouching = boredom; turning away = rejection |
| **Proxemics (personal space)** | Distance kept between people | Respecting comfortable distance | Standing too close makes others uneasy |
| **Haptics (touch)** | Handshake, pat | Firm handshake shows confidence | Weak or crushing handshake; touch where not acceptable |
| **Appearance** | Dress, grooming, apron, name badge | Clean apron and badge build trust | Untidy appearance lowers credibility |
| **Chronemics (time)** | Punctuality, how time is used | Being on time shows respect | Keeping a patient waiting without explanation |

Typical **distances of personal space** (described by Edward Hall): intimate (very close, family), personal (about an arm's length, friends), social (formal business and counter conversations), public (speaking to a group). A pharmacy counter naturally keeps a social distance; private counselling may move to personal distance.

**Functions of non-verbal communication** (useful for a 5-mark answer):

1. **Repeating** — nodding while saying "yes".
2. **Substituting** — a thumbs-up instead of words.
3. **Complementing** — a smile adds warmth to "welcome".
4. **Contradicting** — saying "I'm fine" with tears in the eyes.
5. **Regulating** — raising a hand to ask to speak, looking at the next person in the queue.
6. **Accenting** — banging the table to stress a point.

Cautions: body language differs between cultures and individuals, so read a group of signals together (a cluster) rather than judging by one gesture.

### 2.2.3 Verbal communication

**Verbal communication** uses words, spoken or written.

- **Oral** — face-to-face talk, telephone, meetings, interviews, presentations, counselling.
- **Written** — letters, e-mails, labels, reports, notices, prescriptions, patient information leaflets (covered in Unit 3).

**Advantages of oral communication:** quick, personal, immediate feedback, can clear doubts at once, uses tone and gestures. **Limitations:** no permanent record, can be forgotten or misquoted, not suitable for long or complex detail, depends on the speaker's skill.

**Guidelines for effective verbal communication**

1. Know your purpose and your listener.
2. Use simple, familiar words; avoid jargon or explain it.
3. Speak in short sentences, one idea at a time.
4. Be specific: "Take one tablet after breakfast and one after dinner" rather than "take twice daily".
5. Be polite: please, thank you, sorry.
6. Check understanding with open questions and teach-back.
7. Match your words with your tone and body language.

### 2.2.4 Physical communication

Physical communication is the meaning carried by the **physical presence and actions** of the communicator and by the physical setting — the parts of the message that are seen rather than heard. Textbooks describe it as the body, gestures, posture, dress and the arrangement of the surroundings working together.

Examples:

- Standing up to greet a doctor who walks into the pharmacy shows respect.
- Coming out from behind the counter to sit beside an elderly patient removes a physical barrier.
- A clean, well-arranged pharmacy communicates professionalism before a word is spoken.
- Demonstrating a device with your own hands (showing how to press an insulin pen) is a physical message that is easier to follow than words.
- Touch (a supportive hand on the shoulder of a grieving relative) where culturally appropriate.

## 2.3 Communication styles

### Introduction

A **communication style** is the typical way a person prefers to give and receive information. People are not good or bad communicators because of their style; problems arise when two very different styles meet and neither adjusts. Knowing the styles helps a pharmacist deal with an impatient doctor, a chatty colleague, a detail-loving inspector or a worried patient.

### The communication styles matrix

The matrix sorts people along two dimensions:

1. **Directness (assertiveness)** — how strongly and quickly a person states views and pushes for action. Direct people tell; indirect people ask.
2. **Openness (responsiveness)** — how much a person shows feelings and focuses on relationships. Open people share feelings; self-contained (guarded) people keep them private and focus on tasks.

Crossing the two dimensions gives four styles:

```text
                         OPEN (people / feelings)
                                  ▲
          CONSIDERATE             │             SPIRITED
   (indirect + open)              │       (direct + open)
   warm, supportive,              │       enthusiastic, talkative,
   seeks harmony                  │       big-picture, persuasive
 INDIRECT ◄───────────────────────┼───────────────────────► DIRECT
 (asks, reserved)                 │               (tells, assertive)
          SYSTEMATIC              │             DIRECT
   (indirect + self-contained)    │       (direct + self-contained)
   logical, precise,              │       decisive, results-first,
   wants facts and detail         │       to the point
                                  ▼
                     SELF-CONTAINED (task / facts)
```

(Books draw the axes in slightly different orders; learn which two dimensions combine to make each style.)

### 1. Direct communication style

- **Traits:** decisive, confident, competitive, results-oriented, gets to the point, likes control, impatient with detail and small talk, values time.
- **Strengths:** quick decisions, takes charge in a crisis.
- **Weaknesses:** can seem blunt, bossy or insensitive; may not listen.
- **How to communicate with them:** be brief, give the bottom line first, offer options, avoid long stories, be well prepared.
- **Example:** A senior consultant says, "Just tell me — is there an interaction or not?" The pharmacist answers, "Yes, a significant one. I suggest drug X instead; here is the reason in one line."

### 2. Spirited communication style

- **Traits:** enthusiastic, expressive, talkative, imaginative, focuses on the big picture and new ideas, likes recognition and fun, may jump between topics.
- **Strengths:** motivates people, creative, persuasive, good at presentations and sales.
- **Weaknesses:** may skip details, exaggerate, lose focus, overpromise.
- **How to communicate with them:** show interest in their ideas, allow some conversation, keep them on track gently, put details in writing afterwards.
- **Example:** A marketing colleague excitedly pitches a health camp idea. The pharmacist appreciates it and then says, "Great idea. Let us list the three things we need by Friday."

### 3. Systematic communication style

- **Traits:** logical, analytical, precise, organised, values facts, data and accuracy, cautious, slow to decide, prefers written communication, little display of emotion.
- **Strengths:** careful, reliable, good at quality control and documentation.
- **Weaknesses:** may seem cold or rigid; can delay decisions seeking more data ("analysis paralysis").
- **How to communicate with them:** be organised, give evidence and references, stick to facts, allow time to think, avoid exaggeration.
- **Example:** A quality-assurance manager asks for batch numbers, test results and the exact SOP clause before approving a change. The pharmacist brings a written summary with data.

### 4. Considerate communication style

- **Traits:** warm, patient, supportive, good listener, values relationships and harmony, avoids conflict, asks others' opinions, decides by consensus.
- **Strengths:** builds trust and team spirit; excellent with patients and in counselling.
- **Weaknesses:** may avoid saying "no", hide disagreement, take criticism personally, decide slowly.
- **How to communicate with them:** be friendly and sincere, show personal interest, give reassurance, invite their opinion directly, avoid pressure.
- **Example:** A nurse is hesitant to report that a patient missed doses. The pharmacist says kindly, "You did the right thing telling me. Let us work out together how to prevent it."

### Comparison table

| Feature | Direct | Spirited | Systematic | Considerate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Results | Ideas, vision | Facts, process | People, relationships |
| Pace | Fast | Fast | Slow | Slow |
| Emotion shown | Little | Much | Little | Much (warmth) |
| Decision style | Quick, alone | Quick, by intuition | Careful, by data | Careful, by consensus |
| Fears | Losing control, wasting time | Being ignored | Being wrong | Conflict, letting people down |
| Best approach | Be brief | Be enthusiastic | Be precise | Be supportive |

**Flexing your style.** Good communicators keep their own style but adjust ("flex") to the listener: speeding up with a direct person, adding data for a systematic one, showing warmth to a considerate one and energy to a spirited one.

## Remember

- Face-to-face communication = words + tone of voice + body language; when they conflict about feelings, tone and body language win.
- Paralanguage: pitch, volume, pace, pause, intonation, stress, clarity.
- Non-verbal forms: face, eyes, gestures, posture, space (proxemics), touch (haptics), appearance, time (chronemics).
- Functions of non-verbal cues: repeat, substitute, complement, contradict, regulate, accent.
- Styles matrix: directness × openness → Direct, Spirited, Systematic, Considerate.
- Know one trait, one strength, one weakness and one "how to deal with them" tip for each style.
