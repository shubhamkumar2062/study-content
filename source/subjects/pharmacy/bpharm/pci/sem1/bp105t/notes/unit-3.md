# Unit 3: Listening skills and written communication

Most people think good communication means speaking well. In practice, a pharmacist spends more time listening — to a patient describing symptoms, to a doctor giving a verbal order, to a supplier on the phone. The second half of this unit is about writing: when to put something in writing, when not to, and how to write messages that are read and acted on.

## 3.1 Basic listening skills: introduction

**Hearing** and **listening** are not the same.

| Hearing | Listening |
| --- | --- |
| Physical: sound waves reach the ear | Mental: paying attention and understanding |
| Passive and automatic | Active and deliberate |
| Needs only working ears | Needs attention, interest and effort |
| Example: hearing the fan running | Example: noticing a patient say "the tablets make me dizzy" |

**Definition.** Listening is the active process of receiving a spoken (and non-verbal) message, paying attention to it, understanding its meaning, remembering it and responding to it.

**Stages of listening**

```text
RECEIVING → ATTENDING → UNDERSTANDING → REMEMBERING → EVALUATING → RESPONDING
(hearing)   (focusing)   (interpreting)  (storing)     (judging)     (feedback)
```

**Types of listening**

| Type | Purpose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Informational (comprehensive) | To learn and understand | Attending a lecture on a new drug |
| Critical (evaluative) | To judge and evaluate | Assessing a medical representative's claims |
| Empathic (therapeutic) | To understand feelings and support | A patient upset about a diagnosis |
| Appreciative | For enjoyment | Listening to music |
| Discriminative | To pick up differences in sound and tone | Noticing anxiety in a caller's voice |

**Why listening matters for a pharmacist:** correct patient history, detecting adverse drug reactions and misuse, understanding verbal orders correctly, building trust, reducing errors and complaints.

**Common poor-listening habits:** pretending to listen, interrupting, planning a reply instead of listening, judging the speaker, being distracted by a phone, listening only for facts and ignoring feelings, finishing other people's sentences.

## 3.2 Self-awareness

**Self-awareness** is knowing your own feelings, habits, strengths, weaknesses, biases and how you come across to others. It is the starting point of good listening, because many listening failures come from inside the listener.

How self-awareness helps listening:

- You notice when your mind wanders and bring it back.
- You recognise your prejudices (about age, dress, accent) and stop them from colouring what you hear.
- You notice your emotional "triggers" — words or situations that make you defensive — and stay calm.
- You know your listening habits (for example interrupting) and work on them.
- You become aware of your own body language while listening.

Ways to build self-awareness: reflect after important conversations ("Did I let him finish?"), ask colleagues for honest feedback, keep a short journal, record and review a practice session, and note the moments you felt irritated or bored and why.

## 3.3 Active listening

**Active listening** means listening with full attention to understand both the content and the feelings of the speaker, and showing the speaker that you have understood. It is the opposite of passive listening, where the listener merely hears.

**Components / techniques of active listening**

| Technique | What you do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Paying attention** | Face the speaker, eye contact, remove distractions | Put the phone away, stop typing |
| **Showing that you are listening** | Nod, lean forward, small encouragers | "I see", "go on", "mm-hmm" |
| **Paraphrasing** | Repeat the message in your own words | "So the cough started after you began the new BP tablet?" |
| **Reflecting feelings** | Name the emotion you notice | "You sound worried about the side effects." |
| **Clarifying** | Ask questions to remove doubt | "When you say 'regularly', how many times a day?" |
| **Summarising** | Pull the main points together | "Let me sum up: three problems — dizziness, dry mouth and missed evening doses." |
| **Deferring judgement** | Let the speaker finish before forming opinions | Do not interrupt with advice |
| **Responding appropriately** | Give an honest, respectful, useful reply | Explain, suggest, refer if needed |

**Open and closed questions**

- **Open questions** invite a full answer: "How are you taking this medicine?", "What happens when you feel the pain?"
- **Closed questions** get a short answer, often yes/no: "Are you allergic to penicillin?"
- Start with open questions to gather information; use closed ones to confirm specific facts.

## 3.4 Becoming an active listener

Steps a student can practise:

1. **Prepare to listen.** Clear your mind, stop other work, choose a quiet place.
2. **Stop talking.** You cannot listen while you speak.
3. **Put the speaker at ease.** Smile, welcome, give privacy.
4. **Look and listen for non-verbal cues.** Tone, face and posture often tell more than words.
5. **Be patient.** Allow pauses; do not finish the speaker's sentences.
6. **Hold back your judgement and your solution** until you have the full picture.
7. **Ask, paraphrase and summarise** to confirm understanding.
8. **Take short notes** when the details matter (dose, dates, phone numbers), and tell the speaker why you are writing.
9. **Respond and follow up.** Act on what was said; it shows the listening was real.

A simple memory aid used in many training courses is to listen with **ears, eyes and heart**: ears for the words, eyes for the body language, heart for the feelings.

## 3.5 Listening in difficult situations

Listening is hardest exactly when it matters most.

| Difficult situation | What helps |
| --- | --- |
| **An angry or upset person** (patient angry about a stock-out or a wrong bill) | Stay calm, do not argue or take it personally, let the person speak, acknowledge the feeling ("I understand this is frustrating"), apologise for the inconvenience, then focus on a solution. |
| **Criticism directed at you** | Listen fully before replying, separate the point from the tone, ask for specifics, thank the person, accept what is fair. |
| **Emotional distress or bad news** (a relative of a terminally ill patient) | Empathic listening, allow silence, avoid false reassurance, give simple information, offer to talk later. |
| **A person who talks too much or rambles** | Listen for the key point, summarise and gently steer: "So the main problem is…". |
| **Language or accent differences** | Slow down, ask to repeat, use simple words, use an interpreter or a family member, confirm in writing. |
| **Noisy or rushed environment** | Move to a quiet area, ask for a minute to finish with the current customer, call back if needed. |
| **Disagreement with a senior** (doubtful prescription) | Listen to the reasoning, ask respectful questions, present facts, keep the patient's safety first. |
| **Telephone calls** (no body language) | Concentrate on tone, repeat back numbers and drug names, write down and read back verbal orders. |

**A handy memory aid (LEAP)** for an upset person: **L**isten, **E**mpathise, **A**pologise or acknowledge, **P**roblem-solve.

## 3.6 Effective written communication: introduction

**Written communication** is the exchange of messages through written words: letters, memos, e-mails, reports, notices, circulars, minutes, labels, prescriptions, SOPs, patient leaflets, text messages.

**Advantages**

1. Permanent record — useful as evidence and for reference (legal requirement for many pharmacy records).
2. Suitable for long, detailed or technical information.
3. Can be read again and at the reader's convenience.
4. Can reach many people at once (circular, notice).
5. The writer can plan and revise; fewer careless errors.
6. Gives an official, formal character.

**Limitations**

1. Slow; no instant feedback.
2. Costly in time and effort for long documents.
3. Can be misunderstood without tone and body language.
4. Useless for readers who cannot read the language.
5. Once sent, hard to take back.

## 3.7 When and when not to use written communication

The syllabus asks you to judge four things before choosing writing over a conversation.

### 1. Complexity of the topic

- **Use writing** when the information is detailed, technical or has many steps, numbers or figures — dosage schedules, a tapering regimen, a procedure, a policy, a report. Readers can go back and check.
- **Avoid writing alone** when the topic is complex and needs explanation with examples and questions; send the document but also discuss it.

### 2. Amount of discussion required

- **Use writing** when little or no discussion is needed: confirming a decision, giving notice of a meeting, sharing data.
- **Do not use writing** when a lot of back-and-forth is needed to reach agreement, solve a problem or brainstorm. Long e-mail chains waste time; a meeting or phone call is faster. Write a short summary afterwards.

### 3. Shades of meaning

- Written words lose tone, facial expression and gestures, so subtle meaning, humour, sarcasm or emotion can be misread.
- **Do not use writing** for sensitive matters — criticism, disciplinary issues, bad news, disagreement with feelings involved, personal problems. Speak face to face first.
- **Use writing** when you need an exact, unambiguous wording that everyone reads the same way (a contract term, a dose).

### 4. Formal communication

- **Use writing** for formal and official matters: orders, agreements, appointments, complaints, legal and regulatory records, anything that must be documented or may be needed later as evidence.
- Pharmacy examples: purchase orders, drug registers, prescription records, adverse drug reaction reports, inspection replies, SOPs.

**Quick decision table**

| Situation | Better choice |
| --- | --- |
| Monthly stock report to the manager | Written |
| Telling a colleague about a serious mistake he made | Face to face, then a written note if required |
| Explaining a new dosage schedule to a patient | Talk + written instruction card |
| Planning a health camp with five people | Meeting; minutes in writing |
| Confirming a supplier order | Written (e-mail/purchase order) |
| Condolence or sensitive news | Personal conversation |

## 3.8 Writing effectively

### 3.8.1 Subject lines

In e-mails, memos and letters the subject line decides whether the message is opened and how fast.

- Make it **specific and informative**: "Stock-out of insulin glargine pens — order needed by 15 March" rather than "Urgent".
- Keep it short (a few words).
- Put any action or deadline in it ("Action required", "For approval", "Reply by Friday").
- Change the subject line when the topic changes; do not reuse old threads for new matters.
- Avoid all capitals and many exclamation marks.

### 3.8.2 Put the main point first

Busy readers may read only the first lines. Use the **"inverted pyramid"**: the most important point first, then supporting details, then background.

```text
 ┌──────────────────────────────┐
 │  MAIN POINT / REQUEST        │  ← first sentence
 ├──────────────────────────────┤
 │   Key supporting details     │
 ├──────────────────────────────┤
 │    Background, extra info    │
 └──────────────────────────────┘
```

Weak: "As you know, last month we reviewed our stock levels and after several discussions… therefore we need 200 vials."
Better: "Please supply 200 vials of ceftriaxone 1 g by Monday. Our stock will run out on Tuesday because admissions have doubled."

### 3.8.3 Know your audience

Before writing, ask:

- **Who** will read it? (doctor, patient, manager, supplier, regulator)
- **What do they already know?** Use technical terms for professionals; plain words for patients.
- **What do they need from me?** Information, a decision, an action?
- **How will they feel?** Adjust tone for good news, bad news or a request.
- **What is my relationship?** Formal for seniors and outsiders; friendly but professional for colleagues.

The same information written for a doctor ("Atorvastatin 40 mg increases serum levels when given with clarithromycin; consider an alternative macrolide") and for a patient ("Do not take this antibiotic with your cholesterol tablet without asking your doctor") looks very different.

### 3.8.4 Organisation of the message

A well-organised message has:

1. **Opening** — purpose of writing, main point or request.
2. **Body** — details arranged logically: in time order, in order of importance, problem → cause → solution, or by topic with headings.
3. **Closing** — action required, deadline, contact details, polite close.

Tools that help organisation: short paragraphs (one idea each), headings, numbered steps, bullet lists, tables, bold for deadlines.

**Other features of effective writing (the 7 Cs again):** clear, concise, concrete, correct (facts, grammar, spelling, drug names, numbers and units), coherent, complete and courteous. Always **proofread** before sending — especially numbers, doses, names and attachments.

## Remember

- Hearing is physical; listening is active and mental.
- Stages: receive, attend, understand, remember, evaluate, respond.
- Self-awareness of your biases, triggers and habits is the base of good listening.
- Active listening tools: attention, encouragers, paraphrase, reflect feelings, clarify, summarise, defer judgement.
- Difficult situations: stay calm, acknowledge feelings, do not argue, summarise, solve.
- Choose writing by four tests: complexity, amount of discussion, shades of meaning, formality.
- Write effectively: clear subject line, main point first, know your audience, organise opening–body–closing, proofread.
