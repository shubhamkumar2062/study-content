# Unit 4: Budget preparation and implementation, clinical pharmacy and over-the-counter sales

This unit has three parts of very different sizes. **Budget** is a short management topic. **Clinical pharmacy** is the heart of the unit – its concept, the clinical pharmacist's daily work (medication chart review, clinical review, interventions, ward rounds, medication history), **pharmaceutical care**, and how drug therapy is adjusted for **pharmacokinetics and disease**. **OTC sales** cover non-prescription medicines and their rational use. Clinical pharmacy is a near-certain long question.

## 4.1 Budget preparation and implementation

### Definition

A **budget** is a **financial plan, expressed in money**, of expected **income and expenditure** for a fixed future period (usually one financial year, April to March in India). The **pharmacy budget** sets how much the pharmacy will spend on drugs, staff, equipment and services, and is a tool for **planning and control**.

### Types of budget relevant to a hospital pharmacy

| Type | Covers |
| --- | --- |
| **Operating (revenue) budget** | Recurring yearly costs – drugs and supplies, salaries, maintenance, utilities, training |
| **Capital budget** | Large one-time purchases with long life – equipment (refrigerators, laminar air-flow units, computers, automated dispensing), building alterations |
| **Personnel budget** | Salaries, allowances, new posts (often part of the operating budget) |
| **Cash budget** | Timing of cash inflows and outflows |

### Approaches

- **Incremental (traditional) budgeting** – last year's figures adjusted for price rise and new services. Simple but carries forward waste.
- **Zero-based budgeting** – every activity justified afresh each year, as if starting from zero. Controls waste but takes more work.
- **Performance / programme budgeting** – money tied to activities and outcomes.

### Steps in budget preparation

1. **Collect past data** – drug consumption, expenditure, patient load, bed occupancy for previous years.
2. **Forecast** the coming year – new departments or services, change in patient numbers, new drugs added to the formulary, expected **price changes** (inflation, price control), staffing changes.
3. **Estimate expenditure** under heads – drugs (often by therapeutic class), surgical items, salaries, equipment, maintenance, training.
4. **Estimate income** where the pharmacy recovers charges.
5. **Prepare the draft budget** with justification for each head.
6. **Discuss and negotiate** with the hospital administrator / finance committee.
7. **Approval** by the governing body.

### Implementation and control

1. Approved amounts are allotted to each head; purchases made within these limits.
2. **Monthly or quarterly comparison** of actual expenditure with the budget (**variance analysis**).
3. Reasons for over- or under-spending found and corrected – e.g. unexpected outbreaks, price changes, wastage, pilferage.
4. Cost-saving steps – formulary control, generic purchase, ABC/VED analysis, reducing expiry losses.
5. **Revision** of the budget if conditions change greatly; data used for the next year's budget.

```text
 Past data → Forecast → Estimate heads → Draft → Approval
       ▲                                           │
       └──── Review & revise ◄── Monitor variance ◄─ Implement
```

## 4.2 Clinical pharmacy

### Introduction and concept

**Clinical pharmacy** is the branch of pharmacy in which pharmacists provide **patient care that optimises medication therapy** and promotes health and disease prevention. The clinical pharmacist works **with doctors and nurses at the patient's bedside**, using knowledge of drugs and diseases to make therapy **safe, effective, appropriate and economical**. The shift is from a **product-focused** role (supplying drugs) to a **patient-focused** role (outcomes of drug therapy).

In India, clinical pharmacy grew mainly after the **Pharm.D** programme was introduced (**Pharm.D Regulations, 2008**), with clinical pharmacy departments now in many teaching hospitals.

### Functions and responsibilities of a clinical pharmacist

1. **Ward round participation** with the medical team.
2. **Medication history** taking and **medication reconciliation**.
3. **Medication chart review** and **clinical review** of drug therapy.
4. Identifying and solving **drug-related problems** – **pharmacist interventions**.
5. **ADR monitoring** and reporting; medication error prevention.
6. **Therapeutic drug monitoring** and pharmacokinetic dose individualisation.
7. **Drug information** to the health-care team.
8. **Patient counselling** at discharge and during stay.
9. **Drug utilisation evaluation** and antimicrobial stewardship.
10. Education, training and research.

### Drug therapy monitoring

**(a) Medication chart review**

The clinical pharmacist reviews the patient's **treatment (medication) chart** and case notes daily to check:

- Each drug has a clear **indication**; no indication left untreated.
- **Correct drug, dose, frequency, route, duration** for the patient's age, weight, kidney and liver function.
- **Drug interactions**, duplications, contraindications, allergies.
- **Administration problems** – IV compatibility, crushing tablets, timing with food.
- Correct **documentation** – doses given or missed.

**(b) Clinical review**

A deeper review combining drug therapy with **clinical findings and laboratory data** – vital signs, symptoms, lab results (renal function, liver tests, electrolytes, blood counts, cultures), drug levels – to judge whether therapy is **effective and safe** and whether it should change.

**(c) Pharmacist interventions**

A **pharmacist intervention** is any action by the pharmacist that leads to a **change in the patient's management or therapy**. Drug-related problems (DRPs) commonly found:

| Drug-related problem | Example of intervention |
| --- | --- |
| Untreated indication | Suggest adding a needed drug (e.g. prophylaxis) |
| Drug without indication | Suggest stopping it |
| Wrong drug | Suggest a safer or more effective alternative |
| Dose too low / too high | Suggest dose adjustment (e.g. for renal function) |
| Adverse drug reaction | Report and suggest management |
| Drug interaction | Suggest alternative or monitoring |
| Non-adherence | Counsel patient, simplify regimen |
| Drug use without need / duplication | Suggest stopping one |

Interventions are **communicated** to the prescriber, the **acceptance** is recorded, and the **outcome** is documented. Documentation shows the value of the service.

**(d) Ward round participation**

- The pharmacist **prepares beforehand** – reviews charts, lab results and new admissions.
- During the round, gives **drug information**, suggests changes, answers questions about dosing, interactions and ADRs.
- Observes the patient, notes new orders, and later follows up and documents.
- Benefits – fewer errors, more rational prescribing, better team learning.

**(e) Medication history** – see Unit 2 (2.5). The clinical pharmacist takes it at admission and uses it for **reconciliation** at admission, transfer and discharge.

### Pharmaceutical care

**Pharmaceutical care** was defined by **Hepler and Strand (1990)** as the **responsible provision of drug therapy for the purpose of achieving definite outcomes that improve a patient's quality of life**. The outcomes are: **cure of disease, elimination or reduction of symptoms, arresting or slowing a disease process, and preventing a disease or symptoms**.

Pharmaceutical care is a **philosophy and process**:

```text
 Assessment of patient's drug-related needs
        → Identify drug-related problems
        → Care plan (goals, interventions, monitoring)
        → Implement with patient and team
        → Follow-up evaluation of outcomes → (repeat)
```

Key features: **patient-centred**, the pharmacist **accepts responsibility** for drug-therapy outcomes, care is **documented**, and it is **continuous**.

### Dosing pattern and drug therapy based on pharmacokinetics and disease pattern

Doses must be individualised because patients differ in **absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion**. The clinical pharmacist applies pharmacokinetic principles to adjust therapy.

**1. Renal impairment**

- Drugs excreted unchanged by the kidney (for example aminoglycosides, vancomycin, digoxin, lithium, many beta-lactams) accumulate when kidney function falls.
- Kidney function is estimated by **creatinine clearance**, often with the **Cockcroft–Gault equation**:

```text
 CrCl (mL/min) = (140 − age in years) × body weight (kg)
                 ──────────────────────────────────────────
                    72 × serum creatinine (mg/dL)
 (multiply by 0.85 for women)
```

- Adjustment is done by **reducing the dose**, **lengthening the dosing interval**, or both, following the product information or standard references, and by **TDM** where available. Nephrotoxic drugs are avoided where possible.

**2. Hepatic impairment**

- Drugs cleared by liver metabolism (e.g. many benzodiazepines, opioids, theophylline) may accumulate; **no single test** predicts the change as creatinine clearance does for the kidney.
- Use lower doses or alternatives, monitor closely, and avoid hepatotoxic drugs; consider low albumin (more free drug) and clotting changes.

**3. Paediatric patients**

- Organ maturity, body water and enzyme activity change with age; neonates clear many drugs slowly.
- Doses are based on **body weight or body surface area**, using paediatric references; suitable dosage forms are chosen.

**4. Elderly patients**

- Reduced renal function, more body fat, less lean mass, reduced liver blood flow, more sensitivity of the brain and heart; many drugs (polypharmacy).
- "**Start low, go slow**", review the need for each drug, avoid potentially inappropriate drugs (lists such as Beers criteria), monitor for falls and confusion.

**5. Pregnancy and lactation** – changed volume of distribution and clearance; consider effects on the fetus or breast-fed infant.

**6. Obesity** – lipophilic drugs distribute into fat; dosing may use **ideal, adjusted or actual body weight** depending on the drug.

**7. Other disease states** – heart failure (reduced liver blood flow and oedema), thyroid disease, burns, critical illness, dialysis.

**Dosing patterns:**

| Pattern | Use |
| --- | --- |
| **Loading dose** | Reach target concentration quickly for drugs with a long half-life (e.g. digoxin, phenytoin, amiodarone) |
| **Maintenance dose** | Keeps concentration at steady state |
| **Continuous infusion** | Drugs with short half-life needing steady levels (e.g. heparin, insulin in ICU) |
| **Intermittent / extended-interval dosing** | Concentration-dependent antibiotics such as aminoglycosides |

With maintenance dosing alone, steady state is reached in about **4–5 half-lives**, whatever the size of the dose. A loading dose brings the concentration near the target at once, which matters when the half-life is long.

## 4.3 Over-the-counter (OTC) sales

### Introduction

**Over-the-counter (OTC) or non-prescription medicines** are medicines that the public can buy **without a prescription**, for **self-medication of minor, self-limiting ailments**. They are considered safe enough for use without medical supervision when used as directed.

**Indian position:** The Drugs and Cosmetics Act and Rules do **not define "OTC drugs"** as a legal category. Drugs **not included in Schedules H, H1 or X** (and not otherwise restricted) may in practice be sold without a prescription, but most still need a **drug sale licence**. A small number of household remedies may be sold by non-pharmacy shops with a restricted licence. A formal **OTC policy** has been under discussion by the regulator; check the current position before the exam.

### Features of a good OTC product

- Wide **safety margin**; low potential for misuse or dependence.
- Treats conditions that the patient can **self-recognise**.
- **Clear labelling** with directions, warnings and when to see a doctor.
- Not likely to **mask a serious disease**.

### Role of the pharmacist in OTC sales (responding to symptoms)

The pharmacist must decide whether to **supply a product, give advice only, or refer to a doctor**. A common structured approach is **WWHAM**:

| Letter | Question |
| --- | --- |
| **W** | **Who** is the patient (age, pregnant, other illnesses)? |
| **W** | **What** are the symptoms? |
| **H** | **How long** have the symptoms been present? |
| **A** | **Action** already taken (medicines tried)? |
| **M** | **Medicines** the patient is already taking? |

**Refer to a doctor ("red flags")** when symptoms are **severe, prolonged or recurrent**, in very young children, pregnant women or the frail elderly, when there is **blood** (in stool, vomit, sputum, urine), **unexplained weight loss**, **chest pain**, **high or persistent fever**, **difficulty in breathing**, or when OTC treatment has failed.

### Rational use of common OTC medications

| Condition | Common OTC groups | Rational-use points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pain and fever** | Paracetamol, ibuprofen (and other NSAIDs) | Do not exceed the labelled daily amount; check **combination products** for hidden paracetamol (risk of liver damage); NSAIDs can irritate the stomach and affect kidneys – avoid in peptic ulcer, kidney disease, late pregnancy; refer if fever persists |
| **Cough and cold** | Antihistamines, decongestants, cough suppressants, expectorants, saline nasal drops, steam | Most colds are viral and self-limiting – **no antibiotics**; sedating antihistamines cause drowsiness (driving); nasal decongestant sprays only for a few days (rebound congestion); avoid many cough-cold combinations in young children |
| **Acidity and heartburn** | Antacids, alginates, H2 blockers | Antacids interfere with absorption of other drugs – separate doses; refer for persistent symptoms, difficulty swallowing, weight loss |
| **Diarrhoea** | **Oral rehydration salts (ORS)**, zinc for children | **ORS is the main treatment**; antidiarrhoeals (loperamide) not for children, bloody diarrhoea or fever; antibiotics not routinely needed |
| **Constipation** | Bulk laxatives (ispaghula), osmotic laxatives (lactulose), stimulant laxatives | Fibre and fluids first; avoid long-term stimulant laxative use |
| **Allergy** | Non-sedating antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine) | Some drowsiness possible; check other sedatives and alcohol |
| **Skin conditions** | Antiseptics, antifungal creams, calamine, emollients | Avoid self-use of potent steroid creams and steroid–antifungal–antibiotic combinations |
| **Vitamins and minerals** | Multivitamins, iron, calcium | Not a substitute for diet; fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate; iron overdose dangerous to children – store safely |
| **Smoking cessation** | Nicotine replacement (gum, patches, lozenges) where permitted | Counsel on correct use and duration |

**Rational OTC use – general rules:** right product for a correctly identified minor ailment; shortest effective course; read the label; avoid duplication of ingredients; consider interactions with prescribed drugs; special care in children, pregnancy, lactation, the elderly and chronic diseases; and **refer when in doubt**. Misuse of OTC products (laxatives, analgesics, cough syrups containing codeine-like substances, decongestants) should be recognised and handled.

## Remember

- Budget = financial plan; **operating vs capital**; incremental vs **zero-based**; prepare → approve → implement → monitor variance → revise.
- Clinical pharmacy = **patient-focused**; Pharm.D in India from **2008**.
- Drug therapy monitoring: **medication chart review, clinical review, interventions, ward rounds, medication history**.
- **Pharmaceutical care** (Hepler and Strand, 1990) – responsible drug therapy for definite outcomes that improve **quality of life**.
- Renal dosing uses **creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault)**; reduce dose or extend interval.
- Steady state in **4–5 half-lives**; loading dose for long half-life drugs.
- India has **no legal OTC category**; drugs outside Schedules H, H1, X sold without prescription.
- **WWHAM** and red flags for referral; **ORS** first in diarrhoea; watch hidden paracetamol in combinations.
