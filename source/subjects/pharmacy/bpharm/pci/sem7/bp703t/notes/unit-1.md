# Unit 1: Hospital and hospital pharmacy organisation, adverse drug reactions, drug interactions and community pharmacy

This unit sets the scene for the whole subject. It explains **what a hospital is and how it is organised**, where the **hospital pharmacy** fits in, how **adverse drug reactions (ADRs)** and **drug interactions** are classified, detected and reported, and how a **community (retail or wholesale) pharmacy** is set up under Indian law. Long questions often come from hospital pharmacy organisation, ADR classification and reporting, and the legal requirements for opening a drug store.

## 1.1 Hospital and its organisation

### Definition

A **hospital** is an institution that gives **diagnosis, treatment, nursing and rehabilitation** to sick and injured people, usually with **beds for in-patients**, and also serves as a centre for **training** of health workers and for **research**. The World Health Organization describes the hospital as an integral part of the social and medical system whose job is to give the population complete health care, both curative and preventive, and whose outpatient services reach the family in its home.

### Functions of a hospital

1. **Patient care** – diagnosis, medical and surgical treatment, nursing, emergency care.
2. **Prevention of disease and promotion of health** – immunisation, health check-ups, health education.
3. **Education and training** of doctors, nurses, pharmacists and other health workers.
4. **Research** – clinical studies, drug trials, audits.
5. **Rehabilitation** – physiotherapy, occupational therapy, counselling after illness.

### Classification of hospitals

**(a) By level of care (the most commonly asked):**

| Level | What it does | Indian examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | First contact with the health system; basic curative and preventive care, minor ailments, immunisation, maternal and child health, referral | Sub-centres (health and wellness centres), Primary Health Centres (PHC) |
| **Secondary** | Care by specialists in general fields (medicine, surgery, obstetrics, paediatrics); takes referrals from primary level | Community Health Centres (CHC), sub-district and district hospitals |
| **Tertiary** | Highly specialised care, super-specialities, advanced diagnostics, teaching and research; takes referrals from secondary level | Medical college hospitals, AIIMS, regional cancer centres, super-speciality hospitals |

```text
        Tertiary care  (medical college, super-speciality)
              ▲  referral
        Secondary care (CHC, district hospital)
              ▲  referral
        Primary care   (sub-centre, PHC)
              ▲
          Community
```

**(b) Clinical basis** – based on the kind of illness treated:

- **General hospitals** – treat many types of illness in many departments (medicine, surgery, gynaecology, paediatrics, orthopaedics, ENT and others).
- **Special (speciality) hospitals** – treat one disease or one group of patients: TB sanatoria, cancer hospitals, maternity hospitals, children's hospitals, mental (psychiatric) hospitals, eye hospitals, leprosy hospitals, infectious disease hospitals, cardiac or orthopaedic centres.

**(c) Non-clinical basis** – based on features other than the disease:

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership / control | Government (Central, State, local body, defence, railways, ESI), private, voluntary or charitable trust, corporate |
| Teaching | Teaching hospitals (attached to medical colleges), non-teaching hospitals |
| Length of stay | Short-stay (acute care) and long-stay (chronic illness, rehabilitation, psychiatric) |
| Size (bed strength) | Small, medium, large hospitals |
| System of medicine | Allopathic, Ayurvedic, Unani, Siddha, Homoeopathic, Naturopathy |
| Financial basis | Profit-making and non-profit hospitals |

### Organisation structure of a hospital

A hospital is managed by a **governing body** (board of management or trustees) that frames policy. Day-to-day work is run by the **chief executive** – the **Director or Medical Superintendent** – helped by deputy and assistant superintendents.

```text
                    Governing body / Board
                              │
               Director / Medical Superintendent
                              │
   ┌──────────────┬───────────┼──────────────┬────────────────┐
 Medical        Nursing     Paramedical     Administrative   Supportive
 services       services    services        services         services
 (clinical      (nursing    (pharmacy,      (finance,         (kitchen,
 departments)   superint.)  laboratory,     personnel,        laundry, CSSD,
                            radiology,      medical records,  maintenance,
                            physiotherapy)  stores)           security)
```

### Medical staff and their functions

| Staff | Main functions |
| --- | --- |
| Medical Superintendent / Director | Overall administration, policy, discipline, co-ordination of departments |
| Heads of clinical departments (professors, consultants) | Run the department, supervise treatment, teaching and research |
| Consultants / specialists | Diagnose and treat patients in their speciality, perform procedures |
| Resident doctors and house officers | Day-to-day patient care, admission notes, orders, emergencies |
| Nursing superintendent and nurses | Nursing care, giving medicines, observing patients, maintaining ward records |
| Pharmacists | Procurement, storage, dispensing, drug information, counselling, clinical pharmacy services |
| Laboratory and radiology staff | Investigations that guide diagnosis and treatment |
| Dietitian, physiotherapist, medical social worker | Nutrition, rehabilitation, social and financial support |

## 1.2 Hospital pharmacy and its organisation

### Definition

**Hospital pharmacy** is the department or service of a hospital, run by **qualified registered pharmacists**, that **procures, stores, compounds, dispenses and distributes medicines and medical supplies** to in-patients and out-patients, and gives **drug information and clinical pharmacy services** to make drug use safe, effective and economical.

### Functions of the hospital pharmacy

1. Forecasting need, **purchasing** and **inventory control** of drugs and surgical items.
2. **Storage** under correct conditions, including cold storage, controlled drugs and flammables.
3. **Dispensing** to out-patients and **distribution** to wards (unit dose, floor stock and others).
4. **Manufacturing / compounding** – non-sterile preparations, sterile products, IV admixtures, total parenteral nutrition, where facilities exist.
5. **Drug information service** to doctors, nurses and patients.
6. Working with the **Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee**, preparing the **hospital formulary**.
7. **Clinical pharmacy services** – ward rounds, medication chart review, therapeutic drug monitoring, ADR monitoring, patient counselling.
8. **Education and training** of pharmacy students, nurses and junior staff; **research**.
9. Control of **narcotic and psychotropic drugs** and of **investigational drugs**.

### Organisation structure

```text
             Chief Pharmacist / Director of Pharmacy
                              │
        Deputy Chief Pharmacist (administration)
                              │
 ┌──────────┬──────────┬─────────────┬────────────┬─────────────┐
Purchase   Dispensing  Manufacturing  Drug info.   Clinical      Education
& stores   (OPD, IP,   (sterile and   centre       pharmacy      & research
           emergency)  non-sterile)                services
 │            │            │             │            │
 Pharmacists, pharmacy assistants (technicians), store keepers, clerks, helpers
```

The **chief pharmacist** reports to the Medical Superintendent and is a member (often secretary) of the Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee.

### Location

- **Ground floor**, easy to reach for out-patients, close to the **OPD** and **emergency**.
- Good links to **wards** (lifts, corridors) for quick supply.
- Easy access for **vehicles** delivering stock.
- Away from dampness and with room for future expansion.

### Layout

| Area | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Out-patient dispensing area | Counter with queue space, waiting area, privacy corner for counselling |
| In-patient dispensing / unit dose area | Preparation of ward supplies and unit doses |
| Main (bulk) store | Shelving, pallets, separate sections by type of material |
| Cold room / refrigerators | Vaccines, insulin, biologicals at 2–8 °C |
| Controlled drug store | Double-locked cupboard for narcotics and Schedule X drugs |
| Flammable store | Separate, fire-proof, ventilated room for spirits and ether |
| Manufacturing areas | Separate sterile (clean room) and non-sterile sections |
| Drug information centre | References, computers, internet access |
| Office and records | Chief pharmacist, accounts, registers |

### Staff requirements

The number of pharmacists depends on **bed strength, out-patient load, number of shifts and the services offered** (24-hour dispensing, clinical pharmacy, manufacturing). Staff include the **chief pharmacist, deputy and senior pharmacists, pharmacists, pharmacy technicians, storekeepers, clerks and helpers**. Government norms (for example, the Indian Public Health Standards for each level of hospital) fix minimum numbers; learn the principle rather than a single figure, since norms differ.

### Responsibilities and functions of the hospital pharmacist

1. Selection, procurement and storage of quality drugs at the best price.
2. Accurate dispensing with proper labelling and counselling.
3. Maintaining records of purchase, issue and controlled drugs as required by law.
4. Serving on the PTC and helping prepare the formulary.
5. Providing drug information, ADR monitoring and reporting, TDM, medication reviews.
6. Preparing sterile and non-sterile products where needed.
7. Training staff and students, and educating patients.
8. Ensuring rational drug use and controlling drug expenditure.

## 1.3 Adverse drug reactions

### Definition

The WHO defines an **adverse drug reaction** as a response to a drug which is **noxious and unintended** and which occurs at **doses normally used in man** for prophylaxis, diagnosis or therapy of disease, or for modification of physiological function. It is different from an **adverse event** (any untoward occurrence during treatment, not necessarily caused by the drug) and from a **medication error** (a preventable mistake in prescribing, dispensing or administration).

### Classification in the syllabus

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Excessive pharmacological effect** | The known main effect is too strong (dose-related) | Hypoglycaemia with insulin or sulphonylureas; bleeding with anticoagulants; bradycardia with beta-blockers |
| **Secondary pharmacological effect** | A known effect other than the one wanted (side effect) | Dry mouth with anticholinergics; sedation with first-generation antihistamines |
| **Idiosyncrasy** | Unusual, unexpected response not related to the known pharmacology, seen in a few people | Chloramphenicol-associated aplastic anaemia |
| **Allergic (hypersensitivity) reaction** | Immune-mediated; not dose-related; needs earlier sensitisation | Penicillin anaphylaxis, sulphonamide rashes, Stevens–Johnson syndrome |
| **Genetically determined toxicity** | Due to inherited differences in enzymes or receptors | Haemolysis with primaquine or dapsone in G6PD deficiency; prolonged apnoea with succinylcholine in pseudocholinesterase deficiency; isoniazid toxicity in slow acetylators |
| **Toxicity after sudden withdrawal** | Rebound or withdrawal effects when a drug is stopped abruptly | Rebound hypertension after clonidine; adrenal crisis after stopping long-term corticosteroids; seizures after stopping antiepileptics; withdrawal after benzodiazepines or opioids |

### Rawlins and Thompson (extended) classification – useful for answers

| Type | Name | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Augmented | Dose-related, predictable, common, low mortality |
| B | Bizarre | Not dose-related, unpredictable, uncommon, may be serious (allergy, idiosyncrasy) |
| C | Chronic | Related to long-term use (e.g. adrenal suppression with steroids) |
| D | Delayed | Appear after a long time (carcinogenicity, teratogenicity) |
| E | End of use | Withdrawal reactions |
| F | Failure | Unexpected failure of therapy (e.g. due to interaction) |

### Predisposing factors

Age (elderly and neonates), female sex for some reactions, **multiple drugs (polypharmacy)**, renal or hepatic disease, genetic factors, history of allergy, pregnancy, and high doses or long courses.

### Detection and reporting of ADRs

**Methods of detection (pharmacovigilance methods):**

1. **Spontaneous case reports** – doctors, pharmacists, nurses and patients voluntarily report suspected ADRs on a form. Cheap and covers all drugs and all patients; but **under-reporting** is common and incidence cannot be calculated. It is the main way rare reactions are first noticed.
2. **Intensive (prospective) monitoring** – all patients in selected wards are watched closely for ADRs.
3. **Cohort studies** – a group taking the drug is followed and compared with a group not taking it.
4. **Case–control studies** – people with the reaction are compared with people without it for drug exposure; good for rare reactions.
5. **Record linkage studies** – separate records of the same person (prescriptions, hospital admissions, diagnoses, deaths) are linked by computer to find associations between drugs and later events. Large populations can be studied cheaply, but data quality depends on the records.
6. **Post-marketing surveillance (phase IV)** and **literature reports**.

**In India**, the **Pharmacovigilance Programme of India (PvPI)** was started in **2010**, with the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad** as the **National Coordination Centre**. Reports are sent through **ADR Monitoring Centres (AMCs)**, mostly in medical colleges, on the **Suspected Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Form**, or by patients and consumers through the consumer form, the PvPI mobile app or the toll-free helpline **1800-180-3024**. Data go to the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre's global database.

**Steps in managing an ADR in hospital:**

1. **Detect** – suspect an ADR from new symptoms, abnormal lab results or a sudden need for an antidote (for example, naloxone, vitamin K, antihistamine).
2. **Collect information** – patient details, suspected drug(s), dates of start and stop, description of the reaction, other drugs, outcome.
3. **Assess causality** – WHO–UMC scale (certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, unassessable) or **Naranjo algorithm** (10 questions; score **≥9 definite, 5–8 probable, 1–4 possible, ≤0 doubtful**).
4. **Assess severity** (mild, moderate, severe – e.g. Hartwig scale) and **preventability** (e.g. Schumock and Thornton criteria).
5. **Manage** – stop or reduce the suspected drug, treat the reaction, give an alternative.
6. **Document and report** – enter in the case notes and send the form to the AMC/PvPI.
7. **Prevent** – record allergies on the patient's file, educate the patient, and inform prescribers through bulletins.

### Role of the pharmacist in ADR monitoring

Spotting ADRs during chart review and counselling, filling reporting forms, causality assessment, maintaining an ADR database, running an ADR committee, educating staff, and publishing ADR alerts.

## 1.4 Drug interactions

### Definition

A **drug interaction** occurs when the effect of one drug is **changed by the presence of another drug, food, drink or chemical**. The drug whose effect changes is the **object drug**; the one causing the change is the **precipitant drug**.

### Beneficial and adverse interactions

| Beneficial (used on purpose) | Adverse (to be avoided) |
| --- | --- |
| Probenecid delays renal excretion of penicillin, prolonging its action | Warfarin with aspirin or NSAIDs – increased bleeding risk |
| Carbidopa stops peripheral breakdown of levodopa | Rifampicin (enzyme inducer) reduces the effect of oral contraceptives |
| Trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole – sequential blockade | Ketoconazole or erythromycin (enzyme inhibitors) raise levels of drugs metabolised by CYP3A4 |
| Combination antitubercular and antiretroviral therapy delays resistance | Tetracyclines chelate with antacids, iron and milk – poor absorption |
| Adrenaline with local anaesthetics prolongs local action | Alcohol with sedatives – additive CNS depression |

### Mechanisms

**Pharmacokinetic interactions** – one drug changes what the body does to another:

| Stage | Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Absorption | Chelation, change in gastric pH, change in gut motility, binding resins | Tetracycline + calcium/iron; cholestyramine binds many drugs |
| Distribution | Displacement from plasma protein binding | Sulphonamides displacing bilirubin in neonates |
| Metabolism | Enzyme induction or inhibition | Rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine (inducers); cimetidine, ketoconazole, erythromycin (inhibitors) |
| Excretion | Competition for tubular secretion; change in urine pH | Probenecid with penicillin; alkalinising urine speeds salicylate excretion |

**Pharmacodynamic interactions** – drugs act on the same or related sites: **additive or synergistic** (two CNS depressants; two QT-prolonging drugs) or **antagonistic** (beta-agonist with beta-blocker; naloxone with morphine).

**Pharmaceutical (physical/chemical) interactions** – incompatibility outside the body, for example in an IV infusion bag.

### Methods for detecting drug interactions

1. **In vitro studies** – enzyme systems, protein-binding studies.
2. **Animal studies.**
3. **Clinical (human) studies** – pharmacokinetic studies in volunteers or patients.
4. **Spontaneous case reports** of unexpected effects.
5. **Record linkage and epidemiological studies** (cohort, case–control) using prescription and hospital records.
6. **Computerised interaction checking** in dispensing and prescribing software, and standard references (drug interaction compendia, national formularies).

The pharmacist screens every prescription for interactions, judges their clinical importance, advises the prescriber (change of drug, change in timing, monitoring) and counsels the patient.

## 1.5 Community pharmacy

### Definition

A **community pharmacy** is a pharmacy outside the hospital – a **retail chemist** – where medicines are dispensed to the public on prescription and sold over the counter, and where pharmacists give advice on health and medicines. The **wholesale drug store** supplies medicines in bulk to retailers, hospitals and doctors, not to individual patients.

### Types of drug stores

| Type | Features |
| --- | --- |
| Independent (single) pharmacy | Owned and run by one person or a family |
| Chain pharmacies | Several outlets under one management with central purchase |
| Franchise outlets | Brand of a company run by a local owner (including Jan Aushadhi Kendras under PMBJP) |
| Hospital-attached retail pharmacy | Retail outlet within hospital premises |
| Wholesale (distributor/stockist) | Bulk supply to retailers and institutions |
| Online pharmacy | Order through internet; requires a licensed premises and a valid prescription |

### Organisation and design

**Retail drug store:** owner or manager, **registered pharmacist** (compulsory for dispensing), sales assistants, accountant. Design should give a **dispensing area away from public reach**, a **counter**, display shelves for non-prescription products, a **refrigerator**, a **locked cupboard** for Schedule X drugs, a quiet corner for counselling, good lighting and ventilation, and storage away from sunlight and moisture.

**Wholesale drug store:** manager, **competent person** in charge, store keepers, sales and delivery staff, accountant. It needs a large godown, racks, pallets, cold storage and loading space.

```text
 Entrance → Display (OTC, cosmetics, health aids) → Customer counter
                                                       │
         Prescription receiving & counselling ─────────┤
                                                       │
            Dispensing area (prescription drugs, Sch. H, H1) ── Refrigerator
                                                       │
                    Locked cabinet (Sch. X) ── Records & billing ── Back store
```

### Legal requirements for establishing a drug store

Under the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940 and Rules, 1945** (sale is controlled by the **State Licensing Authority / State Drugs Controller**):

1. **Application** to the Licensing Authority with the prescribed fee, plan and ownership documents of the premises.
2. **Licence forms** – retail: **Form 20 and Form 21** (Form 20 for drugs other than those in Schedules C, C1 and X; Form 21 for Schedule C and C1 drugs); wholesale: **Form 20B and 21B**; restricted licence (for general stores in villages, for non-prescription items): **Form 20A and 21A**; Schedule X drugs: **Form 20F (retail) and 20G (wholesale)**. Licence form numbers are revised from time to time; confirm with the current Rules.
3. **Premises** – adequate area (at least **10 m²** for retail or wholesale; **15 m²** when both are run together), clean, dry, well lit and ventilated.
4. **Storage** – a **refrigerator** for drugs needing cold storage, and protection from light, heat and moisture.
5. **Qualified person** – retail sale and dispensing under the personal supervision of a **registered pharmacist**; wholesale under a **competent person** (graduate with experience or a registered pharmacist, as specified).
6. **Display of the licence** and the name of the pharmacist at a conspicuous place.
7. Other registrations – **GST**, the State **Shops and Establishments** registration, and local trade licence.

### Conditions for maintaining a drug store

- Sell **Schedule H, H1 and X** drugs only against a valid prescription of a registered medical practitioner.
- Keep **records** for the period fixed by the Rules.
- Do not sell **expired** or **not-of-standard-quality** drugs; return or destroy them as per rules.
- Allow **inspection** by the Drugs Inspector.
- Renew the licence (licences are now valid till surrendered or cancelled, subject to a **retention fee** every five years).

### Dispensing of proprietary products

**Proprietary (brand) products** are ready-made preparations sold under a manufacturer's trade name. The pharmacist:

1. Reads and checks the prescription (patient, prescriber, drug, strength, dosage form, directions).
2. Selects the **correct brand, strength and dosage form**; checks the **expiry date** and physical condition.
3. Does **not substitute** a brand without the prescriber's or patient's consent, as required by local practice, though generic dispensing is encouraged.
4. **Labels** with patient name, directions and any warning; endorses the prescription.
5. **Counsels** the patient on use, storage and side effects.
6. Records the sale where required (Schedule H1 and X).

### Records of retail and wholesale drug stores

| Record | Retail store | Wholesale store |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchase invoices / bills | Kept for the period required | Kept for the period required |
| Sale bills / cash memos | Copies of bills for prescription drugs | Bills with name and address of purchaser, licence number, batch, expiry |
| Prescription register | Details of prescription drugs dispensed | – |
| **Schedule H1 register** | Name and address of prescriber and patient, drug name, quantity; **kept for three years** | – |
| **Schedule X records** | Prescription kept in duplicate; register maintained; records **kept for two years** | Separate records of purchase and sale |
| NDPS records | Under NDPS Rules for narcotic drugs | Under NDPS Rules |
| Stock register, expiry register, cold-chain temperature log | Good practice | Good practice |

## Remember

- Hospital levels: **primary** (PHC), **secondary** (CHC, district), **tertiary** (medical college, super-speciality).
- Clinical basis = **general vs speciality**; non-clinical = ownership, teaching, length of stay, size, system of medicine.
- Hospital pharmacy sits on the **ground floor near OPD**, with separate cold, controlled and flammable stores.
- Syllabus ADR types: **excessive effect, secondary effect, idiosyncrasy, allergy, genetic, withdrawal**.
- Detection: **spontaneous reports** (cheap, under-reported) and **record linkage** (large linked databases).
- **PvPI 2010**, IPC Ghaziabad; helpline **1800-180-3024**; **Naranjo** ≥9 definite.
- Drug interactions are **pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic or pharmaceutical**; some are **beneficial**.
- Retail licence **Forms 20/21**, wholesale **20B/21B**, Schedule X **20F/20G**; premises **10 m²** (15 m² combined).
- H1 register **3 years**; Schedule X records **2 years**.
