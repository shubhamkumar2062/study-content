# Unit 2: Drug distribution, hospital formulary, therapeutic drug monitoring, medication adherence, medication history interview and community pharmacy management

This unit is about **getting the right drug to the right patient** in hospital (drug distribution systems and the formulary), **checking that therapy works safely** (therapeutic drug monitoring and adherence), **collecting a reliable drug history**, and **running a community pharmacy as a business**. Drug distribution systems – especially the **unit dose system** – and **TDM** are the most frequent long questions.

## 2.1 Drug distribution system in a hospital

### Meaning

The **drug distribution system** is the method by which medicines ordered by the prescriber move from the pharmacy to the patient. A good system makes sure that the **right drug, in the right dose and dosage form, reaches the right patient at the right time**, with little wastage, low cost, control over pilferage and proper records.

### Dispensing of drugs to in-patients – types of distribution systems

**1. Individual prescription order system**

Each patient's medicines are dispensed separately against an individual prescription or order, usually for a few days' supply, and sent to the ward labelled for that patient.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Pharmacist reviews every order | Delay in supply, especially at night |
| Better control of stock and charging | More work for pharmacy staff |
| Fewer drugs lying on the ward | Returns and wastage when therapy changes |

Suited to small and private hospitals.

**2. Complete floor (ward) stock system**

A stock of commonly used drugs is kept on each ward; nurses take doses from it. Two forms:

- **Charged floor stock** – drugs are charged to the patient when used.
- **Non-charged (free) floor stock** – cheap, commonly used items (antiseptics, common tablets, IV fluids) not charged to individual patients.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Drugs available at once; fewer orders to the pharmacy | Pharmacist does not review orders – more medication errors |
| Fewer pharmacy staff needed | Large stock on wards – expiry, pilferage, deterioration |
| Useful in emergencies | Nursing time spent on drug handling |

**3. Combination system** – individual orders for most drugs plus limited floor stock for emergencies and cheap items. Used by most hospitals.

**4. Unit dose drug distribution system (UDDS)**

Medicines are dispensed in **single-unit packages, ready to give**, for **not more than a 24-hour supply** at a time, each dose labelled with drug name, strength, batch and expiry, and placed in the patient's own drawer or cassette.

```text
 Doctor's order → copy to pharmacy → pharmacist checks & enters in patient profile
        → doses packed in unit packs → patient-specific drawer in cart
        → cart sent to ward → nurse gives dose, records it
        → unused doses return to pharmacy and are credited
```

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| **Fewer medication errors**; pharmacist checks every order | High start-up cost (packaging, carts, staff) |
| Less wastage and pilferage; credit for unused doses | Needs more pharmacy staff and 24-hour service |
| Accurate charging per dose | Needs good communication between wards and pharmacy |
| Saves nursing time | |
| Patient medication profile in pharmacy aids monitoring | |

UDDS can be **centralised** (all doses from the main pharmacy) or **decentralised** (from satellite pharmacies on floors).

**5. Automated systems** – **automated dispensing cabinets** on wards, **barcode-based** dispensing and administration, and robotic dispensing in large hospitals.

### Charging policy

Ways of charging patients for drugs:

1. **Per item / per dose charging** – each drug or dose used is charged (UDDS makes this accurate).
2. **Flat (inclusive) charge** – a fixed daily or package charge covers all drugs (common in package-based schemes).
3. **Free supply** – in government hospitals drugs may be given free of cost.
4. Charges usually include the **cost of the drug plus a handling or service charge** fixed by management.

### Labelling

Labels must be clear, typed or printed, and include: **patient name and ward/bed number**, **drug name and strength**, **dosage form and quantity**, **directions** (for out-patients), **date of dispensing**, **expiry**, **auxiliary labels** (shake well, for external use only, store in a refrigerator), and the **name of the pharmacy**.

### Dispensing to ambulatory (out-patient) patients

```text
 Receive prescription → check legality, completeness, appropriateness
   → check interactions / allergies → select, count, pack
   → label → final check by pharmacist → hand over with counselling
   → record and file
```

Good OPD dispensing needs adequate counters, a queue or token system, a **double check** before issue, and a place to counsel.

### Dispensing of controlled drugs

Narcotic and psychotropic drugs (under the **NDPS Act, 1985**) and **Schedule X** drugs need special care:

1. Stored in a **double-locked** cupboard; keys with the pharmacist in charge.
2. Supplied only against a **written, signed and dated prescription** by a registered medical practitioner; for wards, a special requisition signed by the doctor.
3. Entry in a **controlled drug register** – date, patient, prescriber, quantity received, issued and balance.
4. **Empty ampoules** and unused stock returned and accounted for.
5. Regular **stock verification**; any loss reported.

## 2.2 Hospital formulary

### Definition

A **hospital formulary** is a **continually revised compilation of pharmaceuticals** (with important ancillary information) that reflects the **current clinical judgement of the medical staff**, prepared by the **Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee (PTC)**, listing the drugs approved for use in the hospital. It is both a **list** and a **policy document** for rational drug use.

### Contents of a hospital formulary

**Part 1 – Information on hospital policies and procedures:** formulary rules, how to request non-formulary drugs, prescribing rules, controlled-drug rules, automatic stop orders, generic and therapeutic substitution, pharmacy hours and services, ADR reporting procedure.

**Part 2 – Drug products list:**

- Arranged alphabetically and/or by **therapeutic class** (often both, with an index).
- For each drug: generic name, common brand names, dosage forms and strengths available, main uses, precautions, cost class.

**Part 3 – Special information:** abbreviations allowed, metric and household equivalents, calculations, normal laboratory values, antidotes and poison information, nutritional products, sodium content of preparations, drug interactions, list of drugs needing TDM, emergency drug list.

### Formulary versus drug list

| Hospital formulary | Drug list |
| --- | --- |
| Drug names **plus** information on use, precautions, policies | Only names (and strengths) of stocked drugs |
| Prepared by the PTC as a policy tool | May be prepared by stores for purchase |
| Promotes rational prescribing and education | Only a stock and purchase reference |
| Revised regularly with evidence | Updated as stock changes |

### Preparation and revision

1. PTC decides the **format and criteria** (efficacy, safety, cost, need).
2. Pharmacy prepares a **draft** from current usage and reviews.
3. Drafts are circulated to departments for comments.
4. PTC approves; **medical staff and administration** adopt it.
5. Printed and/or electronic copies issued to wards and doctors.
6. **Revised regularly** (often yearly) with supplements in between.

### Addition and deletion of drugs

**Addition:** a doctor submits a **formulary request form** stating drug, indication, advantages over existing drugs, evidence, cost; the pharmacist prepares a **drug monograph** comparing it with current agents; PTC decides; if approved, the drug is added and staff informed.

**Deletion:** drugs are removed when they are **unused or rarely used, outdated, replaced by better or cheaper drugs, banned by the government, or shown to be unsafe**. Duplicates in the same class are reduced.

## 2.3 Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM)

### Definition

**Therapeutic drug monitoring** is the **measurement of drug concentrations in a biological fluid (usually plasma or serum)**, interpreted along with clinical information, to **individualise dosage** so that concentrations remain within the **therapeutic range** – high enough to work but low enough to avoid toxicity.

### Need for TDM – which drugs

TDM is useful when the drug has:

1. A **narrow therapeutic index** (small gap between effective and toxic concentration).
2. **Large inter-individual variation** in pharmacokinetics.
3. A **good relationship between concentration and effect**, but effect is hard to measure clinically.
4. **Non-linear (saturable) kinetics** (e.g. phenytoin).
5. Suspected **toxicity, non-adherence, therapeutic failure or interactions**.
6. Changing patient states – renal or hepatic impairment, pregnancy, children, elderly.

| Group | Examples of drugs commonly monitored |
| --- | --- |
| Antiepileptics | Phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, phenobarbitone |
| Cardiac drugs | Digoxin |
| Mood stabiliser | Lithium |
| Antibiotics | Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin), vancomycin |
| Bronchodilator | Theophylline |
| Immunosuppressants | Cyclosporine, tacrolimus |

Drugs whose effect is easily measured (antihypertensives by blood pressure, antidiabetics by glucose, anticoagulants like warfarin by INR) are monitored by **effect**, not by drug level.

### Factors to be considered during TDM

| Factor | Points |
| --- | --- |
| **Time of sampling** | Usually at **steady state** (after about **4–5 half-lives**); **trough** sample just before the next dose; peak samples for some antibiotics; record the exact time of the last dose and the sample |
| **Sample type and handling** | Plasma or serum; correct tube; avoid haemolysis; proper storage and transport |
| **Assay method** | Validated, sensitive and specific method (immunoassay, HPLC); quality control |
| **Patient factors** | Age, weight, renal and hepatic function, disease state, pregnancy, genetics, smoking |
| **Drug factors** | Formulation, dose, route, protein binding (free vs total level), active metabolites |
| **Interactions and adherence** | Other drugs changing levels; whether doses were actually taken |
| **Clinical response** | Treat the patient, not the number; a level is interpreted with signs and symptoms |
| **Communication** | Clear report with interpretation and dose advice to the prescriber |

```text
 Indication for TDM → plan sampling time → collect sample with full details
   → laboratory assay → pharmacokinetic interpretation
   → recommendation to prescriber → dose adjusted → follow-up level / response
```

### Indian scenario

- TDM services are available mainly in **tertiary and teaching hospitals** and a few private laboratories; most district hospitals have none.
- Limits: **cost of assays and equipment**, few trained clinical pharmacists and pharmacologists, lack of awareness among prescribers, long reporting times, and patients' inability to pay.
- Departments of pharmacology in medical colleges and some clinical pharmacy departments run TDM, mainly for antiepileptics, lithium, digoxin and immunosuppressants.
- Growth of **Pharm.D programmes**, clinical pharmacy services and accredited laboratories is expanding the service.

## 2.4 Medication adherence

### Definitions

**Adherence** is the extent to which a patient's behaviour – taking medicines, following diet or changing lifestyle – **matches the recommendations agreed with the health-care provider**. The term replaced "compliance", which suggested passive obedience. **Non-adherence** may be **intentional** (patient decides not to take) or **unintentional** (forgets, cannot afford, cannot understand).

### Causes of medication non-adherence (WHO's five dimensions)

| Dimension | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| **Patient-related** | Forgetfulness, poor understanding, wrong beliefs, fear of side effects, depression, cognitive impairment, low literacy |
| **Condition-related** | Chronic, symptom-free disease (hypertension, diabetes), feeling better, severity of illness |
| **Therapy-related** | Complex regimens, many drugs, frequent doses, long duration, side effects, bad taste, difficult devices |
| **Health-system related** | Short consultations, poor communication, lack of counselling, poor follow-up, drug shortages |
| **Socio-economic** | Cost, distance from pharmacy, poverty, lack of family support, cultural beliefs |

### Pharmacist's role in improving adherence

1. **Counselling** – explain purpose, benefit, how and when to take, what to expect.
2. **Simplifying the regimen** with the prescriber – once-daily forms, fixed-dose combinations.
3. **Adherence aids** – pill boxes, calendars, blister/compliance packs, mobile reminders.
4. **Written information** in the patient's language, pictograms for low-literacy patients.
5. **Managing side effects** and addressing fears.
6. **Involving family** and caregivers.
7. **Follow-up**, refill reminders and medication reviews.

### Monitoring medication adherence

| Method | Type | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Patient interview / self-report | Indirect | Simple; tends to overestimate |
| Structured questionnaires (e.g. Morisky scale) | Indirect | Standard, quick scoring |
| **Pill count** | Indirect | Count remaining dosage units at visit |
| **Refill / pharmacy records** | Indirect | Gaps in refills show non-adherence |
| Electronic monitoring (pill bottle caps recording opening) | Indirect | Accurate pattern; costly |
| Clinical response | Indirect | Influenced by other factors |
| **Drug or metabolite levels** in blood or urine; markers | **Direct** | Objective but costly and only shows recent intake |
| Directly observed therapy (e.g. TB programmes) | Direct | Most reliable; labour-intensive |

## 2.5 Patient medication history interview

### Meaning

A **medication history** is a detailed, accurate record of **all medicines a patient is taking or has taken recently**, including prescription and non-prescription drugs, herbal and traditional remedies, supplements, and **allergies and previous ADRs**. The **medication history interview** is the structured conversation by which the pharmacist collects it, usually on admission.

### Need (importance)

1. Detects **drug-related problems** – ADRs, interactions, duplication, non-adherence, drug-induced illness behind the admission.
2. Records **allergies** and previous reactions.
3. Supports **medication reconciliation** – matching home medicines with admission orders, preventing omissions and errors at transfer and discharge.
4. Helps the doctor choose therapy and the pharmacist plan monitoring.
5. Gives baseline information on **adherence and beliefs**.
6. Builds rapport for later counselling.

### Conducting the interview

1. **Prepare** – review case notes, choose a quiet time and place.
2. **Introduce** yourself and explain the purpose; take consent.
3. Use **open questions** first, then specific ones.
4. Ask about **each medicine**: name, strength, how taken, since when, for what, who prescribed, any problem.
5. Ask about **OTC, herbal, Ayurvedic, homoeopathic** products and supplements.
6. Ask about **allergies** and **past ADRs** (what happened).
7. Ask about **social habits** – alcohol, tobacco, diet.
8. Check **adherence** in a non-judgemental way.
9. **Verify** with other sources – medicine containers brought, previous prescriptions, family, the patient's pharmacy or GP.
10. **Document** on the form and communicate discrepancies to the doctor.

### Medication history interview form – typical contents

| Section | Details |
| --- | --- |
| Patient details | Name, age, sex, IP number, ward, weight, admitting diagnosis |
| Sources of information | Patient, relative, prescriptions, containers |
| Current medicines | Drug, strength, dose and frequency as taken, duration, indication |
| Past medicines | Recently stopped drugs and reason |
| Non-prescription / herbal products | Name and use |
| Allergies / ADR history | Drug and nature of reaction |
| Social history | Alcohol, smoking, tobacco chewing, diet |
| Adherence assessment | Missed doses, reasons |
| Discrepancies found | Action taken, communicated to whom |
| Pharmacist's signature and date | |

## 2.6 Community pharmacy management

Running a community pharmacy well needs good management of four resources:

### Financial requirements

- **Fixed capital** – premises (purchase or rent deposit), furniture, fixtures, refrigerator, computer and software.
- **Working capital** – initial stock, salaries, rent, electricity, licence fees.
- **Sources of finance** – own savings, partners, **bank loans**, government schemes for small businesses.
- Financial control – daily cash book, sales and purchase records, credit control, **GST** accounts, profit and loss account and balance sheet; keeping stock lean to avoid blocked capital.

### Materials requirements

- Stock matched to **local demand** and prescriptions of nearby doctors.
- Reliable suppliers; **purchase planning** to avoid both shortage and overstocking.
- **First-expiry, first-out (FEFO)** issue; regular **expiry checks**; return of near-expiry items.
- Correct storage (cold chain, protection from light and moisture).
- Computerised stock control, reorder levels.

### Staff requirements

- **Registered pharmacist** must be present for dispensing.
- Supporting staff – sales assistants, accountant, delivery person.
- **Training** in dispensing practice, customer service, software and legal rules.
- Clear duties, work schedules, uniforms and identity badges.

### Infrastructure requirements

- Premises of required **area**, in a suitable location (near hospitals, clinics, markets).
- Furniture – counters, shelves, glass display cases, **locked cabinets**.
- **Refrigerator**, power back-up, temperature records.
- Computer with billing and inventory software, internet.
- Signboard with name and licence details; a **counselling area**; reference books; fire safety.

## Remember

- Distribution systems: **individual prescription, floor stock (charged/non-charged), combination, unit dose, automated**.
- **UDDS** = single unit, ready to give, **≤24-hour supply**, fewer errors, credit for returns.
- Controlled drugs: **double lock, written order, register, return empties**.
- Formulary = **policies + drug list + special information**; made by the **PTC**; revised regularly.
- TDM: narrow therapeutic index, variable kinetics; sample at **steady state (4–5 half-lives)**, usually **trough**.
- Adherence barriers follow **five WHO dimensions**; pill count, refill records, questionnaires, drug levels.
- Medication history supports **medication reconciliation**; always ask about OTC and herbal products and allergies.
- Community pharmacy management = **finance, materials, staff, infrastructure**.
