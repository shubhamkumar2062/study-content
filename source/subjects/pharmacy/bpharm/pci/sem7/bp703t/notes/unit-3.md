# Unit 3: Pharmacy and therapeutics committee, drug information services, patient counselling, education and training, prescriptions and communication skills

This unit covers the **committee that controls drug use in a hospital**, the **drug and poison information service**, **patient counselling**, the pharmacist's role in **education, training and community health**, the **code of ethics**, and how to **interpret a prescription** and **communicate** with prescribers and patients. The PTC, drug information sources and the steps of patient counselling are the favourite long questions.

## 3.1 Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee (PTC)

### Definition

The **Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee** is an **advisory committee of the medical staff** that acts as the **link between the medical staff and the pharmacy**. It develops **policies on selection, procurement, prescribing, distribution and safe use of drugs** in the hospital and evaluates their use.

### Organisation (composition)

| Member | Role in committee |
| --- | --- |
| Senior physicians from major departments (medicine, surgery, paediatrics, obstetrics, anaesthesia and others) | Members; one acts as **chairman** |
| **Chief pharmacist** | Usually **secretary** – prepares agenda, minutes, drug monographs |
| Nursing superintendent / senior nurse | Member – nursing practice aspects |
| Hospital administrator / medical superintendent | Member, often ex officio |
| Clinical pharmacologist, microbiologist, clinical pharmacist | Members or invited experts |

- The committee meets **regularly** (for example monthly or every two months) and records **minutes**.
- Recommendations go to the **medical staff / administration** for approval.
- Sub-committees may handle **antimicrobial stewardship**, **ADR monitoring** and **formulary** matters.

### Functions of the PTC

1. **Advisory** – advises medical staff and administration on all matters of drug use.
2. **Educational** – programmes and bulletins on new drugs, rational use, ADRs.
3. Preparing and revising the **hospital formulary**.
4. Framing **policies and procedures** on drug use, prescribing and administration.
5. Evaluating new drugs and approving **additions and deletions**.
6. Studying **drug use** through **drug utilisation reviews**, prescription audits and antibiotic policies.
7. Running **ADR monitoring and medication error** programmes.
8. Controlling **investigational drugs** together with the ethics committee.
9. Helping to **reduce drug costs** while keeping quality.

### Policies of the PTC

**(a) Including drugs in the formulary** – drugs chosen on **proven efficacy, safety, need, quality and cost**; generic names preferred; duplicates avoided; requests through a formal form and monograph (see Unit 2).

**(b) In-patient and out-patient prescriptions**

- Prescriptions to be **legible**, preferably by **generic name**, with dose, route, frequency and duration.
- Only **formulary drugs** normally; non-formulary drugs on a special request.
- Rules on **verbal and telephone orders** (to be confirmed in writing within a set time).
- Who may prescribe (consultants, residents) and what quantities may be given to out-patients.
- Rules for **controlled drugs**, **antimicrobials** (restricted antibiotics need approval) and **look-alike, sound-alike** drugs.

**(c) Automatic stop order (ASO)**

A policy that certain drugs **are automatically stopped after a set period** unless the prescriber **re-orders** them. It prevents drugs being continued longer than needed. Drugs usually covered: **narcotics and sedatives, antimicrobials, anticoagulants, corticosteroids, potent IV drugs**. The time limit for each class is fixed by the committee. The pharmacist or nurse informs the prescriber before the stop time so therapy is not interrupted unintentionally.

**(d) Emergency drug list**

A list of drugs that must be **available at once** in emergency areas, operation theatres, ICUs and on every ward (in the **crash cart / emergency tray**). Prepared by the PTC with emergency physicians and anaesthetists. Typical groups:

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiac arrest and shock | Adrenaline, atropine, amiodarone, vasopressors |
| Anaphylaxis | Adrenaline, antihistamine, hydrocortisone |
| Hypoglycaemia | Dextrose injection, glucagon |
| Seizures | Diazepam or midazolam, phenytoin |
| Poisoning | Naloxone, atropine, activated charcoal |
| Fluids | Normal saline, Ringer lactate |

The pharmacist **checks the trays regularly**, replaces used items, removes expired items, and seals the cart.

## 3.2 Drug information services

### Definitions

- **Drug information (DI)** is current, critically evaluated and unbiased information about drugs and their use.
- A **Drug Information Centre (DIC)** is a dedicated place, staffed by trained pharmacists, which **answers drug-related questions** (queries) from health professionals and the public, and runs educational and research activities.
- A **Poison Information Centre (PIC)** gives information on **diagnosis and management of poisoning** (drugs, pesticides, household chemicals, bites and stings), usually round the clock. In India, the **National Poisons Information Centre** is at **AIIMS, New Delhi**; several medical colleges and pharmacy colleges also run DICs and PICs.

### Functions of a DIC

1. Answering queries – dosage forms, ADRs, interactions, drug use in pregnancy and lactation, compatibility, identification of tablets, poisoning.
2. Publishing **drug bulletins, newsletters and alerts**.
3. Supporting the **PTC** with drug monographs and evaluations.
4. Supporting **ADR monitoring** and **medication safety**.
5. **Education** of staff, students and patients.
6. **Research** – drug utilisation studies.

### Sources of drug information

| Type | What it is | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary** | Original research articles and reports | Journal articles – *British Medical Journal*, *The Lancet*, *New England Journal of Medicine*, *Indian Journal of Pharmacology*, *Journal of Pharmacy Practice* |
| **Secondary** | Indexing and abstracting services that lead to primary literature | MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, International Pharmaceutical Abstracts, Cochrane Library |
| **Tertiary** | Compiled, reviewed information in textbooks and compendia | Martindale, Indian Pharmacopoeia, National Formulary of India, British National Formulary, AHFS Drug Information, Goodman and Gilman, drug interaction references, CIMS / MIMS / IDR (Indian drug indexes), package inserts |
| **Other** | | Pharmaceutical companies' medical information departments, regulatory sites (CDSCO, WHO, US FDA), poison centres, experts |

**Tertiary sources** are quick and easy but may be out of date; **primary sources** are the most current but need critical evaluation.

### Computerised services

- **Online databases** (PubMed, Cochrane, Micromedex, Lexicomp and similar subscription databases).
- **CD-ROM / downloadable** versions of compendia.
- **Hospital information systems** with drug interaction and allergy checking.
- Websites of regulators and WHO; mobile drug reference applications.
- E-mail and electronic newsletters for disseminating information.

### Systematic approach to answering a query

1. **Receive the query** and note requester's details (name, profession, contact) – **demographics**.
2. **Obtain background information** – patient age, weight, condition, other drugs, urgency.
3. **Determine and categorise the real (ultimate) question.**
4. **Develop a search strategy** – tertiary → secondary → primary sources.
5. **Evaluate, analyse and synthesise** the information found.
6. **Formulate and give the response** – clear, referenced, in time.
7. **Follow up and document** the query and answer.

### Storage and retrieval of information

- Queries and answers are **documented** on query forms or in a computer database for audit and re-use.
- Literature is filed systematically – by **drug name, therapeutic class or subject**, using a **classification or coding system** and keyword indexing.
- Old methods – card files, vertical files, microfilm; modern methods – **electronic databases**, scanned documents, reference-management software.
- Regular **updating and weeding** of outdated material.

## 3.3 Patient counselling

### Definition

**Patient counselling** is a **two-way process** in which the pharmacist gives the patient (or caregiver) **information, advice and assistance** about their medicines and disease, so that they **understand and use medicines correctly** and achieve the best outcome. It is more than handing out information – the pharmacist also listens and checks understanding.

### Aims

Better **adherence**, fewer **ADRs and errors**, better **self-management** of disease, and a stronger patient–pharmacist relationship.

### Steps involved in patient counselling

```text
 1. Preparation  →  2. Opening  →  3. Content (information)
        →  4. Process (check understanding)  →  5. Closing  →  6. Documentation
```

| Step | What the pharmacist does |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Preparation** | Review the prescription and patient record; check for problems; prepare leaflets, devices; choose a private place |
| **2. Opening the session** | Greet, introduce self, confirm patient identity, explain purpose and time needed, gain trust |
| **3. Content** | Name and purpose of each drug; how much, how often, how long; how to take (with food, swallow whole); special techniques (inhaler, insulin pen, eye drops); common side effects and what to do; precautions and interactions (alcohol, OTC drugs); storage; what to do if a dose is missed; lifestyle and diet advice |
| **4. Process** | Use **open questions**; ask the patient to **repeat or demonstrate** (teach-back, show-and-tell); answer doubts; address barriers |
| **5. Closing** | Summarise key points, check understanding, give written information, explain follow-up and how to contact the pharmacist |
| **6. Documentation** | Record what was counselled and any problems referred |

**Three prime questions** – a simple way to find out what the patient already knows:

1. What did your doctor tell you this medicine is for?
2. How did your doctor tell you to take it?
3. What did your doctor tell you to expect?

### Barriers to counselling

Lack of time and privacy, language differences, low literacy, hearing or visual problems, anxiety or illness, patient's attitude, and the pharmacist's own poor communication skills.

### Special cases that require the pharmacist's attention

| Patient group | Points to stress |
| --- | --- |
| **Children** | Counsel parents; correct measuring device; flavoured forms; storage away from reach |
| **Elderly** | Many drugs, memory problems, poor vision and hearing; large-print labels, pill boxes, simple regimens |
| **Pregnancy and lactation** | Safety of drugs; avoid self-medication; folic acid, iron |
| **Chronic diseases** (diabetes, hypertension, asthma, epilepsy, TB, HIV) | Long-term adherence, monitoring, device technique, lifestyle |
| **Narrow-therapeutic-index drugs** | Regular monitoring, warning signs of toxicity |
| **Special dosage forms and devices** | Inhalers, spacers, insulin pens, eye drops, suppositories, transdermal patches |
| **Low literacy, language barriers** | Pictograms, local language, involve family |
| **Patients with disabilities** | Large print, sign aids, caregiver involvement |
| **Terminally ill / psychiatric patients** | Sensitivity, caregiver counselling, safe custody of drugs |

## 3.4 Education and training programme in the hospital

### Role of pharmacist

The pharmacist educates **other health professionals, students and patients** about drugs and trains pharmacy staff.

### Internal training programmes

For staff within the hospital:

- In-service training for **pharmacists and technicians** (new procedures, software, aseptic techniques).
- Lectures to **nurses** on drug administration, storage, calculations, IV compatibility, ADR reporting.
- **Clinical meetings** and journal clubs with doctors.
- Orientation of new staff.

### External training programmes

- **Internship / practical training** for B.Pharm, D.Pharm and Pharm.D students.
- **Continuing education** for community pharmacists.
- Workshops and seminars with professional bodies.
- Training of staff from other hospitals and health centres.

### Services to nursing homes and clinics

Small nursing homes and clinics often have no pharmacist. A hospital or community pharmacist can **supply drugs**, give **drug information**, advise on **storage and expiry control**, review **medication charts**, train nursing staff and help with **record keeping and legal requirements**.

### Code of ethics for community pharmacy

The PCI's **Code of Pharmaceutical Ethics** (also reflected in the **Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015**) describes the pharmacist's duties:

| In relation to | Main points |
| --- | --- |
| **His/her job** | Maintain the pharmacy properly, follow the law, keep proper records, handle prescriptions with care, do not substitute without permission, refuse to supply drugs likely to be misused, keep professional secrets |
| **His/her trade** | Fair pricing, no misleading advertisement, no hawking of drugs, honest dealing with suppliers |
| **The medical profession** | Do not diagnose or prescribe beyond the pharmacist's role; respect the prescriber; contact the prescriber about errors without criticising them before the patient; no secret arrangements (kickbacks) with doctors |
| **His/her profession** | Uphold dignity of the profession, keep up to date, help colleagues, join professional associations |
| **The public** | Put patient welfare first, give honest information, maintain confidentiality, serve in emergencies |

### Interdepartmental communication

The pharmacy works with **wards, nursing, laboratory, finance, stores, medical records and administration**. Good communication includes clear **written procedures**, **requisition and return forms**, **newsletters and circulars**, **meetings and committees** (PTC, infection control), and **computerised hospital information systems**. The pharmacist explains new policies, shortages and substitutions promptly.

### Community health education

The pharmacist is often the most accessible health professional. Roles:

- Health education on **hygiene, nutrition, family planning, immunisation, oral health**.
- Awareness of **communicable diseases** (TB, malaria, HIV) and **non-communicable diseases** (diabetes, hypertension, cancer).
- **Screening** – blood pressure, blood glucose, body weight.
- **Smoking and tobacco cessation**, alcohol and drug abuse prevention.
- **Rational use of medicines** and avoiding antibiotic misuse.
- Supporting **national health programmes** and campaigns.

## 3.5 Prescribed medication order and communication skills

### Prescription and medication order

A **prescription** is a written order from a **registered medical practitioner (RMP)** to the pharmacist to supply medicines for a patient. In hospital, the doctor's order on the in-patient chart is called a **medication order**.

### Parts of a prescription

| Part | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| Prescriber's details | Name, qualification, registration number, address, contact |
| Date | Date of writing |
| Patient's details | Name, age, sex, address; weight for children |
| **Superscription** | The symbol **Rx** ("take thou") |
| **Inscription** | Name and strength of the drug(s) – the main part |
| **Subscription** | Instructions to the pharmacist – dosage form and quantity to supply |
| **Signatura** | Directions for the patient (abbreviated "Sig.") |
| Renewal instructions, prescriber's signature | |

### Interpretation of the prescription – checks by the pharmacist

1. **Legality** – genuine prescriber, signature, date, registration number.
2. **Completeness** – patient and drug details, quantity, directions.
3. **Appropriateness** – drug, dose, frequency, route and duration suit the patient (age, weight, kidney and liver function, pregnancy).
4. **Interactions, allergies, duplications, contraindications.**
5. **Legibility and abbreviations** – avoid ambiguous ones; clarify any doubt with the prescriber, never guess.
6. **Translate Latin abbreviations** correctly:

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| a.c. / p.c. | Before food / after food |
| b.d. (b.i.d.) / t.d.s. (t.i.d.) / q.i.d. | Twice / three times / four times a day |
| o.d. | Once a day |
| h.s. | At bedtime |
| s.o.s. / p.r.n. | When required |
| stat | Immediately |
| p.o. | By mouth |

### Legal requirements (Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945)

- **Schedule H, H1 and X** drugs are sold only on the prescription of an **RMP**.
- A prescription must be **in writing, signed and dated** by the RMP, with **name and address of the patient** and the **total amount** of drug to be supplied (and, for Schedule X, the prescription kept by the pharmacist).
- The pharmacist **stamps or endorses** the prescription with name and address of the pharmacy and date of supply, so it is not reused.
- Records are kept as required (H1 register; Schedule X records).
- Narcotic and psychotropic drugs also follow the **NDPS Act and Rules**.
- The National Medical Commission's regulations ask doctors to **prescribe by generic name, legibly**.

### Communication skills

**Communication** is the exchange of information and understanding between people. It has a **sender, message, channel, receiver and feedback**.

```text
 Sender ──(encodes message)──► Channel ──► Receiver (decodes)
    ▲                                            │
    └──────────────── Feedback ◄─────────────────┘
```

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Verbal | Speaking, tone, choice of words |
| Non-verbal | Eye contact, posture, facial expression, gestures, distance |
| Written | Labels, leaflets, notes, letters |
| Listening | Attentive and **active listening** – paraphrasing, summarising, empathy |

**Barriers:** noise, lack of privacy, language, jargon, emotional state, hearing loss, time pressure, cultural differences, prejudice.

**Communicating with prescribers**

- Be **prepared** – know the patient, the problem and possible solutions.
- Be **brief, polite and evidence-based**; suggest alternatives rather than only pointing out errors.
- Use a structured format, for example: **situation → background → assessment → recommendation**.
- **Document** the intervention and the prescriber's response.

**Communicating with patients**

- Greet warmly, introduce yourself, ensure privacy.
- Use **simple language**, avoid jargon, use the patient's language.
- Ask **open-ended questions**, listen actively, show **empathy**.
- Check understanding by **teach-back**.
- Respect culture, beliefs and confidentiality.

## Remember

- PTC = **advisory link** between medical staff and pharmacy; chairman a physician, **secretary the chief pharmacist**.
- PTC policies: **formulary inclusion, prescriptions, automatic stop orders, emergency drug list**.
- Information sources: **primary** (journals), **secondary** (PubMed, IPA), **tertiary** (Martindale, IP, NFI, BNF).
- Answering queries: **demographics → background → real question → search → evaluate → respond → follow-up**.
- Counselling: **preparation, opening, content, process, closing, documentation**; use the **three prime questions** and teach-back.
- Code of ethics: pharmacist in relation to **job, trade, medical profession, profession, public**.
- Prescription parts: **superscription (Rx), inscription, subscription, signatura**.
- Communication: **sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback**; active listening; structured talk with prescribers.
