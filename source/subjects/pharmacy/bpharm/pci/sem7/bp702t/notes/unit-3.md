# Unit 3: Regulatory affairs and regulatory requirements for drug approval

No medicine can be sold until a government regulator is satisfied that it is safe, effective and of good quality. **Regulatory affairs (RA)** is the profession that sits between the company and the regulator: it plans what data are needed, collects them from every department, presents them in the required format and keeps the product compliant for as long as it is on the market.

This unit has two halves: regulatory affairs as a function (Part A) and the data and documents needed to get a new drug approved, using the US FDA system named in the syllabus as the model (Part B).

## Part A: Regulatory affairs

## 3.1 Introduction and definition

**Regulatory affairs** is the function in a pharmaceutical company that ensures its products, processes and activities comply with the laws, regulations and guidelines of every country where it operates, from early development through approval to post-marketing.

Scope of RA:

1. Drug development strategy from a regulatory point of view.
2. Preparation and submission of applications (clinical trial, marketing, variations, renewals).
3. Communication with regulators (meetings, queries, inspections).
4. Labelling and promotional material review.
5. Post-approval changes and life-cycle management.
6. Pharmacovigilance and safety reporting support.
7. Keeping track of new laws and guidelines (regulatory intelligence).

## 3.2 Historical overview of regulatory affairs

Most drug laws were written after a disaster showed that the earlier law was not enough.

| Year | Event | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1906 | USA: unsafe and mislabelled foods and drugs | Pure Food and Drugs Act — banned adulterated and misbranded products |
| 1937–38 | USA: "Elixir Sulfanilamide" made with diethylene glycol killed over a hundred people | Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act 1938 — proof of **safety** required before marketing |
| Late 1950s–1962 | Thalidomide, used in pregnancy, caused severe limb defects in thousands of babies, mainly in Europe | USA: Kefauver–Harris Amendments 1962 — proof of **efficacy** as well as safety; informed consent; GMP |
| 1964 | World Medical Association | Declaration of Helsinki — ethics of research on humans |
| 1984 | USA | Hatch–Waxman Act — Abbreviated NDA (ANDA) route for generics, patent term restoration |
| 1990 | Regulators and industry of USA, EU and Japan | International Council (then Conference) for Harmonisation, ICH — common guidelines (Q, S, E, M series) |
| 1995 | Europe | European Medicines Agency (EMA, first called EMEA) begins work |

**India**

| Year | Law |
| --- | --- |
| 1940 | Drugs and Cosmetics Act (originally the Drugs Act) |
| 1945 | Drugs and Cosmetics Rules |
| 1948 | Pharmacy Act |
| 1954 | Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act |
| 1985 | Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act |
| 2019 | New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules (replaced the earlier Schedule Y provisions for new drugs and trials) |

## 3.4 Role of the regulatory affairs department

```text
         R&D ──┐
     Clinical ─┤
   Production ─┤──→  REGULATORY AFFAIRS  ──→  Regulators (CDSCO, FDA, EMA …)
     QA / QC ──┤          ↑      │
   Marketing ──┘   queries│      │approvals, conditions
                          └──────┘
```

Roles:

1. **Strategy:** advise management which markets, which route (new drug, generic, 505(b)(2)), what studies are needed and how long approval may take.
2. **Dossier preparation:** collect, check and compile data in CTD/eCTD format.
3. **Submission and follow-up:** file applications, answer queries, attend meetings.
4. **Labelling:** prepare and check package inserts, labels and patient information.
5. **Life-cycle management:** variations, renewals, new indications, new strengths.
6. **Compliance:** check that manufacturing and promotion follow approved conditions; support inspections.
7. **Regulatory intelligence:** watch new rules and inform departments.
8. **Training** of staff on regulatory requirements.

## 3.5 Responsibilities of a regulatory affairs professional

- Know current regulations and guidelines of the target markets.
- Plan and track submissions and timelines.
- Ensure data are complete, accurate and consistent across modules.
- Write clear summaries and responses to queries.
- Maintain records of all submissions and approvals.
- Review change controls for regulatory impact.
- Review labels and promotional material.
- Communicate honestly with regulators and keep confidential information safe.
- Support pharmacovigilance reporting.
- Skills needed: scientific knowledge, attention to detail, writing, negotiation, project management.

## Part B: Regulatory requirements for drug approval

## 3.6 Overview of drug development

```text
Discovery → Preclinical (non-clinical) → IND → Phase I → Phase II → Phase III
          → NDA / marketing application → Review and approval → Phase IV (post-marketing)
```

It commonly takes 10–15 years from discovery to approval, and only a small fraction of molecules entering trials are finally approved.

## 3.7 Drug development teams

Drug development is done by cross-functional teams:

| Team member | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Discovery / medicinal chemists | Design and synthesis of the molecule |
| Pharmacologists | Efficacy and mechanism studies |
| Toxicologists | Safety studies in animals |
| DMPK scientists | Absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion |
| Formulation scientists | Dosage form development |
| Analytical scientists | Methods, specifications, stability |
| Process chemists and engineers | Scale-up of API and product |
| Clinical team (physicians, clinical research associates) | Design and conduct of trials |
| Biostatisticians and data managers | Study design, analysis, data quality |
| Regulatory affairs | Strategy and submissions |
| QA | Compliance with GLP, GCP, GMP |
| Project manager | Timelines, budget, coordination |

## 3.8 Non-clinical drug development

Non-clinical (preclinical) studies are done in vitro and in animals before and alongside human trials, under **Good Laboratory Practice (GLP)**.

### a. Pharmacology

- **Primary pharmacodynamics:** effect on the intended target and in disease models.
- **Secondary pharmacodynamics:** effects on other targets.
- **Safety pharmacology:** effects on vital systems — cardiovascular (including QT prolongation, hERG assay), central nervous system and respiratory system (the "core battery").

### b. Drug metabolism and pharmacokinetics

- Absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME) in animal species.
- Metabolic pathways and metabolites, enzymes involved (CYP450), enzyme induction or inhibition.
- Plasma protein binding, tissue distribution.
- **Toxicokinetics:** drug exposure in toxicity studies, to relate toxicity to exposure.

### c. Toxicology

| Study | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Single-dose (acute) toxicity | Effects after one dose; helps choose doses for longer studies |
| Repeated-dose toxicity (subacute, subchronic, chronic) | Target organs and no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL); duration linked to proposed trial duration |
| Genotoxicity | Mutations and chromosome damage (Ames test, chromosomal aberration, micronucleus) |
| Carcinogenicity | For drugs taken long term |
| Reproductive and developmental toxicity | Fertility, embryo–fetal development, pre- and post-natal development |
| Local tolerance | Irritation at the site of application or injection |
| Immunotoxicity, special studies | Where needed |

Results are used to choose a safe starting dose for the first human study and to decide what to monitor.

## 3.9 Investigational New Drug (IND) application

An **IND** is the application to the US FDA for permission to give an unapproved drug to humans and to ship it across state lines for clinical trials. (Rules are in the US Code of Federal Regulations, 21 CFR Part 312.)

**Types of IND**

1. **Investigator IND:** filed by a physician who starts and conducts a study himself.
2. **Emergency use IND:** for urgent use in a situation that does not allow time for a normal application.
3. **Treatment IND:** for promising drugs for serious or life-threatening conditions while final trials are going on.
4. **Commercial IND:** filed by a company planning to market the drug.

**General considerations / contents**

1. Cover form (Form FDA 1571) and table of contents.
2. Introductory statement and general investigational plan.
3. Investigator's Brochure.
4. Clinical protocols for the planned studies.
5. Chemistry, manufacturing and controls (CMC) information.
6. Pharmacology and toxicology data.
7. Previous human experience, if any.
8. Statement of the investigator (Form FDA 1572) and other information.

If the FDA does not place a clinical hold within 30 days of receiving the IND, the trial may begin. During the IND the sponsor files protocol amendments, information amendments, safety reports and annual reports.

## 3.10 Investigator's Brochure (IB)

The **Investigator's Brochure** is a compilation of the clinical and non-clinical data on the investigational product that are relevant to its study in humans. It helps investigators understand the drug, the reasons for the protocol, and how to manage the subjects safely. (Its contents are described in ICH E6, Good Clinical Practice.)

Contents:

1. Title page and confidentiality statement.
2. Summary.
3. Introduction.
4. Physical, chemical and pharmaceutical properties and formulation.
5. Non-clinical studies (pharmacology, pharmacokinetics, toxicology).
6. Effects in humans (pharmacokinetics, safety, efficacy, marketing experience if any).
7. Summary of data and guidance for the investigator (risks, adverse reactions expected, precautions, monitoring).

The IB is reviewed and updated at least once a year and when important new information appears.

## 3.11 New Drug Application (NDA)

An **NDA** is the application to the US FDA to approve a new drug for marketing. It must show that the drug is safe and effective for the proposed use, that its benefits outweigh its risks, that the labelling is appropriate, and that manufacturing methods and controls maintain its identity, strength, quality and purity. (Rules in 21 CFR Part 314; the application form is Form FDA 356h.)

**Application routes in the USA**

| Route | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| 505(b)(1) "full" NDA | New molecule with full safety and efficacy data from the applicant's own studies |
| 505(b)(2) NDA | New product relying partly on published data or on FDA's earlier findings (new salt, dosage form, combination) |
| 505(j) ANDA | Generic copy shown to be bioequivalent to the reference listed drug; no new clinical efficacy trials |
| BLA | Biological products (under the Public Health Service Act) |

**NDA content (in CTD format)**

1. Administrative information, forms and proposed labelling.
2. Summaries (quality, non-clinical, clinical).
3. Chemistry, manufacturing and controls.
4. Non-clinical pharmacology and toxicology reports.
5. Human pharmacokinetics and bioavailability.
6. Clinical study reports (efficacy and safety), statistics.
7. Patent information, financial disclosure of investigators.
8. Samples, methods validation package, case report forms and tabulations.

**Review:** the FDA checks completeness (filing), reviews each discipline, may inspect manufacturing sites and clinical sites, may consult an advisory committee, and then issues an approval letter or a complete response letter listing deficiencies. Standard and priority reviews have different target times under user-fee goals; check the current FDA figures.

## 3.12 Clinical research and BE studies

### a. Phases of clinical trials

| Phase | Subjects | Main aim |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phase 0 (exploratory, micro-dosing) | Very few volunteers | Early human PK at sub-therapeutic doses |
| Phase I | Usually healthy volunteers (patients for toxic drugs such as anticancer agents); small numbers | Safety, tolerability, PK, dose range |
| Phase II | Patients with the disease; a few hundred | Efficacy, dose finding, short-term safety |
| Phase III | Larger groups of patients, often multicentre | Confirm efficacy and safety against placebo or standard drug |
| Phase IV | Patients after approval | Post-marketing surveillance, rare adverse reactions, new uses |

### b. Bioavailability and bioequivalence (BE) studies

- **Bioavailability:** the rate and extent to which the active drug is absorbed and becomes available at the site of action.
- **Bioequivalence:** two pharmaceutically equivalent products show no significant difference in rate and extent of absorption when given at the same molar dose under similar conditions.

Typical BE study:

1. Healthy adult volunteers (after informed consent), usually a randomised, two-period, two-sequence crossover design.
2. Single dose of test and reference products, separated by a washout period long enough for the drug to be eliminated (commonly at least five half-lives).
3. Blood samples at planned times; drug measured by a validated bioanalytical method.
4. Parameters: Cmax (peak concentration), Tmax (time to peak), AUC0–t and AUC0–∞ (extent).
5. Acceptance: the 90% confidence interval of the test/reference ratio of the geometric means of AUC and Cmax should lie within 80.00–125.00% (wider limits are allowed in special cases by some regulators).

BE studies support generic approval (ANDA) and some post-approval changes.

### c. Good Clinical Practice (GCP)

GCP (ICH E6) is the international ethical and scientific standard for designing, conducting, recording and reporting trials. Key principles: rights, safety and well-being of subjects come first; informed consent; independent ethics committee approval; qualified investigators; accurate records; investigational product made under GMP; quality systems.

## 3.13 Clinical research protocols

A **protocol** is the document that describes the objectives, design, method, statistics and organisation of a trial.

Contents:

1. Title, protocol number, version and date; sponsor and investigators.
2. Background and rationale.
3. Objectives (primary, secondary) and endpoints.
4. Study design: randomisation, blinding (single, double), control (placebo or active), parallel or crossover.
5. Selection of subjects: inclusion and exclusion criteria, withdrawal criteria.
6. Treatment: dose, route, duration, concomitant medicines.
7. Assessment of efficacy and of safety; schedule of visits and tests.
8. Adverse event recording and reporting.
9. Statistics: sample size calculation, analysis plan, handling of missing data.
10. Data handling, record keeping, quality control and audit.
11. Ethics: ethics committee approval, informed consent.
12. Financing, insurance, compensation, publication policy.

## 3.14 Biostatistics in pharmaceutical product development

Statistics are needed at every stage:

| Stage | Statistical use |
| --- | --- |
| Formulation and process development | Design of experiments (factorial designs, response surfaces), process capability |
| Quality control | Sampling plans, control charts, specification limits |
| Stability | Regression analysis to estimate shelf life |
| BE studies | ANOVA, 90% confidence intervals, log transformation |
| Clinical trials | Sample size and power, randomisation, hypothesis testing |

Key terms:

- **Null hypothesis (H0):** no difference between treatments. **Alternative (H1):** there is a difference.
- **Type I error (α):** rejecting a true H0 (false positive); usually set at 0.05.
- **Type II error (β):** accepting a false H0 (false negative). **Power = 1 − β**, usually 80–90%.
- **p-value:** probability of obtaining the observed result (or more extreme) if H0 were true; p < 0.05 is taken as significant.
- **Confidence interval:** a range likely to contain the true value (e.g. 95% CI).
- **Tests:** t-test (two means), ANOVA (more than two means), chi-square (proportions), non-parametric tests for non-normal data.
- **Intention-to-treat analysis:** all randomised subjects analysed in the groups assigned.

## 3.15 Data presentation for FDA submissions

- Data are submitted electronically in the **eCTD** (electronic Common Technical Document) structure.
- Clinical study reports follow **ICH E3** (structure and content of clinical study reports).
- Data sets follow **CDISC** standards (SDTM for collected data, ADaM for analysis data) so reviewers can re-analyse them.
- Results are shown as **tables, listings and figures (TLFs):** demographic tables, efficacy tables with confidence intervals, adverse event tables, laboratory shift tables, Kaplan–Meier plots, forest plots.
- Integrated summaries of safety and efficacy pool all studies.
- Presentation must be clear, consistent across sections, traceable to the raw data, and honest about missing data and protocol deviations.

## 3.16 Management of clinical studies

Roles:

| Party | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Sponsor | Starts, funds and manages the trial; selects investigators; monitors; reports safety; may delegate to a CRO |
| Contract research organisation (CRO) | Carries out sponsor's tasks under contract |
| Investigator | Conducts the trial at the site; protects subjects; obtains consent; keeps records |
| Ethics committee | Reviews and approves protocol and consent; monitors subject safety |
| Regulator | Grants permission; inspects |
| Monitor (clinical research associate) | Visits sites, checks data against source documents and GCP compliance |

Steps in managing a trial:

```text
Feasibility and site selection → approvals (regulator + ethics committee)
→ investigator meeting and training → site initiation → subject recruitment and consent
→ treatment and data collection (CRF/eCRF) → monitoring visits → adverse event reporting
→ database lock → statistical analysis → clinical study report → archiving
```

Important controls: drug accountability, randomisation codes, serious adverse event reporting within set timelines, audit trails, protocol deviation records, and archiving of essential documents.

- RA = link between company and regulator; strategy, dossiers, labelling, life cycle, compliance, intelligence.
- History: 1906 Pure Food and Drugs Act; 1938 FD&C Act after sulfanilamide elixir (safety); 1962 Kefauver–Harris after thalidomide (efficacy); 1984 Hatch–Waxman (ANDA); 1990 ICH. India: D&C Act 1940, Rules 1945, NDCT Rules 2019.

- Biostatistics: α 0.05, power 80–90%, CI, t-test, ANOVA; eCTD, ICH E3, CDISC for submissions.
