# Unit 1: Pilot plant scale-up techniques

A formula that works in a 500 g laboratory batch does not automatically work in a 500 kg production batch. Mixing takes longer, heat moves more slowly through a large mass, granules dry unevenly and machines run much faster. The **pilot plant** is the bridge between the research laboratory and the factory floor, where a process is tried at an intermediate size so that these problems are found and solved before full production begins.

## 1.1 Definitions

- **Pilot plant:** a part of the pharmaceutical industry where a laboratory-scale formula is turned into a viable product by developing a reliable, practical and economical manufacturing procedure, using equipment similar in design to production equipment.
- **Scale-up:** the process of increasing the batch size (laboratory → pilot → production) while keeping the product quality the same.
- **Scale-down:** decreasing the batch size, for example to make a smaller batch for a small market, or to study a production problem at a convenient size.
- **Pilot-scale batch:** for solid oral dosage forms, a batch is commonly described as pilot scale when it is at least one-tenth (10%) of the full production batch or 100,000 units, whichever is larger. (This is the US FDA description used in SUPAC; check the guideline that applies to your product.)

```text
Laboratory (bench) scale  →  Pilot plant scale  →  Production (commercial) scale
   grams to a few kg           tens of kg             hundreds of kg to tonnes
   prove the formula          prove the process      routine manufacture
```

## 1.2 Objectives and significance of a pilot plant

**Objectives**

1. Try the laboratory formula and process on equipment that resembles production machines.
2. Find out which process variables (mixing time, granulating fluid, drying temperature, compression speed) are critical and fix their limits.
3. Produce batches for clinical trials, stability studies and regulatory submissions (exhibit or bio-batches).
4. Write the master manufacturing formula, process instructions and in-process controls for production.
5. Check that raw materials from approved suppliers behave the same from lot to lot.
6. Train production staff and transfer the process smoothly to the plant.
7. Estimate cost, yield and time of manufacture.

**Significance (why it matters)**

- Avoids costly failures of full-size batches.
- Gives data needed for validation and for the regulatory dossier.
- Shows whether a change in equipment or batch size will change the product.
- Allows the company to make enough product for clinical and stability work before the production line is ready.

## 1.3 General considerations

### a. Reporting responsibility

The pilot plant group works between Research and Development (R&D) and production. It reports to R&D for formulation matters and works closely with production, quality assurance, quality control, engineering and regulatory affairs. Clear lines of reporting are needed so that every change is documented and approved.

```text
             R&D (formulation)
                    │
          Pilot plant / scale-up group
         ┌────────┬─────────┬──────────┐
     Production  QA / QC   Engineering  Regulatory affairs
```

### b. Personnel requirements

The staff should have both theory and hands-on skill:

- Scientific knowledge of pharmaceutics, chemistry and engineering (often pharmacists, chemical engineers).
- Practical experience in the operation of production-type equipment.
- Ability to communicate with R&D, production and quality staff and to write clear reports.
- Understanding of GMP, documentation and validation.
- Knowledge of computers, data handling and process instruments.

### c. Space requirements

| Area | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Administration and information processing | Offices, records, data processing, report writing |
| Physical testing area | In-process and finished product tests (hardness, friability, viscosity, particle size) |
| Standard pilot-plant equipment floor | Production-type machines (mixers, granulators, dryers, tablet press, coaters, liquid tanks), arranged to allow cleaning and movement |
| Storage area | Separate areas for approved raw materials, quarantine, in-process material and finished pilot batches; controlled temperature and humidity where needed |

Space should be flexible, so equipment can be moved and new trials set up. Utilities (purified water, compressed air, steam, electrical supply, dust extraction, HVAC) must match production standards.

### d. Review of the formula

The laboratory formula is examined ingredient by ingredient: why each excipient is there, its grade and supplier, and whether its quantity can be produced reliably at a larger scale. Order of mixing, granulating fluid, drying and lubrication steps are reviewed.

### e. Raw materials

- Raw materials used in the laboratory may have come from small, specially selected lots. The pilot plant must test materials from the actual commercial suppliers.
- Physical properties (particle size, bulk density, moisture, flow, polymorphic form) can vary between suppliers and lots and may change the product.
- Specifications should be set for each material, and approved suppliers qualified.

### f. Relevant processing equipment

Equipment should be economical, simple, easy to clean and similar in principle to production machines. The size chosen should allow realistic shear, mixing and heat transfer.

### g. Production rates

The expected market demand decides the batch size and the number of batches per year. Equipment and process should be chosen to meet that rate without excessive overtime or idle capacity.

### h. Process evaluation (parameters studied)

1. Order of mixing of ingredients.
2. Mixing speed and mixing time.
3. Rate of addition of granulating agent, solvents or solutions of drug.
4. Heating and cooling rates.
5. Filter size (for liquids) and screen size (for solids).
6. Drying temperature and drying time.

### i. Master manufacturing procedures

These include the weighing sheet (each ingredient, quantity, lot number), processing directions in clear steps, equipment and settings, in-process checks with limits, sampling instructions, yields and signatures of the person who did each step and the person who checked it.

### j. Product stability and uniformity

Pilot batches are put on stability study and tested for content uniformity, because scale-up can change both.

### k. GMP considerations

Equipment qualification, process validation, regular calibration, cleaning and its validation, written procedures, change control, and orderly records are needed even in the pilot plant.

## 1.4 Pilot plant scale-up considerations for solids (tablets and capsules)

The main problems arise in handling and processing powders.

| Step | Scale-up points to study |
| --- | --- |
| Material handling | Small batches are handled by hand; large batches need bins, vacuum or gravity transfer. Dust control and segregation during transfer must be checked. |
| Dry blending | Blender type, load (fill volume), speed and time. Over-mixing can demix or coat particles with lubricant. Blend uniformity is tested with a sampling thief. |
| Granulation | Wet granulation in high-shear mixers is sensitive to impeller and chopper speed, fluid amount and rate, and end point (often judged by power consumption or torque). Dry granulation (slugging, roller compaction) needs control of roll pressure and gap. |
| Drying | Tray dryer vs fluid bed dryer; inlet air temperature, air flow, load, drying time; loss on drying end point. |
| Size reduction (milling) | Mill type, screen size, speed; granule size distribution affects flow and weight variation. |
| Blending and lubrication | Lubricant (e.g. magnesium stearate) mixing time is critical; too long reduces hardness and slows dissolution. |
| Compression | Machine speed (dwell time) on high-speed rotary presses, feeder type (forced feeders), punch and die tooling, precompression, hardness, weight control. Problems such as capping and sticking may appear only at high speed. |
| Coating | Pan size and speed, spray rate, atomising air, inlet temperature, number of guns, tablet load. |
| Capsule filling | Change from hand or semi-automatic filling to automatic dosator or tamping machines; powder flow and plug formation must suit the machine. |

```text
Weigh → dry mix → wet granulate → dry → mill → blend + lubricate → compress → coat → pack
   ↑         ↑            ↑          ↑       ↑           ↑               ↑
 order    time/speed   fluid, end  temp,   screen   lubricant time   speed, dwell
                       point       airflow  size                     time, hardness
```

## 1.5 Scale-up considerations for liquid orals

Liquid orals (solutions, syrups, suspensions, emulsions) look simple, but scale-up still needs care.

1. **Equipment:** jacketed stainless-steel tanks with suitable agitators, homogenisers or colloid mills for emulsions and suspensions, filtration units, transfer pumps and filling machines.
2. **Mixing:** the type of agitator, speed, and position of the impeller change with tank size. Solids must dissolve or be wetted completely; over-agitation can trap air.
3. **Order of addition and temperature:** for example, dissolving sugar with heat for syrups, adding preservative at the right temperature, and adding flavour after cooling so it is not lost.
4. **Heating and cooling time:** a large tank takes much longer to heat and cool, which can increase degradation or microbial growth.
5. **Filtration:** filter type and area, pressure, and clarity must be checked; filters must not adsorb preservatives.
6. **Suspensions:** particle size of the solid, wetting, viscosity of the vehicle, sedimentation volume and ease of redispersion. Homogenisation may be needed.
7. **Emulsions:** phase volume, emulsifier, temperature of each phase, rate of addition, and homogenisation pressure decide globule size.
8. **Physical stability checks:** appearance, colour, odour, taste, pH, viscosity, specific gravity, sedimentation and creaming.
9. **Microbial control:** clean, sanitised equipment and suitable water quality; preservative efficacy.
10. **Filling:** fill volume accuracy, foaming, and compatibility with containers and closures.

## 1.6 Scale-up considerations for semisolids

For ointments, creams, gels and pastes:

1. **Mixing equipment:** jacketed vessels with anchor (scraping) agitators plus high-shear homogenisers; vacuum mixing to avoid air entrapment.
2. **Heating and cooling rates:** the melting of the fatty phase, the temperature at which phases are combined, and the cooling rate affect consistency and crystal formation.
3. **Shear and mixing time:** too much shear can break the structure of some gels; too little leaves lumps or poor drug distribution.
4. **Incorporation of the drug:** dissolved or dispersed; particle size of a dispersed drug must be controlled (milling or levigation).
5. **Transfer and pumping:** viscous products need suitable pumps and pipes; air pockets must be prevented.
6. **Filling:** tubes or jars; filling temperature affects flow and final consistency.
7. **Tests:** appearance, homogeneity, viscosity and rheology, spreadability, pH, drug content, and stability under heat and cold.

## 1.7 Relevant documentation

Pilot plant work is useful only if it is written down well. Typical documents:

| Document | Contents |
| --- | --- |
| Master formula record (master manufacturing formula) | Product name, strength, batch size, list of ingredients with quantities and grades, equipment, process steps, in-process controls, yield limits |
| Batch manufacturing record | The actual record of one batch: lot numbers, weights, times, settings, results and signatures |
| Scale-up / development report | What was tried, problems found, critical parameters and the reasons for the final process |
| Raw material specifications | Tests and limits for each excipient and the API |
| In-process and finished product specifications | Tests, methods and limits |
| Standard operating procedures | Operation, cleaning and calibration of each machine |
| Process validation protocol and report | Plan and results showing the process gives consistent quality |
| Stability protocol and data | Storage conditions, time points, results |
| Change control records | Any change after the process is fixed |

## 1.8 SUPAC guidelines

**SUPAC** stands for **Scale-Up and Post-Approval Changes**. These are guidance documents of the US FDA that tell a company what tests and what kind of filing are needed when it changes an already approved product. The aim is to keep quality and performance the same while allowing reasonable changes.

**The SUPAC family**

| Guideline | Covers |
| --- | --- |
| SUPAC-IR | Immediate-release solid oral dosage forms |
| SUPAC-MR | Modified-release solid oral dosage forms |
| SUPAC-SS | Non-sterile semisolid dosage forms (creams, ointments, gels, lotions) |

**Types of change covered**

1. Components and composition (excipient amounts).
2. Site of manufacture.
3. Batch size (scale-up or scale-down).
4. Manufacturing equipment.
5. Manufacturing process.

**Levels of change**

| Level | Meaning | Usual reporting |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Level 1 | Unlikely to have any detectable effect on quality and performance | Annual report |
| Level 2 | Could have a significant effect | A supplement to the application (the type depends on the change) |
| Level 3 | Likely to have a significant effect | Prior approval supplement, with more testing (sometimes bioequivalence) |

**Examples**

- Batch size: an increase up to about ten times the pilot or bio-batch, using equipment of the same design and operating principle, is a Level 1 change; beyond ten times is Level 2.
- Excipients: a small change in the amount of an excipient, within limits set for each excipient class (filler, disintegrant, binder, lubricant, glidant, film coat), is Level 1; a larger but limited change is Level 2; anything bigger, or a change in excipient type, is Level 3.
- Equipment: change to equipment of the same design and operating principle is Level 1; change to a different design and principle is Level 2.
- Site (SUPAC-IR): a move within the same facility is Level 1; within the same contiguous campus is Level 2; to a different campus is Level 3.

**Tests required** depend on level and BCS class of the drug: stability data (long-term and accelerated), dissolution profiles in one or several media compared with the earlier product (similarity factor f2 between 50 and 100 shows similar profiles), and in vivo bioequivalence for some Level 3 changes.

**Equipment addendum:** a companion FDA document classifies manufacturing equipment (blenders, granulators, dryers, mills, presses, coaters) into classes and subclasses by operating principle and design, so a company can decide whether a new machine counts as "same" or "different".

Note: SUPAC is a US guideline. Indian manufacturers follow it for products exported to the USA. In India, post-approval changes are handled under the Drugs and Cosmetics rules and CDSCO notices; check the current CDSCO guidance before filing any change.

## 1.9 Introduction to platform technology

A **platform technology** is a proven, reusable system or process that can be applied to many different drugs or products with only small changes, rather than inventing a new process for each product.

**Examples**

| Platform | How it is reused |
| --- | --- |
| Osmotic pump tablets (OROS type) | Same push–pull design used for different drugs needing controlled release |
| Matrix tablets of hydrophilic polymers (e.g. HPMC) | Same polymer system and process for many once-daily tablets |
| Liposomes, nanoparticles, solid lipid particles | Same carrier and process for different drugs |
| Hot-melt extrusion / solid dispersions | Same process to improve solubility of many poorly soluble drugs |
| Orally disintegrating tablet technologies | Same base and process for many drugs |
| Prefilled syringes, auto-injectors, inhalers | Same device for several drugs |
| Monoclonal antibody manufacturing platform | Same cell line type, purification steps and analytical methods for many antibodies |
| mRNA and viral-vector vaccine platforms | Same production and delivery system, new antigen code |

**Advantages**

1. Shorter development time and lower cost.
2. Existing knowledge, equipment and validated processes are reused.
3. Lower technical risk, since the platform has worked before.
4. Easier regulatory review where earlier data support the new product.
5. Faster scale-up and technology transfer.

**Limitations:** not every drug suits a platform; patents may restrict use; each new product still needs its own data on quality, safety and efficacy.

## Remember

- Pilot plant = intermediate stage between laboratory and production where a reliable, economical process is developed on production-like equipment.
- Pilot-scale batch for solid orals: at least 10% of production batch or 100,000 units, whichever is larger (FDA SUPAC description).
- General considerations: reporting responsibility, personnel, space (administration, physical testing, equipment floor, storage), formula review, raw materials, equipment, production rates, process evaluation, master manufacturing procedures, stability, GMP.
- Solids: blending, granulation end point, drying, milling, lubrication time, compression speed and dwell time.
- Liquids: agitation, order of addition, heating/cooling rates, filtration, homogenisation, filling.
- Semisolids: heating/cooling, shear, vacuum mixing, transfer of viscous mass.
- SUPAC (FDA): IR, MR, SS; changes in components, site, batch size, equipment, process; Levels 1, 2, 3; f2 50–100 = similar dissolution.
- Platform technology = a reusable delivery or manufacturing system applied to many products.
