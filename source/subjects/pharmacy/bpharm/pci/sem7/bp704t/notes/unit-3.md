# Unit 3: Transdermal, gastroretentive and nasopulmonary drug delivery systems

This unit covers three routes that get round the limits of ordinary tablets. **Part A** is transdermal delivery: drug crosses intact skin into the blood. **Part B** is gastroretentive delivery: the dosage form stays in the stomach for hours. **Part C** is nasal and pulmonary delivery: drug is sprayed into the nose or inhaled into the lungs.

## Part A: Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDS)

## 3.1 Introduction

**Transdermal drug delivery systems** (transdermal patches) are self-contained dosage forms which, when applied to intact skin, deliver the drug through the skin into the systemic circulation at a controlled rate.

Topical products (creams, ointments) act locally on the skin; transdermal products act on the whole body.

**Advantages**

1. Avoid first-pass hepatic metabolism and gastrointestinal breakdown.
2. Constant, controlled input for a long time (one to seven days with one patch).
3. Fewer peaks and troughs; fewer side effects.
4. Easy to apply; better compliance; useful in unconscious or vomiting patients.
5. Therapy stopped at once by removing the patch.
6. Painless and non-invasive.

**Disadvantages**

1. Only potent drugs with suitable properties pass through the skin in useful amounts.
2. Skin irritation or allergy from drug, adhesive or enhancer.
3. Patch may fall off; adhesion varies with sweat and hair.
4. Slow onset, not for emergencies.
5. Skin barrier varies with site, age and disease.
6. Costly; large molecules (most peptides) cannot pass without physical enhancement.

**Ideal drug properties for TDDS**

| Property | Desired value (approximate) |
| --- | --- |
| Molecular weight | Below about 500 Da |
| Partition coefficient (log P) | About 1–3 (soluble in both oil and water) |
| Melting point | Low (below about 200 °C) |
| Daily dose | Small (a few milligrams to about 20 mg) |
| Half-life | Short |
| Skin reaction | Non-irritant and non-sensitising |

Examples of transdermal products: nitroglycerin (angina), scopolamine (motion sickness), nicotine (smoking cessation), fentanyl (chronic pain), estradiol (hormone replacement), clonidine (hypertension), rivastigmine (dementia), buprenorphine (pain).

## 3.2 Permeation through skin

```text
 Skin surface
 ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Stratum corneum (dead keratinised   │  ← main barrier, about 10–20 µm
 │ cells in a lipid "brick and mortar")│
 ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ Viable epidermis (no blood vessels) │
 ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ Dermis: capillaries, nerves,        │  ← drug taken into blood here
 │ hair follicles, sweat glands        │
 ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
 │ Subcutaneous fat                    │
 └─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The **stratum corneum** is the main barrier. It is often compared to a brick wall: dead corneocytes (bricks) packed in layers of intercellular lipids (mortar: ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids).

**Routes of permeation**

1. **Transcellular (intracellular)**: straight through corneocytes and lipids, alternating hydrophilic and lipophilic regions.
2. **Intercellular**: around the cells through the lipid mortar; the main route for most drugs; tortuous and long.
3. **Appendageal (shunt)**: through hair follicles and sweat ducts; small area (about 0.1% of skin) but important early after application and for ions and large molecules.

**Kinetics of skin permeation.** Passive diffusion obeys Fick's first law. At steady state:

J = (D K / h) ΔC = Kp × Cv

where J is the flux (amount per cm² per hour), D the diffusion coefficient in the stratum corneum, K the partition coefficient between stratum corneum and vehicle, h the thickness of the barrier, ΔC the concentration difference (≈ concentration in the vehicle Cv when sink conditions hold in the skin) and Kp = DK/h the **permeability coefficient**.

**Worked example (hypothetical).** If Kp = 1 × 10⁻³ cm/h and the drug concentration in the patch vehicle is 10 mg/ml:

- J = 1 × 10⁻³ × 10 = 0.01 mg/cm²/h = **10 µg/cm²/h**
- A 20 cm² patch delivers 10 × 20 = 200 µg/h, that is **4.8 mg per day**.
- Doubling the patch area doubles the amount delivered, which is why patch size is used to set the strength of transdermal products.

A plot of cumulative amount permeated against time gives a curved start (lag phase) followed by a straight line; the slope is the steady-state flux and the x-intercept is the **lag time**.

## 3.3 Factors affecting permeation

**Biological factors**

1. Skin condition: damaged, diseased or burnt skin is more permeable.
2. Skin site: permeability differs over the body (scrotum and behind the ear are more permeable than the sole and palm). Scopolamine patches are applied behind the ear.
3. Skin hydration: hydrated stratum corneum is more permeable (occlusion by the patch raises hydration).
4. Skin temperature and blood flow.
5. Age: neonates and the elderly differ; premature infants have a poorly formed barrier.
6. Skin metabolism by enzymes in the epidermis.
7. Species differences (important when using animal skin in tests).

**Physicochemical factors**

1. Partition coefficient of the drug (optimum log P about 1–3).
2. Molecular size and weight (small molecules pass better).
3. Solubility and melting point (low melting point → better solubility in skin lipids).
4. Ionisation: un-ionised form permeates better, so pH of the vehicle matters.
5. Drug concentration in the vehicle (thermodynamic activity).
6. Drug–vehicle interactions and the effect of the vehicle on the skin.

## 3.4 Permeation enhancers

Chemicals or physical methods that reversibly reduce the barrier of the stratum corneum.

**Chemical enhancers**

| Class | Examples | Main action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sulfoxides | Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), decylmethyl sulfoxide | Change keratin, extract lipids |
| Azone (laurocapram) | Laurocapram | Fluidises lipid bilayers |
| Alcohols and glycols | Ethanol, propylene glycol | Solvent, increase drug partitioning |
| Fatty acids | Oleic acid, lauric acid | Disorder lipid packing |
| Surfactants | Sodium lauryl sulfate, polysorbates | Solubilise lipids, act on keratin |
| Terpenes | Menthol, limonene, cineole, eugenol | Disrupt lipid structure |
| Urea and pyrrolidones | Urea, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone | Hydrate and soften stratum corneum |
| Phospholipids | Lecithin | Vesicle carriers |

**Mechanisms**: disruption of intercellular lipids (lipid–protein–partitioning concept), interaction with keratin in corneocytes, and better partitioning of drug into the skin.

**Physical methods**

1. **Iontophoresis**: a small electric current drives charged drug molecules across the skin.
2. **Electroporation**: short high-voltage pulses create temporary pores.
3. **Sonophoresis (phonophoresis)**: ultrasound disturbs lipids and increases permeation.
4. **Microneedles**: arrays of tiny needles pierce only the stratum corneum, painlessly.
5. **Thermal methods, laser ablation and jet injectors.**

Ideal enhancer: non-toxic, non-irritant, pharmacologically inert, quick and reversible action, acts in one direction only (does not let body fluids out), compatible with drug and excipients.

## 3.5 Basic components of TDDS

```text
   ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐ backing laminate (impermeable,
   │            backing layer            │ flexible: polyester, aluminised film)
   ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
   │   drug reservoir / drug-in-polymer  │ drug + polymer + enhancer
   ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
   │   rate-controlling membrane         │ (reservoir patches only: EVA)
   ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
   │   pressure-sensitive adhesive       │ sticks to skin
   ├─────────────────────────────────────┤
   │   release liner (peeled off)        │ siliconised film
   └─────────────────────────────────────┘
                 skin
```

| Component | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Polymer matrix / membrane | Holds drug and controls release | EVA, silicone, polyisobutylene, HPMC, ethyl cellulose, polyvinylpyrrolidone |
| Drug | Active | As in 3.1 |
| Permeation enhancer | Increases flux | As in 3.4 |
| Pressure-sensitive adhesive (PSA) | Holds patch on skin; sticks with light pressure and peels off without residue | Polyisobutylene, acrylates, silicones |
| Backing laminate | Protects patch, stops drug loss, gives occlusion | Polyester film, polyethylene, aluminised laminates |
| Release liner | Protects adhesive until use | Siliconised polyester |
| Others | Plasticisers, solvents, antioxidants | Propylene glycol, PEG |

## 3.6 Formulation approaches (types of TDDS)

| Type | Construction | Release |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Membrane-moderated (reservoir) | Drug in a reservoir sealed between an impermeable backing and a rate-controlling membrane (EVA) with adhesive on the outside | Zero order; membrane controls. Examples: nitroglycerin, scopolamine, estradiol patches |
| Adhesive-dispersion (drug-in-adhesive) | Drug dispersed directly in the adhesive polymer laid on the backing; may have layers with different drug concentration | Simple, thin; examples: many nicotine and estradiol patches |
| Matrix diffusion-controlled | Drug dispersed in a hydrophilic or lipophilic polymer disc, with a peripheral adhesive rim | Rate falls with √t; example: nitroglycerin matrix patches |
| Microreservoir (micro-sealed dissolution) | Drug suspended in aqueous polymer, then dispersed as tiny droplets in a lipophilic polymer (silicone) and cross-linked | Combination of reservoir and matrix; example: nitroglycerin microreservoir system |

**Preparation methods**: solvent casting (drug, polymer and plasticiser cast on a backing and dried), heat-seal / form-fill-seal of reservoir patches, and lamination of layers.

**Evaluation of TDDS**

- Physical: thickness, weight uniformity, drug content, moisture content and uptake, folding endurance, tensile strength, flatness.
- Adhesion: peel adhesion, tack (rolling ball, probe tack) and shear strength (creep).
- In vitro release (paddle-over-disc method) and in vitro skin permeation (Franz diffusion cell with excised skin or synthetic membrane).
- Skin irritation and sensitisation studies; stability.

## Part B: Gastroretentive drug delivery systems (GRDDS)

## 3.7 Introduction

**Gastroretentive drug delivery systems** stay in the stomach for a long time (several hours) and release drug slowly there, so that the drug reaches its absorption site in the upper gut over a long period.

**Physiology behind GRDDS.** In the fasted state, the stomach and intestine show the **migrating myoelectric complex (MMC)**, a cycle of about 1.5–2 hours with four phases. Phase III ("housekeeper wave") consists of strong contractions that sweep undigested material, including tablets, out of the stomach. Food interrupts this cycle and delays emptying. The pyloric opening is about 12 mm in diameter in humans, so large objects stay longer.

**Drugs that benefit**

| Reason | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Narrow absorption window in stomach or upper small intestine | Levodopa, riboflavin, furosemide, ciprofloxacin |
| Local action in the stomach | Antacids, misoprostol, antibiotics against Helicobacter pylori |
| Unstable in intestinal or colonic fluid | Captopril, ranitidine |
| Poorly soluble at alkaline pH | Diazepam, verapamil |

**Unsuitable drugs**: unstable in acid, irritant to gastric mucosa (many NSAIDs), absorbed equally well throughout the gut, or intended to act in the colon.

## 3.8 Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

1. Better bioavailability of drugs with an upper-gut absorption window.
2. Sustained release with fewer doses.
3. Local therapy in the stomach (ulcers, H. pylori).
4. Less fluctuation in plasma levels; reduced colon exposure, so less disturbance of gut flora by antibiotics.

**Disadvantages**

1. Floating systems need enough fluid in the stomach and usually work only in the fed state.
2. Gastric emptying varies a lot between and within people.
3. Not suitable for drugs that irritate the stomach or are unstable in acid.
4. Bioadhesive systems may be dislodged by mucus turnover.
5. Swelling systems must not block the pylorus or gut.

## 3.9 Approaches for GRDDS

```text
GRDDS
├── Floating (low density) systems
│     ├── Non-effervescent: hydrodynamically balanced systems (HBS),
│     │   alginate beads, hollow microspheres (microballoons), raft-forming
│     └── Effervescent: gas-generating (NaHCO3 + citric acid → CO2),
│         volatile-liquid systems
├── High-density (sinking) systems
├── Expandable: swelling and unfolding systems
├── Inflatable systems
├── Bioadhesive / mucoadhesive (gastroadhesive) systems
└── Magnetic systems
```

### a. Floating systems

The density of gastric fluid is about 1.004 g/cm³. A dosage form lighter than this floats on the stomach contents and is not swept out as quickly.

1. **Hydrodynamically balanced system (HBS).** A capsule or tablet containing the drug with a gel-forming hydrocolloid (HPMC, HPC, sodium CMC, agar). In gastric fluid the outer layer hydrates to a gel barrier; trapped air keeps the bulk density below 1, so it floats while drug diffuses slowly through the gel. Examples: levodopa–benserazide HBS capsules, diazepam floating capsules.

2. **Gas-generating (effervescent) systems.** Contain sodium bicarbonate (with citric or tartaric acid). In acid, carbon dioxide forms and is trapped in the swollen polymer, lowering the density. Single-layer, bilayer (separate gas-generating layer) and multiple-unit pellets are possible. The **floating lag time** (time to start floating) should be short.

   NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2↑

3. **Volatile-liquid / vacuum systems.** An inflatable chamber contains a liquid (ether, cyclopentane) that turns to gas at body temperature and inflates the chamber; mainly experimental.

4. **Hollow microspheres (microballoons).** Made by emulsion–solvent diffusion with polymers such as Eudragit or polycarbonate; the hollow core keeps them floating for many hours; multiple units reduce the risk of all-or-none emptying.

5. **Raft-forming systems.** Liquid alginate formulations with bicarbonate form a cohesive gel "raft" that floats on stomach contents; used against acid reflux (alginate–antacid liquids).

### b. High-density (sinking) systems

Pellets with density above about 2.5 g/cm³ settle into the folds of the stomach (antrum) and resist peristalsis. Weighting agents: barium sulfate, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, iron powder. Results in humans have been less reliable than in animals.

### c. Expandable and swelling systems

The dosage form swells or unfolds in the stomach to a size larger than the pyloric opening, so it cannot pass until it softens or erodes. Must be small enough to swallow, expand fast, and later break down so it can leave safely. Superporous hydrogels swell very fast and to a large size.

### d. Inflatable systems

A drug reservoir with an inflatable chamber containing a gas-forming or volatile substance. After swallowing, the chamber inflates and holds the device in the stomach. An erodible plug or polymer lets it deflate and leave later.

### e. Bioadhesive (gastroadhesive) systems

Mucoadhesive polymers (carbomer, polycarbophil, chitosan, lectins) attach the system to the gastric mucosa. Limitation: the stomach lining renews mucus quickly and is very wet, so adhesion is often short-lived.

### f. Magnetic systems

A small internal magnet in the dosage form is held in place by an external magnet over the stomach. Accurate placement of the external magnet is difficult, so use is limited.

## 3.10 Evaluation and applications

**Evaluation**: floating lag time and total floating time (buoyancy test in 0.1 N HCl), swelling index, density, in vitro release, mucoadhesive strength, and in vivo gastric residence by gamma scintigraphy or X-ray with a radio-opaque marker.

**Applications**

1. Sustained delivery of drugs with an upper-gut absorption window.
2. Local delivery in the stomach for ulcers and H. pylori eradication.
3. Reducing fluctuation and doses for short half-life drugs.
4. Minimising colonic exposure to antibiotics.
5. Improving absorption of drugs poorly soluble at intestinal pH.

## Part C: Nasopulmonary drug delivery systems

## 3.11 Nasal route

The nasal cavity has a surface area of roughly 150 cm², a rich blood supply and a fairly permeable epithelium (ciliated pseudostratified columnar cells in the respiratory region). Drug absorbed from the nose enters the systemic circulation directly, avoiding first-pass metabolism. The **olfactory region** at the top of the cavity offers a possible route to the brain.

**Advantages**: rapid absorption and onset; avoids first-pass metabolism and gut breakdown; needle-free; self-administration; possible delivery of peptides (desmopressin, calcitonin) and vaccines; possible nose-to-brain delivery.

**Limitations**: small volume per dose (about 0.1 ml per nostril); mucociliary clearance removes the dose within about 15–20 minutes; enzymes in the mucosa; local irritation and effect on ciliary function; absorption altered by colds and allergy; large molecules absorbed poorly without enhancers.

**Mechanisms of absorption**: transcellular (lipophilic drugs) and paracellular through tight junctions (hydrophilic drugs; slow for large molecules).

**Factors affecting nasal absorption**: molecular weight and lipophilicity, pH and osmolality of the formulation, viscosity, particle or droplet size and site of deposition, mucociliary clearance, enzymes and disease.

**Strategies to improve nasal absorption**: permeation enhancers (bile salts, surfactants, cyclodextrins, chitosan), enzyme inhibitors, mucoadhesive polymers that slow clearance (carbomer, chitosan), prodrugs and in situ gels.

### Nasal formulations and nasal sprays

Nasal drops, sprays, gels, powders and in situ gelling liquids. A **nasal spray** is a solution or suspension delivered by a **metered-dose pump** that gives a fixed volume per actuation as a fine mist.

Typical excipients: buffers (to a mildly acidic to neutral pH), tonicity agents, preservatives (benzalkonium chloride; preservative-free multi-dose pumps also exist), viscosity enhancers (HPMC, carbomer), solubilisers, antioxidants, enhancers. **Evaluation**: dose per actuation and its uniformity, spray pattern and plume geometry, droplet size distribution, pH, viscosity, and preservative efficacy.

Examples of nasal products: decongestants and corticosteroids (local), sumatriptan (migraine), desmopressin, salmon calcitonin, butorphanol and a live attenuated influenza vaccine.

## 3.12 Pulmonary route

The lungs have a huge absorbing surface (about 70–100 m² of alveoli), a very thin alveolar–capillary barrier and a large blood flow. Inhaled drugs act locally in asthma and COPD with small doses and few systemic effects, and some drugs (for example inhaled insulin, now largely withdrawn) can be absorbed systemically.

**Deposition depends on aerodynamic particle size**

| Aerodynamic diameter | Main site of deposition | Mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Above about 5 µm | Mouth, throat and large airways | Inertial impaction |
| About 1–5 µm | Small airways and alveoli (the useful range) | Sedimentation (gravity) |
| Below about 1 µm | Mostly exhaled; some alveolar deposition | Diffusion (Brownian motion) |

**Advantages**: direct action in the lung, rapid onset, low dose, fewer systemic side effects, avoids first-pass metabolism. **Limitations**: patient technique matters greatly; only a fraction of the dose reaches the lung; irritation and cough; mucociliary and macrophage clearance; devices can be complex.

## 3.13 Formulation of inhalers

### a. Pressurised metered-dose inhaler (pMDI)

```text
        ┌────────┐
        │ canister│  drug (solution or suspension) + propellant
        │(alum.)  │  + surfactant / co-solvent
        └───┬────┘
            │ metering valve (fixed volume per press, e.g. 25–100 µl)
        ┌───┴──────────┐
        │   actuator   │  patient presses canister → dose atomised
        │   (plastic)  ├──→ mouthpiece → aerosol cloud
        └──────────────┘
```

| Component | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canister | Holds formulation under pressure | Aluminium, sometimes internally coated |
| Metering valve | Delivers a fixed volume each time | Elastomer seals |
| Actuator / mouthpiece | Forms the spray | Plastic |
| Propellant | Provides energy to atomise; forms the vehicle | Hydrofluoroalkanes HFA-134a and HFA-227 (CFCs phased out under the Montreal Protocol because they damage the ozone layer) |
| Surfactant | Keeps suspension dispersed; lubricates valve | Oleic acid, sorbitan trioleate, lecithin |
| Co-solvent | Dissolves drug or surfactant in HFA | Ethanol |
| Drug | Micronised to about 1–5 µm (suspension) or dissolved | Salbutamol, beclomethasone, budesonide |

**Filling**: cold filling (product and propellant chilled and filled at low temperature) or pressure filling (concentrate filled, valve crimped, propellant forced through the valve under pressure).

**Problems**: coordination of pressing and breathing; cold "Freon effect" on the throat; high oropharyngeal deposition. **Spacers** and valved holding chambers slow the aerosol, let large particles settle, and remove the need for exact coordination. Breath-actuated MDIs fire on inhalation.

### b. Dry powder inhaler (DPI)

The drug, micronised to about 1–5 µm, is blended with a coarse carrier, usually **lactose** (inhalation grade), to improve flow and dosing. The patient's own inspiration disperses the powder and separates drug from carrier.

| Type | Description | Examples of device types |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single unit-dose | Capsule pierced in the device before each use | Rotahaler, Spinhaler type devices |
| Multi-unit dose | Several pre-measured doses in blisters or discs | Diskus type devices |
| Multi-dose reservoir | Bulk powder; device meters each dose | Turbuhaler type devices |

**Advantages**: no propellant; breath-actuated, so no coordination needed; stable dry formulation. **Limitations**: needs a strong enough inspiratory flow; sensitive to moisture; dose may vary with flow rate.

### c. Nebulisers

Nebulisers turn a liquid (solution or suspension) into a fine mist breathed through a mouthpiece or mask over several minutes. No special technique is needed, so they suit children, the elderly and acute attacks.

| Type | Principle |
| --- | --- |
| Jet (air-jet) nebuliser | Compressed air or oxygen passes through a narrow orifice; the low pressure (Venturi/Bernoulli effect) draws liquid up and breaks it into droplets; baffles remove large droplets |
| Ultrasonic nebuliser | A vibrating piezoelectric crystal creates high-frequency waves that produce a fine mist from the liquid surface; may heat the liquid |
| Vibrating mesh nebuliser | A mesh with thousands of tiny holes vibrates and pushes liquid through, giving fine droplets; quiet, portable, little residual volume |

Nebuliser solutions must be sterile, near isotonic, with a suitable pH, and generally free of irritant preservatives. Limitations: bulky, slow, needs power, and wastes part of the dose.

**Evaluation of inhalation products**: delivered dose uniformity, aerodynamic particle size distribution by cascade impactor or twin impinger (giving mass median aerodynamic diameter and fine particle fraction), spray pattern, leak rate and valve delivery for pMDIs, moisture content for DPIs.

## Remember

- TDDS: stratum corneum (about 10–20 µm) is the barrier; flux J = (DK/h)ΔC = Kp Cv; ideal drug MW below about 500, log P about 1–3, potent, low dose.
- Enhancers: DMSO, azone, propylene glycol, oleic acid, terpenes, surfactants; physical: iontophoresis, electroporation, sonophoresis, microneedles.
- Patch layers: backing, reservoir/matrix, rate-controlling membrane, pressure-sensitive adhesive, release liner. Types: reservoir, drug-in-adhesive, matrix, microreservoir.
- GRDDS: MMC phase III empties the stomach; gastric fluid density about 1.004 g/cm³. Floating (HBS, effervescent NaHCO3, microballoons, rafts), high density (above about 2.5 g/cm³), expandable, inflatable, gastroadhesive, magnetic.
- Test floating systems by floating lag time and total floating time.
- Nasal: about 150 cm², avoids first-pass, mucociliary clearance about 15–20 min, about 0.1 ml per nostril; metered-dose spray pumps.
- Lung: 1–5 µm reach small airways and alveoli; pMDI (HFA-134a, HFA-227 + surfactant + ethanol), DPI (micronised drug + lactose carrier), nebulisers (jet, ultrasonic, vibrating mesh); cascade impactor for particle size.
