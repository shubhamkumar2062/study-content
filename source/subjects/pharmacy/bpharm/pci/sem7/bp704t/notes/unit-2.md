# Unit 2: Microencapsulation, mucosal and implantable drug delivery systems

This unit has three parts. **Part A** covers microencapsulation: wrapping tiny particles or droplets in a polymer coat. **Part B** covers mucoadhesive (bioadhesive) systems, which stick to a moist mucous membrane such as the inside of the cheek. **Part C** covers implants and osmotic pumps, which release drug inside the body for weeks to years.

## Part A: Microencapsulation

## 2.1 Definition and terms

**Microencapsulation** is the process of enclosing small solid particles, liquid droplets or gases (the **core**) in a thin continuous coat of polymer (the **wall**, shell or coat), giving particles roughly 1–1000 µm in size.

| Term | Structure | Drug location |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microcapsule | Reservoir type: distinct core surrounded by a wall | Core only |
| Microsphere | Matrix type: drug dispersed or dissolved throughout a polymer sphere | Spread through the particle |
| Microparticle | General term covering both | Either |
| Nanoparticle | Same idea below about 1 µm | Unit 4 |

```text
   Microcapsule (reservoir)          Microsphere (matrix)
      ┌──────────┐                      ┌──────────┐
     /  polymer   \                    / • •  • •  \
    |  ┌────────┐  |                  | •  • •  •  |
    |  │  core  │  |                  |  • • polymer|
    |  └────────┘  |                  | •  •  • •  |
     \    wall    /                    \ • •  •  • /
      └──────────┘                      └──────────┘
```

Microcapsules may be **mononuclear** (one core), **polynuclear** (many cores in one wall) or **matrix** type. Important formulation terms are the **core material** (drug, with stabilisers), the **coating material** and the **core-to-coat ratio** (payload).

**Ideal coating material**: forms a film around the core; does not react with the core; strong, flexible and impermeable as needed; gives the desired release; inexpensive. Examples: gelatin, gum acacia, sodium alginate, chitosan, albumin, ethyl cellulose, cellulose acetate phthalate, HPMC, polyvinyl alcohol, PLGA, waxes and paraffin.

## 2.2 Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages / reasons for microencapsulation**

1. Sustained or controlled release (for example aspirin or potassium chloride microcapsules).
2. Masking of bitter taste and unpleasant odour.
3. Conversion of liquids (oils, vitamins A and E) into free-flowing powders.
4. Protection of the drug from moisture, light, oxygen and stomach acid.
5. Reduction of gastric irritation by spreading the dose over many small units.
6. Separation of incompatible substances in one product.
7. Reduction of volatility of volatile substances (flavours, essential oils).
8. Targeting (for example magnetic or bioadhesive microspheres) and parenteral depots (PLGA microspheres given by injection).
9. Better flow and handling of powders.

**Disadvantages**

- Coat may be uneven, leading to variable release.
- Process is often costly and needs skilled control.
- Residual organic solvents may remain.
- Stability of the coat and core during storage must be checked.
- Scale-up can be difficult; reproducibility between batches is a challenge.

## 2.3 Methods of microencapsulation

```text
Methods
├── Physicochemical
│     ├── Coacervation–phase separation (simple, complex)
│     ├── Solvent evaporation / solvent extraction
│     └── Ionic gelation
├── Chemical
│     ├── Interfacial polymerisation
│     └── In situ polymerisation
└── Mechanical (physical)
      ├── Air suspension (Wurster)
      ├── Pan coating
      ├── Spray drying and spray congealing
      ├── Multi-orifice centrifugal process
      └── Extrusion and fluidised-bed methods
```

### a. Coacervation–phase separation

A polymer dissolved in a liquid is made to separate as a polymer-rich liquid phase (**coacervate**), which collects around dispersed core particles and is then hardened.

**Three steps**

1. Formation of three immiscible phases: liquid vehicle, core material and coating material.
2. Deposition of the liquid coacervate around the core.
3. Rigidisation (hardening) of the coat by heat, cross-linking (for example with an aldehyde for gelatin) or removal of solvent.

**Ways of inducing phase separation**

| Method | How the polymer is made to separate |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature change | Polymer soluble at high temperature separates on cooling (ethyl cellulose in cyclohexane) |
| Addition of incompatible polymer | A second polymer competes for the solvent (polybutadiene added to ethyl cellulose in toluene) |
| Addition of non-solvent | A liquid in which the polymer does not dissolve is added |
| Addition of salt (salting out) | Sodium sulfate added to gelatin solution: simple coacervation |
| Polymer–polymer interaction | Oppositely charged polymers combine: complex coacervation |

**Complex coacervation** uses two oppositely charged colloids, classically gelatin and gum acacia. Gelatin is positively charged below its isoelectric point, while acacia is negatively charged. When the pH is lowered to about 4–4.5 and the mixture is diluted and warmed, the two attract each other and separate as a coacervate around the core. Cooling and cross-linking harden the wall.

### b. Solvent evaporation

1. Polymer (PLGA, ethyl cellulose) dissolved in a volatile organic solvent (dichloromethane).
2. Drug dissolved or dispersed in this solution.
3. The organic phase emulsified in an aqueous phase containing an emulsifier (polyvinyl alcohol) to form an o/w emulsion.
4. Solvent evaporated by stirring, heat or reduced pressure; polymer precipitates around the drug.
5. Microspheres collected by filtration, washed and dried.

Water-soluble drugs are better handled by a double emulsion (w/o/w) method. In **solvent extraction**, the solvent is removed into an excess of a second liquid instead of by evaporation.

### c. Ionic gelation

Sodium alginate solution containing drug is dropped into calcium chloride solution. Calcium ions cross-link the alginate chains instantly ("egg-box" structure) to form gel beads. Chitosan with tripolyphosphate works the same way.

### d. Interfacial and in situ polymerisation

- **Interfacial polymerisation**: two reactive monomers, one in the dispersed phase and one in the continuous phase, react at the droplet surface to form a polymer film (for example polyamide walls from a diamine and a diacid chloride).
- **In situ polymerisation**: monomer polymerises only in one phase, depositing polymer on the core surface.

### e. Air suspension (Wurster process)

Solid core particles are suspended in an upward stream of warm air in a vertical chamber. Coating solution is sprayed onto them; the air dries the coat. Particles circulate repeatedly through the spray zone until the required thickness is built up. Suitable for solid cores above about 50 µm; widely used for coating pellets.

```text
        exhaust
          ↑
   ┌──────────────┐
   │  expansion   │   particles fall back outside the partition
   │   chamber    │
   │  ↑  ┌──┐  ↓  │
   │  ↑  │  │  ↓  │   Wurster column (partition)
   │  ↑  │↑↑│  ↓  │
   │     │↑↑│     │
   │   spray nozzle│  coating sprayed upward into the column
   └──────┬───────┘
          ↑ hot air through perforated plate
```

### f. Pan coating

Larger solid cores (above about 600 µm) are tumbled in a rotating pan while coating solution is sprayed on, as in tablet coating.

### g. Spray drying and spray congealing

The core is dispersed in a solution or melt of the coating material, and the mixture is atomised into a chamber.

- **Spray drying**: hot air evaporates the solvent, leaving coated particles. Fast and continuous; used for flavours, vitamins and heat-tolerant drugs.
- **Spray congealing**: the coating is a molten wax, fatty acid or fat; the droplets solidify on cooling. No solvent needed.

### h. Multi-orifice centrifugal process

A rotating cylinder with orifices throws core particles through a film of molten or dissolved coating material held across the orifices; the coated particles are then hardened. Gives high output.

## 2.4 Evaluation of microcapsules and microspheres

1. Percentage yield.
2. Particle size and size distribution (optical microscopy, sieving, laser diffraction).
3. Surface morphology (scanning electron microscopy).
4. Drug content and encapsulation (entrapment) efficiency = (actual drug content ÷ theoretical drug content) × 100.
5. Flow properties (angle of repose, Carr's index).
6. In vitro release (dissolution apparatus).
7. Swelling, buoyancy (for floating microspheres), mucoadhesion (for mucoadhesive microspheres).
8. Stability and residual solvent content.

## 2.5 Applications

| Use | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Sustained release | Aspirin and potassium chloride microcapsules |
| Taste masking | Bitter antibiotics in suspensions |
| Liquid to solid | Vitamin A and fish oil powders |
| Protection | Vitamins and probiotics from moisture and acid |
| Depot injection | Biodegradable PLGA microspheres of peptide drugs releasing over weeks |
| Reducing irritation | Microencapsulated potassium chloride spread over the gut |
| Non-pharmaceutical | Carbonless copy paper, fragrances in textiles, agrochemicals |

## Part B: Mucosal (mucoadhesive) drug delivery systems

## 2.6 Introduction

**Bioadhesion** is the attachment of a material to a biological surface for a long time by interfacial forces. When the surface is a mucous membrane covered with mucus, it is called **mucoadhesion**. Mucoadhesive systems keep the dosage form in close contact with the absorbing surface, so that absorption is better and longer.

Mucosal routes include buccal and sublingual (oral cavity), nasal, ocular, vaginal, rectal and gastrointestinal.

**Mucus** is a sticky gel secreted by goblet cells. It is mostly water with **mucin** glycoproteins (protein backbone carrying many sugar chains ending in sialic acid and sulfate groups), so it has a negative charge and many hydrogen-bonding sites.

## 2.7 Mechanism of mucoadhesion

Mucoadhesion happens in two stages:

```text
1. Contact stage        dosage form touches mucus; it wets and swells
        ↓
2. Consolidation stage  moisture activates the polymer; polymer chains
                        loosen and interpenetrate mucin chains; weak
                        bonds form → firm, long-lasting adhesion
```

### Theories of mucoadhesion

| Theory | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| Electronic | Transfer of electrons between polymer and mucus creates an electrical double layer and attraction |
| Adsorption | Hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces and hydrophobic interactions after initial contact |
| Wetting | Applies to liquid systems: the better the system spreads on mucus (low contact angle), the better it sticks |
| Diffusion (interpenetration) | Polymer chains and mucin chains diffuse into each other and entangle; depends on chain flexibility and contact time |
| Fracture | Relates adhesion strength to the force needed to separate the two surfaces after adhesion |
| Mechanical | Liquid adhesive fills irregularities of a rough surface and locks in |

No single theory explains all cases; usually several act together.

### Factors affecting mucoadhesion

**Polymer-related**: molecular weight (an optimum, often high), chain flexibility, degree of cross-linking (high cross-linking reduces chain mobility), hydrogen-bonding groups (–COOH, –OH), concentration (optimum), swelling and charge (anionic polymers bond well; cationic chitosan binds negatively charged mucin).

**Environment-related**: pH (affects ionisation of polymer and mucin), contact time, applied pressure, initial hydration.

**Physiological**: mucin turnover, disease states, saliva flow and movement of tongue or food.

### Mucoadhesive polymers

| Generation / type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Anionic | Carbomer (polyacrylic acid), polycarbophil, sodium carboxymethylcellulose, sodium alginate |
| Cationic | Chitosan |
| Non-ionic | HPMC, hydroxypropyl cellulose, polyvinylpyrrolidone, polyethylene oxide |
| Newer (second generation) | Thiolated polymers (thiomers) that form disulfide bonds with mucin, lectins that bind specific sugars |

Ideal properties: non-toxic and non-irritant, adheres quickly to moist tissue, has site specificity if possible, compatible with the drug, does not hinder release, inexpensive.

## 2.8 Advantages and disadvantages of mucosal delivery

**Advantages**

1. Prolonged residence time at the site, allowing once- or twice-daily dosing.
2. Intimate contact gives better absorption.
3. Buccal and sublingual absorption bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism and acid or enzyme breakdown in the gut.
4. Rapid onset for sublingual systems (glyceryl trinitrate).
5. Easy to give and to remove if needed.
6. Local action at the site (mouth ulcers, fungal infections).

**Disadvantages**

1. Small surface area for absorption in the mouth.
2. Saliva washes away dissolved drug, which is then swallowed.
3. Eating and drinking may be restricted.
4. Unsuitable for drugs that irritate the mucosa, taste bad or are unstable at buccal pH.
5. Only small doses can be given.
6. Patient may swallow the system accidentally.

## 2.9 Transmucosal permeability

The oral cavity is lined by stratified squamous epithelium. The buccal mucosa (inside of the cheek) is thicker and non-keratinised; the sublingual mucosa is thinner and more permeable; the gums and hard palate are keratinised and less permeable.

| Feature | Sublingual | Buccal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Epithelium | Thin, non-keratinised | Thicker, non-keratinised |
| Permeability | High | Moderate |
| Onset | Rapid | Slower, suitable for sustained delivery |
| Retention of dosage form | Poor (tongue movement, saliva) | Good with mucoadhesive patches |

**Main barrier**: the upper layers of the epithelium, together with lipids secreted from membrane-coating granules into the intercellular spaces. Saliva and mucus also act as barriers.

**Routes of transport**

1. **Paracellular** (between cells, through intercellular lipids): used by hydrophilic drugs.
2. **Transcellular** (through cells): used by lipophilic drugs; the main route for most small drugs.

**Permeation enhancers** for buccal delivery: surfactants (sodium lauryl sulfate), bile salts (sodium glycocholate, sodium deoxycholate), fatty acids (oleic acid), chelators (EDTA), cyclodextrins, azone, chitosan.

## 2.10 Formulation considerations of buccal delivery systems

**Types of buccal dosage forms**: mucoadhesive tablets, patches and films, gels and ointments, sprays, lozenges, wafers.

**Ideal drug**: small single dose, good stability at mouth pH, no bitter taste, no irritation, short half-life, significant first-pass metabolism by the oral route.

**Components of a buccal patch**

```text
     ┌─────────────────────────────┐  backing layer (impermeable: ethyl
     │       backing membrane      │  cellulose) — stops drug loss into saliva
     ├─────────────────────────────┤
     │  drug + mucoadhesive polymer│  drug reservoir / matrix layer
     │  + plasticiser + enhancer   │
     ├─────────────────────────────┤
     │   mucoadhesive surface      │  sticks to buccal mucosa
     └─────────────────────────────┘  → unidirectional release toward mucosa
```

| Component | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug | Active | Nitroglycerin, buprenorphine, nicotine |
| Mucoadhesive polymer | Adhesion and release control | Carbomer, HPMC, sodium CMC, chitosan |
| Backing membrane | Impermeable to drug, gives one-way release | Ethyl cellulose |
| Permeation enhancer | Increases absorption | Bile salts, surfactants |
| Plasticiser | Flexibility of film | Propylene glycol, PEG, glycerol |
| Others | Taste and stability | Sweeteners, flavours, enzyme inhibitors |

**Evaluation**: thickness, weight and drug content uniformity, surface pH, swelling index, folding endurance (for films), mucoadhesive strength (force needed to detach the system from mucosa, measured with a modified balance or texture analyser), residence time, in vitro release and ex vivo permeation through excised buccal mucosa (Franz diffusion cell).

## Part C: Implantable drug delivery systems

## 2.11 Introduction

**Implants** are sterile solid or semisolid devices placed in the body (usually under the skin, in a muscle or in a body cavity) by surgery or by injection through a wide needle, to release drug for a long period, from weeks to several years.

```text
Implants
├── Non-biodegradable (removed after use)
│     ├── Reservoir (membrane-controlled): silicone capsules (Norplant)
│     └── Matrix: EVA rod (Implanon)
├── Biodegradable (no removal)
│     └── PLGA, PLA, polyanhydrides: goserelin implant (Zoladex),
│         carmustine wafer (Gliadel) placed in the brain after surgery
├── Pump-type (activated)
│     ├── Osmotic pumps (Alzet for research, DUROS implant)
│     ├── Infusion pumps (insulin, chemotherapy)
│     └── Vapour-pressure-powered pumps (Infusaid)
└── In situ forming implants: polymer solution injected, sets in the body
```

## 2.12 Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages**

1. Very long duration of action, from weeks to years.
2. Better patient compliance; no daily dosing.
3. Avoids first-pass metabolism and the gut.
4. Delivers drug close to the site of action (brain wafer, intravitreal implant).
5. Lower total amount of drug and fewer side effects.
6. Treatment can be stopped by removing a non-biodegradable implant.

**Disadvantages**

1. Surgery or a trocar is needed to insert and sometimes to remove.
2. Pain, discomfort, infection or scarring at the site.
3. Possible migration or breakage of the device.
4. Dose cannot be changed once implanted.
5. Toxicity of polymer or degradation products must be ruled out.
6. Dose dumping if a reservoir ruptures.
7. High cost; limited to potent drugs.

**Ideal drug for implants**: potent, needed for a long time, stable at body temperature for the whole period, small dose, short half-life.

## 2.13 Concept of implants

Release from implants follows the same principles as in Unit 1:

- **Membrane-controlled (reservoir)**: drug core inside a polymer membrane (silicone or EVA); zero-order release while the core stays saturated. Example: levonorgestrel capsules of silicone rubber used for long-term contraception.
- **Matrix-diffusion controlled**: drug dispersed in a polymer rod; release falls as √t, unless the matrix is designed to give near-constant release. Example: etonogestrel EVA rod.
- **Biodegradable (erosion) systems**: drug released as the polymer erodes. PLGA erodes in bulk; polyanhydrides erode at the surface, giving more constant release.
- **Activated systems**: release driven by osmotic pressure, vapour pressure, magnetism, ultrasound or hydrolysis.

## 2.14 Osmotic pump

**Osmosis** is the movement of water through a semipermeable membrane from a dilute solution to a concentrated one. Osmotic pumps use the osmotic pressure created inside a device to push drug out at a constant rate.

### a. Elementary osmotic pump (EOP)

```text
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐
   │  semipermeable membrane       │   cellulose acetate: lets water in,
   │  ┌─────────────────────────┐  │   not drug out
   │  │ drug + osmotic agent    │  │
   │  │  (core forms saturated  │  │   water drawn in by osmosis
   │  │   solution)             │  ○ ← laser-drilled delivery orifice
   │  └─────────────────────────┘  │   saturated drug solution pumped out
   └───────────────────────────────┘
```

Water from the gut enters through the membrane, dissolves the drug and osmogen, and the saturated solution is pushed out through a small orifice. While solid drug remains in the core, the concentration inside stays constant, so release is **zero order**.

Release rate (simplified):

dM/dt = (A k / h) (Δπ − ΔP) Cs

where A is the membrane area, k the membrane permeability to water, h the membrane thickness, Δπ the osmotic pressure difference, ΔP the hydrostatic pressure difference (negligible when the orifice is large enough) and Cs the drug concentration in the pumped solution.

### b. Push–pull osmotic pump

A two-layer core: an upper drug layer and a lower "push" layer of a swelling polymer (polyethylene oxide) with an osmogen. Water makes the push layer swell and drives the drug layer out through the orifice. Suitable for poorly soluble drugs (example: nifedipine gastrointestinal therapeutic system).

### c. Components of osmotic systems

| Component | Role | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drug | Active | Water-soluble drugs suit EOP; insoluble drugs need push–pull |
| Osmotic agent (osmogen) | Creates osmotic pressure | Sodium chloride, potassium chloride, mannitol, sucrose |
| Semipermeable membrane | Lets water in, keeps drug in | Cellulose acetate, ethyl cellulose |
| Plasticiser and flux regulator | Adjust membrane flexibility and water flux | PEG, triacetin |
| Swelling polymer (push layer) | Pushes drug out | Polyethylene oxide |
| Delivery orifice | Exit for drug | Laser or mechanical drilling |

### d. Implantable osmotic pumps

- **Alzet pump**: small osmotic pump used in laboratory animals for research; delivers solutions at a constant rate for days to weeks.
- **DUROS implant**: titanium cylinder with an osmotic engine, piston and drug reservoir, delivering a potent peptide for months.

**Advantages of osmotic systems**: zero-order release, release largely independent of gut pH, food and motility, good in vitro–in vivo correlation. **Disadvantages**: cost, orifice blockage, irritation if a concentrated solution sits on the mucosa, dose dumping if the membrane fails.

## Remember

- Microcapsule = core + wall (reservoir); microsphere = matrix; size about 1–1000 µm.
- Coacervation steps: three phases → deposition of coacervate → hardening. Complex coacervation = gelatin (+) and acacia (−) at pH about 4–4.5.
- Other methods: solvent evaporation, ionic gelation (alginate + Ca²⁺), interfacial polymerisation, Wurster air suspension, pan coating, spray drying and congealing, multi-orifice centrifugal.
- Encapsulation efficiency = actual ÷ theoretical drug content × 100.
- Mucoadhesion: contact stage then consolidation stage; theories: electronic, adsorption, wetting, diffusion, fracture, mechanical.
- Mucoadhesive polymers: carbomer, polycarbophil, sodium CMC, HPMC, chitosan, alginate, thiomers.
- Buccal patch: backing layer + drug/polymer layer + adhesive surface; tests include mucoadhesive strength, surface pH and residence time.
- Implants: non-biodegradable (reservoir, matrix), biodegradable (PLGA, polyanhydrides), pumps, in situ forming.
- Elementary osmotic pump: cellulose acetate membrane + osmogen core + laser-drilled orifice → zero order; push–pull for poorly soluble drugs.
