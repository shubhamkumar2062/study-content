# Determination of potassium by flame photometry

**Aim:** To determine the potassium content of a given sample (for example a reconstituted oral rehydration salts solution or potassium chloride preparation) by flame photometry.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual, the instrument instructions and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles; keep the flame area clear.

## Principle

Potassium atoms in the flame are thermally excited and emit radiation at about **766 nm** (the violet colour seen in the flame comes from weaker lines). A potassium filter (about 767–768 nm) isolates this emission and the detector measures its intensity, which is proportional to concentration over a working range. Potassium is easily ionised in hot flames, which lowers the emission; a low-temperature flame (LPG–air) is used, and an ionisation suppressor may be added. The sample concentration is found from a calibration curve.

## Requirements

Flame photometer with potassium filter, LPG and air supply, volumetric flasks (50, 100, 1000 ml), pipettes; potassium chloride (dried at about 110 °C), deionised water, sample.

## Procedure

1. **Stock (1000 ppm K):** dissolve 1.907 g dried KCl in deionised water and make up to 1000 ml (1.907 g KCl contains 1 g K, from 74.55/39.10).
2. **Intermediate (100 ppm):** dilute 10 ml stock to 100 ml.
3. **Standards:** prepare 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 ppm K.
4. **Sample:** dilute to fall within the range (for ORS, 2 ml to 50 ml, factor 25).
5. Light the flame, select the potassium filter and allow warm-up.
6. Set zero with deionised water and full scale (100) with the 50 ppm standard.
7. Read each standard and the sample, aspirating water in between.
8. Recheck zero and full scale; plot the curve and calculate.

## Observations and calculations

> Example readings, for practice only — record your own.

| Solution | K (ppm) | Emission reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blank | 0 | 0 |
| S1 | 5 | 11 |
| S2 | 10 | 21 |
| S3 | 20 | 41 |
| S4 | 30 | 61 |
| S5 | 40 | 80 |
| S6 | 50 | 100 |
| Sample (diluted 1:25) | ? | 62.5 |

**Least-squares line:** reading = 1.975 × C + 1.32 (r = 0.99997).

**Diluted sample:** C = (62.5 − 1.32)/1.975 = **31.0 ppm K**.

**Original sample:** 31.0 × 25 = **775 mg/L K**, that is 775/39.10 = **19.8 mmol/L** potassium.

**As KCl (if needed):** mg KCl = mg K × 1.907.

## Precautions

1. Use deionised water and rinsed plastic or glassware.
2. Keep gas and air pressures steady; let the flame stabilise.
3. Large amounts of sodium in the sample can raise potassium readings; match the standards with the sample matrix if needed.
4. Clean the nebuliser by aspirating water between solutions.
5. Work within the linear range.

## Viva questions

1. **What wavelength is used for potassium?** About 766 nm (filter 767–768 nm).
2. **Why does potassium show ionisation interference?** It has a low ionisation energy, so in hot flames some atoms become ions that do not emit the atomic line.
3. **How is ionisation interference reduced?** Cooler flame, or adding an ionisation suppressor such as caesium.
4. **Why do we use a calibration curve?** Emission is linear only over a limited range and depends on flame conditions.
5. **What detectors are used?** Photovoltaic cell, phototube or photomultiplier tube.
6. **Clinical relevance?** Serum potassium monitoring in kidney disease and diuretic therapy.
