# Separation of sugars by thin layer chromatography

**Aim:** To separate and identify sugars in a given mixture by thin layer chromatography (TLC) and to calculate their Rf values.

> Solvent and detecting reagent vary between laboratories (other common systems use ethyl acetate–isopropanol–water or chloroform–acetic acid–water). Follow your lab manual and your teacher. Wear goggles and gloves; spray and heat plates in a fume hood.

## Principle

Sugars are very polar and are held strongly on silica gel. On a thin layer of silica gel (sometimes impregnated with boric acid or sodium acetate to sharpen the spots), sugars separate by adsorption and partition with a polar mobile phase. Disaccharides (sucrose, lactose) are more polar and larger than monosaccharides (glucose, fructose), so they usually move less. Sugars are colourless and do not absorb at 254 nm, so they are made visible with a spray reagent and heating, for example **aniline–diphenylamine–phosphoric acid** reagent or **anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid**, which give coloured spots. Identification is by Rf and comparison with standards on the same plate.

## Requirements

Glass plates (20 × 20 or 20 × 5 cm) or pre-coated silica gel plates, TLC spreader, silica gel G, developing tank with lid, capillaries, oven (100–110 °C), sprayer, hot plate or oven for colour development; mobile phase, for example n-butanol : acetic acid : water (4 : 1 : 1 or as per manual); detecting reagent; 1% solutions of glucose, fructose, sucrose and lactose in water–ethanol, unknown mixture.

## Procedure

1. **Plate preparation:** make a slurry of silica gel G with water (about 1 : 2 w/v), spread a uniform 0.25 mm layer, air dry and activate at 100–110 °C for 30 minutes. (Pre-coated plates need only activation.)
2. Saturate the tank with mobile phase (filter paper lining) for 30 minutes.
3. Draw a light pencil line 1.5–2 cm from the bottom; spot standards and mixture 1.5 cm apart with fine capillaries; dry.
4. Place the plate in the tank (spots above the solvent), close and develop until the solvent is about 1 cm from the top (for long runs, develop twice after drying).
5. Remove the plate, mark the solvent front and dry in warm air.
6. Spray evenly with the detecting reagent and heat at about 105–120 °C for 5–10 minutes until spots appear.
7. Measure distances, calculate Rf values and identify the sugars in the mixture.

## Precautions

1. Spread an even layer and activate it; moist plates give poor separation.
2. Keep spots small; overloading causes streaking (sugars streak easily).
3. Saturate the tank and keep the lid closed.
4. Do not disturb the tank during development.
5. Handle acid spray reagents and hot plates with care, in a fume hood.

## Viva questions

1. **What does G in silica gel G mean?** Gypsum (calcium sulphate) binder.
2. **Why are plates activated?** Heating removes adsorbed water and makes the adsorbent active.
3. **Why do disaccharides have lower Rf?** They are more polar and larger, so they are held more strongly.
4. **How are sugars detected?** Spray reagents such as aniline–diphenylamine–phosphoric acid or anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid with heating.
5. **Advantages of TLC over paper chromatography?** Faster, sharper spots, more sensitive, corrosive reagents can be used.
6. **What is HPTLC?** High-performance TLC with finer particles, automatic application and densitometric scanning for quantitation.
