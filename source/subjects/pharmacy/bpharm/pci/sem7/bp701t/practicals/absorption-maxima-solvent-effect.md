# Absorption maxima and effect of solvents on the absorption maxima of organic compounds

**Aim:** To record the UV absorption spectrum of an organic compound, find its absorption maximum (λmax), and study how the λmax changes in solvents of different polarity.

> Quantities and compounds are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles, a lab coat and gloves; keep organic solvents away from flames.

## Principle

When a molecule absorbs UV or visible light, electrons move from bonding or non-bonding orbitals to antibonding orbitals (π → π*, n → π*). The wavelength of greatest absorption is λmax. The solvent changes the energy gap:

- **π → π* bands** move to a **longer** wavelength (bathochromic shift) in more polar solvents, because the polar excited state is stabilised more than the ground state.
- **n → π* bands** move to a **shorter** wavelength (hypsochromic shift) in polar, hydrogen-bonding solvents, because the lone pair in the ground state is stabilised.
- Polar solvents also smooth out vibrational fine structure.

Each solvent must be transparent above its cut-off wavelength (water about 190 nm, methanol and ethanol about 205–210 nm, cyclohexane or hexane about 200–210 nm, chloroform about 245 nm).

## Requirements

Double-beam UV-visible spectrophotometer, matched 1 cm quartz cells, volumetric flasks (10, 100 ml), pipettes; test compound (for example p-nitroaniline, benzoic acid, acetone or a drug named by the teacher); spectroscopic-grade solvents: hexane or cyclohexane, chloroform, ethanol or methanol, water (and 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH for ionisable compounds).

## Procedure

1. Switch on the spectrophotometer 15–30 minutes before use so the lamps stabilise.
2. Prepare a stock solution of the compound (for example 100 µg/ml) in each solvent.
3. Dilute each stock to give a working solution (for example 5–10 µg/ml) so that the peak absorbance lies between 0.3 and 0.8.
4. Fill both cells with the pure solvent and record a baseline (blank) over the scan range (for example 200–400 nm; start above the solvent cut-off).
5. Replace the solvent in the sample cell with the working solution and scan the spectrum.
6. Note the λmax and the absorbance at λmax.
7. Repeat steps 4–6 for each solvent, rinsing the cell with the next solution before filling.
8. For an ionisable compound, record spectra in 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH as well.

## Observations and calculations

> Example readings, for practice only — record your own.

| Solvent | Polarity | λmax (nm) | Absorbance at λmax | Shift compared with hexane |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hexane | Non-polar | 320 | 0.52 | — |
| Chloroform | Moderate | 340 | 0.55 | +20 nm (bathochromic) |
| Ethanol | Polar, protic | 365 | 0.58 | +45 nm (bathochromic) |
| Water | Most polar | 380 | 0.60 | +60 nm (bathochromic) |

**Molar absorptivity at λmax:** ε = A/(b × c). For a 5 µg/ml solution of a compound of M = 138 (c = 5 × 10⁻³ g/L ÷ 138 = 3.62 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L) with A = 0.58, ε = 0.58/(1 × 3.62 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 16,000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. A value above 10,000 points to a π → π* band.

**Interpretation:** a steady red shift with rising solvent polarity and a large ε indicate a π → π* transition. A weak band (ε below about 100) that moves to shorter wavelength in polar solvents is an n → π* transition.

## Precautions

1. Use spectroscopic-grade solvents and never scan below the solvent cut-off.
2. Hold cells by the frosted sides; clean the clear faces with lens tissue.
3. Rinse cells with the next solution before filling; remove air bubbles.
4. Keep absorbance within 0.2–0.8 by dilution.
5. Close volatile solvents quickly to avoid concentration changes.

## Viva questions

1. **What is λmax?** The wavelength at which the compound absorbs most strongly; assays are done there because the curve is flat and sensitivity is highest.
2. **Why does a π → π* band shift to longer wavelength in polar solvents?** The excited state is more polar and is stabilised more, so the energy gap falls.
3. **Why does an n → π* band show a blue shift in water?** Hydrogen bonding stabilises the lone pair in the ground state, raising the energy needed.
4. **What is a solvent cut-off?** The wavelength below which the solvent itself absorbs strongly.
5. **Why are quartz cells used?** Glass absorbs UV radiation below about 350 nm.
6. **Name the shifts.** Bathochromic (red), hypsochromic (blue), hyperchromic (more intense), hypochromic (less intense).
