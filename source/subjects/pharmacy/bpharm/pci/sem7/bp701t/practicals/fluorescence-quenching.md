# Study of quenching of fluorescence

**Aim:** To study the quenching of quinine sulfate fluorescence by chloride ions and to find the Stern–Volmer constant.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles and gloves.

## Principle

**Quenching** is any process that lowers fluorescence intensity. Halide ions quench quinine fluorescence mainly by **collision** with the excited molecule (dynamic quenching), taking away its energy before it can be emitted. For collisional quenching the **Stern–Volmer equation** applies:

**F0/F = 1 + Ksv [Q]**

where F0 is the fluorescence without quencher, F the fluorescence with quencher concentration [Q], and Ksv the Stern–Volmer quenching constant. A plot of F0/F against [Q] is a straight line with intercept 1 and slope Ksv. Bromide and iodide quench more strongly than chloride.

## Requirements

Fluorimeter, fluorescence cells, volumetric flasks (50 or 100 ml), pipettes; quinine sulfate solution (for example 1 µg/ml in 0.05 M H2SO4), sodium chloride solution (for example 1 M NaCl), 0.05 M sulphuric acid.

## Procedure

1. Prepare a series of flasks each containing the same volume of quinine solution (so that the final quinine concentration is the same, for example 0.5 µg/ml).
2. Add increasing volumes of NaCl solution so that the final chloride concentrations are 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03 and 0.04 M.
3. Make up each flask to volume with 0.05 M H2SO4 and mix.
4. Set the fluorimeter (excitation about 350 nm, emission about 450 nm); set zero with 0.05 M H2SO4 and 100 with the flask containing no chloride (F0).
5. Read the fluorescence (F) of each solution without changing the settings.
6. Calculate F0/F and plot it against chloride concentration.
7. Optionally repeat with KBr to compare quenching strength.

## Observations and calculations

> Example readings, for practice only — record your own.

| [Cl⁻] (M) | Fluorescence F | F0/F |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 100.0 | 1.000 |
| 0.005 | 88.9 | 1.125 |
| 0.010 | 80.0 | 1.250 |
| 0.020 | 66.7 | 1.499 |
| 0.030 | 57.1 | 1.751 |
| 0.040 | 50.0 | 2.000 |

**Stern–Volmer plot:** F0/F = 25.0 × [Cl⁻] + 1.00 (r = 1.000).

**Ksv = slope = 25 M⁻¹.** The chloride concentration that halves the fluorescence (F0/F = 2) is 1/Ksv = 0.04 M.

A linear plot is typical of a single (collisional) quenching mechanism; upward curvature suggests combined static and dynamic quenching.

## Result

1. Fluorescence of quinine decreases as chloride concentration increases.
2. The Stern–Volmer constant for quenching by chloride is ___ M⁻¹.

## Precautions

1. Keep the quinine concentration, temperature and acid strength the same in all flasks.
2. Use chloride-free water and glassware for the reference solution.
3. Read all solutions within a short time and at the same settings.
4. Rinse the cell with the next solution before filling.

## Viva questions

1. **Define quenching.** Any process that reduces the intensity of fluorescence.
2. **Types of quenching?** Collisional (dynamic), static (complex formation), concentration (self-quenching, inner filter), chemical, temperature.
3. **Write the Stern–Volmer equation.** F0/F = 1 + Ksv[Q].
4. **Why is HCl avoided in quinine assay?** Chloride quenches its fluorescence.
5. **How do temperature and viscosity affect collisional quenching?** Higher temperature and lower viscosity increase collisions and quenching.
6. **Practical use of quenching?** Estimation of halides, study of drug–protein binding.
