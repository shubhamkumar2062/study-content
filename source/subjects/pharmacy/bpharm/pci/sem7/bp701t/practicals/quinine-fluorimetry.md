# Estimation of quinine sulfate by fluorimetry

**Aim:** To estimate the concentration of quinine sulfate in a given sample by fluorimetry using a calibration curve.

> Quantities are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear goggles and gloves.

## Principle

Quinine is strongly fluorescent in dilute sulphuric acid. It absorbs UV light near **350 nm** (the 365 nm mercury line is often used in filter fluorimeters) and emits blue fluorescence near **450 nm**. For dilute solutions the fluorescence intensity is directly proportional to concentration:

**F = K φ I0 ε b c**

A calibration curve of fluorescence against concentration of standard solutions is used to find the unknown. Halide ions (chloride) quench quinine fluorescence, so HCl must not be used, and glassware must be free of chloride.

## Requirements

Fluorimeter or spectrofluorimeter, quartz (or glass for filter instruments) fluorescence cells polished on four sides, volumetric flasks (100 ml), pipettes; quinine sulfate, 0.05 M (0.1 N) sulphuric acid, distilled water, sample.

## Procedure

1. **Stock:** dissolve 10 mg quinine sulfate in 0.05 M H2SO4 and make up to 100 ml (100 µg/ml). Dilute 1 ml to 100 ml with 0.05 M H2SO4 (1 µg/ml).
2. **Standards:** dilute the 1 µg/ml solution with 0.05 M H2SO4 to give 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 µg/ml.
3. **Sample:** dilute with 0.05 M H2SO4 so that it falls in the standard range.
4. Set the excitation at about 350 nm (or select the primary filter) and emission at about 450 nm (secondary filter).
5. Set zero with 0.05 M H2SO4 (blank).
6. Set the reading of the highest standard to 100 (full scale) with the sensitivity control.
7. Read the fluorescence of each standard and the sample without changing settings.
8. Plot fluorescence against concentration and read the sample concentration.

## Observations and calculations

> Example readings, for practice only — record your own.

| Solution | Quinine sulfate (µg/ml) | Relative fluorescence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blank | 0 | 0.0 |
| S1 | 0.2 | 19.8 |
| S2 | 0.4 | 40.1 |
| S3 | 0.6 | 60.3 |
| S4 | 0.8 | 79.6 |
| S5 | 1.0 | 100.0 |
| Sample | ? | 54.0 |

**Least-squares line:** F = 99.95 × C − 0.01 (r = 0.99996).

**Sample:** C = (54.0 + 0.01)/99.95 = **0.540 µg/ml** in the measured solution. Multiply by the dilution factor to get the concentration in the original sample.

## Result

The given sample contains ___ µg/ml of quinine sulfate.

## Precautions

1. Use 0.05 M sulphuric acid, never HCl, and chloride-free glassware.
2. Keep all solutions at the same temperature; fluorescence falls as temperature rises.
3. Protect solutions from strong light; read promptly.
4. Do not touch the clear faces of the cell (all four sides are used).
5. Work at low concentrations; high concentrations give self-quenching and a curved plot.

## Viva questions

1. **Why is quinine measured in sulphuric acid?** Protonated quinine is strongly fluorescent; sulphate does not quench.
2. **Why is the detector at 90°?** To avoid transmitted excitation light reaching it.
3. **What are the excitation and emission wavelengths?** About 350 nm and 450 nm.
4. **Why is fluorimetry more sensitive than absorption spectroscopy?** Emitted light is measured against a dark background, not a small fall in a bright beam.
5. **What is Stokes shift?** Emission occurs at a longer wavelength than excitation because energy is lost by vibrational relaxation.
6. **What is a quencher? Example?** A substance that lowers fluorescence, such as chloride ions or dissolved oxygen.
