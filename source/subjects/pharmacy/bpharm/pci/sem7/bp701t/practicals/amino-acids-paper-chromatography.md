# Separation of amino acids by paper chromatography

**Aim:** To separate and identify the amino acids in a given mixture by ascending paper chromatography and to calculate their Rf values.

> Quantities and solvent are typical for a student batch. Follow your lab manual and your teacher where they differ. Wear gloves when handling the paper (fingerprints contain amino acids) and use ninhydrin spray in a fume hood.

## Principle

Paper chromatography is mainly **partition chromatography**. Water held by the cellulose fibres of the paper is the stationary phase; the moving organic solvent is the mobile phase. Amino acids distribute between the two according to their polarity: more polar amino acids stay longer in the water phase and move less, while less polar ones (with larger non-polar side chains such as leucine) move further. After development, the colourless amino acids are located by spraying with **ninhydrin**, which gives purple spots (Ruhemann's purple; proline gives yellow). Each spot is identified by its **Rf value** and by comparison with reference amino acids run on the same paper.

**Rf = distance travelled by the amino acid / distance travelled by the solvent front**

## Requirements

Whatman No. 1 chromatography paper, chromatography jar with lid, capillary tubes, pencil and ruler, hair dryer, oven (100–105 °C), sprayer; solvent: n-butanol : glacial acetic acid : water (4 : 1 : 5, upper layer after shaking and separating; or 4 : 1 : 1 as per manual); 0.2% ninhydrin in acetone or n-butanol; 1% solutions of standard amino acids (for example glycine, alanine, leucine), unknown mixture.

## Procedure

1. Prepare the solvent, place it in the jar to a depth of about 1 cm, close the lid and let the jar saturate for 30–60 minutes.
2. Cut the paper to size; draw a pencil base line about 2–3 cm from the bottom and mark points 2 cm apart.
3. Spot each standard and the mixture on separate points with a fine capillary; keep spots small (2–3 mm) and dry between applications.
4. Hang or stand the paper in the jar so that the base line is above the solvent level and the paper does not touch the walls; close the lid.
5. Develop until the solvent rises 12–15 cm (or near the top).
6. Remove the paper, mark the solvent front with pencil at once and dry it.
7. Spray evenly with ninhydrin reagent and heat at 100–105 °C for 3–5 minutes until purple spots appear.
8. Outline the spots, measure the distance from the base line to the centre of each spot and to the solvent front, and calculate Rf values.
9. Identify the components of the mixture by matching Rf values and colours with the standards.

## Precautions

1. Handle the paper only at the edges and wear gloves.
2. Draw lines with pencil, never ink (ink runs with the solvent).
3. Keep spots small and the base line above the solvent.
4. Saturate the jar and keep it closed during development.
5. Mark the solvent front immediately after taking out the paper.
6. Spray ninhydrin in a fume hood; avoid inhaling solvents.

## Viva questions

1. **What is the stationary phase in paper chromatography?** Water held by the cellulose fibres.
2. **Why is ninhydrin used?** It reacts with α-amino acids to give purple Ruhemann's purple; proline and hydroxyproline give yellow.
3. **Which amino acid moves furthest and why?** Those with larger non-polar side chains (leucine), since they are less polar.
4. **What is Rf? Can it exceed 1?** Ratio of solute to solvent-front distance; it is always below 1.
5. **What is two-dimensional chromatography?** Development in two solvents at right angles to separate complex mixtures.
6. **Why saturate the chamber?** To prevent evaporation from the paper and give reproducible Rf values.
