# Unit 3: Column, thin layer and paper chromatography; electrophoresis

Chromatography separates the parts of a mixture so that each can be identified or measured. The simple forms covered here (column, thin layer and paper) are still used every day for identity tests, purity checks and purification. Electrophoresis separates charged substances in an electric field and is the standard tool for proteins and nucleic acids.

## 3.1 Introduction to chromatography

**Chromatography** is a separation method in which the components of a mixture are distributed between two phases: a **stationary phase** (solid, or a liquid held on a solid) and a **mobile phase** (liquid or gas) that moves through it. Components that interact more strongly with the stationary phase move more slowly, so they separate.

The method was first described by Mikhail Tswett (1906), who separated plant pigments on a column of calcium carbonate and saw coloured bands, which gave the name (chroma = colour, graphein = to write).

**Basic terms**

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Stationary phase | Phase that stays fixed: silica gel, alumina, paper, liquid film |
| Mobile phase (eluent) | Phase that moves: solvent or gas |
| Elution | Washing components through the column with the mobile phase |
| Eluate | Liquid that comes out of the column |
| Chromatogram | The visible pattern of separated spots or bands, or the detector trace |
| Distribution (partition) coefficient K | Concentration in stationary phase / concentration in mobile phase; larger K, slower movement |
| Rf (retardation factor) | Distance moved by the solute / distance moved by the solvent front (always 0 to 1) |

**Classification**

| Basis | Types |
| --- | --- |
| Mechanism | Adsorption, partition, ion exchange, size exclusion (gel), affinity |
| Physical state of phases | Liquid–solid (column, TLC), liquid–liquid (paper, partition column), gas–liquid (GLC), gas–solid (GSC) |
| Shape of bed | Column (planar or packed tube) vs planar (paper, thin layer) |
| Purpose | Analytical vs preparative |

**Adsorption** depends on attraction of solutes to the surface of a solid (polar groups held on polar silica or alumina). **Partition** depends on the different solubility of solutes in two liquid phases, one of which is held on a support.

## 3.2 Adsorption column chromatography

### Methodology

```text
 COLUMN CHROMATOGRAPHY

        solvent reservoir
              |
        [  mobile phase  ]
        [ sample band    ]   <- sample applied as a narrow band on top
        [ ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ ]   <- sand or filter paper disc protects the surface
        [  adsorbent     ]   <- e.g. silica gel or alumina, packed without air bubbles
        [  band A        ]
        [  band B        ]
        [  glass wool    ]
              |
           stopcock -> fractions collected in tubes
```

1. **Column:** a glass tube with a stopcock; length to diameter ratio about 10:1 to 30:1; plug of glass wool or a sintered disc at the bottom.
2. **Adsorbent (stationary phase):** silica gel, alumina (acidic, neutral or basic), magnesium oxide, calcium carbonate, charcoal, starch, sucrose, cellulose. It should be insoluble, inert, colourless, of uniform particle size (about 60–200 mesh) and of suitable activity. Alumina activity is graded by water content (Brockmann grades I to V; grade I most active).

Order of adsorbent strength (strongest first): alumina, charcoal > silica gel > magnesium oxide > calcium carbonate > starch, sucrose, cellulose.

3. **Packing:**
   - **Wet (slurry) packing:** adsorbent mixed with the solvent and poured in, tapping to settle evenly; gives a uniform bed without air bubbles (preferred).
   - **Dry packing:** dry adsorbent added in portions and tapped down, then solvent run through; air may be trapped and cracks may form.
4. **Sample application:** sample dissolved in a minimum of the least polar solvent and applied evenly on top as a narrow band (or adsorbed on a little adsorbent and placed on top).
5. **Elution:** solvents are passed through in order of increasing polarity (the **eluotropic series**):

   Petroleum ether < cyclohexane < carbon tetrachloride < toluene < benzene < chloroform < diethyl ether < ethyl acetate < acetone < ethanol < methanol < water < acetic acid.

   - **Isocratic elution:** the same solvent throughout.
   - **Stepwise (gradient) elution:** solvent polarity increased step by step or continuously.
   - **Frontal analysis** and **displacement analysis** are other modes.
6. **Detection and collection:** coloured bands are seen directly; colourless ones by collecting fractions and testing each (TLC, UV, chemical tests) or by UV fluorescence on the column.
7. **Recovery:** fractions with the same component are pooled and the solvent is evaporated, or the column is extruded and bands cut out (dry column).

### Factors affecting separation

Particle size and activity of adsorbent, column dimensions, solvent polarity, flow rate, temperature, sample size (overloading spoils separation), and even packing.

## 3.3 Partition column chromatography

The stationary phase is a **liquid** (usually water or a buffer) held on an inert support such as silica gel, kieselguhr (diatomaceous earth), Celite or cellulose. The mobile phase is an immiscible organic solvent. Solutes separate according to their **partition coefficient** between the two liquids. When the stationary liquid is non-polar and the mobile phase polar, it is called **reversed-phase** partition. Martin and Synge (1941) used it to separate amino acids and received the Nobel Prize.

**Advantages of column chromatography (adsorption and partition)**

- Any amount from milligrams to kilograms can be separated (preparative work).
- Simple apparatus, wide choice of adsorbents and solvents.
- Separated components can be recovered for further study.

**Disadvantages**

- Slow; large volumes of solvent; tedious collection of fractions.
- Resolution lower than HPLC; colourless substances need extra detection.
- Some substances decompose or are lost by irreversible adsorption on active surfaces.

**Applications**

- Purification of synthetic drugs and natural products; isolation of alkaloids, glycosides, steroids and plant pigments (chlorophylls, carotenoids).
- Separation of geometric isomers and diastereomers; removal of impurities.
- Concentrating dilute solutions and clean-up of samples before analysis.

## 3.4 Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

### Principle

A thin layer (about 0.25 mm) of adsorbent coated on a glass plate, aluminium foil or plastic sheet is the stationary phase. The sample is spotted near one end and the plate is developed in a closed chamber with solvent, which rises by **capillary action**. Separation is mainly by **adsorption** (on silica gel or alumina), and by partition when the layer holds water or is cellulose or reversed-phase.

### Methodology

1. **Preparation of plates:** a slurry of adsorbent with water is spread evenly by pouring, dipping, spraying or with a spreader (Stahl applicator). **Silica gel G** contains about 13% calcium sulphate (gypsum) as a binder; **silica gel H** has no binder; **F254** indicates a fluorescent indicator that glows under UV light at 254 nm. Ready-made pre-coated plates are now common.
2. **Activation:** plates are air-dried and then heated at **100–110 °C for 30–60 minutes** to remove water and activate the adsorbent.
3. **Application of sample:** dilute solution applied as a small spot (2–5 mm) with a capillary or micropipette about 1.5–2 cm above the lower edge; spots dried and kept apart.
4. **Development chamber:** a closed glass tank lined with filter paper wet with the solvent so that the air inside is **saturated** with solvent vapour (gives reproducible Rf values and avoids edge effects). Solvent level must be below the spots.
5. **Development:** usually ascending, until the solvent front is about 1 cm from the top (10–15 cm run). The front is marked at once.
6. **Detection (visualisation):**
   - Coloured substances: seen directly.
   - UV lamp at 254 nm (dark spots on fluorescent plates) or 366 nm (fluorescent compounds).
   - **Iodine vapour** (general; brown spots for most organic compounds).
   - Spray reagents: **ninhydrin** (amino acids, purple), **Dragendorff's reagent** (alkaloids, orange), anisaldehyde–sulphuric acid (sugars, steroids, terpenes), ferric chloride (phenols), 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (aldehydes and ketones), concentrated sulphuric acid with heat (charring of organic matter).
7. **Calculation of Rf** and comparison with reference standards run on the same plate.

### Rf value

**Rf = distance travelled by the centre of the spot / distance travelled by the solvent front** (both from the origin).

Example: solvent front 10.0 cm, spot centre 4.5 cm; Rf = 4.5/10.0 = **0.45**.

Rf depends on the adsorbent and its activity, thickness of the layer, solvent, temperature, chamber saturation, amount of sample and distance run. Because of this, a reference standard is run alongside, or **Rx** (distance moved by solute/distance moved by a reference substance) is used.

### Special techniques

- **Two-dimensional TLC:** develop in one solvent, turn the plate 90° and develop in a second solvent; separates complex mixtures.
- **Preparative TLC:** thicker layers (0.5–2 mm); bands scraped off and extracted.
- **HPTLC (high-performance TLC):** fine particles, automated application and densitometric scanning; quantitative.
- **Reversed-phase TLC** (C18-bonded silica) and **argentation TLC** (silver nitrate for unsaturated compounds).

### Advantages

- Fast (minutes), cheap, simple; many samples and standards run side by side.
- Very small amounts (µg) detected; corrosive spray reagents can be used on glass plates (not on paper).
- Better resolution and sharper spots than paper chromatography.

### Disadvantages

- Mainly qualitative unless a densitometer is used; reproducibility of Rf is limited.
- Plates are fragile; limited loading capacity; volatile compounds may be lost.

### Applications

- **Identity tests and related-substances (impurity) tests** in the pharmacopoeia.
- Checking the progress of reactions and purity of synthetic products.
- Identification of herbal drugs and their constituents (fingerprinting), detection of adulteration.
- Separation of amino acids, sugars, lipids, steroids, vitamins, dyes; forensic and toxicological screening.
- Choosing a solvent system for column chromatography.

## 3.5 Paper chromatography

### Introduction and principle

The stationary phase is **water held in the cellulose fibres** of chromatography paper (cellulose holds about 20% water), so the main mechanism is **partition** between this water and the moving organic solvent. Adsorption and ion exchange on cellulose also play a small part. Consden, Gordon and Martin (1944) introduced it for amino acids.

### Methodology

1. **Paper:** Whatman No. 1 (medium flow, general use), No. 3 (thicker, preparative), No. 4 (fast flow); modified papers (acetylated, impregnated with silicone or paraffin for reversed-phase, ion-exchange papers).
2. **Sample application:** spot with a capillary on a pencil line about 2–3 cm from the edge; dry between applications.
3. **Solvent (mobile phase):** usually a mixture of organic solvent with water and acid or base, such as **n-butanol : acetic acid : water (4 : 1 : 5, upper layer)** or phenol saturated with water.
4. **Chamber saturation** with solvent vapour, then development.
5. **Drying** the paper and **locating** spots (ninhydrin for amino acids, aniline hydrogen phthalate for sugars, UV light).
6. **Rf** calculation and comparison with standards. Quantitation by cutting out and eluting spots, or by measuring spot area.

### Development techniques

```text
 ASCENDING            DESCENDING                 RADIAL (circular)
  _____               trough with solvent         paper disc, wick in centre
 | top |              ___|___
 |     |             |       |                      .  .  .
 |  ^  | solvent     |   v   | solvent runs        .  (o)  .   rings spread outward
 |  |  | rises       |   v   | down by capillarity   .  .  .
 |__.__| spots       |       | and gravity
 ~~~~~~~ solvent     |_______|
```

| Technique | How | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ascending** | Paper dips into solvent at the bottom; solvent rises | Simple; slow at the top; most common |
| **Descending** | Solvent in a trough at the top flows down by capillarity and gravity | Faster; longer runs possible; solvent can run off the paper for slow components |
| **Ascending–descending** | Paper hung over a rod; solvent rises then descends | Longer path |
| **Radial (circular, horizontal)** | Spot at the centre of a paper disc; solvent fed through a wick; components form rings | Quick; sharp circular zones |
| **Two-dimensional** | Develop in one solvent, dry, turn 90°, develop in a second solvent | For complex mixtures such as protein hydrolysates |

### Advantages and disadvantages

**Advantages:** cheap, simple, small sample, good for polar compounds (amino acids, sugars), records can be kept.
**Disadvantages:** slow (hours), poorer resolution than TLC, cannot use corrosive reagents, limited to small quantities, paper may tear when wet.

### Applications

- Separation and identification of amino acids, sugars, organic acids, inorganic ions and vitamins.
- Identity tests of some drugs and check of purity; analysis of plant extracts.
- Historically used in metabolic studies with radioactive tracers.

**TLC vs paper chromatography**

| Point | TLC | Paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stationary phase | Adsorbent layer (silica, alumina) | Water held in cellulose |
| Main mechanism | Adsorption | Partition |
| Time | Short (20–60 min) | Long (hours) |
| Spot | Compact, sharp | Diffuse |
| Corrosive reagents | Can be used | Cannot be used |
| Sensitivity | Higher | Lower |

## 3.6 Electrophoresis

### Introduction

**Electrophoresis** is the movement of charged particles through a medium under an applied electric field. Positively charged particles move to the cathode (−) and negatively charged ones to the anode (+). Substances separate because they move at different speeds.

**Electrophoretic mobility (µ)** = velocity / field strength = **v/E**. For a spherical particle, µ = q / (6π η r), where q is the charge, η the viscosity of the medium and r the radius.

### Factors affecting electrophoretic mobility

1. **Charge of the molecule:** more charge, faster movement. For amphoteric molecules (proteins, amino acids) the charge depends on the buffer pH relative to the **isoelectric point (pI)**; at the pI the net charge is zero and the molecule does not move.
2. **Size and shape:** larger or more irregular molecules move more slowly (more friction).
3. **Electric field strength (voltage):** higher voltage, faster movement, but more heat.
4. **Buffer:** pH (sets the charge), ionic strength (high ionic strength lowers mobility and raises heating; low ionic strength gives broad zones), composition (some ions bind to solutes).
5. **Temperature:** heat lowers viscosity and raises mobility, but uneven heating distorts zones and may denature proteins.
6. **Supporting medium:** adsorption of solutes, **molecular sieving** in gels, and **electro-osmosis** (movement of the buffer itself relative to a charged support).
7. **Viscosity** of the medium and time of run.

### Paper electrophoresis

```text
 PAPER (ZONE) ELECTROPHORESIS

  (-) cathode                                   (+) anode
   [buffer]===== paper strip soaked in buffer =====[buffer]
                     ^ sample applied at centre
   cations move <-                           -> anions move
                  (power supply 100-500 V, or higher)
```

A strip of filter paper wet with buffer dips at its ends into buffer tanks holding the electrodes. The sample is applied, a voltage is applied for a set time, the paper is dried and stained (bromophenol blue or amido black for proteins, ninhydrin for amino acids). Used for serum proteins (albumin, α1, α2, β, γ globulins), amino acids and haemoglobin variants. Cellulose acetate is a better supporting medium (less adsorption, faster, clearer).

### Gel electrophoresis

The supporting medium is a gel that also acts as a molecular sieve.

- **Agarose gel:** large pores; used for DNA and RNA fragments and large proteins; stained with ethidium bromide or safer dyes.
- **Polyacrylamide gel (PAGE):** made by polymerising acrylamide with N,N′-methylene-bis-acrylamide (cross-linker) using ammonium persulphate and TEMED; pore size controlled by concentration; very high resolution for proteins.
- **SDS-PAGE:** sodium dodecyl sulphate denatures proteins and gives them a uniform negative charge per unit mass, so they separate by **molecular weight** only; mercaptoethanol breaks disulphide bonds. Proteins are stained with Coomassie blue or silver stain.
- **Isoelectric focusing:** proteins move in a pH gradient until they reach their pI, giving very sharp bands; combined with SDS-PAGE in **two-dimensional electrophoresis**.
- Gels can be slab (vertical or horizontal) or in tubes (disc electrophoresis).

### Capillary electrophoresis (CE)

```text
 CAPILLARY ELECTROPHORESIS

 [Source vial (+)] ====== fused-silica capillary (25-100 um ID, 30-100 cm) ======> [Destination vial (-)]
       ^ sample injected here                         |
                                              [On-column UV window -> detector -> data]
                High voltage supply 10-30 kV across the two vials
```

- A narrow **fused-silica capillary** filled with buffer; high voltage (10–30 kV); small heat because the capillary loses heat quickly.
- **Electro-osmotic flow (EOF):** the silanol groups on the inner wall are ionised, attract cations, and the bulk liquid moves toward the cathode; all species (cations, neutrals, anions) are carried past the detector.
- Injection by pressure (hydrodynamic) or by voltage (electrokinetic); nanolitre volumes.
- Detection by **UV absorbance** through a window in the capillary, fluorescence (laser-induced) or mass spectrometry.
- Modes: capillary zone electrophoresis, micellar electrokinetic chromatography (separates neutral molecules), capillary gel electrophoresis, capillary isoelectric focusing.
- Advantages: very high efficiency, fast, tiny sample and solvent use, automation.

### Applications of electrophoresis

- Separation and identification of serum proteins in diagnosis (myeloma, liver disease); haemoglobin variants (sickle cell).
- Purity and molecular weight of proteins and peptide drugs (insulin, vaccines, monoclonal antibodies) by SDS-PAGE.
- DNA fingerprinting, sequencing, PCR product analysis.
- Isoenzyme analysis (lactate dehydrogenase, creatine kinase).
- Chiral separation and assay of small drugs by CE (with cyclodextrins in the buffer).

## Remember

- Chromatography: stationary + mobile phase; separation by adsorption, partition, ion exchange, size, affinity.
- Column: wet packing preferred; elute with solvents of increasing polarity (eluotropic series).
- TLC: silica gel G (gypsum binder), activate at 100–110 °C, saturated chamber, detect with UV, iodine, ninhydrin, Dragendorff; Rf = solute distance/solvent front distance.
- Paper chromatography is partition (water in cellulose); ascending, descending, radial, two-dimensional development.
- Electrophoretic mobility µ = v/E = q/(6πηr); factors: charge (pH vs pI), size, voltage, buffer, temperature, medium, electro-osmosis.
- SDS-PAGE separates proteins by molecular weight; CE uses a fused-silica capillary, 10–30 kV and electro-osmotic flow.
