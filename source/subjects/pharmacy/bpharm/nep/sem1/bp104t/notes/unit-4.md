# Unit 4: Peripheral nervous system and special senses with their disorders

The peripheral nervous system links the brain and spinal cord to the rest of the body. Its autonomic part controls the heart, glands and smooth muscle, which makes it the base of autonomic pharmacology. This unit covers the divisions of the peripheral nervous system, the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems, spinal and cranial nerves, the eye, ear, nose and tongue, and the common disorders of these special senses.

## 4.1 Organisation of the nervous system

```text
NERVOUS SYSTEM
 |-- Central nervous system (CNS): brain and spinal cord
 |-- Peripheral nervous system (PNS): cranial nerves (12 pairs), spinal nerves (31 pairs),
      ganglia and sensory receptors
        |-- Afferent (sensory) division: carries impulses from receptors to the CNS
        |      somatic sensory (skin, muscles, joints, special senses)
        |      visceral sensory (internal organs)
        |-- Efferent (motor) division: carries impulses from the CNS to effectors
               |-- Somatic nervous system: to skeletal muscle (voluntary);
               |      one neuron from CNS to muscle; transmitter acetylcholine
               |-- Autonomic nervous system (ANS): to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle
                      and glands (involuntary); two neurons in series
                      |-- Sympathetic division
                      |-- Parasympathetic division
                      |-- (Enteric nervous system: network in the gut wall)
```

## 4.2 Autonomic nervous system: general plan

Each autonomic pathway has two neurons: a **preganglionic neuron** with its cell body in the CNS and a myelinated axon (B fibre), and a **postganglionic neuron** with its cell body in an autonomic ganglion and an unmyelinated axon (C fibre) to the effector.

```text
CNS --- preganglionic neuron ---> [ganglion] --- postganglionic neuron ---> effector
         (releases ACh,                          (sympathetic: mostly noradrenaline;
          acts on nicotinic NN                     parasympathetic: ACh on
          receptors in ganglion)                   muscarinic receptors)
```

The adrenal medulla is a special case: preganglionic sympathetic fibres end directly on its chromaffin cells, which act like postganglionic neurons and release adrenaline (about 80%) and noradrenaline into blood.

### Comparison of sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin (outflow) | Thoracolumbar: lateral horn of spinal segments T1–L2 | Craniosacral: cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X and sacral segments S2–S4 |
| Ganglia | Close to the spinal cord: paravertebral sympathetic chain and prevertebral ganglia (coeliac, superior and inferior mesenteric) | Close to or inside the target organ (ciliary, pterygopalatine, submandibular, otic ganglia; intramural ganglia) |
| Preganglionic fibre | Short | Long |
| Postganglionic fibre | Long | Short |
| Ratio pre:post | One preganglionic neuron to many postganglionic neurons (widespread effect) | One to few (localised effect) |
| Transmitter at ganglion | Acetylcholine (nicotinic) | Acetylcholine (nicotinic) |
| Transmitter at effector | Noradrenaline (α and β adrenergic receptors); acetylcholine at sweat glands | Acetylcholine (muscarinic receptors) |
| General role | "Fight or flight": prepares for stress and exercise | "Rest and digest": saves and restores energy |

### Effects on organs

| Organ | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eye – pupil | Dilates (mydriasis; radial muscle, α1) | Constricts (miosis; sphincter pupillae) |
| Eye – ciliary muscle | Relaxes slightly (far vision) | Contracts (near vision, accommodation) |
| Heart rate and force | Increased (β1) | Rate decreased (vagus) |
| Bronchi | Dilated (β2) | Constricted, more secretion |
| Blood vessels of skin and gut | Constricted (α1) | Little direct effect |
| Blood vessels of skeletal muscle | Dilated (β2) | — |
| Gut motility and secretion | Decreased; sphincters contracted | Increased; sphincters relaxed |
| Salivary glands | Thick scanty saliva | Profuse watery saliva |
| Urinary bladder | Detrusor relaxed, sphincter contracted (retention) | Detrusor contracted, sphincter relaxed (voiding) |
| Liver | Glycogen breakdown, glucose released | Glycogen formation |
| Sweat glands | Increased sweating (cholinergic sympathetic) | — |
| Adrenal medulla | Releases adrenaline | — |
| Male sex organs | Ejaculation | Erection |

Most organs have **dual innervation**, and the two divisions usually oppose each other. Some structures (most blood vessels, sweat glands, adrenal medulla) receive only sympathetic fibres.

## 4.3 Spinal nerves: origin and functions

There are **31 pairs** of spinal nerves: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal. (There are 8 cervical nerves but 7 cervical vertebrae, because C1 leaves above the atlas.)

**Origin:** each spinal nerve is formed by two roots from one spinal segment:
- the **dorsal (posterior) root**, carrying sensory fibres, with the dorsal root ganglion containing the cell bodies of sensory neurons;
- the **ventral (anterior) root**, carrying motor fibres from cells in the anterior (and lateral) horn.

The two roots join in the intervertebral foramen to form a **mixed nerve** (sensory and motor). It then divides into a **dorsal ramus** (muscles and skin of the back), a **ventral ramus** (limbs and front and sides of the trunk), a meningeal branch, and rami communicantes (white and grey) that link with the sympathetic chain.

**Plexuses:** ventral rami (except T2–T12) join to form networks:

| Plexus | Roots | Main nerves and areas supplied |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cervical | C1–C4 | Skin and muscles of the neck; **phrenic nerve** (C3–C5) to the diaphragm |
| Brachial | C5–T1 | Upper limb: musculocutaneous, axillary, radial, median and ulnar nerves |
| Lumbar | L1–L4 | Front and inner thigh: femoral and obturator nerves |
| Sacral | L4–S4 | Buttock, back of thigh, leg and foot: **sciatic nerve** (largest nerve of the body), pudendal nerve |

Thoracic ventral rami T2–T12 do not form plexuses; they run between the ribs as **intercostal nerves**.

**Dermatome:** an area of skin supplied by one spinal nerve. Dermatome maps help to locate spinal injuries; herpes zoster (shingles) produces a rash along a dermatome.

**Functions of spinal nerves:** carry sensations (touch, pain, temperature, position) from the body to the CNS, carry motor commands to skeletal muscles, carry autonomic fibres, and form the pathways for spinal reflexes (for example the knee jerk).

## 4.4 Cranial nerves: origin and functions

Twelve pairs arise from the brain (I from the cerebrum, II from the diencephalon, III–XII from the brainstem). A common memory aid for names: "Oh Oh Oh To Touch And Feel Very Good Velvet, Such Heaven"; and for type (S = sensory, M = motor, B = both): "Some Say Marry Money, But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter More".

| No. | Name | Origin (attachment) | Type | Main functions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | Olfactory | Olfactory bulb (cerebrum) | Sensory | Smell |
| II | Optic | Diencephalon (optic chiasma) | Sensory | Vision |
| III | Oculomotor | Midbrain | Motor (+ parasympathetic) | Moves eye (most extrinsic muscles), lifts upper eyelid, constricts pupil, accommodation |
| IV | Trochlear | Midbrain (back) | Motor | Superior oblique muscle (moves eye down and out) |
| V | Trigeminal | Pons | Mixed | Sensation of face, scalp, cornea, teeth (ophthalmic, maxillary, mandibular branches); muscles of chewing |
| VI | Abducens | Pons–medulla junction | Motor | Lateral rectus (moves eye outward) |
| VII | Facial | Pons–medulla junction | Mixed | Muscles of facial expression; taste from anterior two-thirds of tongue; tears and saliva (submandibular, sublingual glands) |
| VIII | Vestibulocochlear | Pons–medulla junction | Sensory | Hearing (cochlear) and balance (vestibular) |
| IX | Glossopharyngeal | Medulla | Mixed | Taste from posterior one-third of tongue; swallowing; parotid saliva; carotid sinus and body reflexes |
| X | Vagus | Medulla | Mixed | Parasympathetic supply to heart, lungs and gut; swallowing and speech muscles of pharynx and larynx; visceral sensation |
| XI | Accessory | Medulla and upper spinal cord | Motor | Sternocleidomastoid and trapezius (turning head, shrugging) |
| XII | Hypoglossal | Medulla | Motor | Muscles of the tongue |

**Clinical notes:** facial nerve palsy (Bell's palsy) causes drooping of one side of the face; trigeminal neuralgia causes severe facial pain; the vagus carries about three-quarters of all parasympathetic outflow.

## 4.5 Special senses: the eye

### Structure

```text
              conjunctiva
        cornea )  aqueous humour (anterior chamber)
   iris  ====( lens )====   ciliary body (ciliary muscle, suspensory ligaments)
        pupil      \
                    vitreous body (jelly in posterior cavity)
   Wall (outside to inside):
     Fibrous tunic:  sclera (white) + cornea (clear, front)
     Vascular tunic (uvea): choroid + ciliary body + iris
     Nervous tunic:  retina (rods and cones) -> optic disc (blind spot)
                     macula lutea with fovea centralis (sharpest vision)
   Optic nerve leaves at the back
```

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Cornea | Transparent front window; does most of the focusing (refraction); no blood vessels |
| Sclera | Tough white outer coat; protects and gives shape |
| Choroid | Blood vessels and pigment; nourishes the retina, absorbs stray light |
| Ciliary body | Ciliary muscle changes lens shape (accommodation); ciliary processes secrete aqueous humour |
| Iris | Coloured part; sphincter (parasympathetic) and dilator (sympathetic) muscles control pupil size |
| Lens | Transparent, elastic, biconvex; fine focusing |
| Aqueous humour | Fluid in front of the lens; nourishes lens and cornea; maintains intraocular pressure (about 10–21 mm Hg) |
| Vitreous body | Gel that keeps the eyeball's shape and holds the retina in place |
| Retina | Rods (dim light, black-and-white vision; pigment rhodopsin needs vitamin A) and cones (bright light, colour, sharp vision; most dense in the fovea) |

**Accessory structures:** eyelids, eyelashes, eyebrows, conjunctiva, lacrimal apparatus (lacrimal gland → tears → lacrimal puncta → lacrimal sac → nasolacrimal duct → nose; this is why eye drops can be tasted), and six extrinsic muscles (four recti, two obliques).

### Physiology of vision

Light passes through cornea → aqueous humour → pupil → lens → vitreous → retina. The image is refracted and focused upside down on the retina. **Accommodation** for near objects: ciliary muscle contracts → suspensory ligaments slacken → lens becomes rounder; pupils constrict and eyes converge. In the retina, light changes rhodopsin, producing signals that pass through bipolar and ganglion cells to the optic nerve, optic chiasma (where fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross), optic tract, lateral geniculate body and visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

## 4.6 Special senses: the ear

```text
EXTERNAL EAR        MIDDLE EAR (air-filled)          INNER EAR (fluid-filled, temporal bone)
auricle (pinna)     tympanic membrane                bony labyrinth with perilymph,
external acoustic   ossicles: malleus -> incus ->    membranous labyrinth with endolymph:
meatus (wax glands)           stapes -> oval window    - cochlea (hearing; organ of Corti)
                    auditory (Eustachian) tube          - vestibule (utricle, saccule)
                    to nasopharynx                      - semicircular canals (3)
```

| Part | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Auricle and canal | Collect and direct sound; wax (cerumen) traps dust and protects |
| Tympanic membrane | Vibrates with sound waves |
| Ossicles | Carry and amplify vibrations to the oval window |
| Eustachian tube | Equalises air pressure on both sides of the eardrum; drains the middle ear |
| Cochlea (organ of Corti) | Hair cells convert vibrations into nerve impulses (hearing) |
| Utricle and saccule | Static balance and linear acceleration (otoliths) |
| Semicircular canals | Dynamic balance: rotational movements of the head |

**Physiology of hearing:** sound waves → eardrum vibrates → ossicles → oval window → pressure waves in perilymph and endolymph → basilar membrane moves → hair cells bend against the tectorial membrane → nerve impulses in the cochlear nerve → auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. High-pitched sounds stimulate the base of the cochlea, low-pitched sounds the apex.

## 4.7 Special senses: the nose (smell)

The **olfactory epithelium** lies in the roof of the nasal cavity. It contains olfactory receptor cells (bipolar neurons with cilia carrying odour receptors), supporting cells and basal stem cells; olfactory receptor neurons are replaced throughout life. Odour molecules dissolve in mucus and bind receptors (G-protein coupled) → impulses pass through the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone along the olfactory nerve → olfactory bulb → olfactory tract → olfactory cortex in the temporal lobe and limbic system (hence the link between smell, emotion and memory). Smell adapts quickly and contributes much of what we call "flavour".

**Other functions of the nose:** passage for air, warming, moistening and filtering of inspired air (hairs, mucus, cilia), resonance of the voice, and drainage of the paranasal sinuses and tears. Its rich blood supply is used for nasal drug delivery.

## 4.8 Special senses: the tongue (taste)

**Taste buds** lie mainly on the papillae of the tongue (circumvallate papillae at the back, fungiform at the tip and sides, foliate on the sides; filiform papillae have no taste buds and give a rough surface), and also on the soft palate and pharynx. Each taste bud has gustatory (receptor) cells with microvilli projecting into a taste pore, supporting cells and basal cells; receptor cells are replaced about every 10 days.

**Basic tastes:** sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami (savoury, glutamate). The old "tongue map" with strict zones is not accurate; all tastes can be sensed over most of the tongue.

**Pathway:** facial nerve (VII, anterior two-thirds), glossopharyngeal nerve (IX, posterior one-third), vagus nerve (X, throat and epiglottis) → medulla → thalamus → gustatory cortex. **Other functions of the tongue:** chewing, mixing food with saliva, swallowing, speech, and the sublingual route for drug absorption.

## 4.9 Pathophysiology of special sense disorders

### Glaucoma

**Definition:** a group of eye diseases with damage to the optic nerve and loss of visual field, usually associated with raised intraocular pressure (IOP). It is a leading cause of irreversible blindness.

**Normal flow of aqueous humour:**

```text
Ciliary processes secrete aqueous -> posterior chamber -> through pupil
 -> anterior chamber -> angle between iris and cornea
 -> trabecular meshwork -> canal of Schlemm -> veins
 (smaller uveoscleral route through the ciliary muscle)
```

**Pathogenesis:** when outflow is blocked, aqueous builds up and IOP rises (usually above 21 mm Hg). High pressure compresses the axons and blood supply of the optic nerve head → ganglion cell death → "cupping" of the optic disc → loss of peripheral vision first, then central vision.

| Type | Mechanism | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary open-angle glaucoma** (commonest) | Angle is open but the trabecular meshwork resists outflow | Slow, painless, no early symptoms; gradual loss of side vision ("tunnel vision"); detected by screening |
| **Primary angle-closure glaucoma** | Iris is pushed forward and blocks the angle (narrow angles, hypermetropic eyes); can be triggered by pupil dilatation (dim light, anticholinergic drugs) | Acute attack: sudden severe eye pain, red eye, blurred vision, haloes around lights, headache, nausea and vomiting; an emergency |
| **Secondary glaucoma** | Due to another cause: long-term corticosteroids, uveitis, injury, swollen cataract | |
| **Congenital glaucoma** | Developmental defect of the angle | Large eyes in infants (buphthalmos), watering, light sensitivity |

**Risk factors:** age over 40, family history, myopia (open-angle), hypermetropia (angle-closure), diabetes, steroid use.

### Cataract

**Definition:** opacity (loss of transparency) of the lens, causing blurred vision. It is the commonest cause of curable blindness in India.

**Aetiology:** ageing (senile cataract, the commonest), diabetes, long-term corticosteroids, ultraviolet light, smoking, eye injury, inflammation (uveitis), radiation, and congenital cataract (rubella in pregnancy, galactosaemia).

**Pathogenesis:** the lens is made of long fibre cells packed with ordered crystallin proteins. With age and oxidative stress, crystallins are oxidised, cross-linked and aggregated; the lens loses antioxidant glutathione, water and ion balance are disturbed, and the proteins clump and scatter light. In diabetes, excess glucose in the lens is converted by aldose reductase into sorbitol, which draws in water and makes fibres swell and become opaque.

**Features:** gradual painless blurring of vision, glare and haloes at night, faded colours, frequent change of glasses, and a white or grey pupil in advanced cases. Treatment is surgical replacement with an intraocular lens.

### Myopia (short-sightedness)

**Definition:** a refractive error in which parallel rays of light are focused **in front of** the retina when the eye is at rest, so distant objects look blurred while near objects are clear.

**Cause:** an eyeball that is too long (axial myopia, commonest) or a cornea or lens with too much refractive power (refractive myopia). Genetic tendency and prolonged near work with little time outdoors increase the risk; it often starts in school-age children and progresses during growth.

```text
Normal eye:  rays -> (lens) -> focus ON retina
Myopic eye:  rays -> (lens) -> focus IN FRONT of retina -> blurred image
Correction:  concave (diverging, minus) lens moves the focus back onto the retina
```

High myopia stretches the retina and raises the risk of retinal detachment and glaucoma. (For comparison: hypermetropia focuses behind the retina and is corrected by a convex lens; presbyopia is age-related loss of accommodation.)

### Otitis externa

**Definition:** inflammation of the external ear canal.

**Aetiology:** bacteria (mostly *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus*) and fungi (*Aspergillus*, *Candida*). Predisposing factors: moisture from swimming ("swimmer's ear") and humidity, which wash away protective wax; injury from cotton buds or fingernails; skin conditions (eczema); hearing aids.

**Pathogenesis:** loss of the acidic, water-repelling wax layer and damage to the thin canal skin let microbes enter → inflammation and swelling of the canal.

**Features:** ear pain made worse by pulling the pinna or pressing the tragus, itching, discharge, swelling of the canal, and muffled hearing. Malignant (necrotising) otitis externa is a severe spreading infection in elderly diabetics.

### Otitis media

**Definition:** inflammation of the middle ear; common in children.

**Aetiology:** usually follows a cold or upper respiratory infection. Bacteria: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*; also viruses. Children are prone because their Eustachian tube is shorter, wider and more horizontal; enlarged adenoids, bottle feeding while lying down and passive smoking add to the risk.

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
Upper respiratory infection -> swelling of the Eustachian tube lining
 -> tube blocked -> air absorbed, negative pressure in middle ear
 -> fluid collects (effusion); microbes from nasopharynx multiply in it
 -> pus forms, pressure rises -> bulging red eardrum, pain, fever
 -> may perforate -> discharge (relieves pain)
```

**Types and features:** acute otitis media (earache, fever, irritability, pulling at the ear in infants, reduced hearing, bulging drum); otitis media with effusion ("glue ear", fluid without infection, hearing loss); chronic suppurative otitis media (long-standing perforation with discharge). Complications: hearing loss, mastoiditis, meningitis.

### Vertigo

**Definition:** a false sensation that oneself or the surroundings are spinning or moving. It is a symptom, usually of a disorder of the vestibular system.

| Type | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| **Peripheral** (inner ear or vestibular nerve; commoner) | **Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo** (BPPV: loose otoconia crystals move into a semicircular canal, so turning the head triggers brief spinning); **Ménière's disease** (excess endolymph – endolymphatic hydrops – causing attacks of vertigo with hearing loss, tinnitus and fullness in the ear); **vestibular neuritis** (viral inflammation of the vestibular nerve); labyrinthitis; motion sickness |
| **Central** (brainstem or cerebellum) | Stroke, multiple sclerosis, tumours, migraine |

**Pathophysiology:** balance depends on matching signals from both vestibular organs, the eyes and joint receptors. When one side sends an abnormal signal, the brain senses movement that is not happening. **Features:** spinning sensation, nausea and vomiting, sweating, unsteadiness, and nystagmus (jerky eye movements). Some drugs (aminoglycoside antibiotics) can damage the vestibular apparatus.

### Anosmia

**Definition:** complete loss of the sense of smell (hyposmia = reduced smell).

**Aetiology and pathogenesis:**

| Type | Causes |
| --- | --- |
| Conductive (odours cannot reach the receptors) | Common cold, allergic rhinitis, sinusitis, nasal polyps, deviated nasal septum, tumours |
| Sensorineural (receptors or nerve pathway damaged) | Viral infections (including COVID-19, which damages supporting cells of the olfactory epithelium), head injury shearing the olfactory nerve fibres at the cribriform plate, ageing, neurodegenerative diseases (Parkinson's, Alzheimer's), brain tumours, smoking, toxins and some drugs, congenital (Kallmann syndrome) |

**Effects:** reduced flavour of food and loss of appetite, inability to detect smoke, gas leaks or spoiled food, and depression.

## Remember

- PNS = 12 cranial + 31 spinal nerve pairs; efferent PNS = somatic + autonomic.
- Sympathetic: T1–L2, short pre/long post, noradrenaline; parasympathetic: III, VII, IX, X and S2–S4, long pre/short post, acetylcholine.
- All autonomic ganglia use acetylcholine on nicotinic receptors; sweat glands are sympathetic but cholinergic.
- Spinal nerve: dorsal root sensory (with ganglion), ventral root motor; plexuses cervical, brachial, lumbar, sacral.
- Cranial nerves: I smell, II vision, III/IV/VI eye movements, V face sensation and chewing, VII face muscles and taste, VIII hearing and balance, IX/X swallowing and viscera, XI neck, XII tongue.
- Aqueous humour drains through trabecular meshwork and canal of Schlemm; blockage → glaucoma.
- Cataract = lens opacity (crystallin aggregation, sorbitol in diabetes); myopia = focus in front of retina, concave lens.
- Otitis externa = canal (Pseudomonas, swimmer's ear); otitis media = middle ear after Eustachian tube blockage, common in children.
- Vertigo is a spinning illusion (BPPV, Ménière's, vestibular neuritis); anosmia is loss of smell (conductive or sensorineural).
