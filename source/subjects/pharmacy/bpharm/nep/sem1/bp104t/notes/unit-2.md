# Unit 2: Skin and wound healing, skeletal system, muscle, joints and their diseases

This unit covers the body's outer covering and its frame. It starts with the skin, three skin diseases (psoriasis, dermatitis and leprosy) and how wounds heal. It then describes the skeleton, skeletal muscle and how it contracts, the neuromuscular junction, and joints. It ends with three common bone and joint diseases: rheumatoid arthritis, osteoporosis and gout.

## 2.1 Structure of the skin

The skin (integument) is the largest organ of the body, about 1.5–2 m² in an adult. It has two layers, the **epidermis** and the **dermis**, resting on the **hypodermis** (subcutaneous tissue), which is not part of the skin proper.

```text
 Surface  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
          | stratum corneum   (dead keratin-filled cells)  |
 EPIDERMIS| stratum lucidum   (thick skin only)            |
          | stratum granulosum (keratohyalin granules)     |
          | stratum spinosum  (desmosomes, Langerhans cells)|
          | stratum basale    (dividing cells, melanocytes) |
          |----------------- basement membrane ------------|
 DERMIS   | papillary layer  (loose tissue, capillary loops, Meissner corpuscles)
          | reticular layer  (dense collagen, hair follicles, sweat and sebaceous
          |                   glands, blood vessels, nerves, Pacinian corpuscles)
 HYPODERMIS  adipose and areolar tissue, larger vessels
```

### Epidermis

Keratinised stratified squamous epithelium with no blood vessels. Its cells are:

| Cell | Function |
| --- | --- |
| Keratinocytes (about 90%) | Make keratin, a tough waterproof protein; move up and die as they mature |
| Melanocytes (stratum basale) | Make melanin, which protects DNA from ultraviolet light |
| Langerhans cells | Pick up antigens and take part in immune responses |
| Merkel cells | Touch receptors |

New cells are made in the stratum basale and take about four weeks to reach the surface and flake off.

### Dermis

Dense irregular connective tissue with collagen and elastic fibres, giving strength and stretch. It contains blood vessels, lymphatics, nerves, sensory receptors, hair follicles with arrector pili muscles, sebaceous glands (secrete sebum into hair follicles) and sweat glands (eccrine all over the body for temperature control; apocrine in axilla and groin, opening into hair follicles).

## 2.2 Functions of the skin

1. **Protection** – against mechanical injury, microbes (acid mantle, keratin), chemicals, ultraviolet light (melanin) and water loss.
2. **Temperature regulation** – sweating, and widening or narrowing of skin blood vessels.
3. **Sensation** – touch, pressure, pain, heat and cold receptors.
4. **Excretion** – small amounts of water, salts and urea in sweat.
5. **Synthesis of vitamin D** – ultraviolet light converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in skin to cholecalciferol.
6. **Immunity** – Langerhans cells.
7. **Absorption** – lipid-soluble substances; the basis of ointments and transdermal patches.
8. **Blood reservoir** – the dermal vessels hold a good fraction of blood volume.

## 2.3 Skin disorders

### Psoriasis

**Definition:** a chronic, relapsing, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease with well-defined red plaques covered by silvery scales.

**Aetiology:**
- Genetic tendency (runs in families; linked with HLA-Cw6).
- Triggers: streptococcal throat infection, skin injury (new lesions at the site of trauma, the Koebner phenomenon), stress, cold dry weather, alcohol, smoking, and some drugs (lithium, beta blockers, antimalarials, withdrawal of systemic corticosteroids).

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
Genetic tendency + trigger
      -> dendritic cells in skin activated
      -> release IL-12 and IL-23 -> activate Th1 and Th17 lymphocytes
      -> cytokines: TNF-alpha, IL-17, IL-22, interferon-gamma
      -> keratinocytes divide very fast (epidermal turnover falls from about
         4 weeks to a few days) and do not mature properly
      -> thick epidermis, nuclei kept in surface layer (parakeratosis),
         dilated dermal capillaries, neutrophils in epidermis
      -> red, raised, scaly plaques
```

**Clinical features:** sharply demarcated red plaques with silvery white scales on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp and lower back; scraping the scale shows pinpoint bleeding (Auspitz sign); nail pitting and separation; itching may be present. About a fifth to a third of patients develop psoriatic arthritis. Types: plaque (commonest), guttate (small drop-like lesions after throat infection), pustular, erythrodermic, inverse (skin folds).

### Dermatitis (eczema)

**Definition:** inflammation of the skin with itching, redness, oedema, vesicles and oozing in the acute stage, and thickened, dry, scaly skin (lichenification) in the chronic stage.

| Type | Cause and mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| **Atopic dermatitis** | Inherited tendency to allergy (with asthma and hay fever); defective skin barrier (filaggrin defect), Th2 response with IgE; childhood onset; flexures of elbows and knees |
| **Irritant contact dermatitis** | Direct damage by detergents, soaps, solvents, acids, alkalis; no immune memory needed; common in hands of cleaners and health workers |
| **Allergic contact dermatitis** | Type IV (delayed, T-cell mediated) hypersensitivity to nickel, chromium (cement), rubber, hair dyes, topical medicines; appears 24–72 hours after contact in a sensitised person |
| **Seborrhoeic dermatitis** | Greasy scales on scalp (dandruff), face, chest; linked to Malassezia yeast |
| **Photodermatitis** | Sun exposure with a drug or chemical |

**Pathogenesis (common pattern):** the irritant or allergen damages keratinocytes or activates immune cells → release of inflammatory mediators → dilated vessels, oedema between epidermal cells (spongiosis) → vesicles, oozing, itching; scratching thickens the skin.

### Pathophysiology of leprosy

**Definition:** a chronic infectious disease caused by *Mycobacterium leprae*, affecting mainly skin and peripheral nerves.

**Aetiology:** *M. leprae*, an acid-fast bacillus that grows very slowly (generation time about 12–14 days) and prefers cooler parts of the body; it cannot be grown on artificial media. Spread is mainly by droplets from the nose of untreated patients after prolonged close contact. The incubation period is long, usually years. Most exposed people do not develop disease because of good immunity.

**Pathogenesis:** the bacilli enter Schwann cells of peripheral nerves and macrophages in the skin. The type of disease depends on the patient's cell-mediated immunity.

| Feature | Tuberculoid leprosy | Lepromatous leprosy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cell-mediated immunity | Strong (Th1) | Weak (Th2) |
| Bacilli | Few (paucibacillary) | Many (multibacillary) |
| Skin lesions | One or a few well-defined, pale or red, dry, hairless anaesthetic patches | Many symmetrical nodules and plaques; thickened facial skin (leonine face), loss of eyebrows |
| Nerves | Early, marked damage near lesions; thickened nerves | Slow, symmetrical glove-and-stocking loss |
| Lepromin test | Positive | Negative |
| Infectivity | Low | High |

Borderline forms lie between the two. Nerve damage causes loss of sensation, so painless injuries, burns and ulcers occur; muscle weakness causes claw hand, foot drop and eye problems. Loss of fingers and toes is due to repeated unnoticed injury and infection, not to the bacillus "eating" them. Immune reactions (type 1 reversal reaction and type 2 reaction, erythema nodosum leprosum) can occur. Leprosy is curable with multidrug therapy and is notifiable in India.

## 2.4 Basic principles of wound healing

**Healing** is the replacement of destroyed tissue by living tissue. It happens by **regeneration** (replacement by the same type of cells, possible in tissues whose cells can divide, such as epidermis and liver) or by **repair** (replacement by fibrous connective tissue, forming a scar).

### Phases of wound healing

| Phase | Time | Main events |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemostasis | Minutes | Vessel constriction, platelet plug, fibrin clot seals the wound |
| Inflammation | Day 1–3 | Neutrophils, then macrophages, clean debris and bacteria; macrophages release growth factors |
| Proliferation | Day 3 to about 3 weeks | Granulation tissue (new capillaries plus fibroblasts) fills the gap; fibroblasts lay down collagen; epithelial cells cover the surface; myofibroblasts pull the edges together (contraction) |
| Maturation (remodelling) | Weeks to a year or more | Collagen reorganised and cross-linked; scar becomes paler and stronger, reaching about 70–80% of original strength |

### Healing by first and second intention

| Feature | First (primary) intention | Second (secondary) intention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wound | Clean, edges close together (surgical incision with sutures) | Large, gaping, infected, tissue lost (ulcers, burns) |
| Granulation tissue | Little | Abundant, fills from the base |
| Contraction | Minimal | Marked |
| Scar | Thin, neat | Large, may deform |
| Time | Short | Long |

**Factors affecting wound healing.** Local: infection (most important), poor blood supply, foreign bodies, movement, large gap, radiation. Systemic: age, poor nutrition (protein, vitamin C, zinc), diabetes, corticosteroid treatment, anaemia, poor circulation, immune deficiency.

**Complications:** wound infection, keloid (excess scar beyond the wound margin), contractures, wound breakdown (dehiscence), incisional hernia.

## 2.5 Skeletal system: divisions and functions

The adult skeleton has 206 bones.

**Functions:** support, protection of organs (skull, rib cage), movement (bones act as levers for muscles), mineral store (calcium and phosphate), blood cell formation (red bone marrow), and fat storage (yellow marrow).

| Division | Number | Bones |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Axial skeleton** | 80 | Skull (cranium 8, face 14), 6 ear ossicles, hyoid (1), vertebral column (26 in adult), thoracic cage (sternum 1, ribs 24) |
| **Appendicular skeleton** | 126 | Pectoral girdles (clavicle and scapula, 4), upper limbs (60), pelvic girdle (hip bones, 2), lower limbs (60) |

## 2.6 Types of bones

| Type | Shape | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long | Longer than wide; shaft (diaphysis) and two ends (epiphyses) | Femur, humerus, radius, phalanges |
| Short | Cube-like | Carpals, tarsals |
| Flat | Thin, flat, often curved | Skull vault bones, sternum, ribs, scapula |
| Irregular | Complex shapes | Vertebrae, some facial bones |
| Sesamoid | Develop inside tendons | Patella |
| Sutural (Wormian) | Small bones in skull sutures | Variable |

**Parts of a long bone:** diaphysis (shaft of compact bone around the medullary cavity with yellow marrow), epiphyses (spongy bone with red marrow, covered by articular cartilage), metaphysis with the epiphyseal (growth) plate in children, periosteum (outer membrane for growth in width, repair and attachment) and endosteum (lines the cavity).

**Bone tissue:** compact bone is made of osteons (Haversian systems: concentric lamellae around a central canal with vessels); spongy bone is a network of trabeculae with red marrow between them. Bone cells: osteoprogenitor cells, osteoblasts (build bone), osteocytes (mature cells in lacunae), osteoclasts (large multinucleated cells that resorb bone). Bone is constantly remodelled by osteoblasts and osteoclasts under the control of parathyroid hormone, calcitonin, vitamin D, oestrogen and mechanical stress.

## 2.7 Salient features of the axial skeleton

**Skull.** Cranial bones: frontal, two parietal, two temporal, occipital, sphenoid, ethmoid. They are joined by immovable joints called sutures (coronal, sagittal, lambdoid). The occipital bone has the foramen magnum through which the spinal cord passes. The temporal bone houses the middle and inner ear. Facial bones: two nasal, two maxillae, two zygomatic, mandible (the only movable skull bone), two lacrimal, two palatine, two inferior nasal conchae, vomer. In the newborn, gaps called fontanelles allow the skull to mould during birth.

**Vertebral column.** 33 vertebrae in a child (7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 fused sacral, about 4 fused coccygeal), counted as 26 in the adult. A typical vertebra has a body, a vertebral arch (pedicles and laminae) enclosing the vertebral foramen, a spinous process, two transverse processes and articular processes. Special features: cervical vertebrae have foramina in their transverse processes; the atlas (C1) has no body and holds the skull; the axis (C2) has the dens on which the atlas turns; thoracic vertebrae have facets for ribs; lumbar vertebrae have large bodies to bear weight. The column has four curves (cervical and lumbar forward, thoracic and sacral backward) and intervertebral discs of fibrocartilage.

**Thoracic cage.** Sternum (manubrium, body, xiphoid process) and 12 pairs of ribs: true ribs 1–7 joined directly to the sternum by costal cartilage, false ribs 8–10 joined indirectly, floating ribs 11–12 not joined in front. Protects heart and lungs and helps breathing.

## 2.8 Salient features of the appendicular skeleton

| Region | Bones and key features |
| --- | --- |
| Pectoral girdle | Clavicle (S-shaped strut, commonly fractured); scapula (flat triangular bone with spine, acromion, coracoid process and glenoid cavity for the humerus) |
| Arm | Humerus: head fits the glenoid cavity; surgical neck; deltoid tuberosity; trochlea and capitulum at the elbow |
| Forearm | Radius (lateral, thumb side) and ulna (medial; olecranon process forms the elbow point) |
| Hand | 8 carpals, 5 metacarpals, 14 phalanges |
| Pelvic girdle | Two hip bones, each formed by fusion of ilium, ischium and pubis; acetabulum for the head of femur; joined in front at the pubic symphysis and behind to the sacrum. The female pelvis is wider with a rounder inlet, suited to childbirth |
| Thigh | Femur, the longest and strongest bone: head, neck (common fracture site in the elderly), greater and lesser trochanters, two condyles at the knee |
| Knee | Patella (sesamoid bone in the quadriceps tendon) |
| Leg | Tibia (medial, weight-bearing, medial malleolus) and fibula (lateral, lateral malleolus) |
| Foot | 7 tarsals (talus, calcaneus or heel bone, and others), 5 metatarsals, 14 phalanges; arches spread body weight |

## 2.9 Organisation of skeletal muscle

```text
Muscle (covered by epimysium)
  -> fascicles (bundles, covered by perimysium)
     -> muscle fibres (cells, covered by endomysium; many nuclei)
        -> myofibrils (rows of sarcomeres)
           -> myofilaments: thick (myosin) and thin (actin, tropomyosin, troponin)
```

Each fibre has a cell membrane called the **sarcolemma**, which dips inward as **transverse (T) tubules**, and a **sarcoplasmic reticulum** that stores calcium. A T tubule with the two terminal cisternae of sarcoplasmic reticulum on either side is a **triad**.

**Sarcomere** (the unit of contraction, between two Z lines):

```text
 Z|-------- I band --------|=========== A band ===========|-------- I band -----|Z
  |  thin (actin) only     |  thick (myosin) + overlap     |                     |
  |                        |      | H zone | M line        |                     |
```

During contraction the I band and H zone shorten, the A band stays the same length, and the Z lines come closer.

## 2.10 Physiology of muscle contraction (sliding filament theory)

1. A nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction and acetylcholine is released.
2. An action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down the T tubules.
3. The sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺ into the cytoplasm.
4. Ca²⁺ binds troponin C; tropomyosin moves off the myosin-binding sites on actin.
5. Myosin heads (already energised by splitting ATP) bind actin, forming **cross-bridges**.
6. **Power stroke:** the heads pivot and pull the thin filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere; ADP and phosphate are released.
7. A new ATP binds and the myosin head detaches; ATP is split and the head is re-cocked. The cycle repeats while Ca²⁺ and ATP are present.
8. **Relaxation:** acetylcholinesterase destroys acetylcholine; Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (Ca²⁺-ATPase); tropomyosin again covers the binding sites.

Without ATP the heads cannot detach, which explains the stiffness of rigor mortis after death.

**Energy sources:** creatine phosphate (seconds), anaerobic glycolysis (producing lactic acid), aerobic respiration in mitochondria (long-lasting). **Types of contraction:** isotonic (muscle shortens, tension constant) and isometric (tension rises, length constant). Other terms: twitch, summation, tetanus (sustained contraction), muscle tone, fatigue, motor unit (one motor neuron and the fibres it supplies).

## 2.11 Neuromuscular junction

The junction between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fibre.

```text
 motor axon terminal (synaptic vesicles with ACh)
   ||  ACh released by exocytosis when Ca2+ enters
   vv
 ---------- synaptic cleft (acetylcholinesterase here) ----------
 motor end plate (folded sarcolemma with nicotinic ACh receptors)
   -> Na+ enters -> end-plate potential -> muscle action potential
```

Steps: the impulse opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels in the terminal → Ca²⁺ entry causes vesicles to release acetylcholine → acetylcholine binds nicotinic (Nm) receptors on the motor end plate → Na⁺ enters, producing an end-plate potential → a muscle action potential starts → acetylcholinesterase rapidly breaks acetylcholine into choline and acetate, ending the signal.

**Clinical links:** myasthenia gravis (autoantibodies destroy acetylcholine receptors → fatiguable weakness, drooping eyelids); neuromuscular blocking drugs used in surgery act here; anticholinesterases prolong acetylcholine action; botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release.

## 2.12 Joints: structural and functional classification

**Functional classification:**

| Type | Movement | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synarthrosis | Immovable | Skull sutures |
| Amphiarthrosis | Slightly movable | Intervertebral discs, pubic symphysis |
| Diarthrosis | Freely movable | Knee, shoulder (all synovial joints) |

**Structural classification:**

| Type | Bones joined by | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fibrous | Dense fibrous tissue | Sutures; syndesmosis (distal tibiofibular joint); gomphosis (tooth in socket) |
| Cartilaginous | Cartilage | Synchondrosis (epiphyseal plate, first rib–sternum); symphysis (pubic symphysis, intervertebral discs) |
| Synovial | Joint cavity with synovial fluid | Knee, hip, elbow |

**Synovial joint structure:** articular (hyaline) cartilage on bone ends, a joint cavity, an articular capsule (outer fibrous layer and inner synovial membrane that secretes synovial fluid for lubrication and nourishment), ligaments, and in some joints menisci and bursae.

**Types of synovial joints:**

| Type | Movement | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plane (gliding) | Sliding | Intercarpal joints |
| Hinge | Flexion and extension in one plane | Elbow, knee, interphalangeal |
| Pivot | Rotation around one axis | Atlas on axis, proximal radioulnar |
| Condyloid (ellipsoid) | Two planes | Wrist (radiocarpal) |
| Saddle | Two planes, wide range | Carpometacarpal joint of thumb |
| Ball and socket | All directions, including rotation | Shoulder, hip |

## 2.13 Diseases of bones and joints

### Rheumatoid arthritis

**Definition:** a chronic systemic autoimmune disease causing symmetrical inflammation of synovial joints, mainly small joints of hands and feet, leading to joint destruction.

**Aetiology:** genetic tendency (HLA-DR4 and related genes), female sex (about three times commoner in women), smoking, and possibly infections that trigger autoimmunity. Autoantibodies: rheumatoid factor (IgM against the Fc part of IgG) and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies.

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
Genetic tendency + trigger (e.g. smoking -> citrullinated proteins)
  -> loss of tolerance: T cells and B cells react to joint antigens
  -> CD4 T cells activate macrophages and B cells; autoantibodies and immune complexes
  -> cytokines TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6 -> synovial inflammation
  -> synovium thickens into a pannus (inflamed granulation tissue)
  -> pannus erodes cartilage and bone; osteoclasts activated
  -> joint destruction, deformity, fibrous or bony fusion (ankylosis)
```

**Clinical features:** symmetrical pain and swelling of small joints (proximal interphalangeal and metacarpophalangeal, wrists), morning stiffness lasting more than an hour, fatigue, low-grade fever. Deformities: ulnar deviation, swan-neck and boutonnière deformity. Extra-articular: rheumatoid nodules, anaemia, lung fibrosis, vasculitis, dry eyes. Raised ESR and CRP.

### Osteoporosis

**Definition:** a systemic skeletal disease with low bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, causing fragile bones and a high risk of fracture. The bone that is present is normally mineralised, but there is too little of it.

**Aetiology:**
- **Primary:** postmenopausal (loss of oestrogen) and senile (ageing).
- **Secondary:** long-term corticosteroid therapy, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, immobilisation, low calcium and vitamin D intake, smoking, alcohol, low body weight, some drugs (heparin, some anticonvulsants).

**Pathogenesis:** peak bone mass is reached in early adulthood; afterwards resorption by osteoclasts slowly exceeds formation by osteoblasts. Oestrogen normally restrains osteoclasts (it reduces RANKL and raises osteoprotegerin); after menopause its loss increases cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF) and RANKL, so osteoclast activity and bone loss rise quickly, especially in spongy bone. Ageing reduces osteoblast activity and calcium absorption. Thin, broken trabeculae make the bone weak.

**Clinical features:** often silent until a fracture. Typical fractures: vertebral compression (loss of height, kyphosis or "dowager's hump", back pain), neck of femur after minor falls, distal radius (Colles' fracture). Diagnosis by bone mineral density (DEXA scan; T-score of −2.5 or lower).

### Gout

**Definition:** a disorder of purine metabolism in which high uric acid in blood (hyperuricaemia) leads to deposition of monosodium urate crystals in joints and tissues, causing attacks of acute arthritis.

**Aetiology:**
- **Overproduction of uric acid:** high purine diet (red meat, organ meats, seafood), alcohol (especially beer), rapid cell breakdown (cancer chemotherapy, leukaemia), enzyme defects.
- **Under-excretion (commoner):** kidney disease, diuretics (thiazides), low-dose aspirin, dehydration, lead.
- More common in men; obesity and metabolic syndrome increase the risk.

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
Purines -> hypoxanthine -> xanthine -> uric acid (enzyme xanthine oxidase)
High blood urate -> supersaturation -> urate crystals form in cooler,
  less vascular joints (big toe)
  -> crystals engulfed by neutrophils and macrophages
  -> NLRP3 inflammasome activated -> IL-1 beta released
  -> intense acute inflammation (acute gouty arthritis)
Repeated attacks -> chronic arthritis, tophi (urate deposits in ear, joints),
  urate kidney stones and kidney damage
```

**Clinical features:** sudden, very painful, red, hot swollen joint, often the first metatarsophalangeal joint of the big toe (podagra), frequently at night after a heavy meal or alcohol; attacks settle over days; between attacks the patient is free of symptoms. Diagnosis: needle-shaped negatively birefringent crystals in joint fluid.

| Feature | Rheumatoid arthritis | Gout | Osteoporosis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basic problem | Autoimmune synovitis | Urate crystal deposition | Low bone mass |
| Typical site | Small joints, symmetrical | Big toe, one joint | Spine, hip, wrist |
| Sex | More in women | More in men | More in women after menopause |
| Key test | RF, anti-CCP | Joint fluid crystals, serum uric acid | DEXA scan |

## Remember

- Epidermis (5 layers in thick skin) has no vessels; melanocytes and dividing cells sit in the stratum basale.
- Skin makes vitamin D and regulates temperature.
- Psoriasis = fast keratinocyte turnover driven by Th17/IL-23 and TNF; silvery plaques on extensor surfaces.
- Contact allergic dermatitis is type IV hypersensitivity; atopic dermatitis is a barrier defect with Th2 allergy.
- Leprosy: *M. leprae* in Schwann cells; tuberculoid (strong immunity, few bacilli) vs lepromatous (weak immunity, many bacilli).
- Wound healing: haemostasis → inflammation → proliferation (granulation tissue) → remodelling.
- 206 bones: axial 80, appendicular 126.
- Contraction: Ca²⁺ binds troponin, cross-bridges form, ATP is needed to detach myosin.
- NMJ uses acetylcholine on nicotinic receptors; acetylcholinesterase ends the signal.
- RA = pannus; gout = urate crystals and IL-1β; osteoporosis = resorption exceeds formation, oestrogen loss.
