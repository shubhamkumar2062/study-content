# Unit 1: Introduction to the human body, cells, tissues, and cell injury and adaptation

This unit lays the foundation for the whole subject. It defines anatomy, physiology and pathophysiology, shows how the body is organised from atoms to systems, explains how the body keeps its internal conditions steady, and then goes down to the cell: its parts, how substances cross its membrane, how it divides and how cells signal to each other. It then covers the four basic tissues. The last part is the first taste of pathophysiology: what injures a cell, how a cell adapts, and how it finally dies.

## 1.1 Definition and scope

| Term | Definition | Examples of branches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anatomy** | Study of the structure (form) of the body and its parts and how the parts are related | Gross anatomy, histology (tissues), cytology (cells), embryology, surface and radiological anatomy |
| **Physiology** | Study of the normal functions of the body and its parts | Cell physiology, neurophysiology, cardiovascular, renal and endocrine physiology |
| **Pathophysiology** | Study of the disordered functions that cause or follow disease or injury | Cardiovascular, haematological, joint and skin pathophysiology |
| **Pathology** | Study of the structural and functional changes in cells, tissues and organs in disease | General pathology (cell injury, inflammation), systemic pathology (disease of each organ) |

**Scope for a pharmacy student.** Every drug acts on a cell, a tissue or an organ. Pharmacology, pharmacokinetics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion), dosage-form design, clinical pharmacy and patient counselling all assume a working picture of normal structure and function, and of what goes wrong in disease. For example, a transdermal patch is designed around the layers of skin, an eye drop around the cornea and tear drainage, and an antihypertensive drug around the factors that set blood pressure.

Key words used in pathophysiology:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Aetiology (etiology) | The cause of a disease |
| Pathogenesis | The sequence of events by which the cause produces the disease |
| Morphological changes | Structural changes in cells and tissues |
| Clinical features | Signs (found by the examiner) and symptoms (felt by the patient) |
| Complications | Further problems that arise from the disease |
| Prognosis | Likely outcome |

## 1.3 Basic life processes

1. **Metabolism** – all chemical reactions in the body: catabolism (breakdown, releases energy) and anabolism (synthesis, uses energy).
2. **Responsiveness** – detecting and reacting to changes (a nerve impulse, a gland secreting).
3. **Movement** – of the whole body, of organs (the beating heart), of cells (white cells moving into tissue) and inside cells.
4. **Growth** – increase in size by more cells or larger cells.
5. **Differentiation** – unspecialised cells becoming specialised (stem cells to red cells).
6. **Reproduction** – new cells for growth and repair, and a new individual.

## 1.4 Homeostasis and feedback systems

**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment (the extracellular fluid around the cells) in spite of changes outside. Variables kept within narrow limits include body temperature (about 37 °C), blood glucose, blood pressure, blood pH (7.35–7.45), oxygen and carbon dioxide levels, and water and electrolyte concentrations. Disease can be seen as a failure of homeostasis.

### Components of a feedback system

```text
Stimulus (change in a controlled condition)
      |
Receptor  -- detects the change, sends input (afferent path) -->
      |
Control centre (usually brain) -- compares with the set point, decides -->
      |
Effector (muscle or gland) -- output (efferent path) produces a response -->
      |
Controlled condition returns towards normal (or is pushed further away)
```

| Component | Example: control of body temperature |
| --- | --- |
| Controlled condition | Body temperature |
| Receptor | Thermoreceptors in skin and hypothalamus |
| Control centre | Hypothalamus (set point about 37 °C) |
| Effector | Sweat glands, skin blood vessels, skeletal muscle (shivering) |

### Types of feedback

| Negative feedback | Positive feedback |
| --- | --- |
| Response **reverses** the original change | Response **strengthens** the original change |
| Keeps conditions stable; the usual type | Must be stopped by an outside event |
| Examples: control of blood pressure by baroreceptors; blood glucose by insulin and glucagon; body temperature | Examples: uterine contractions during labour (oxytocin), blood clotting, milk ejection |

**Homeostatic imbalance.** When feedback fails, disorders arise: fever beyond control, diabetes mellitus (failed glucose control), hypertension (failed pressure control).

## 1.5 Basic anatomical terminology

**Anatomical position:** body erect, facing forward, head level, eyes looking forward, arms at the sides with palms facing forward, feet flat and forward. All directions are described from this position.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Superior / inferior | Towards the head / towards the feet | Heart is superior to the diaphragm |
| Anterior (ventral) / posterior (dorsal) | Front / back | Sternum is anterior to the heart |
| Medial / lateral | Nearer / farther from the midline | Ulna is medial to the radius |
| Proximal / distal | Nearer / farther from the attachment of a limb | Elbow is proximal to the wrist |
| Superficial / deep | Towards / away from the surface | Skin is superficial to muscle |
| Ipsilateral / contralateral | Same side / opposite side | |

**Planes:** sagittal (divides into right and left; the midsagittal plane into equal halves), frontal or coronal (anterior and posterior parts), transverse or horizontal (superior and inferior parts), oblique.

**Body cavities:** dorsal cavity (cranial cavity with brain, vertebral canal with spinal cord) and ventral cavity (thoracic cavity with pleural and pericardial cavities and the mediastinum; abdominopelvic cavity), separated by the diaphragm. The abdomen is divided into nine regions (right and left hypochondriac, epigastric, right and left lumbar, umbilical, right and left iliac, hypogastric) or four quadrants.

## 1.6 Structure and functions of the cell

A typical human cell has three main parts: the plasma membrane, the cytoplasm (cytosol plus organelles) and the nucleus.

```text
        ______________ plasma membrane _______________
       /   ribosomes   rough ER        mitochondrion  \
      |       .  .   ========           (  ~~~~  )     |
      |  Golgi   ))))    ( NUCLEUS )                    |
      |  apparatus       ( nucleolus)   lysosome (o)    |
      |                   nuclear envelope with pores   |
      |  smooth ER ~~~~      centrosome ::   cytosol     |
       \_______________________________________________/
```

| Part | Structure | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plasma membrane** | Phospholipid bilayer with cholesterol, integral and peripheral proteins and surface glycocalyx (fluid mosaic model) | Selective barrier, transport, receptors for signals, cell identity, adhesion |
| **Cytosol** | Watery fluid with ions, nutrients, enzymes | Site of glycolysis and many reactions |
| **Cytoskeleton** | Microfilaments, intermediate filaments, microtubules | Shape, movement, transport of organelles |
| **Centrosome** | Pair of centrioles | Organises the mitotic spindle |
| **Cilia and flagella** | Microtubule projections | Move fluid over the cell (respiratory tract) or move the cell (sperm) |
| **Ribosomes** | rRNA and protein; free or on rough ER | Protein synthesis |
| **Rough ER** | Membrane sacs with ribosomes | Makes proteins for secretion and membranes |
| **Smooth ER** | Tubules without ribosomes | Lipid and steroid synthesis, drug detoxification (cytochrome P450 in liver), calcium store in muscle (sarcoplasmic reticulum) |
| **Golgi apparatus** | Stack of flattened sacs | Modifies, sorts and packages proteins into vesicles |
| **Lysosomes** | Vesicles with acid hydrolases | Digest worn-out organelles and engulfed material (autophagy) |
| **Peroxisomes** | Vesicles with oxidases and catalase | Break down fatty acids, destroy hydrogen peroxide |
| **Mitochondria** | Double membrane, inner folds (cristae), own DNA | Aerobic respiration, ATP production ("powerhouse"); trigger apoptosis |
| **Nucleus** | Nuclear envelope with pores, chromatin (DNA + histones), nucleolus | Holds genetic information, controls cell activity; nucleolus makes ribosomes |

## 1.7 Transport across the cell membrane

| Process | Energy | Mechanism | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple diffusion** | Passive | Movement down a concentration gradient through the lipid bilayer | O2, CO2, lipid-soluble drugs |
| **Facilitated diffusion** | Passive | Down a gradient with a carrier or channel protein; shows saturation | Glucose into most cells (GLUT carriers) |
| **Osmosis** | Passive | Movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane towards higher solute concentration | Water movement in red cells |
| **Filtration** | Passive (pressure) | Movement of water and small solutes due to hydrostatic pressure | Glomerular filtration, capillary filtration |
| **Primary active transport** | ATP used directly | Carrier pumps against the gradient | Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in |
| **Secondary active transport** | Uses the Na⁺ gradient made by the pump | Symport (same direction) or antiport (opposite) | Na⁺–glucose symport in intestine; Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger |
| **Endocytosis** | ATP | Membrane folds inward to form vesicles: phagocytosis (cell eating), pinocytosis (cell drinking), receptor-mediated endocytosis | Bacteria by neutrophils; LDL uptake |
| **Exocytosis** | ATP | Vesicles fuse with the membrane and release contents | Neurotransmitter and hormone release |

**Tonicity:** in an isotonic solution (0.9% NaCl) red cells keep their shape; in a hypotonic solution they swell and may burst (haemolysis); in a hypertonic solution they shrink (crenation). This is why injections and eye drops are made isotonic.

## 1.8 Cell division

**Cell cycle:** interphase (G1 growth, S DNA replication, G2 preparation) followed by the mitotic (M) phase.

**Mitosis** gives two identical diploid (46-chromosome) daughter cells; used for growth and repair.

| Stage | Events |
| --- | --- |
| Prophase | Chromatin condenses into chromosomes (two chromatids joined at the centromere); spindle forms; nuclear envelope breaks down |
| Metaphase | Chromosomes line up at the equator (metaphase plate) |
| Anaphase | Centromeres split; sister chromatids pulled to opposite poles |
| Telophase | Nuclear envelopes re-form, chromosomes uncoil |
| Cytokinesis | Cytoplasm divides by a cleavage furrow |

**Meiosis** occurs in the gonads: two divisions give four haploid (23-chromosome) gametes; crossing over in prophase I creates genetic variety.

## 1.9 Cell junctions

| Junction | Structure | Function | Site |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tight junction | Membranes fused by proteins in a band | Seals the gap; stops leakage between cells | Intestine, blood–brain barrier, kidney tubules |
| Adherens junction | Cadherins linked to actin | Holds cells together | Epithelia |
| Desmosome | Spot-like plaque with cadherins and intermediate filaments | Strong attachment against stretching | Skin, heart muscle |
| Hemidesmosome | Half desmosome with integrins | Anchors cells to the basement membrane | Base of epithelium |
| Gap junction | Channels of connexons | Passage of ions and small molecules; electrical coupling | Heart and smooth muscle |

## 1.10 Cell communication and intracellular signalling

Cells coordinate their activity by sending signals (ligands) that bind receptors on or in the target cell. The receptor then starts an internal chain of events (signal transduction) that changes the cell's activity.

| Form | How the signal travels | Distance | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **a) Contact-dependent** | Signal molecule stays on the surface of the sending cell and binds a receptor on a touching cell | Direct contact | Immune cells recognising antigen on other cells; signals that guide development |
| **b) Paracrine** | Local mediator released into tissue fluid acts on nearby cells | Short | Histamine, prostaglandins, growth factors, nitric oxide (autocrine when it acts on the same cell) |
| **c) Synaptic** | Neurotransmitter released at a synapse acts on the next neuron or effector | Very short gap, very fast | Acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction |
| **d) Endocrine** | Hormone released into blood, carried to distant targets | Long, slower | Insulin, thyroxine, adrenaline |

Main receptor types (background for pharmacology): ligand-gated ion channels (nicotinic acetylcholine receptor), G-protein coupled receptors (adrenergic and muscarinic receptors, using cAMP, IP3 and calcium as second messengers), enzyme-linked receptors (insulin receptor tyrosine kinase), and intracellular receptors (steroid and thyroid hormones acting on genes).

## 1.11 Tissues: classification, structure, location and functions

A tissue is a group of similar cells and their surrounding material that perform a common function. There are four basic tissues.

### Epithelial tissue

Closely packed cells, little matrix, a free (apical) surface, resting on a basement membrane, no blood vessels (nourished by diffusion), good power of regeneration.

| Type | Structure | Location | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Simple squamous | One layer of flat cells | Alveoli, lining of blood vessels (endothelium), serous membranes (mesothelium) | Diffusion, filtration |
| Simple cuboidal | One layer of cube-shaped cells | Kidney tubules, thyroid follicles, gland ducts | Secretion, absorption |
| Simple columnar | One layer of tall cells, may have microvilli or goblet cells | Stomach and intestine lining | Absorption, secretion of mucus |
| Ciliated columnar | Columnar with cilia | Uterine tubes | Moves the ovum |
| Pseudostratified ciliated columnar | One layer that looks layered, cilia and goblet cells | Trachea, bronchi | Traps and moves mucus |
| Stratified squamous | Many layers, flat surface cells; keratinised or not | Skin epidermis (keratinised); mouth, oesophagus, vagina (non-keratinised) | Protection against wear |
| Transitional | Layers that change shape when stretched | Urinary bladder, ureters | Allows stretching |
| Glandular | Exocrine (with ducts: sweat, salivary) and endocrine (ductless: thyroid) | Glands | Secretion |

### Connective tissue

Widely spaced cells in an abundant matrix of ground substance and fibres (collagen, elastic, reticular); usually vascular (except cartilage).

| Type | Main features | Location | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Loose (areolar) | Fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, all three fibres | Under skin, around organs | Binding, support, defence |
| Adipose | Fat cells | Subcutaneous tissue, around kidneys | Energy store, insulation |
| Reticular | Reticular fibres | Spleen, lymph nodes, bone marrow | Framework |
| Dense regular | Parallel collagen bundles | Tendons, ligaments | Strong attachment |
| Dense irregular | Collagen in many directions | Dermis | Strength in all directions |
| Elastic | Elastic fibres | Walls of large arteries | Stretch and recoil |
| Hyaline cartilage | Chondrocytes in lacunae, fine collagen | Articular surfaces, costal cartilage, trachea | Smooth surface, support |
| Fibrocartilage | Thick collagen bundles | Intervertebral discs, menisci, pubic symphysis | Shock absorption |
| Elastic cartilage | Elastic fibres | Ear auricle, epiglottis | Flexible support |
| Bone | Calcified matrix, osteocytes in lacunae, osteons | Skeleton | Support, protection, calcium store |
| Blood | Cells in liquid plasma | Vessels | Transport |

### Muscular tissue

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Long cylindrical fibres | Branched cells | Spindle-shaped |
| Nuclei | Many, peripheral | One (or two), central | One, central |
| Striations | Present | Present | Absent |
| Special feature | — | Intercalated discs (gap junctions and desmosomes) | Gap junctions in single-unit type |
| Control | Voluntary | Involuntary, autorhythmic | Involuntary |
| Location | Attached to bones | Heart | Walls of gut, vessels, bladder, uterus |

### Nervous tissue

**Neurons** carry nerve impulses: a cell body (with Nissl bodies), dendrites (receive signals) and one axon (carries impulses away), often covered by a myelin sheath with nodes of Ranvier (faster conduction). **Neuroglia** support neurons: astrocytes (blood–brain barrier support), oligodendrocytes (myelin in the CNS), microglia (phagocytes), ependymal cells (line ventricles), Schwann cells (myelin in the PNS), satellite cells (around ganglion cells).

## 1.12 Basic principles of cell injury and adaptation

When a cell meets stress it first tries to **adapt**. If the stress is too severe or lasts too long, the cell is **injured**; mild injury is **reversible**, but severe injury is **irreversible** and ends in **cell death**.

```text
Normal cell --(increased demand or stimulus)--> ADAPTATION (hypertrophy, hyperplasia,
     |                                           atrophy, metaplasia)
     |--(injurious agent, mild)--> REVERSIBLE INJURY (cell swelling, fatty change)
     |                                 |  stimulus removed -> recovery
     |--(severe or persistent)----> IRREVERSIBLE INJURY --> CELL DEATH
                                                         (necrosis or apoptosis)
```

### Causes of cellular injury

1. **Oxygen deprivation:** hypoxia (low oxygen, as in anaemia, lung disease, carbon monoxide poisoning) and ischaemia (reduced blood supply; the most common cause, as in myocardial infarction).
2. **Physical agents:** trauma, extremes of temperature (burns, frostbite), radiation, electric shock, sudden pressure change.
3. **Chemical agents and drugs:** poisons, alcohol, overdose of drugs such as paracetamol, environmental pollutants, even excess salt or glucose.
4. **Infectious agents:** viruses, bacteria and their toxins, fungi, parasites.
5. **Immunological reactions:** autoimmune disease, allergy (anaphylaxis).
6. **Genetic defects:** sickle cell disease, inborn errors of metabolism.
7. **Nutritional imbalance:** protein-energy malnutrition, vitamin deficiencies; also excess (obesity, atherosclerosis).
8. **Ageing.**

### Pathogenesis: mechanisms of cell injury

| Target | What goes wrong | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cell membrane damage** | Free radicals cause lipid peroxidation; phospholipases activated by calcium break down membrane lipids; toxins and complement punch holes | Loss of selective permeability, entry of Ca²⁺ and water, leakage of enzymes, damaged lysosomal membranes release enzymes that digest the cell |
| **Mitochondrial damage** | Hypoxia, toxins and Ca²⁺ overload open the mitochondrial permeability transition pore | Fall in ATP; Na⁺/K⁺ pump fails (Na⁺ and water enter → swelling); anaerobic glycolysis → lactic acid and fall in pH; release of cytochrome c starts apoptosis |
| **Ribosome damage** | Lack of ATP makes ribosomes detach from rough ER; some toxins act on ribosomes | Reduced protein synthesis; lipid accumulates (fatty change) because apoproteins are not made |
| **Nuclear damage** | Radiation, free radicals, drugs damage DNA | Failed repair triggers apoptosis; nuclear changes of necrosis (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis) |

Common pathways: ATP depletion, influx of calcium (activates damaging enzymes), free radical (reactive oxygen species) formation, and defects in membrane permeability.

### Morphology of cell injury: adaptive changes

| Adaptation | Definition | Physiological example | Pathological example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Atrophy** | Decrease in cell size and function | Shrinking of uterus after delivery, thymus in adults | Disuse atrophy of a limb in plaster, loss of nerve supply, reduced blood supply, starvation |
| **Hypertrophy** | Increase in cell size, so organ size increases (no new cells) | Skeletal muscle of athletes, uterus in pregnancy | Left ventricle in hypertension or aortic stenosis |
| **Hyperplasia** | Increase in cell number (only in cells that can divide) | Breast at puberty and lactation, liver regeneration | Endometrial hyperplasia from excess oestrogen, benign prostatic hyperplasia, skin warts |
| **Metaplasia** | Replacement of one adult cell type by another better able to stand stress | — | Ciliated columnar epithelium of bronchi changing to squamous epithelium in smokers; squamous epithelium of lower oesophagus changing to columnar in acid reflux (Barrett's oesophagus) |
| **Dysplasia** | Disordered growth: variation in cell size and shape, large dark nuclei, loss of normal arrangement | — | Cervix, bronchi in smokers; can progress to cancer, so it is called a pre-cancerous change |

Hypertrophy and hyperplasia often occur together (uterus in pregnancy). Metaplasia and early dysplasia can reverse if the stimulus is removed.

### Cell swelling

The first and commonest sign of reversible injury (also called hydropic change or vacuolar degeneration). Cause: ATP falls, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump fails, Na⁺ and water enter the cell. The cell looks pale and enlarged; the organ becomes heavier and paler. It is fully reversible if the cause is removed.

### Intracellular accumulations

Abnormal amounts of substances collect inside cells when they are produced too fast, removed too slowly or cannot be broken down.

| Substance | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Lipids (fatty change, steatosis) | Fatty liver in alcoholism, malnutrition, diabetes, obesity |
| Cholesterol | Foam cells in atherosclerotic plaques; xanthomas in skin |
| Proteins | Protein droplets in kidney tubules in heavy proteinuria |
| Glycogen | Diabetes, glycogen storage diseases |
| Pigments – exogenous | Carbon (anthracosis in lungs of city dwellers and miners), tattoo ink |
| Pigments – endogenous | Lipofuscin ("wear-and-tear" pigment of ageing), melanin, haemosiderin (iron overload), bilirubin (jaundice) |

**Pathological calcification:** dystrophic (in dead or damaged tissue with normal blood calcium, e.g. atheroma, damaged heart valves) and metastatic (in normal tissue when blood calcium is high, e.g. hyperparathyroidism).

### Cell death

| Feature | Necrosis | Apoptosis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Always pathological, accidental | Programmed; physiological or pathological |
| Cells involved | Groups of cells | Single cells |
| Cell size | Swelling | Shrinkage |
| Membrane | Breaks, contents leak | Stays intact; apoptotic bodies formed |
| Nucleus | Pyknosis (shrinks), karyorrhexis (breaks up), karyolysis (fades) | Chromatin condenses and fragments |
| Inflammation | Present | Absent (bodies eaten by macrophages) |
| Examples | Myocardial infarction, gangrene | Shedding of endometrium, removal of self-reactive lymphocytes, cell turnover |

**Types of necrosis:** coagulative (most organs after ischaemia, e.g. heart, kidney; outline preserved), liquefactive (brain infarcts, abscesses), caseous (tuberculosis, cheese-like), fat necrosis (acute pancreatitis), fibrinoid (vessel walls in severe hypertension), and gangrene (necrosis with putrefaction: dry, wet or gas gangrene).

Damaged cells leak enzymes into blood; measuring them helps diagnosis, for example troponins and creatine kinase-MB after heart muscle injury, and liver transaminases (ALT, AST) after liver injury.

## Remember

- Anatomy = structure, physiology = function, pathophysiology = disordered function in disease.
- Six levels of organisation: chemical → cellular → tissue → organ → system → organism.
- Negative feedback restores (blood pressure, glucose, temperature); positive feedback amplifies (labour, clotting).
- Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in using ATP; 0.9% NaCl is isotonic with blood.
- Four signalling forms: contact-dependent, paracrine, synaptic, endocrine.
- Four tissues: epithelial, connective, muscular, nervous.
- Adaptations: atrophy (smaller), hypertrophy (bigger cells), hyperplasia (more cells), metaplasia (cell type change), dysplasia (disordered, pre-cancerous).
- Mechanisms of injury hit membranes, mitochondria (ATP falls), ribosomes (protein synthesis falls) and the nucleus.
- Cell swelling is the earliest reversible change; necrosis is messy and inflammatory, apoptosis is tidy and silent.
