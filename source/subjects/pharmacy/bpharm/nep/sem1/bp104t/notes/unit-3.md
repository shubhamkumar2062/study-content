# Unit 3: Blood, lymphatic system, inflammation and repair, and haematological diseases

Blood is the transport medium of the body and the tissue most often sampled in the laboratory. This unit covers body fluids, the make-up and functions of blood, how blood cells and haemoglobin are formed, how blood clots, blood groups and transfusion, and the lymphatic system. It then explains inflammation and repair, the body's response to injury, and finally the main blood diseases: the anaemias and haemophilia.

## 3.1 Body fluids

Water makes up about 60% of body weight in an adult man (less in women and the elderly, more in infants).

```text
Total body water (about 60% of body weight, ~42 L in a 70 kg man)
  |-- Intracellular fluid (ICF): about two-thirds (~40% of body weight)
  |-- Extracellular fluid (ECF): about one-third (~20% of body weight)
         |-- Interstitial fluid (tissue fluid, lymph): about three-quarters of ECF
         |-- Plasma: about one-quarter of ECF (~3 L)
         |-- Transcellular fluid: CSF, synovial, pleural, aqueous humour, digestive juices
```

| Ion | ECF | ICF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main cation | Na⁺ | K⁺ |
| Main anion | Cl⁻, HCO3⁻ | Phosphates, proteins |

The difference is kept by the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump. Water moves between compartments by osmosis; between plasma and tissue fluid it moves under the balance of capillary hydrostatic pressure (pushes fluid out) and plasma colloid osmotic pressure (pulls fluid in), as described by Starling.

## 3.2 Composition of blood

Blood is a fluid connective tissue. Total volume is about 5–6 L in an adult (about 7–8% of body weight). Normal pH 7.35–7.45; it is denser and more viscous than water.

| Component | Share | Contents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plasma** | About 55% | Water (about 91%); plasma proteins (about 7%: albumin, globulins, fibrinogen); nutrients, electrolytes, hormones, gases, waste (urea, creatinine) |
| **Formed elements** | About 45% (haematocrit) | Red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets |

**Serum** is plasma without clotting factors (the fluid left after blood clots).

**Plasma proteins:**

| Protein | Made in | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Albumin | Liver | Colloid osmotic pressure (keeps fluid in vessels), carries drugs, bilirubin, fatty acids |
| Globulins (α, β, γ) | Liver; γ-globulins by plasma cells | Transport (α and β), antibodies (γ) |
| Fibrinogen | Liver | Clotting (converted to fibrin) |

Low albumin (liver disease, malnutrition, kidney disease with protein loss) causes oedema. Many drugs bind albumin, which affects how much free drug is available.

### Formed elements

| Cell | Normal count | Structure | Life span | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Red cells (erythrocytes) | About 4.5–5.5 million/µL in men, 4–5 million/µL in women | Biconcave discs about 7.5 µm, no nucleus, full of haemoglobin | About 120 days | Carry O2 and some CO2; buffering |
| White cells (leucocytes) | 4,000–11,000/µL | Nucleated | Hours to years | Defence |
| Platelets (thrombocytes) | 1.5–4 lakh/µL | Cell fragments from megakaryocytes | About 7–10 days | Haemostasis |

**White cells:**

| Type | Share | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Neutrophils | 50–70% | Multilobed nucleus, fine granules | First phagocytes in acute infection |
| Eosinophils | 1–4% | Bilobed nucleus, red granules | Parasites, allergy |
| Basophils | 0–1% | Blue granules with histamine and heparin | Allergic reactions |
| Lymphocytes | 20–40% | Large round nucleus | B cells (antibodies), T cells (cell-mediated immunity), NK cells |
| Monocytes | 2–8% | Kidney-shaped nucleus | Become macrophages in tissues |

Normal haemoglobin: about 13–17 g/dL in men and 12–15 g/dL in women.

## 3.3 Functions of blood

1. Transport of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, hormones, waste and drugs.
2. Regulation of pH (buffers such as bicarbonate and haemoglobin), body temperature (carries heat) and water balance.
3. Protection: defence by white cells and antibodies, and prevention of blood loss by clotting.

## 3.4 Haemopoiesis

**Haemopoiesis** is the formation of blood cells. In the embryo it occurs in the yolk sac, then the liver and spleen, and from late fetal life in the bone marrow. In adults it happens in the red marrow of flat bones (sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis, skull) and the ends of long bones (femur, humerus).

```text
                    Pluripotent haemopoietic stem cell
                   /                                  \
       Myeloid stem cell                          Lymphoid stem cell
   /      |        |          \                    /          \
proerythroblast  myeloblast  monoblast  megakaryoblast   B lymphocyte  T lymphocyte
   |              |           |           |                (plasma cells) (thymus)
reticulocyte  neutrophil,   monocyte   megakaryocyte
   |          eosinophil,      |           |
red cell      basophil     macrophage   platelets
```

**Erythropoiesis** (red cell formation): proerythroblast → early, intermediate and late normoblasts (haemoglobin builds up, nucleus shrinks and is pushed out) → reticulocyte (released into blood) → mature red cell in 1–2 days. It takes about a week.

**Control:** erythropoietin, a hormone made mainly by the kidneys when oxygen delivery falls (anaemia, high altitude, lung disease), stimulates red cell production. White cells are controlled by colony-stimulating factors and interleukins; platelets by thrombopoietin (from the liver).

**Needs for normal red cell formation:** protein, iron, vitamin B12 and folic acid (for DNA synthesis), vitamin C (iron absorption), vitamin B6, copper, cobalt, and thyroid and androgen hormones.

## 3.5 Formation of haemoglobin

Haemoglobin (Hb) is made of four **globin** chains, each with one **haem** group carrying one iron (Fe²⁺) atom; one Hb molecule therefore carries four O2 molecules. Adult Hb (HbA) has two α and two β chains (α2β2); fetal Hb (HbF, α2γ2) binds oxygen more tightly; HbA2 (α2δ2) is a minor form.

```text
Haem synthesis (in mitochondria and cytoplasm of developing red cells)
  succinyl CoA + glycine --(ALA synthase, needs vitamin B6)--> delta-aminolevulinic acid
  -> porphobilinogen -> ... -> protoporphyrin IX
  protoporphyrin IX + Fe2+ --(ferrochelatase)--> haem
Globin chains made on ribosomes
  haem + globin chain -> Hb subunit; four subunits -> haemoglobin
```

**Breakdown:** old red cells are destroyed by macrophages in the spleen and liver. Globin → amino acids (reused); iron → stored as ferritin or carried by transferrin back to the marrow; haem → biliverdin → bilirubin → carried by albumin to the liver → conjugated → excreted in bile. Excess bilirubin causes jaundice.

## 3.6 Mechanisms of coagulation

**Haemostasis** is the stopping of bleeding. Steps:

1. **Vascular spasm** – the injured vessel constricts.
2. **Platelet plug formation** – platelets stick to exposed collagen (helped by von Willebrand factor), change shape, release ADP, thromboxane A2 and serotonin, and attract more platelets (aggregation).
3. **Coagulation** – a cascade of clotting factors converts fibrinogen into fibrin threads, which bind the plug into a firm clot.
4. **Clot retraction and dissolution (fibrinolysis).**

### Clotting factors

| Factor | Name | Factor | Name |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | Fibrinogen | VIII | Antihaemophilic factor |
| II | Prothrombin | IX | Christmas factor |
| III | Tissue factor (thromboplastin) | X | Stuart–Prower factor |
| IV | Calcium | XI | Plasma thromboplastin antecedent |
| V | Labile factor (proaccelerin) | XII | Hageman factor |
| VII | Stable factor (proconvertin) | XIII | Fibrin-stabilising factor |

(There is no factor VI.) Factors II, VII, IX and X need vitamin K for their synthesis in the liver.

### The cascade

```text
INTRINSIC PATHWAY                      EXTRINSIC PATHWAY
(contact with collagen / glass)        (tissue damage)
XII -> XIIa                            Tissue factor (III) + VII -> VIIa
XI  -> XIa                                    |
IX  -> IXa (+ VIIIa, Ca2+, phospholipid)      |
          \                                   /
           ----------> X -> Xa  <-------------
                COMMON PATHWAY
     Xa + Va + Ca2+ + phospholipid (prothrombinase)
     Prothrombin (II) --------------> Thrombin (IIa)
     Fibrinogen (I) --(thrombin)----> Fibrin monomers
     Fibrin --(XIIIa)--> stable cross-linked fibrin clot
```

Thrombin also activates factors V, VIII, XI and XIII and platelets (positive feedback). The extrinsic pathway is fast (seconds) and is tested by **prothrombin time (PT)**; the intrinsic pathway is slower and tested by **activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)**.

**Natural anticoagulants:** antithrombin III (inactivates thrombin and Xa; action increased by heparin), protein C and protein S (inactivate Va and VIIIa), prostacyclin from endothelium.

**Fibrinolysis:** plasminogen → plasmin (by tissue plasminogen activator), which digests fibrin into fibrin degradation products.

**Normal values:** bleeding time about 1–6 minutes (platelet function), clotting time about 4–10 minutes (depends on method).

## 3.7 Blood grouping

### ABO system

Red cells carry antigens (agglutinogens) A and/or B; plasma contains naturally occurring antibodies (agglutinins) against the antigen that is absent.

| Group | Antigen on red cells | Antibody in plasma | Can receive red cells from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | A | Anti-B | A, O |
| B | B | Anti-A | B, O |
| AB | A and B | None | A, B, AB, O (universal recipient) |
| O | None | Anti-A and anti-B | O only (universal donor) |

### Rh factor

The Rh (D) antigen is present on the red cells of about 95% of Indians (Rh-positive). Rh-negative people do not have natural anti-D antibodies; they form them only after exposure to Rh-positive blood (transfusion or pregnancy).

**Haemolytic disease of the newborn (erythroblastosis fetalis):** an Rh-negative mother carrying an Rh-positive fetus is exposed to fetal cells at delivery and forms anti-D. In a later Rh-positive pregnancy, these IgG antibodies cross the placenta and destroy fetal red cells, causing anaemia, jaundice and, in severe cases, hydrops fetalis. It is prevented by giving anti-D immunoglobulin to the mother after delivery of an Rh-positive baby (and after miscarriage or other sensitising events).

## 3.8 Blood transfusion

**Indications:** acute blood loss, severe anaemia, surgery, burns (plasma), bleeding disorders (platelets, clotting factors). Today blood components are usually given instead of whole blood: packed red cells, platelets, fresh frozen plasma, cryoprecipitate.

**Before transfusion:** ABO and Rh grouping of donor and recipient, cross-matching (donor cells with recipient serum), screening of donor blood for HIV, hepatitis B and C, syphilis and malaria.

**Transfusion reactions:**
- Acute haemolytic reaction from ABO mismatch: recipient antibodies destroy donor cells → fever, chills, back pain, low blood pressure, haemoglobin in urine, acute kidney failure, clotting disorders. It can be fatal.
- Febrile and allergic reactions, circulatory overload, transmission of infection, iron overload after many transfusions.

## 3.9 Lymphatic system

### Lymphatic vessels and lymph formation

About 20 L of fluid filters out of blood capillaries each day; about 17 L is reabsorbed, and the remaining 3 L (with proteins that leaked out) enters blind-ended **lymphatic capillaries**. These have overlapping endothelial cells that act as one-way valves. Fluid inside them is called **lymph**: clear, like plasma but with less protein; lymph from the intestine is milky (chyle) because of absorbed fat carried by **lacteals**.

### Circulation of lymph

```text
Lymph capillaries -> lymphatic vessels (with valves) -> lymph nodes
 -> lymph trunks -> two ducts:
    Thoracic duct (from both legs, abdomen, left side of thorax, left arm,
                   left head and neck) -> left subclavian vein
    Right lymphatic duct (right arm, right side of head, neck, thorax)
                   -> right subclavian vein
```

Lymph is moved by the skeletal muscle pump, breathing movements (respiratory pump), contraction of smooth muscle in vessel walls and the valves that prevent backflow.

### Lymphatic organs and tissues

| Organ | Features | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red bone marrow | Primary organ | Forms all blood cells; B cells mature here |
| Thymus | Primary organ above the heart; large in children, shrinks in adults | T cells mature here |
| Lymph nodes | Bean-shaped, along vessels (neck, axilla, groin); cortex with follicles (B cells), paracortex (T cells), medulla | Filter lymph, trap microbes, start immune responses |
| Spleen | Largest lymphatic organ, left upper abdomen; white pulp (lymphocytes) and red pulp (sinusoids) | Filters blood, removes old red cells, immune responses, stores platelets |
| Tonsils, Peyer's patches, appendix (MALT) | Lymphoid tissue under mucous membranes | Defence at body entry points |

### Functions of the lymphatic system

1. Returns excess tissue fluid and leaked proteins to the blood (prevents oedema).
2. Absorbs and transports dietary fats (lacteals).
3. Defence: filters microbes and produces immune responses.

Blocked lymphatics cause **lymphoedema**, for example in filariasis (elephantiasis) or after removal of lymph nodes.

## 3.10 Inflammation: introduction and classification

**Inflammation** is the protective response of living vascularised tissue to injury. It aims to remove the cause (microbes, toxins) and the dead tissue, and to start repair. It can also cause harm (allergy, autoimmune disease).

**Cardinal signs:** rubor (redness), tumor (swelling), calor (heat), dolor (pain) (described by Celsus) and functio laesa (loss of function, added by Virchow).

| Feature | Acute inflammation | Chronic inflammation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Fast (minutes to hours) | Slow |
| Duration | Days | Weeks to years |
| Main cells | Neutrophils | Lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells |
| Tissue changes | Oedema, exudate | Tissue destruction with fibrosis and new vessels |
| Examples | Acute appendicitis, boil, sore throat | Tuberculosis, rheumatoid arthritis, leprosy |

**Granulomatous inflammation** is a special chronic type with collections of activated macrophages (epithelioid cells) and giant cells, as in tuberculosis and leprosy.

## 3.11 Pathophysiology of acute inflammation

### A. Vascular events

1. Brief vasoconstriction of arterioles (seconds).
2. **Vasodilatation** → more blood flow → redness and heat.
3. **Increased vascular permeability** (endothelial cells contract and gaps open) → protein-rich fluid (exudate) leaks into tissue → swelling.
4. Slowing of blood flow (stasis) as fluid is lost and blood becomes thicker.

**Exudate vs transudate:** exudate (inflammation) is rich in protein and cells, specific gravity above 1.020; transudate (heart failure, low albumin) is poor in protein, specific gravity below 1.012.

### B. Cellular events

```text
Margination  (white cells move to the edge of the vessel as flow slows)
 -> Rolling  (loose binding by selectins on endothelium)
 -> Adhesion (firm binding by integrins to ICAM-1, VCAM-1)
 -> Emigration / diapedesis (white cells squeeze between endothelial cells)
 -> Chemotaxis (movement towards chemical attractants: bacterial products,
               C5a, leukotriene B4, IL-8)
 -> Phagocytosis (recognition helped by opsonins such as IgG and C3b,
                  engulfment, killing by oxygen radicals and lysosomal enzymes)
```

Neutrophils arrive first (6–24 hours); monocytes/macrophages follow (24–48 hours).

### Outcomes

Complete resolution (mild injury, little tissue loss); healing by fibrosis (scar); abscess formation (pus walled off); progression to chronic inflammation.

**Systemic effects:** fever (IL-1, TNF and prostaglandin E2 act on the hypothalamus), raised white cell count (leucocytosis), acute phase proteins (CRP), raised ESR, malaise. In severe infection, sepsis and shock.

## 3.12 Mediators of inflammation

| Mediator | Source | Main actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Histamine** | Mast cells, basophils, platelets | Vasodilatation, increased permeability (immediate), itching |
| **Serotonin (5-HT)** | Platelets | Increased permeability |
| **Prostaglandins** (PGE2, PGI2) | Arachidonic acid via cyclooxygenase (COX) | Vasodilatation, pain, fever |
| **Thromboxane A2** | Platelets (COX pathway) | Platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction |
| **Leukotrienes** (LTB4, LTC4, LTD4, LTE4) | Arachidonic acid via lipoxygenase | LTB4: chemotaxis; LTC4–E4: bronchoconstriction, permeability |
| **Platelet-activating factor** | Many cells | Platelet aggregation, permeability, bronchoconstriction |
| **Cytokines** (IL-1, TNF-α, IL-6) and **chemokines** (IL-8) | Macrophages, lymphocytes | Endothelial activation, fever, acute phase response, chemotaxis |
| **Nitric oxide** | Endothelium, macrophages | Vasodilatation, killing microbes |
| **Lysosomal enzymes and oxygen radicals** | Neutrophils, macrophages | Kill microbes; can damage tissue |
| **Complement** (C3a, C5a, C3b, MAC) | Plasma | C3a/C5a release histamine; C5a chemotaxis; C3b opsonin; membrane attack complex lyses cells |
| **Kinins** (bradykinin) | Plasma (kallikrein system) | Permeability, vasodilatation, pain |
| **Clotting and fibrinolytic products** | Plasma | Fibrinopeptides increase permeability; thrombin activates endothelium |

```text
Membrane phospholipids --(phospholipase A2)--> arachidonic acid
   |-- cyclooxygenase (COX-1, COX-2) --> prostaglandins, prostacyclin, thromboxane A2
   |-- 5-lipoxygenase -----------------> leukotrienes
```

Corticosteroids reduce phospholipase A2 activity and NSAIDs block COX, which is why both reduce inflammation.

## 3.13 Repair

Repair restores tissue after inflammation, by regeneration of the original cells where possible and by fibrosis where not. Tissues are grouped by their ability to divide: **labile** cells (always dividing: epidermis, gut lining, bone marrow), **stable** cells (divide when needed: liver, kidney tubules) and **permanent** cells (cannot divide: neurons, heart muscle, which heal only by scar). Repair by connective tissue passes through granulation tissue, collagen deposition and remodelling (the same steps as wound healing in Unit 2).

## 3.14 Haematological diseases

**Anaemia** is a fall in haemoglobin (or red cell mass) below the normal level for age and sex, reducing the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood. Common symptoms of any anaemia: tiredness, weakness, breathlessness on effort, palpitations, pallor, dizziness, fast heart rate; severe anaemia can cause heart failure.

| Classification by red cell size | MCV | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microcytic hypochromic | Low | Iron deficiency, thalassaemia |
| Normocytic normochromic | Normal | Acute blood loss, haemolytic, chronic disease |
| Macrocytic | High | Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency (megaloblastic) |

### Iron deficiency anaemia

The commonest anaemia in India and the world, especially in women, children and pregnant women.

**Aetiology:**
1. Low intake: vegetarian diet poor in iron, poverty.
2. Increased need: pregnancy, lactation, growth in infancy and adolescence.
3. Reduced absorption: achlorhydria, gastrectomy, coeliac disease, tea with meals.
4. Chronic blood loss (most important in adults): heavy menstruation, hookworm infestation, bleeding peptic ulcer, piles, colon cancer, long-term NSAID use.

**Pathogenesis:** body iron stores (ferritin) are used up first → serum iron and transferrin saturation fall, total iron-binding capacity rises → too little iron for haem → small (microcytic), pale (hypochromic) red cells and low haemoglobin. Iron is also needed by other tissues, so epithelial changes appear.

**Features:** general anaemia symptoms; koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), angular stomatitis, glossitis, brittle nails and hair, pica (craving for non-food items like clay or ice), dysphagia from oesophageal webs (Plummer–Vinson syndrome). **Blood:** low Hb, low MCV and MCH, low serum ferritin.

### Megaloblastic anaemia (vitamin B12 and folic acid deficiency)

**Basic defect:** vitamin B12 and folic acid are both needed for DNA synthesis (thymidine formation). When either is lacking, DNA synthesis is slowed but RNA and protein synthesis continue, so the nucleus matures more slowly than the cytoplasm (nuclear–cytoplasmic asynchrony). Red cell precursors become large (**megaloblasts**); many die in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis), and those released are large oval red cells (**macrocytes**). Neutrophils show more than five nuclear lobes (hypersegmented). All dividing cells are affected (tongue, gut lining).

| Feature | Vitamin B12 deficiency | Folic acid deficiency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Causes | Pernicious anaemia (autoimmune loss of intrinsic factor from gastric parietal cells), gastrectomy, ileal disease or resection, strict vegetarian diet, fish tapeworm, long-term metformin or acid suppressants | Poor diet, alcoholism, pregnancy, malabsorption, haemolytic anaemia (high demand), drugs (methotrexate, phenytoin, trimethoprim) |
| Body stores | Large; last several years | Small; last a few months |
| Nervous system | Damaged: subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord, numbness, tingling, loss of position sense, memory loss, depression | Not affected (but low folate in pregnancy causes neural tube defects) |

Vitamin B12 absorption needs intrinsic factor (from the stomach) and the terminal ileum. B12 is also needed to turn methylmalonyl CoA into succinyl CoA; its lack damages myelin. **Features:** anaemia symptoms, mild jaundice (from destroyed precursors), smooth red sore tongue, and in B12 deficiency the neurological signs. Giving folic acid alone in B12 deficiency can improve the blood picture while nerve damage worsens, so the cause must be identified.

### Sickle cell anaemia

**Aetiology:** an inherited (autosomal recessive) disorder in which a single point mutation in the β-globin gene replaces glutamic acid by valine at position 6, producing haemoglobin S (HbS). Homozygotes (HbSS) have the disease; heterozygotes (sickle cell trait, HbAS) are usually healthy and partly protected against falciparum malaria. In India it is common in tribal populations of central, western and southern states.

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
HbS deoxygenated -> HbS molecules polymerise into long rigid fibres
  -> red cell becomes sickle-shaped and stiff
  -> triggers: low oxygen, dehydration, acidosis, infection, cold
  -> repeated sickling damages the membrane -> irreversibly sickled cells
  -> (1) haemolysis: cells destroyed early (life span 10-20 days) -> anaemia, jaundice,
         gallstones
  -> (2) vaso-occlusion: sickled cells block small vessels -> ischaemia, pain crises,
         infarcts in bone, lungs (acute chest syndrome), spleen, brain (stroke)
```

**Features:** chronic haemolytic anaemia; painful crises in bones, chest and abdomen; hand-foot syndrome (swollen hands and feet in infants); repeated splenic infarcts shrink the spleen (autosplenectomy) → infections; leg ulcers; stroke; aplastic crisis with parvovirus B19. **Diagnosis:** sickling test, haemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC showing HbS.

### Thalassaemia

**Aetiology:** inherited (autosomal recessive) disorders with reduced or absent synthesis of one type of globin chain: α-thalassaemia (α chains reduced; gene deletions) and β-thalassaemia (β chains reduced; point mutations). β-thalassaemia is a major health problem in India; carrier screening is advised before marriage or pregnancy.

**Pathogenesis (β-thalassaemia major):**

```text
Little or no beta chain -> less HbA -> microcytic hypochromic red cells
Excess free alpha chains precipitate inside red cell precursors
  -> damage membranes -> precursors die in marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis)
     and mature cells are destroyed in spleen (haemolysis) -> severe anaemia
Anaemia -> high erythropoietin -> massive marrow expansion
  -> bone deformity (frontal bossing, "hair-on-end" skull X-ray, prominent cheek bones)
  -> extramedullary haemopoiesis -> big liver and spleen
Increased iron absorption + repeated transfusions -> iron overload
  -> damage to heart, liver, endocrine glands (haemosiderosis)
```

**Forms:** β-thalassaemia major (homozygous; severe anaemia from about 6 months of age, needs regular transfusions), thalassaemia intermedia, and β-thalassaemia minor or trait (heterozygous; mild microcytosis, often no symptoms, raised HbA2). **Blood:** microcytic hypochromic cells, target cells, nucleated red cells; HbF raised in major.

### Hereditary and acquired haemolytic anaemias

**Haemolytic anaemia** results from early destruction of red cells (life span shorter than 120 days) faster than the marrow can replace them. Features: anaemia, jaundice (unconjugated bilirubin), dark urine, enlarged spleen, raised reticulocyte count, gallstones.

| Hereditary (defect inside the red cell) | Acquired (cause outside the red cell) |
| --- | --- |
| Membrane defects: hereditary spherocytosis (spectrin or ankyrin defect; small round fragile cells destroyed in the spleen) | Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (warm IgG or cold IgM antibodies against red cells) |
| Enzyme defects: G6PD deficiency (X-linked; red cells cannot withstand oxidant stress, so haemolysis follows some drugs such as primaquine, dapsone, sulphonamides, and fava beans or infection) | Drug-induced immune haemolysis; transfusion reactions; haemolytic disease of the newborn |
| Haemoglobin defects: sickle cell anaemia, thalassaemia | Mechanical damage (artificial heart valves), infections (malaria), burns, snake venom, hypersplenism |

### Haemophilia

**Definition:** an inherited bleeding disorder caused by deficiency of a clotting factor.

| Type | Missing factor | Inheritance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Haemophilia A (classic, commonest, about 80–85%) | Factor VIII | X-linked recessive |
| Haemophilia B (Christmas disease) | Factor IX | X-linked recessive |

Because the genes are on the X chromosome, males (one X) are affected and females are usually carriers; an affected father passes the gene to all daughters (carriers) and none of his sons.

**Pathogenesis:** lack of factor VIII or IX blocks the intrinsic pathway (the tenase complex), so thrombin and fibrin form too slowly; the platelet plug forms but is not stabilised, and bleeding restarts.

**Features:** severity depends on factor level. Bleeding into joints (haemarthrosis of knees, elbows, ankles) and muscles, often without injury in severe cases, leading to joint damage; prolonged bleeding after injury, tooth extraction or surgery; easy bruising. **Tests:** aPTT prolonged; PT, bleeding time and platelet count normal; low factor VIII or IX assay. Aspirin and intramuscular injections are avoided because they worsen bleeding.

## Remember

- ECF has Na⁺ and Cl⁻; ICF has K⁺; plasma is about 55% of blood.
- Albumin keeps fluid in vessels; low albumin causes oedema.
- Erythropoietin from the kidney drives red cell formation; B12 and folate are needed for DNA synthesis.
- Hb = 4 globin chains + 4 haem; HbA is α2β2.
- Extrinsic (VII, PT) and intrinsic (XII, XI, IX, VIII; aPTT) pathways meet at factor X → thrombin → fibrin.
- Vitamin K factors: II, VII, IX, X.
- O = universal donor, AB = universal recipient; anti-D prevents Rh disease of the newborn.
- Lymph returns about 3 L of fluid daily via the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct.
- Inflammation: vascular events (dilatation, leakage) then cellular events (margination, rolling, adhesion, diapedesis, chemotaxis, phagocytosis).
- Iron deficiency = microcytic; B12/folate = megaloblastic; sickle cell = Glu6Val; thalassaemia = reduced globin chain; haemophilia A = factor VIII, X-linked.
