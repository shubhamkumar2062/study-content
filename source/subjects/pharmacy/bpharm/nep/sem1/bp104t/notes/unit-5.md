# Unit 5: Cardiovascular system and cardiovascular diseases

The heart and blood vessels deliver oxygen and nutrients to every cell and carry away wastes. Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death in India, and drugs for them form a large part of every pharmacy. This unit covers the anatomy of the heart, the circulation, blood vessels, the conduction system and heartbeat, cardiac output and the cardiac cycle, regulation of blood pressure, pulse and the ECG, and then the pathophysiology of hypertension, arrhythmias, heart failure and ischaemic heart disease.

## 5.1 Anatomy of the heart

**Position and size:** a hollow muscular organ about the size of a closed fist, weighing about 250–350 g, lying in the **mediastinum** between the lungs, behind the sternum, with about two-thirds to the left of the midline. Its pointed **apex** points down, forward and left (at the fifth left intercostal space in the midclavicular line, where the apex beat is felt); its **base** faces up and back.

**Coverings (pericardium):**

```text
Fibrous pericardium   tough outer sac; anchors heart, prevents over-filling
Serous pericardium    parietal layer (lines fibrous sac)
                      pericardial cavity with a little lubricating fluid
                      visceral layer = epicardium (on the heart surface)
```

**Layers of the heart wall:** epicardium (outer), **myocardium** (thick cardiac muscle; thickest in the left ventricle), endocardium (inner endothelial lining continuous with vessels and covering the valves).

**Chambers:** two thin-walled **atria** (receiving chambers) and two thick-walled **ventricles** (pumping chambers). The interatrial septum (with the fossa ovalis, the remnant of the fetal foramen ovale) and interventricular septum separate right and left sides. The left ventricle wall is about three times thicker than the right because it pumps against the higher systemic pressure.

**Valves** (keep blood moving in one direction):

| Valve | Position | Cusps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tricuspid (right atrioventricular) | Right atrium → right ventricle | 3 |
| Mitral (bicuspid, left atrioventricular) | Left atrium → left ventricle | 2 |
| Pulmonary (semilunar) | Right ventricle → pulmonary trunk | 3 |
| Aortic (semilunar) | Left ventricle → aorta | 3 |

The atrioventricular valves are held by **chordae tendineae** attached to **papillary muscles**, which stop the cusps from turning back into the atria when the ventricles contract. Closure of AV valves gives the first heart sound ("lub"); closure of semilunar valves gives the second ("dub").

**Blood supply of the heart (coronary circulation):** the right and left coronary arteries arise from the base of the aorta just above the aortic valve. The left coronary artery divides into the **left anterior descending** (interventricular) and **circumflex** arteries; the right coronary artery supplies the right side and usually the SA and AV nodes. Coronary flow occurs mainly during diastole, because contracting muscle squeezes the vessels in systole. Venous blood drains through cardiac veins into the **coronary sinus**, which opens into the right atrium.

## 5.2 Blood circulation

```text
          PULMONARY CIRCULATION (low pressure)
Right atrium -> tricuspid -> right ventricle -> pulmonary valve -> pulmonary trunk
  -> pulmonary arteries (deoxygenated blood) -> lung capillaries (gas exchange)
  -> pulmonary veins (oxygenated blood) -> left atrium

          SYSTEMIC CIRCULATION (high pressure)
Left atrium -> mitral -> left ventricle -> aortic valve -> aorta -> arteries
  -> arterioles -> capillaries of tissues -> venules -> veins
  -> superior and inferior venae cavae (and coronary sinus) -> right atrium
```

Pulmonary arteries are the only arteries that carry deoxygenated blood, and pulmonary veins the only veins that carry oxygenated blood (in the adult). Special circulations include the coronary, cerebral, and **hepatic portal circulation** (blood from the stomach, intestines, spleen and pancreas goes to the liver through the portal vein before reaching the heart; this is the basis of the first-pass effect of oral drugs).

## 5.3 Blood vessels: structure and functions

All vessels except capillaries have three coats: **tunica intima** (endothelium on a basement membrane), **tunica media** (smooth muscle and elastic tissue) and **tunica externa (adventitia)** (connective tissue).

| Feature | Artery | Vein | Capillary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direction of flow | Away from heart | Towards heart | Between arterioles and venules |
| Wall | Thick, much muscle and elastic tissue in media | Thin, less muscle; adventitia thickest | Single layer of endothelium |
| Lumen | Narrow, round | Wide, often collapsed | About the size of one red cell (5–10 µm) |
| Valves | None (except at origin of aorta and pulmonary trunk) | Present, especially in limbs | None |
| Pressure | High, pulsatile | Low | Low |
| Function | Carry blood under pressure; elastic arteries smooth flow, muscular arteries distribute, arterioles control resistance | Return blood; act as a reservoir (about 60–65% of blood volume at rest) | Exchange of gases, nutrients and wastes with tissues |

**Types of arteries:** elastic (conducting) arteries such as the aorta stretch in systole and recoil in diastole, keeping blood moving (the "Windkessel" effect); muscular (distributing) arteries carry blood to organs; **arterioles** are the main resistance vessels that set blood pressure and local flow.

**Types of capillaries:** continuous (muscle, skin, brain — part of the blood–brain barrier), fenestrated (kidney glomeruli, intestine, endocrine glands), and sinusoidal (liver, spleen, bone marrow; large gaps).

**Venous return** is aided by valves, the skeletal muscle pump, the respiratory pump and venoconstriction. Faulty valves cause varicose veins.

## 5.4 Conduction system of the heart and the heartbeat

Cardiac muscle is **autorhythmic**: special cells generate impulses on their own.

```text
SA node (in right atrium wall near opening of superior vena cava)
   pacemaker, about 60-100 impulses per minute
   |  impulse spreads through both atria -> atria contract
   v
AV node (lower interatrial septum)
   delays impulse about 0.1 s so atria finish emptying
   |
   v
Bundle of His (AV bundle) -> only electrical link between atria and ventricles
   |
Right and left bundle branches (along interventricular septum)
   |
Purkinje fibres -> spread rapidly through ventricular muscle
   -> ventricles contract from apex upward
```

If the SA node fails, the AV node can take over at about 40–60 beats/min, and the ventricles alone at about 20–40 beats/min.

**Pacemaker potential:** SA node cells have no stable resting potential. They slowly depolarise because of the "funny" current (If, Na⁺ entry) and Ca²⁺ entry until threshold; the upstroke is due to Ca²⁺ entry, and repolarisation to K⁺ exit.

**Ventricular muscle action potential:**

| Phase | Event | Ion movement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Rapid depolarisation | Fast Na⁺ entry |
| 1 | Early partial repolarisation | K⁺ out |
| 2 | Plateau (about 0.2 s) | Ca²⁺ in (L-type channels) balances K⁺ out |
| 3 | Repolarisation | K⁺ out |
| 4 | Resting potential (about −90 mV) | Na⁺/K⁺ pump |

The plateau gives a long **refractory period**, so heart muscle cannot be tetanised and has time to fill between beats.

### Regulation of the heartbeat by the autonomic nervous system

The **cardiovascular centre** in the medulla oblongata receives input from baroreceptors, chemoreceptors and higher centres.

| | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic (vagus) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Supply | SA node, AV node, atria and ventricles | Mainly SA and AV nodes and atria |
| Transmitter and receptor | Noradrenaline on β1 receptors | Acetylcholine on M2 receptors |
| Heart rate (chronotropic) | Increased | Decreased |
| Force of contraction (inotropic) | Increased | Little effect on ventricles |
| AV conduction (dromotropic) | Faster | Slower |

At rest vagal tone dominates, which is why the resting rate (about 72/min) is lower than the intrinsic SA node rate (about 90–100/min). Hormones (adrenaline, thyroxine), temperature, and K⁺ and Ca²⁺ levels also affect the heart.

## 5.5 Cardiac cycle

The **cardiac cycle** is the sequence of events from one heartbeat to the next. At 72 beats/min it lasts about 0.8 s: atrial systole about 0.1 s and atrial diastole 0.7 s; ventricular systole about 0.3 s and ventricular diastole 0.5 s.

| Phase | Valves | Events |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Atrial systole | AV open, semilunar closed | Atria contract, topping up the ventricles (adds about 20–30% of filling) |
| 2. Isovolumetric contraction | All closed | Ventricles start contracting; pressure rises sharply, volume unchanged; AV valves close (first sound) |
| 3. Ventricular ejection | Semilunar open | Ventricular pressure exceeds aortic (about 80 mm Hg) and pulmonary pressure; blood ejected (rapid then slow ejection) |
| 4. Isovolumetric relaxation | All closed | Ventricles relax; semilunar valves close (second sound) |
| 5. Ventricular filling | AV open | Blood flows from atria into ventricles (rapid, then slow filling – diastasis) |

**Volumes:** end-diastolic volume about 120–130 mL; end-systolic volume about 50–60 mL; **stroke volume** about 70 mL; **ejection fraction** (stroke volume ÷ end-diastolic volume) normally about 55–70%.

## 5.6 Cardiac output

**Cardiac output (CO)** = volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute.

```text
Cardiac output = Stroke volume x Heart rate
Example: 70 mL x 72 beats/min = 5040 mL/min, about 5 L/min at rest
Cardiac index = cardiac output / body surface area (about 3.2 L/min/m2)
```

**Factors controlling stroke volume:**
1. **Preload** — degree of stretch of ventricular muscle before contraction, set by end-diastolic volume and venous return. **Frank–Starling law:** within limits, the more the heart is filled in diastole, the more forcefully it contracts.
2. **Contractility** — strength of contraction at a given preload; increased by sympathetic stimulation, adrenaline and digoxin-type drugs; decreased by hypoxia, acidosis and heart muscle damage.
3. **Afterload** — the pressure the ventricle must overcome to eject blood (aortic pressure); high blood pressure increases afterload and lowers stroke volume.

**Heart rate** is set by autonomic nerves, hormones, age, temperature and exercise. During heavy exercise cardiac output can rise four- to five-fold or more.

## 5.7 Blood pressure and its regulation

**Blood pressure (BP)** is the pressure exerted by blood on the walls of arteries.

| Term | Meaning | Normal adult value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Systolic pressure | Highest pressure during ventricular systole | About 120 mm Hg |
| Diastolic pressure | Lowest pressure during diastole | About 80 mm Hg |
| Pulse pressure | Systolic − diastolic | About 40 mm Hg |
| Mean arterial pressure | Diastolic + one-third of pulse pressure | About 93 mm Hg |

```text
Blood pressure = Cardiac output x Total peripheral resistance
Resistance rises with narrower vessels (resistance varies inversely with radius
to the fourth power), longer vessels and thicker (more viscous) blood.
```

BP is measured with a sphygmomanometer and stethoscope (Korotkoff sounds: first sound = systolic, disappearance = diastolic).

### Short-term regulation (seconds): nervous reflexes

**Baroreceptor reflex:**

```text
BP rises -> stretch of baroreceptors in carotid sinus (nerve IX) and aortic arch (nerve X)
 -> more impulses to cardiovascular centre in medulla
 -> vagal activity up, sympathetic activity down
 -> heart rate and force fall, arterioles and veins dilate
 -> cardiac output and resistance fall -> BP returns to normal
(The reverse happens when BP falls, e.g. on standing up quickly.)
```

**Chemoreceptor reflex:** carotid and aortic bodies sense low O2, high CO2 and low pH → sympathetic vasoconstriction → BP rises. **Higher centres:** emotion, pain and exercise also affect BP.

### Intermediate and long-term regulation: hormones and kidneys

| Mechanism | Action |
| --- | --- |
| **Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system** | Low BP or low Na⁺ at the kidney → renin released from juxtaglomerular cells → angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → (angiotensin-converting enzyme, mainly in lungs) → angiotensin II → strong vasoconstriction, aldosterone release (Na⁺ and water retention), thirst and ADH release → BP rises |
| **Antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin)** | Released from posterior pituitary when blood is concentrated or volume low → water reabsorption in collecting ducts and vasoconstriction |
| **Atrial natriuretic peptide** | Released by stretched atria → Na⁺ and water excretion, vasodilatation → BP falls |
| **Adrenaline and noradrenaline** | Increase heart rate, force and vasoconstriction |
| **Renal pressure natriuresis** | High BP → kidneys excrete more salt and water → blood volume and BP fall (main long-term control) |
| **Local factors** | Nitric oxide and prostacyclin dilate; endothelin constricts |

## 5.8 Pulse

The **pulse** is the rhythmic expansion of an artery wall caused by the pressure wave of each ventricular ejection. It is felt where an artery lies near the surface over a firm structure: radial (wrist, the commonest), carotid (neck, used in emergencies), brachial (elbow, used for BP), femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis and temporal. Normal resting pulse rate in adults is 60–100/min (about 72). Rate, rhythm, volume, character and the condition of the vessel wall are noted. **Tachycardia** is a rate above 100/min; **bradycardia** below 60/min (normal in trained athletes).

## 5.9 Electrocardiogram (ECG)

The ECG is a record of the electrical activity of the heart taken from electrodes on the skin. A standard ECG has 12 leads: three standard limb leads (I, II, III), three augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) and six chest leads (V1–V6).

```text
        R
        |
   P    |      T
  / \   |     / \
_/   \__Q  S_/   \______
   PR interval  QRS   ST segment
```

| Wave or interval | Represents | Normal duration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P wave | Atrial depolarisation | About 0.08–0.1 s |
| PR interval | From start of atrial depolarisation to start of ventricular depolarisation (includes AV nodal delay) | 0.12–0.20 s |
| QRS complex | Ventricular depolarisation (atrial repolarisation hidden in it) | 0.06–0.10 s (under 0.12 s) |
| ST segment | Ventricles fully depolarised; should be at the baseline | — |
| T wave | Ventricular repolarisation | — |
| QT interval | Total ventricular depolarisation and repolarisation (varies with heart rate) | About 0.4 s |

**Uses:** heart rate and rhythm, arrhythmias, conduction blocks, myocardial ischaemia and infarction (ST elevation or depression, Q waves), chamber enlargement, electrolyte disturbances (tall T waves in high K⁺), and drug effects (QT prolongation by some drugs).

## 5.10 Pathophysiology of hypertension

**Definition:** persistently raised arterial blood pressure, usually taken as 140/90 mm Hg or above on repeated readings (guidelines differ slightly; some use 130/80).

**Types:**
- **Primary (essential) hypertension** — about 90–95% of cases; no single cause.
- **Secondary hypertension** — due to a known cause: kidney disease (glomerulonephritis, renal artery stenosis), endocrine causes (primary aldosteronism, Cushing's syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, hyperthyroidism), coarctation of the aorta, pregnancy (pre-eclampsia), drugs (oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, NSAIDs, decongestants).

**Risk factors for essential hypertension:** family history, age, obesity, high salt intake, alcohol, smoking, physical inactivity, stress, diabetes.

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
Genetic tendency + environment (salt, obesity, stress)
  |-- Kidney: reduced ability to excrete Na+ -> more salt and water retained
  |           -> blood volume and cardiac output up
  |-- Overactive sympathetic nervous system -> higher heart rate,
  |           vasoconstriction, renin release
  |-- Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system overactive -> angiotensin II
  |           vasoconstriction + aldosterone (salt retention)
  |-- Endothelial dysfunction: less nitric oxide, more endothelin
  |-- Insulin resistance and obesity -> sympathetic activity, salt retention
  v
Raised cardiac output and/or raised peripheral resistance
  -> sustained high pressure -> structural thickening of arteriolar walls
  -> resistance rises further (a vicious cycle)
```

**Clinical features:** usually none ("silent killer"); sometimes headache, dizziness, nosebleeds or blurred vision. Detected on routine measurement.

**Complications (target-organ damage):**

| Organ | Damage |
| --- | --- |
| Heart | Left ventricular hypertrophy → heart failure; coronary artery disease |
| Brain | Stroke (haemorrhage or thrombosis), hypertensive encephalopathy |
| Kidney | Nephrosclerosis → chronic kidney disease |
| Eyes | Hypertensive retinopathy |
| Arteries | Atherosclerosis, aortic aneurysm and dissection |

**Malignant (accelerated) hypertension:** very high BP (often diastolic above 120 mm Hg) with rapid organ damage, papilloedema and fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles; a medical emergency.

## 5.11 Pathophysiology of cardiac arrhythmias

**Definition:** any abnormality in the rate, rhythm or conduction of the heartbeat.

**Mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | Explanation |
| --- | --- |
| **Abnormal automaticity** | SA node fires too fast or too slow, or other cells (ectopic foci) start firing because of ischaemia, high sympathetic drive, low K⁺ or stretch |
| **Triggered activity** | Extra impulses arise from after-depolarisations: early (with long QT, some drugs) or delayed (with Ca²⁺ overload, digoxin toxicity) |
| **Re-entry** | An impulse travels round a loop of tissue with one-way block and slow conduction and re-excites the same tissue again and again (the commonest mechanism of tachycardias) |
| **Conduction block** | Impulse delayed or stopped at the AV node or bundle branches |

**Causes:** ischaemic heart disease and myocardial infarction, heart failure, hypertension, valve disease, electrolyte imbalance (K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺), thyroid disease, drugs (digoxin, some antiarrhythmics, antipsychotics that prolong QT), caffeine, alcohol, stimulants, and congenital channel defects.

**Common types:**

| Arrhythmia | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| Sinus tachycardia / bradycardia | Normal rhythm, rate above 100 or below 60/min |
| Atrial and ventricular premature beats (ectopics) | Extra early beats, felt as "missed beats" |
| Atrial fibrillation | Chaotic atrial activity, no P waves, irregularly irregular pulse; blood stagnates in the atria → clot → stroke risk |
| Atrial flutter | Regular rapid atrial rate (about 300/min) with "saw-tooth" waves |
| Paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia | Sudden attacks of regular fast rhythm, often by re-entry in or around the AV node |
| Ventricular tachycardia | Wide QRS, fast; can reduce output and progress to fibrillation |
| Ventricular fibrillation | Chaotic ventricular activity, no effective output, cardiac arrest; fatal without defibrillation |
| Heart block | First degree (long PR), second degree (some impulses not conducted), third degree (complete; atria and ventricles beat independently, very slow ventricular rate) |

**Effects:** palpitations, dizziness, fainting (syncope), breathlessness, chest pain, heart failure, stroke (atrial fibrillation) and sudden cardiac death.

## 5.12 Pathophysiology of congestive heart failure

**Definition:** a condition in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at a high filling pressure. "Congestive" refers to the fluid that collects in the lungs and tissues.

**Causes:**
1. **Damage to heart muscle:** ischaemic heart disease and myocardial infarction (commonest), cardiomyopathy, myocarditis.
2. **Pressure overload:** hypertension, aortic stenosis.
3. **Volume overload:** valve regurgitation, congenital shunts.
4. **Filling problems:** constrictive pericarditis, restrictive disease.
5. **Arrhythmias.**
6. **High-output states:** severe anaemia, thyrotoxicosis.

**Types:** left-sided and right-sided; systolic failure (weak contraction, low ejection fraction) and diastolic failure (stiff ventricle, poor filling, preserved ejection fraction); acute and chronic.

**Pathogenesis — compensation that becomes harmful:**

```text
Damaged heart -> cardiac output falls
  |
  |-- Frank-Starling: more filling -> more force (helps at first, later the
  |   overstretched heart weakens)
  |-- Sympathetic activation -> faster rate, stronger beat, vasoconstriction
  |   (raises afterload and oxygen demand; damages heart muscle over time)
  |-- Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone: Na+ and water retention -> more blood volume
  |   -> more congestion and oedema; angiotensin II also causes vasoconstriction
  |   and fibrosis of heart muscle
  |-- ADH release -> water retention
  |-- Ventricular remodelling: hypertrophy and dilatation, fibrosis
  v
Short-term support of output, but long-term worsening of the failing heart
(a vicious cycle). Natriuretic peptides (BNP) rise and are used in diagnosis.
```

| Left heart failure | Right heart failure |
| --- | --- |
| Blood backs up into the lungs (pulmonary congestion, oedema) | Blood backs up into the systemic veins |
| Breathlessness on exertion, orthopnoea (breathless lying flat), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea | Raised jugular venous pressure |
| Cough with frothy pink sputum, crackles at lung bases | Swelling of feet and ankles (pitting oedema), enlarged tender liver, ascites |
| Tiredness, cold extremities (low output) | Loss of appetite, nausea (congested gut) |

The commonest cause of right heart failure is left heart failure. Right failure from lung disease is called **cor pulmonale**.

## 5.13 Ischaemic heart disease

**Ischaemic heart disease (coronary artery disease)** is a group of conditions in which the heart muscle receives too little blood (oxygen supply less than demand), almost always because of atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries.

### Atherosclerosis

**Definition:** a chronic inflammatory disease of large and medium-sized arteries in which lipid-rich plaques (atheromas) form in the intima, narrowing the lumen and weakening the wall.

**Risk factors:**

| Cannot be changed | Can be changed |
| --- | --- |
| Age, male sex (women catch up after menopause), family history, genetics | High LDL cholesterol and low HDL, hypertension, smoking, diabetes, obesity, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, stress |

**Pathogenesis (response-to-injury hypothesis):**

```text
1. Endothelial injury/dysfunction (high BP, smoking, high LDL, high glucose,
   turbulent flow)
2. LDL enters the intima and is oxidised
3. Endothelium expresses adhesion molecules -> monocytes enter and become macrophages
4. Macrophages take up oxidised LDL via scavenger receptors -> foam cells
   -> fatty streak (earliest visible lesion, can be seen even in young people)
5. Growth factors (PDGF) -> smooth muscle cells migrate from media to intima,
   divide and lay down collagen -> fibrous cap over a lipid (necrotic) core
   -> fibrofatty plaque (atheroma)
6. Plaque grows -> narrows lumen -> reduced flow (stable angina)
7. Complications: plaque rupture or erosion -> platelet aggregation and thrombus
   -> sudden blockage (unstable angina, MI, stroke); calcification;
   bleeding into plaque; embolism; weakening of wall -> aneurysm
```

Unstable ("vulnerable") plaques have a thin fibrous cap, a large lipid core and many inflammatory cells, and rupture easily.

### Coronary artery disease

When coronary atherosclerosis narrows a coronary artery by about 70% or more, flow cannot rise to meet demand during exertion. The resulting syndromes range from stable angina to acute coronary syndromes (unstable angina, myocardial infarction) and sudden cardiac death.

### Angina pectoris

**Definition:** chest pain caused by transient myocardial ischaemia without permanent damage (no necrosis).

**Pathogenesis:** oxygen demand (increased by exertion, emotion, cold, heavy meals, faster heart rate) exceeds supply through narrowed coronaries → anaerobic metabolism → lactic acid, adenosine and other substances stimulate pain fibres.

| Type | Mechanism | Features |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stable (classic, exertional)** | Fixed atherosclerotic narrowing | Pain on effort, relieved by rest or sublingual nitrate within minutes |
| **Unstable** | Plaque rupture with a partial, non-occlusive thrombus | Pain at rest, new onset or increasing; high risk of MI; part of acute coronary syndrome |
| **Variant (Prinzmetal's)** | Coronary artery spasm | Pain at rest, often at night; transient ST elevation |

**Clinical features:** central chest pain described as tightness, heaviness or pressure, spreading to the left arm, jaw, neck or back, lasting a few minutes; may be accompanied by breathlessness.

### Myocardial infarction (heart attack)

**Definition:** death (necrosis) of an area of heart muscle due to prolonged ischaemia, usually from sudden blockage of a coronary artery.

**Pathogenesis:**

```text
Atherosclerotic plaque ruptures -> collagen and lipid core exposed
 -> platelets adhere and aggregate (thromboxane A2, ADP)
 -> coagulation cascade -> occlusive thrombus -> no blood flow
 -> within about 20-30 minutes of severe ischaemia: irreversible injury begins
 -> coagulative necrosis spreading from inner (subendocardial) layer outward
    over several hours (so early reopening of the artery saves muscle)
 -> enzymes and proteins leak into blood (troponin I and T, CK-MB)
 -> inflammation (neutrophils, then macrophages) -> granulation tissue
    -> scar over weeks
```

**Clinical features:** severe, crushing central chest pain lasting more than 20–30 minutes, not relieved by rest or nitrates; radiation to left arm, jaw or back; sweating, nausea, vomiting, breathlessness, anxiety; fall in BP. Elderly and diabetic patients may have "silent" MI with little pain.

**Diagnosis:** ECG changes (ST elevation in transmural MI, later Q waves; ST depression or T inversion in non-ST elevation MI) and raised cardiac biomarkers (troponins are the most sensitive and specific).

**Complications:** arrhythmias (ventricular fibrillation is the main cause of early death), heart failure and cardiogenic shock, rupture of the ventricular wall or a papillary muscle, ventricular aneurysm, pericarditis, mural thrombus with embolism, and recurrent infarction.

| Feature | Stable angina | Myocardial infarction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Temporary ischaemia | Prolonged ischaemia with necrosis |
| Pain duration | A few minutes | Over 20–30 minutes |
| Relief | Rest, nitrates | Not relieved by rest or nitrates |
| ECG | Normal or ST depression during pain | ST elevation or depression, Q waves |
| Cardiac enzymes | Normal | Raised troponin, CK-MB |

## Remember

- Heart: 4 chambers, 4 valves; left ventricle wall thickest; coronary flow occurs mainly in diastole.
- Conduction: SA node → atria → AV node (delay) → bundle of His → bundle branches → Purkinje fibres.
- Plateau (Ca²⁺ entry) gives a long refractory period; the heart cannot be tetanised.
- Sympathetic (β1) raises rate and force; vagus (M2) slows the heart.
- Cardiac cycle about 0.8 s; stroke volume about 70 mL; ejection fraction about 55–70%.
- CO = SV × HR (about 5 L/min); BP = CO × peripheral resistance.
- Baroreceptor reflex for quick control; renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and kidneys for long-term control.
- ECG: P = atrial depolarisation, QRS = ventricular depolarisation, T = ventricular repolarisation; PR 0.12–0.20 s.
- Hypertension: 90–95% essential; target organs heart, brain, kidney, eyes, arteries.
- Arrhythmia mechanisms: abnormal automaticity, triggered activity, re-entry, block.
- Heart failure: compensations (sympathetic, RAAS, remodelling) eventually worsen it; left failure → lung congestion, right failure → oedema and raised JVP.
- Atherosclerosis: endothelial injury → oxidised LDL → foam cells → fatty streak → fibrous plaque → rupture and thrombus → MI.
