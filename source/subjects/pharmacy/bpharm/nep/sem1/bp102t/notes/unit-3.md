# Unit 3: Solid dosage forms

Solid dosage forms are the most used medicines: powders, tablets and capsules. This unit covers what each one is, its types, merits and limits, the excipients that go into it, and how it is made at the bench and in industry. Classification of powders, special powders (effervescent, efflorescent, hygroscopic, eutectic), types of tablets, granulation methods and capsule types and sizes are the favourite questions.

## 3.1 Powders

### Definition

A **pharmaceutical powder** is a mixture of finely divided dry drugs and/or chemicals meant for internal or external use. It may be supplied in bulk or divided into single doses.

### Classification

```text
Powders
 ├── Bulk powders (patient measures the dose)
 │     ├── Internal: antacid powders, laxative powders, oral rehydration powder
 │     └── External: dusting powders, insufflations, dentifrices (tooth powders),
 │                   douche powders, snuffs
 └── Divided powders (single doses, chartulae)
       ├── Simple (one drug) or compound (more than one drug)
       └── Packed in powder papers, sachets, foil or strip packs
```

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| More stable than liquids (no water, less hydrolysis) | Unpleasant taste is not masked |
| Quick dissolution and absorption because of large surface area | Unsuitable for drugs that are bitter, corrosive or deliquescent |
| Flexible dose; easy for children and those who cannot swallow tablets | Bulk powders give inaccurate doses |
| Large doses can be given (antacids) | Time-consuming to divide and pack at the bench |
| Easy to carry and cheap | Hygroscopic or volatile drugs are hard to protect |

### Excipients in powders

Diluents (lactose, starch, sucrose), adsorbents (light magnesium oxide, kaolin), glidants (talc, colloidal silica), sweeteners and flavours, colours, and for effervescent powders an acid–base couple.

### Methods of preparation

1. **Size reduction** of each ingredient in a mortar (trituration) or mill, and **sieving** to a uniform size.
2. **Mixing** by trituration in a mortar, **spatulation** on a slab (for light or eutectic powders), **sifting** through a sieve, **tumbling** in a jar or blender, or **geometric dilution** for potent drugs.
3. **Dividing**: by weighing each dose (accurate) or, for non-potent drugs, by the block-and-divide method.
4. **Packing**: wrap divided doses in powder papers (waxed paper for hygroscopic or volatile drugs), fill bulk powders in wide-mouth jars or sifter cans.

### Dusting powders

Finely divided powders applied to the skin for lubricant, protective, absorbent, antiseptic or antipruritic action.
- **Medicated** dusting powders: for intact skin (for example with zinc oxide, boric acid, an antifungal).
- **Surgical** dusting powders: applied to wounds or body cavities; **must be sterile**.
- Requirements: very fine (no grittiness), free-flowing, uniform, non-irritant. Talc and kaolin from natural sources may carry spores, so they are sterilised by dry heat.
- Common bases: talc, starch, zinc oxide, kaolin. Packed in sifter-top containers; labelled "For external use only"; not to be applied on large open wounds unless sterile.

### Effervescent powders and granules

- Contain an **acid** (citric acid and/or tartaric acid) and a **base** (sodium bicarbonate) with the drug. In water they react and release **carbon dioxide**, which masks bitter or salty taste, gives a pleasant drink and helps the drug dissolve.
- Usual weight ratio **citric acid : tartaric acid : sodium bicarbonate = 1 : 2 : 3.44**. A mixture of the two acids is used because citric acid alone gives a sticky mass and tartaric acid alone gives crumbly granules.
- Preparation:
  - **Fusion (dry) method**: the water of crystallisation of citric acid monohydrate, released on warming, acts as the binder. Powders are mixed, warmed in a dish until a damp mass forms, passed through a sieve and dried at a low temperature.
  - **Wet method**: a non-aqueous liquid such as ethanol is used as the moistening agent, the mass is granulated and dried.
- Store in airtight containers; keep away from moisture, which starts the reaction early.

### Efflorescent powders

- Crystalline substances that **lose their water of crystallisation** on exposure to air or warmth, becoming powdery and releasing water that may make a powder damp. Examples: alum, citric acid monohydrate, sodium carbonate decahydrate, ferrous sulphate heptahydrate.
- Handling: use the **anhydrous** (dried) form in an equivalent amount, and pack in well-closed containers.

### Hygroscopic and deliquescent powders

- **Hygroscopic**: absorb moisture from air (for example ammonium chloride, potassium citrate). **Deliquescent**: absorb so much that they dissolve in it (for example calcium chloride, potassium carbonate, zinc chloride).
- Handling: work quickly in a dry place, do not triturate too finely, add an inert adsorbent, wrap divided doses in **waxed or double paper**, and pack in **airtight** containers with a desiccant where suitable.

### Eutectic mixtures

- A **eutectic mixture** is a mixture of two or more solids that **liquefies when mixed** because the mixture melts below room temperature (its melting point is lower than that of either component).
- Examples: **camphor with menthol**, camphor with phenol, menthol with thymol, camphor with chloral hydrate.
- Handling: (a) mix each component separately with an inert adsorbent such as **light magnesium oxide, kaolin or starch**, then combine lightly by spatulation; or (b) allow them to liquefy and then absorb the liquid onto the adsorbent; dispense in capsules or waxed papers with "store in a cool place".

## 3.2 Tablets

### Definition

**Tablets** are solid unit dosage forms containing one or more drugs, with or without excipients, prepared by **compression** (compressed tablets) or by **moulding** (moulded tablets). They vary in shape, size, weight and hardness.

### Types of tablets

| Group | Type | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oral, swallowed | Standard compressed | Plain uncoated tablets |
| | Multiple compressed | Layered tablets (two or three layers) and compression-coated (tablet within a tablet), used to separate incompatible drugs or for two release rates |
| | Sugar-coated | Coat of sugar to mask taste and improve look |
| | Film-coated | Thin polymer film; faster and lighter than sugar coat |
| | Enteric-coated | Coat resists stomach acid and dissolves in the intestine; protects acid-labile drugs or the stomach |
| | Chewable | Chewed before swallowing; for children and antacids |
| | Modified (sustained/controlled) release | Release the drug slowly over time |
| Dissolved or dispersed first | Effervescent | Dropped in water; release CO2 |
| | Dispersible and soluble | Disperse or dissolve in water before taking |
| Oral cavity | Buccal and sublingual | Placed in the cheek or under the tongue; absorbed directly into blood |
| | Lozenges (troches) | Dissolve slowly in the mouth for a local effect on mouth and throat |
| Other routes | Vaginal tablets, implants | Local or long-acting effect |
| Moulded | Tablet triturates | Small, soft, quickly soluble tablets made by moulding a moist mass (drug with lactose, sometimes sucrose) |

### Moulded tablets and pills

- **Moulded tablets (tablet triturates)**: the drug is mixed with lactose (and sucrose), moistened with an alcohol–water mixture to a paste, pressed into the holes of a **tablet-triturate mould**, pushed out with pegs and dried. They are soft, dissolve quickly, and suit small doses of potent drugs.
- **Pills**: small, **spherical** solid dosage forms for oral use, made by rolling a plastic mass (**pill mass**) of drug and **excipients** (liquid glucose, syrup, acacia, powdered liquorice) on a pill machine, then coating if needed. They were common before tablets and are now rarely made in modern practice; the term is often used loosely for tablets.

### Advantages and disadvantages of tablets

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Accurate, uniform dose in each unit | Hard to swallow for children, elderly and unconscious patients |
| Most stable dosage form; long shelf life | Some drugs are difficult to compress (poor flow, low density) |
| Easy to carry, store and take; low cost of manufacture | Bitter or odorous drugs need coating |
| Taste masking and identification by coating, colour, shape and embossing | Bioavailability problems for poorly soluble drugs |
| Controlled and enteric release possible | Coating adds steps and cost |

### Excipients for tablets

| Excipient | Function | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diluent (filler) | Bulk for small doses | Lactose, microcrystalline cellulose, dibasic calcium phosphate, mannitol |
| Binder | Adhesion of particles into granules | Starch paste, PVP, gelatin, acacia, HPMC |
| Disintegrant | Breaks the tablet in fluid | Starch, sodium starch glycolate, croscarmellose sodium, crospovidone |
| Lubricant | Reduces friction with die wall | Magnesium stearate, stearic acid |
| Glidant | Improves flow of granules | Colloidal silica, talc |
| Anti-adherent | Stops sticking to punches | Talc, corn starch |
| Colours, flavours, sweeteners | Appearance, taste, identity | Approved dyes, vanillin, saccharin |
| Wetting agents | Help water penetrate | Sodium lauryl sulphate |

### Methods of preparation

```text
Wet granulation:
 weigh → mix powders → add binder solution (wet massing) → screen wet mass
       → dry granules → dry screening → add lubricant/glidant/disintegrant → compress

Dry granulation (for moisture- or heat-sensitive drugs):
 weigh → mix → compact into slugs (slugging) or sheets (roller compaction)
       → break and screen into granules → add lubricant → compress

Direct compression (simplest):
 weigh → mix drug with directly compressible excipients → add lubricant → compress
```

- **Granulation** improves flow, density and compressibility and prevents segregation of ingredients.
- Compression is done on a **single-punch** or **rotary** tablet machine: the die is filled, the upper punch comes down and compresses, and the lower punch ejects the tablet.
- Coating (sugar, film or enteric) may follow.

## 3.3 Capsules

### Definition

**Capsules** are solid dosage forms in which the drug and excipients are enclosed in a hard or soft soluble shell, usually made of **gelatin**.

### Types of capsules

| Type | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Hard gelatin capsules** (two-piece) | A body and a cap that fit together; filled with powders, granules or pellets; shell made of gelatin, water, colours and opacifier (titanium dioxide) |
| **Soft gelatin capsules** (softgels, one-piece) | Thicker, flexible shell of gelatin with a **plasticiser** (glycerin or sorbitol); sealed; filled with oils, solutions or suspensions |
| Non-gelatin capsules | Made of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) or starch; suitable for vegetarians |
| Modified-release capsules | Contain coated pellets that release over time; enteric-coated capsules |

Gelatin is obtained by partial hydrolysis of collagen: **type A** by acid treatment (mainly pig skin) and **type B** by alkali treatment (bones and hides).

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Tasteless, odourless; mask bitter drugs | Not suitable for very soluble salts that irritate the stomach (high local concentration) |
| Easy to swallow; elegant | Not for efflorescent or deliquescent drugs (shell becomes soft or brittle) |
| Fewer excipients than tablets; less pressure, so faster release | Gelatin is of animal origin (religious and dietary concerns) |
| Easy extemporaneous filling in small numbers | Hard to divide a dose; may stick in the oesophagus if taken without water |
| Liquids can be given as softgels | Sensitive to humidity and heat in storage |

### Capsule sizes

Hard capsules come in standard sizes from **000 (largest) to 5 (smallest)**. Approximate fill volumes:

| Size | 000 | 00 | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Volume (ml) | 1.37 | 0.95 | 0.68 | 0.50 | 0.37 | 0.30 | 0.21 | 0.13 |

The fill weight depends on the powder's bulk density. The pharmacist selects the smallest size that holds the dose plus diluent, by trial filling.

### Excipients in capsule fills

Diluents (lactose, starch, microcrystalline cellulose), glidants (colloidal silica), lubricants (magnesium stearate), disintegrants, and wetting agents (sodium lauryl sulphate) for poorly wettable drugs. Softgel fills use oily vehicles (vegetable oils), PEG, and surfactants.

### Methods of preparation

**Hard gelatin capsules**
1. **Shells** are made by dipping stainless-steel pins into warm gelatin solution, drying, stripping, trimming and joining body and cap.
2. **Filling at the bench (punch method)**: the powder is spread on a slab to a thickness of about one-third the body length; the empty body is pressed (punched) into it repeatedly until full, the cap is put on, and each capsule is weighed.
3. **Capsule-filling boards** (hand-operated) fill many capsules at once.
4. **Automatic machines** separate caps, fill bodies by auger, dosator or tamping disc, rejoin and eject.
5. **Finishing**: dedusting and polishing; optional banding or sealing.

**Soft gelatin capsules**
- Made and filled in **one step**: the **plate process** (two gelatin sheets with the fill between, pressed in a mould) or the **rotary die process** (two ribbons of gelatin pass between rotating dies while the fill is injected, sealing and cutting the capsules). Then dried to the right hardness.

## Remember

- Powders: bulk (internal/external) and divided; surgical dusting powders must be sterile.
- Effervescent ratio citric : tartaric : bicarbonate = 1 : 2 : 3.44; fusion method uses the water of crystallisation of citric acid.
- Efflorescent → use anhydrous form; hygroscopic → waxed paper, airtight; eutectic (camphor + menthol) → adsorbent such as light magnesium oxide.
- Tablets: compressed or moulded; know coated, chewable, effervescent, buccal, sublingual, lozenges; tablet triturates and pills are moulded or rolled.
- Wet granulation → mix, wet mass, screen, dry, size, lubricate, compress; dry granulation uses slugging or roller compaction.
- Capsules: hard (two-piece) and soft (one-piece, plasticiser); sizes 000 largest to 5 smallest.
