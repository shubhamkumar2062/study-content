# Unit 5: Semisolid dosage forms and suppositories

Semisolids (ointments, pastes, creams and gels) are spread on the skin or mucous membranes. Suppositories and pessaries are solid at room temperature but melt or dissolve inside a body cavity. This unit covers their definitions, classification, merits, bases and excipients, and methods of preparation, plus the **displacement value** numerical. Classification of ointment bases, ideal suppository bases and displacement value are the most common questions.

## 5.1 Semisolid dosage forms: definitions

| Form | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ointment** | Soft, greasy or non-greasy semisolid for external application to the skin or mucous membranes, with the drug dissolved or dispersed in a base | Sulphur ointment, simple ointment |
| **Paste** | Stiff semisolid containing a **large proportion of finely powdered solids**; less greasy, forms a protective layer and absorbs exudate | Compound zinc paste, zinc and salicylic acid paste (Lassar's paste) |
| **Cream** | Semisolid **emulsion**, either O/W (vanishing, washable) or W/O (cold, emollient), for external use | Aqueous cream (O/W), cold cream (W/O) |
| **Gel** | Semisolid system in which a liquid is held in a three-dimensional network of a gelling agent; usually clear and non-greasy | Carbomer gel, sodium CMC gel |
| **Jelly** | Gel with a high water content, used as a lubricant or vehicle | Lubricating jelly |

### Classification of semisolids

```text
Semisolid dosage forms
 ├── By use: dermatological (skin), ophthalmic (eye ointments, sterile),
 │           rectal, vaginal, nasal
 ├── By site of action: epidermic (surface), endodermic (deeper layers),
 │                      diadermic (through skin into blood)
 └── By nature: ointments, pastes, creams, gels, jellies
```

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Local action at the site; avoids first-pass metabolism | Greasy bases are messy and stain clothes |
| Easy to apply; prolonged contact with skin | Dose is not accurate |
| Protect, soften and moisturise skin | Can cause irritation or sensitisation |
| Suitable for patients who cannot take oral medicines | Less stable than solids (separation, microbial growth in water-containing bases) |
| | Bulky to carry; may be oxidised or go rancid |

## 5.2 Ointment bases

The **base** is the vehicle that carries the drug. It decides how the product feels, how much it occludes the skin, whether it can be washed off, and how the drug is released.

### Ideal properties of an ointment base

Inert and non-irritant, non-sensitising; chemically and physically stable; compatible with drugs; easy to apply and remove; releases the drug readily; does not dehydrate or damage skin; smooth, non-gritty; free from objectionable odour; spreads easily; does not support microbial growth.

### Classification of ointment bases

| Type | Examples | Properties |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Oleaginous (hydrocarbon) bases** | White and yellow soft paraffin, hard paraffin, liquid paraffin, plastibase; vegetable oils and animal fats (older) | Greasy, occlusive, emollient; water cannot be added; not washable; cheap and stable; slow drug release |
| **Absorption bases** — anhydrous | Wool fat (lanolin), wool alcohols, beeswax, hydrophilic petrolatum | Can **absorb water** to form W/O emulsions; greasy; not washable |
| **Absorption bases** — W/O emulsions | Hydrous wool fat, cold cream | Already contain water; can take up more |
| **Water-removable (emulsifying) bases** | Emulsifying ointment (anionic: emulsifying wax), cetrimide emulsifying ointment (cationic), cetomacrogol emulsifying ointment (non-ionic); hydrophilic ointment | Form **O/W** systems with water; **washable**; non-greasy; good for absorbing exudate |
| **Water-soluble bases** | Polyethylene glycols (**macrogols**, PEG) of various molecular weights | No fat; completely water-soluble; washable; non-occlusive; may reduce activity of some preservatives and drugs |

### Other excipients in semisolids

| Excipient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emulsifying agents | Stabilise creams | Emulsifying wax, cetostearyl alcohol, polysorbates |
| Gelling agents | Form gel networks | Carbomers, HPMC, sodium CMC, gelatin |
| Humectants | Prevent drying, keep moist | Glycerin, propylene glycol, sorbitol |
| Preservatives | Prevent growth in water-containing products | Parabens, chlorocresol, benzalkonium chloride |
| Antioxidants | Prevent rancidity | BHA, BHT, tocopherol |
| Penetration enhancers | Help drug cross the skin | Propylene glycol, dimethyl sulphoxide, surfactants |
| Neutraliser (for carbomer) | Develops gel viscosity | Triethanolamine, sodium hydroxide |
| Buffers, perfumes | pH control, odour | Citrate buffer, approved perfumes |

## 5.3 General methods of preparation

### Ointments

1. **Trituration (incorporation) method**: used when the base is soft and the drug is insoluble or present in small amount. The finely powdered drug is rubbed on a **slab with a spatula** (or in a mortar) with a small amount of base (or a levigating agent such as liquid paraffin) until smooth, then the rest of the base is added gradually by **geometric dilution**. Liquids are incorporated by making a small depression in the base and working them in.
2. **Fusion method**: used when the base contains hard ingredients (hard paraffin, waxes, cetostearyl alcohol). Ingredients are melted in a dish on a water bath **in order of decreasing melting point** (highest first), so that none is overheated; the drug is added and the mass is **stirred until cold** to avoid separation. Volatile ingredients are added near the end at a low temperature.
3. **Chemical reaction method**: the ointment involves a reaction, for example **non-staining iodine ointment** (iodine reacts with a fixed oil to form a non-staining product) or ointments in which a salt is formed.
4. **Emulsification method**: see creams.

### Pastes

- Made by **trituration** (powder levigated with part of the base, then the rest added) or **fusion** (base melted, powder stirred in until cold).
- Powders must be very fine and sieved to avoid grittiness. Pastes are stiffer, less greasy and more porous than ointments, and stay where applied.

### Creams

- **Emulsification method**: the **oil phase** (oils, waxes, oil-soluble ingredients, emulsifier) and the **aqueous phase** (water, water-soluble ingredients, preservative) are heated separately to about the same temperature (around 70–75 °C); the **internal phase is added to the external phase** with continuous stirring, and stirring continues until the cream sets on cooling. Perfumes and volatile drugs are added below about 40 °C.
- O/W creams (vanishing creams) are washable and non-greasy; W/O creams (cold creams) give a cooling feel as water evaporates and are emollient.

### Gels

1. Sprinkle the **gelling agent** onto the vehicle (water, or a water–alcohol–glycol mixture) with continuous stirring to avoid lumps; allow it to hydrate and swell.
2. Add dissolved drug, preservative and humectant.
3. For **carbomer gels**, add a neutraliser (triethanolamine) slowly; viscosity rises sharply at near-neutral pH.
4. Remove air bubbles (stand or apply gentle vacuum) and fill.

**Packing**: collapsible tubes (aluminium or laminated plastic) protect from air and contamination; wide-mouth jars for pastes and some ointments. Label "For external use only". Eye ointments are sterile and packed in small sterile tubes.

## 5.4 Suppositories and pessaries

### Definition

**Suppositories** are solid dosage forms of various weights and shapes, meant for insertion into a body orifice (usually the **rectum**), where they **melt, soften or dissolve** to release the drug for local or systemic action. **Pessaries** are suppositories meant for the **vagina**.

### Types

| Type | Site | Shape and note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rectal suppositories** | Rectum | Cone- or torpedo-shaped, tapered at one end; moulds commonly 1 g and 2 g |
| **Vaginal suppositories (pessaries)** | Vagina | Globular, ovoid or cone-shaped; larger than rectal; vaginal tablets are also used |
| **Urethral suppositories (bougies)** | Urethra | Thin, pencil-shaped |
| Nasal and ear cones | Nose, ear | Small; now rarely used |

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Useful when the patient is vomiting, unconscious, or cannot swallow | Not liked by many patients; embarrassing |
| Drugs that irritate the stomach or are destroyed by stomach acid can be given | Absorption can be slow and irregular |
| Partly avoid first-pass metabolism (lower rectal veins drain into the systemic circulation) | May cause local irritation |
| Local action in the rectum or vagina (piles, constipation, infections) | Need cool storage; may melt in hot climates |
| Suitable for children | Leakage after insertion |

### Formulation excipients

Base (the main excipient), drug in fine powder, surfactants to help release and spreading, preservatives (water-containing bases), antioxidants, hardening agents (waxes) for hot climates, and plasticisers.

### Properties of an ideal suppository base

1. **Melts at body temperature** (about 37 °C) or **dissolves/disperses** in body fluids.
2. Solid at room temperature; not too brittle; not too soft for handling.
3. Non-toxic and non-irritant to mucous membranes.
4. Compatible with the drug; releases the drug readily.
5. **Contracts on cooling** so that suppositories leave the mould easily.
6. Chemically and physically stable on storage; does not go rancid; no unwanted change in melting point (no troublesome polymorphism).
7. Small gap between melting and solidifying points, and enough viscosity when melted to keep a powder suspended.
8. Wetting and emulsifying properties; able to take up water.

### Types of suppository bases

| Type | Examples | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fatty (oleaginous) bases** | **Theobroma oil (cocoa butter)**; synthetic hard fats (hydrogenated vegetable oils, Witepsol, Suppocire-type products) | Theobroma oil melts at about 30–35 °C, just below body temperature. It shows **polymorphism**: if overheated, it sets into unstable forms with low melting points that solidify slowly, so it must be melted gently (not above about 36 °C). Contracts well on cooling. Can go rancid. Synthetic hard fats avoid most of these problems |
| **Water-soluble / water-miscible bases** | **Glycero-gelatin base** (glycerin, gelatin and water); **polyethylene glycols (macrogols)** | Dissolve in body fluids rather than melt. Glycero-gelatin is hygroscopic (dehydrating, may irritate), has a laxative effect and suits vaginal use; needs a preservative. PEG bases do not melt at body temperature, are stable in hot climates and need no cold storage, but may irritate; moistening before insertion helps |
| **Emulsifying bases** | Mixtures of fats with emulsifiers (for example polysorbate–fat blends) | Absorb water; help drug release |

### Lubrication of moulds

Use a lubricant **opposite in nature to the base**: for **theobroma oil**, soap spirit (an alcoholic soap solution) if needed (it usually contracts enough without one); for **glycero-gelatin**, a light oil such as **liquid paraffin**. PEG bases need no lubricant.

## 5.5 Displacement value

When a drug is put into a suppository it takes up space that would otherwise hold base. Because the drug and base have different densities, the weight of base displaced is not the same as the weight of drug.

**Displacement value (DV)** of a drug = the number of parts by weight of drug that displace **1 part by weight of the base** (usually theobroma oil).

```text
Weight of base needed = (N × E) − (total weight of drug / DV)
N = number of suppositories; E = weight of one blank suppository from the mould (mould capacity)
```

### Worked example 1: amount of base

Make 10 suppositories, each with 200 mg of drug Z (DV = 1.5), in a mould that holds 1.0 g of theobroma oil.
- Base for 10 blank suppositories = 10 × 1.0 = 10.0 g
- Total drug = 10 × 0.2 = 2.0 g; base displaced = 2.0/1.5 = 1.33 g
- Base needed = 10.0 − 1.33 = **8.67 g**
In practice an excess is calculated (for example for 12 suppositories) to allow for loss.

### Worked example 2: finding a displacement value

Six blank theobroma oil suppositories weigh 6.0 g. Six medicated suppositories containing 40% w/w of drug W weigh 7.2 g.
- Drug in medicated batch = 40% of 7.2 = 2.88 g; base in medicated batch = 7.2 − 2.88 = 4.32 g
- Base displaced by the drug = 6.0 − 4.32 = 1.68 g
- DV = 2.88/1.68 = **1.71**

When a mould calibrated with theobroma oil is used for **glycero-gelatin base**, the weight of base is multiplied by about **1.2**, because glycero-gelatin is denser.

## 5.6 General methods of preparation of suppositories

1. **Hand rolling (hand moulding)**: the base and drug are kneaded to a plastic mass, rolled into a cylinder and cut into pieces, which are shaped by hand. Simple, for small numbers; less elegant.
2. **Compression (cold compression)**: the grated base mixed with drug is forced into a mould by a compression machine. No heat, so suits heat-sensitive drugs and avoids settling of powders.
3. **Fusion (pour moulding)**, the most common method:

```text
 calibrate mould (find E) → calculate base using DV → melt base gently on water bath
 → disperse finely powdered drug (or dissolve) in part of the melted base
 → add rest of base, stir → pour into lubricated mould just above setting point,
   slightly overfilling → cool (refrigerate if needed) → scrape off excess
 → open mould, remove suppositories → wrap in foil, pack in boxes, store cool
```

- Stir continuously while pouring so that insoluble drug does not settle into the tips.
- Label: "For rectal (or vaginal) use only. Do not swallow." Store in a cool place.

## Remember

- Ointment greasy base; paste = high powder content; cream = emulsion (O/W vanishing, W/O cold); gel = liquid in a polymer network (carbomer needs neutraliser).
- Ointment bases: oleaginous, absorption (anhydrous and W/O), water-removable (O/W emulsifying), water-soluble (PEG).
- Methods: trituration, fusion (melt in decreasing melting-point order, stir until cold), chemical reaction, emulsification.
- Suppository: rectal; pessary: vaginal; bougie: urethral.
- Theobroma oil melts about 30–35 °C, polymorphic, do not overheat; glycero-gelatin hygroscopic; PEG dissolves, heat-stable.
- Base needed = N × E − (drug/DV); DV = weight of drug ÷ weight of base it displaces; glycero-gelatin factor about 1.2.
