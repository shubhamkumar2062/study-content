# Unit 4: Monophasic and biphasic liquids

Liquid dosage forms are easy to swallow and act quickly, which makes them popular for children and the elderly. This unit covers **monophasic** liquids (one phase: true solutions) for internal use and for external use or body cavities, the excipients and methods used for official preparations, and the two **biphasic** liquids: suspensions and emulsions. Flocculated versus deflocculated suspensions, emulsifying agents, identification tests for emulsions, and definitions with examples of the external preparations are asked very often.

## 4.1 Monophasic and biphasic: the idea

```text
Liquid dosage forms
 ├── Monophasic (one phase, clear solution)
 │     ├── Internal: aromatic waters, syrups, elixirs, linctuses, solutions, drops
 │     └── External / body cavities: liniments, lotions, throat paints, applications,
 │         gargles, mouthwashes, enemas, eye drops, ear drops, nasal drops, tinctures*
 └── Biphasic (two phases)
       ├── Suspensions: solid particles in a liquid
       └── Emulsions:   liquid globules in another, immiscible liquid
 (*tinctures are used internally and externally)
```

**Advantages of liquids**: easy to swallow; flexible dose; quick absorption because the drug is already dissolved (in solutions); drugs that irritate the stomach as solids can be given diluted.
**Disadvantages**: bulky and breakable to carry; less stable (hydrolysis, microbial growth); dose measured by the patient may be inaccurate; unpleasant taste is more noticeable.

## 4.2 Monophasic liquids for internal use

### Aromatic waters

**Aromatic waters** are clear, saturated aqueous solutions of volatile oils or other aromatic or volatile substances. They are used mainly as **flavoured vehicles** and some as carminatives.

Methods of preparation:
1. **Distillation**: the plant material or volatile oil is distilled with water and the distillate collected (for fresh, aromatic plant parts).
2. **Solution method**: the volatile oil is shaken with water, often with an inert **distributing agent** such as purified talc to increase the surface for dissolving; the mixture is filtered to give a clear water.
3. **Dilution of a concentrated water**: a concentrated aromatic water (oil dissolved in alcohol and water) is diluted with purified water, for example **1 part concentrate with 39 parts water** for concentrated peppermint water.

Examples: peppermint water, dill water, cinnamon water, chloroform water (used as a preservative vehicle). Store in well-filled, airtight, light-resistant containers; volatile oils are lost and oxidised on exposure.

### Syrups

**Syrups** are concentrated, viscous aqueous solutions of sucrose or other sugars, with or without flavours and medicinal substances. **Simple Syrup IP** contains **66.7% w/w sucrose**. At this concentration the syrup is almost saturated and resists microbial growth by its high osmotic pressure (self-preserving).

- **Flavoured (non-medicated) syrups** are vehicles (for example orange syrup); **medicated syrups** carry a drug (for example compound syrup of ferrous phosphate).
- Methods:
  1. **Solution with heat**: sucrose dissolved in purified water by gentle heating; quick but excess heat causes **inversion** (sucrose → glucose + fructose) and **caramelisation** (browning).
  2. **Agitation without heat**: for heat-sensitive or volatile ingredients; slower.
  3. **Percolation**: purified water percolates slowly through a bed of sucrose.
  4. **Addition of a medicated liquid to syrup**: a tincture or fluid extract is added to simple syrup.
- Problems: **crystallisation** around the cap ("cap-locking") if too concentrated; **fermentation** if too dilute or contaminated; inversion and browning with heat. Preservatives (parabens, benzoic acid) are added to diluted or medicated syrups.

### Elixirs

**Elixirs** are clear, pleasantly flavoured, **sweetened hydroalcoholic solutions** for oral use. The alcohol keeps both water-soluble and alcohol-soluble ingredients in solution.

- **Non-medicated elixirs** are vehicles (aromatic elixir); **medicated elixirs** carry drugs (for example paediatric paracetamol elixir, piperazine citrate elixir).
- Preparation: dissolve the alcohol-soluble ingredients in the alcohol and the water-soluble ones in the aqueous part; **add the aqueous solution slowly to the alcoholic solution** with stirring (to avoid precipitation), make up to volume and filter, using talc if the liquid is cloudy.
- Other ingredients: glycerin, propylene glycol and sorbitol as cosolvents and sweeteners; syrup; colours and flavours.
- Advantages: clear, palatable, stable, easy to prepare. Disadvantages: alcohol is unsuitable for some patients (children, liver disease, those avoiding alcohol); less effective at masking salty tastes.

### Linctuses

**Linctuses** are viscous liquid oral preparations, usually with a high proportion of syrup and glycerin, used for **cough**. They are taken in small doses, **slowly and without dilution**, so that they coat and soothe the throat. Examples: simple linctus (containing citric acid in a syrup base), paediatric simple linctus, pholcodine linctus (a cough suppressant).

## 4.3 Monophasic liquids for external use and body cavities

| Preparation | Definition | Key points | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Liniments** | Liquid or semi-liquid preparations applied to the skin **with friction (rubbing)** | Alcoholic or oily vehicles; counter-irritant, rubefacient or soothing; not on broken skin; "For external use only" | Turpentine liniment, methyl salicylate liniment, camphor liniment |
| **Lotions** | Liquid preparations applied to the skin **without friction**, dabbed on with cotton or gauze | Cooling, soothing, protective or antiseptic; may be solutions or suspensions; shake if suspended | Calamine lotion, salicylic acid lotion |
| **Throat paints** | Viscous liquids applied to the throat or mouth with a brush | **Glycerin** base makes them stick and act longer | Compound iodine paint (Mandl's throat paint) |
| **Applications** | Liquid or semi-liquid preparations applied to the skin, often for parasites | Applied over large areas, left on | Benzyl benzoate application |
| **Gargles** | Aqueous solutions used to treat or rinse the **throat**; not swallowed | Often supplied concentrated, diluted with warm water before use | Povidone-iodine gargle, phenol gargle |
| **Mouthwashes** | Aqueous solutions for the **mouth and gums**: cleansing, deodorant, antiseptic | Diluted before use; not swallowed | Compound sodium chloride mouthwash, chlorhexidine mouthwash |
| **Enemas** | Liquid preparations given **rectally** | **Evacuant** (to empty the bowel: soap, oil, phosphate enemas) or **retention** (medicated, nutritive, sedative; held in the rectum); warm to body temperature before use | Phosphate enema, arachis oil retention enema |
| **Eye drops** | **Sterile** aqueous or oily solutions or suspensions instilled into the eye | Sterile, isotonic, buffered to a comfortable pH, particle-free; preservative in multidose packs; discard a set time after opening (usually four weeks) | Sodium chloride eye drops, chloramphenicol eye drops |
| **Ear drops** | Solutions or suspensions instilled into the ear | Vehicles: water, glycerin, propylene glycol, alcohol; for cleansing, wax removal or infection | Sodium bicarbonate ear drops (wax removal), hydrogen peroxide ear drops |
| **Nasal drops** | Aqueous solutions instilled into the nose | Should be isotonic and near neutral and must not damage ciliary movement; **oily nasal drops are avoided** (risk of lipid pneumonia) | Sodium chloride nasal drops, xylometazoline nasal drops |
| **Tinctures** | Alcoholic or hydroalcoholic solutions made from vegetable drugs or chemicals | Prepared by **maceration** or **percolation**; used as such or to make other preparations | Compound benzoin tincture, orange tincture, belladonna tincture |

### Maceration and percolation (for tinctures)

- **Maceration**: the coarsely powdered drug is soaked in the menstruum (solvent) in a closed vessel for a set time (classically 7 days) with frequent shaking; the liquid is strained, the residue pressed (marc), and the liquids are mixed and filtered. Suits unorganised drugs (resins, gums) and soft tissues.
- **Percolation**: the moistened drug is packed in a **percolator** and the menstruum flows slowly through it; the drug is exhausted completely by fresh solvent. Suits organised drugs (roots, barks, leaves) and potent drugs.

```text
    Percolator
     ┌─────────┐  ← menstruum added on top
     │ filter  │
     │  drug   │  ← moistened, packed drug
     │  bed    │
     └───┬─────┘
         │  ← percolate collected slowly drop by drop
```

## 4.4 Official preparations: excipients and methods of preparation

**Excipients used in liquid dosage forms**

| Excipient | Purpose | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vehicles and solvents | Dissolve or carry drug | Purified water, ethanol, glycerin, propylene glycol, sorbitol solution, vegetable oils |
| Cosolvents and solubilisers | Increase solubility | Ethanol, glycerin, polysorbates |
| Sweeteners | Taste | Sucrose, sorbitol, saccharin sodium |
| Flavours | Taste and smell | Peppermint oil, orange oil, vanillin |
| Colours | Identity and look | Approved water-soluble dyes |
| Preservatives | Prevent microbial growth | Methyl and propyl parabens, benzoic acid, sodium benzoate, chlorocresol, benzalkonium chloride (eye drops) |
| Antioxidants | Prevent oxidation | Ascorbic acid, sodium metabisulphite |
| Buffers | Hold pH | Phosphate, citrate, borate buffers |
| Tonicity agents | Isotonicity (eye, nasal drops) | Sodium chloride, dextrose |
| Viscosity enhancers | Body, slow flow | Methylcellulose, CMC sodium, glycerin |

**General methods**
1. **Simple solution**: dissolve the solute in the vehicle, with warming or size reduction if needed, filter and make up (most solutions, syrups, elixirs).
2. **Solution with chemical reaction**: two ingredients react to form the medicinal substance in solution (for example strong ammonium acetate solution made by neutralising acetic acid with ammonium bicarbonate and ammonia).
3. **Extraction**: maceration and percolation (tinctures, extracts).
4. **Distillation**: some aromatic waters.
5. **Packing and labelling**: suitable container (amber glass for light-sensitive products, dropper bottles for drops), with "For external use only", "Shake well", storage and discard dates.

## 4.5 Suspensions

### Definition

A **suspension** is a biphasic liquid dosage form in which finely divided **insoluble solid particles** (dispersed phase) are spread uniformly through a liquid (continuous phase, usually aqueous), with the help of suspending agents.

**Types by use**: oral suspensions (antacids, antibiotics), topical suspensions (calamine lotion), parenteral suspensions (sterile, for IM injection), ophthalmic suspensions, and dry powders for reconstitution.

### Flocculated and deflocculated suspensions

| Property | Flocculated | Deflocculated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Particles | Form loose aggregates (**flocs**) held by weak forces | Stay as separate particles |
| Rate of settling | Fast | Slow |
| Supernatant | Clears quickly | Stays cloudy for long |
| Sediment | Loose, high volume, easy to redisperse by shaking | Compact, low volume, may form a hard **cake** that cannot be redispersed |
| Appearance | Less elegant (clear layer forms) | More elegant |
| Dose uniformity after shaking | Good | Poor if caked |

**Controlled flocculation**: the ideal is a partly flocculated system kept from settling by a structured vehicle. Flocculating agents: **electrolytes** (reduce zeta potential), **surfactants**, and **polymers** (form bridges between particles).

```text
 Flocculated after standing        Deflocculated after standing
 ┌──────────────┐                  ┌──────────────┐
 │  clear       │                  │ cloudy, slow │
 │  liquid      │                  │ settling     │
 │░░░░░░░░░░░░░░│ ← loose, bulky   │              │
 │░░░░░░░░░░░░░░│   sediment       │▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓│ ← compact cake
 └──────────────┘                  └──────────────┘
```

### Stokes' law

The rate of settling of a particle is given by

```text
v = 2 r² (ρs − ρl) g / 9η
v = settling velocity; r = particle radius; ρs, ρl = density of solid and liquid;
g = gravity; η = viscosity of the medium
```

Settling is slowed by **smaller particle size**, **higher viscosity** of the vehicle and a **smaller density difference**.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
| --- | --- |
| Insoluble drugs can be given as liquids | Physically unstable: settling, caking, crystal growth |
| Better taste for bitter drugs given as insoluble forms | Must be shaken; dose uniformity depends on it |
| Drugs unstable in solution (some antibiotics) are more stable as suspensions or dry powders for reconstitution | Bulky; needs careful formulation |
| Sustained action possible (injectable suspensions) | Freezing or heating may break the system |

### Formulation excipients

- **Wetting agents**: help water wet hydrophobic powders; surfactants (polysorbate 80), glycerin, alcohol.
- **Suspending agents (thickeners)**: natural (acacia, tragacanth, sodium alginate, xanthan gum), semisynthetic (methylcellulose, sodium carboxymethylcellulose, HPMC), clays (bentonite, magnesium aluminium silicate), synthetic (carbomers), and colloidal silica.
- **Flocculating agents**: electrolytes, surfactants, polymers.
- Buffers, preservatives, sweeteners, flavours, colours.

### General methods of preparation

1. **Dispersion method**: triturate the insoluble powder with a little wetting agent or vehicle to a smooth paste; prepare the suspending agent in part of the vehicle; add it gradually to the paste with trituration; transfer and make up to volume. Examples: calamine lotion, magnesium hydroxide mixture.
2. **Precipitation method**: the insoluble solid is formed in the liquid itself, by (a) **change of solvent** (an alcoholic solution poured into water), (b) **change of pH**, or (c) **double decomposition** (two soluble salts react to give an insoluble product, for example in white lotion).
3. Label "**Shake well before use**"; store in wide-mouth bottles with space for shaking.

**Sedimentation volume** F = Vu/Vo (ultimate sediment volume ÷ original volume) is a simple measure of stability; F close to 1 is desirable.

## 4.6 Emulsions

### Definition

An **emulsion** is a biphasic liquid dosage form in which one liquid is dispersed as small **globules** (dispersed or internal phase) throughout another liquid with which it does not mix (continuous or external phase), stabilised by an **emulsifying agent**.

### Types

- **Oil-in-water (O/W)**: oil globules in water; for oral use (masks oily taste), can be washed off the skin. Example: liquid paraffin emulsion.
- **Water-in-oil (W/O)**: water droplets in oil; emollient, occlusive creams. Example: cold cream.
- **Multiple emulsions**: W/O/W or O/W/O, for controlled release.
- **Microemulsions**: clear, thermodynamically stable systems with very small droplets.

Uses: oral (mask taste of oils), topical (creams, lotions), parenteral (fat emulsions for nutrition).

### Emulsifying agents

| Class | Examples | Type usually formed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural, carbohydrate | Acacia, tragacanth, agar, pectin | O/W |
| Natural, protein and sterol | Gelatin, egg yolk (O/W); wool fat, wool alcohols, cholesterol (W/O) | Depends on agent |
| Finely divided solids | Bentonite, aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide (O/W); some solids wetted by oil give W/O | Depends on wetting |
| Synthetic surfactants: anionic | Sodium lauryl sulphate, soaps (sodium, potassium soaps give O/W; calcium soaps give W/O) | O/W or W/O |
| Cationic | Cetrimide, benzalkonium chloride | O/W |
| Non-ionic | Polysorbates (Tweens) O/W; sorbitan esters (Spans) W/O | Depends on HLB |

**HLB (hydrophile–lipophile balance)** scale: low values are oil-loving, high values water-loving. Approximate uses: 3–6 W/O emulsifiers, 7–9 wetting agents, 8–18 O/W emulsifiers, 13–15 detergents, 15–18 solubilisers.

### Tests for identification of emulsion type

| Test | Method | O/W | W/O |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dilution | Add water | Mixes and dilutes | Does not dilute (separates) |
| Dye | Add a water-soluble dye (amaranth) and view under a microscope | Background coloured, globules clear | Globules coloured |
| Dye (oil-soluble) | Add Sudan III | Globules coloured | Background coloured |
| Conductivity | Pass a current with electrodes and a bulb | Conducts, bulb glows | Does not conduct |
| Fluorescence | View under UV light (oils fluoresce) | Spotty fluorescence | Whole field fluoresces |
| Cobalt chloride paper | Filter paper soaked in CoCl2 and dried (blue) is dipped | Turns pink | Stays blue |

### Formulation excipients

Emulsifying agents (above), auxiliary emulsifiers and thickeners (cetostearyl alcohol, methylcellulose), preservatives (parabens, because water and natural gums support growth), **antioxidants** (oils may go rancid: BHA, tocopherol), sweeteners and flavours for oral emulsions, humectants for topical ones.

### General methods of preparation

1. **Dry gum (continental) method**: triturate the oil with powdered acacia in a dry mortar, add the water all at once and triturate rapidly until a clicking primary emulsion forms, then dilute. Ratio **oil : water : gum**:
   - fixed oils **4 : 2 : 1**
   - mineral oils (liquid paraffin) **3 : 2 : 1**
   - volatile oils **2 : 2 : 1**
2. **Wet gum (English) method**: make a mucilage of acacia with water first, then add the oil slowly with trituration.
3. **Bottle (Forbes) method**: for volatile oils and other low-viscosity oils; shake powdered acacia with the oil in a dry bottle, add water and shake vigorously.
4. **Mechanical methods**: mixers, homogenisers, colloid mills, ultrasonics for large scale or fine emulsions.
5. Label "Shake well before use".

**Instability of emulsions** (for understanding): **creaming** (globules rise or settle; reversible by shaking), **cracking or breaking** (globules fuse and the phases separate; irreversible), **phase inversion** (O/W changes to W/O or the reverse).

## Remember

- Aromatic waters: distillation, solution (with talc), dilution of concentrate (1 + 39).
- Simple Syrup IP 66.7% w/w sucrose, self-preserving; heat causes inversion and browning.
- Elixir = sweet hydroalcoholic solution; add aqueous to alcoholic part slowly.
- Liniment rubbed; lotion dabbed; throat paint has glycerin; enema evacuant or retention; eye drops sterile and isotonic; avoid oily nasal drops.
- Tinctures by maceration or percolation.
- Flocculated: fast settling, loose sediment, no cake; deflocculated: slow settling, cake. Stokes: v ∝ r²(ρs − ρl)/η.
- Emulsion tests: dilution, dye, conductivity, fluorescence, CoCl2 paper. Dry gum 4:2:1 fixed, 3:2:1 mineral, 2:2:1 volatile.
