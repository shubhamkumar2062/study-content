# Unit 1: Introduction to the profession of pharmacy

This unit is the "who we are" part of the subject. It explains how pharmacy grew in India as an education, an industry and a set of organisations; what pharmacists do in shops, hospitals and factories; which official books set the standards for medicines; and how a prescription is read and handled. Expect at least one question on prescription and one on pharmacopoeias in most papers.

## 1.1 History of the profession of pharmacy in India

### Before modern pharmacy

- India has an old written tradition of medicine. **Ayurveda** texts such as the **Charaka Samhita** and **Sushruta Samhita** describe how plant, animal and mineral drugs were collected, purified and combined. **Unani** came with Persian and Arab physicians (hakims) and **Siddha** grew in South India.
- In all these systems the healer (vaidya or hakim) also prepared the medicine. Pharmacy was not yet a separate job.
- In the British period, Western (allopathic) medicines were imported and sold through a few chemist shops in port cities. Hospitals used **compounders** who learnt by assisting doctors. There was no qualification, no registration and no law on drug quality, so adulterated and spurious drugs were common.

### Milestones in pharmacy education

| Period | Milestone | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Early 20th century | Compounder training in hospitals | Informal, learnt on the job |
| 1930s | **Prof. M. L. Schroff** starts university pharmacy teaching at **Banaras Hindu University**; BHU begins a degree course in pharmacy (1937) | Schroff is remembered as the **father of pharmacy education in India** |
| 1948 | **Pharmacy Act, 1948** | Created the Pharmacy Council of India and the system of registered pharmacists |
| 1949 | **Pharmacy Council of India (PCI)** constituted | Lays down minimum standards for D.Pharm, B.Pharm, Pharm.D; approves institutions; State Pharmacy Councils keep registers |
| Later decades | Growth of D.Pharm, B.Pharm, M.Pharm and Ph.D. programmes | Shift from compounding to science-based training |
| 2008 | **Pharm.D** introduced | Six-year clinically oriented degree |
| 2014 | Credit-based PCI B.Pharm syllabus | Semester system with internal assessment |
| 2015 | **Pharmacy Practice Regulations** | Defined duties and conduct of registered pharmacists in practice |
| 2026 | B.Pharm syllabus as per **NEP 2020** (this course) | Multidisciplinary, credit-based curriculum from 2026-27 |

### Milestones in the pharmaceutical industry

- **Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works**, Calcutta, founded by **Acharya Prafulla Chandra Ray** (registered as a company in 1901) is usually called the first Indian pharmaceutical company. Other early firms followed in Baroda, Bombay and elsewhere.
- **Drugs Enquiry Committee (Chopra Committee), 1930–31**, chaired by **Col. R. N. Chopra**, reported on poor-quality drugs and recommended a central drug law, a drug-testing laboratory and an Indian pharmacopoeia.
- This led to the **Drugs Act, 1940** (now the **Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940**) and the **Drugs and Cosmetics Rules, 1945**, which control import, manufacture, sale and distribution.
- After independence, public sector units (for example Hindustan Antibiotics Ltd and Indian Drugs and Pharmaceuticals Ltd) were set up for antibiotics and bulk drugs.
- The **Patents Act, 1970** recognised only process patents for medicines, so Indian firms could make generic versions of new drugs by new routes. India became a major producer of bulk drugs and generics and is often called the **"pharmacy of the world"**.
- From 2005 product patents returned (to meet the WTO TRIPS agreement), pushing Indian firms towards research, contract manufacturing and export to regulated markets.

## 1.2 Scope of the pharmacy profession

A pharmacist is the expert on medicines: how they are made, checked, stored, supplied and used. The main fields are below.

### Community (retail) pharmacy

- Receive, check and dispense prescriptions; only a **registered pharmacist** may dispense.
- Counsel patients on how and when to take medicines, storage, side effects and interactions.
- Supply over-the-counter medicines responsibly and refer patients to a doctor when needed.
- Keep records and registers required by law; store medicines properly (cold chain for vaccines and insulin).
- Health promotion: screening (blood pressure, blood sugar), advice on smoking, diet and hygiene.

### Hospital pharmacy

- Purchase, store and distribute medicines to wards and outpatients; inventory control.
- Prepare sterile and non-sterile products in the hospital (for example dilutions, IV admixtures).
- Serve on the **Pharmacy and Therapeutics Committee**, which prepares the hospital formulary.
- Drug information service for doctors and nurses.

### Clinical pharmacy

- Work with doctors on ward rounds; review prescriptions for dose, interactions and duplication.
- Monitor drug therapy and report adverse drug reactions (pharmacovigilance).
- Patient counselling at discharge, therapeutic drug monitoring, medication history.

### Industrial pharmacy, including research and development

| Department | Pharmacist's role |
| --- | --- |
| Research and development (R&D) | Pre-formulation, formulation development, stability studies, scale-up |
| Production | Manufacture of tablets, capsules, liquids, injections under Good Manufacturing Practice |
| Quality control (QC) | Testing raw materials, in-process and finished products against IP or other standards |
| Quality assurance (QA) | Documentation, validation, audits, GMP compliance |
| Regulatory affairs | Preparing dossiers and licences for approval in India and abroad |
| Marketing and sales | Product management, medical representatives |

### Other fields

Government service (Drug Inspector, Government Analyst), teaching and research, clinical research and pharmacovigilance, entrepreneurship (own pharmacy or unit), cosmetics and nutraceuticals.

## 1.3 Pharmacopoeias

**Pharmacopoeia** (Greek *pharmakon* = drug, *poiein* = to make): an official book, published under the authority of a government or recognised body, giving **standards of identity, purity and strength** of drugs and their preparations, together with test methods. A **monograph** is the entry for one substance or one preparation.

### Major pharmacopoeias

| Book | Publisher and facts | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)** | First edition **1955**; later editions 1966, 1985, 1996, 2007, 2010, 2014, 2018 and 2022. Published by the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad**, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare | Official book of standards in India under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act |
| **British Pharmacopoeia (BP)** | First published **1864**; now by the British Pharmacopoeia Commission, revised every year | Official in the UK; includes European Pharmacopoeia texts |
| **United States Pharmacopeia (USP)** | First published **1820**; by the United States Pharmacopeial Convention; issued together with the National Formulary as **USP–NF** | Official in the USA; NF covers excipients |
| **British Pharmaceutical Codex (BPC)** | First published **1907** by the Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain | Gave formulae of preparations and information on drugs, dressings; later continued as the Pharmaceutical Codex |
| **International Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Int.)** | Published by the **World Health Organization** | Model standards, especially for essential medicines; a country may adopt it |
| Other pharmacopoeias | European Pharmacopoeia (Ph. Eur.), Japanese Pharmacopoeia (JP), Chinese Pharmacopoeia, Martindale (a reference book, not a pharmacopoeia) | Used for export and reference |

### National Formulary of India (NFI)

- A **formulary** is a book of formulae and information on medicines for prescribers and pharmacists.
- The NFI (first edition 1960) is now published by the **Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission**.
- It gives drug information in therapeutic groups: indications, contraindications, cautions, side effects, interactions and preparations available, helping **rational use of medicines**. Unlike the IP, it is a guide to use, not a book of legal quality standards.

### Structure and content of the Indian Pharmacopoeia

Recent editions come in several volumes. The broad layout is:

```text
Indian Pharmacopoeia
 ├── Front matter: preface, IPC members, introduction, list of changes (additions, omissions)
 ├── General Notices: rules for reading the book (meaning of "about", temperatures, storage terms,
 │                    solubility terms, "per cent" w/w, w/v, v/v, official titles)
 ├── General Chapters (Appendices): test methods (pH, melting point, UV, IR, HPLC, dissolution,
 │                    sterility, microbial limits), reagents, volumetric solutions, buffers
 ├── General Monographs: dosage forms (tablets, capsules, injections, oral liquids) – common tests
 └── Monographs: drug substances, excipients, dosage forms, vaccines and immunosera,
                 blood products, herbal products, biotechnology products, veterinary products
```

### Study of one model IP monograph (layout)

Every monograph follows a fixed order. Use paracetamol as the illustration of the layout.

| Heading | What it gives (paracetamol as illustration) |
| --- | --- |
| Title | Official name, for example **Paracetamol** (synonym: acetaminophen) |
| Formula and molecular weight | C8H9NO2, about 151.2 |
| Definition and content limit | Chemical name and the permitted range of pure drug, stated as a percentage calculated on the dried basis |
| Category | Analgesic and antipyretic |
| Dose | Usual dose range (pharmacist's reference only) |
| Description | White crystalline powder; odourless |
| Solubility | Uses standard terms (for example "sparingly soluble in water, freely soluble in ethanol") |
| Identification | Tests that prove identity: infrared spectrum compared with a reference, melting point, colour reactions |
| Tests | Purity tests: limit of related impurities (such as 4-aminophenol), heavy metals, sulphated ash, loss on drying |
| Assay | The quantitative method to find the content |
| Storage | For example, store protected from light in a well-closed container |
| Labelling | Any special statement required on the label |

**Pharmacopoeial solubility terms** (parts of solvent for 1 part of solute):

| Term | Parts of solvent |
| --- | --- |
| Very soluble | less than 1 |
| Freely soluble | 1 to 10 |
| Soluble | 10 to 30 |
| Sparingly soluble | 30 to 100 |
| Slightly soluble | 100 to 1000 |
| Very slightly soluble | 1000 to 10 000 |
| Practically insoluble | more than 10 000 |

## 1.4 Introduction to prescription

### Definition

A **prescription** is a written (or electronic) order from a registered medical practitioner (or dentist or veterinarian) to a pharmacist to prepare and/or dispense a specified medicine for a named patient, with directions for use. It is a legal document.

### Parts of a prescription

```text
 ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
 │ Dr. A. B. Name, MBBS, MD          Reg. No. 12345     │  1 Prescriber details
 │ Clinic address, phone                                │
 │                                   Date: 05-07-2026   │  2 Date
 │ Patient: R. Kumar   Age: 34 y   Sex: M   Wt: 62 kg   │  3 Patient details
 │ Address: ...                                         │
 │ Rx                                                   │  4 Superscription
 │   Tab. ......... 500 mg            No. 10            │  5 Inscription
 │   Make 10 such tablets / Mitte 10                    │  6 Subscription
 │   Sig. 1 tab t.d.s. p.c. for 3 days                  │  7 Signatura
 │   Refill: nil                                        │  8 Renewal
 │                               Signature of prescriber │  9 Signature
 └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Part | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Date | Shows when the prescription was written; helps check validity and refills |
| Name, age, sex and address of patient | Identifies the patient; age and weight are essential for children's doses |
| **Superscription** | The symbol **Rx**, from Latin *recipe* = "take thou" |
| **Inscription** | The main part: names and quantities of the ingredients (base, adjuvant, vehicle in a compounded prescription) |
| **Subscription** | Directions to the pharmacist: dosage form to make and quantity to supply |
| **Signatura** (Sig.) | Directions to the patient, to be copied on the label: dose, frequency, route, timing |
| Renewal instructions | Whether and how many times it may be refilled |
| Signature, address and registration number of prescriber | Makes the order legal and genuine |

### Types of prescriptions

- **Precompounded (ready-made)**: proprietary products such as tablets and syrups, dispensed as supplied.
- **Extemporaneous**: the pharmacist must compound the preparation (for example a mixture, ointment or powders).

### Handling of a prescription

1. **Receiving**: receive it personally and courteously; do not discuss the diagnosis loudly.
2. **Reading and checking**: check that it is legal and complete (prescriber's signature, registration number, date, patient details); check legibility, doses, frequency, duration, incompatibilities and interactions. Any doubt is cleared with the **prescriber**, not with the patient.
3. **Collecting materials**: take out the correct drugs and containers; check each label three times (when taking out, when using, when putting back).
4. **Compounding or counting**: weigh and measure accurately; prepare one prescription at a time on a clean bench.
5. **Packing**: choose a suitable container (amber bottle for light-sensitive liquids, wide-mouth jar for ointments, fluted bottle for external use where practised).
6. **Labelling**: patient name, name and strength of medicine, directions, date, pharmacy name and address, and auxiliary labels ("For external use only", "Shake well before use").
7. **Recording**: enter in the prescription register; stamp and return or retain the prescription as the law requires.
8. **Delivering and counselling**: hand over to the patient, explain dose, timing, storage, likely side effects and what to avoid.

### Common errors in prescriptions

- Illegible handwriting; look-alike or sound-alike names.
- Decimal errors: ".5 mg" read as "5 mg", "5.0 mg" read as "50 mg". Write "0.5 mg" and never put a trailing zero.
- Wrong or missing dose, frequency, route or duration; missing age or weight for children.
- Ambiguous abbreviations; incompatibility or interaction between prescribed drugs.
- Missing date, signature or registration number.

## Remember

- Chopra Committee (1930–31) → Drugs Act 1940; Pharmacy Act 1948 → PCI 1949; first IP 1955.
- Prof. M. L. Schroff, BHU: father of pharmacy education in India; P. C. Ray: Bengal Chemical and Pharmaceutical Works.
- IP is published by the IPC, Ghaziabad; BP from 1864; USP from 1820; BPC from 1907; Ph. Int. by WHO; NFI is a guide to rational use, not a legal standard.
- Monograph order: title, formula, definition, category, description, solubility, identification, tests, assay, storage, labelling.
- Prescription parts: superscription (Rx), inscription (drugs), subscription (to pharmacist), signatura (to patient).
- Clear every doubt with the prescriber; check labels three times; write 0.5 mg, never .5 mg.
