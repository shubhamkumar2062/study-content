# Unit 1: Introduction to pharmaceutical analysis, errors, impurities and limit tests

Every medicine has to be checked before it reaches a patient: is it the right substance, does it contain the right amount, and is it free from harmful impurities? Pharmaceutical analysis answers these three questions. This unit lays the foundation for the whole subject: the kinds of analysis, how the strength of a solution is written, standard solutions, the errors that creep into every measurement, and how the Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP) keeps impurities under control with limit tests.

## 1.1 Pharmaceutical analysis and its techniques

**Pharmaceutical analysis** is the branch of chemistry that identifies a drug (qualitative analysis), measures how much of it is present (quantitative analysis) and checks its purity, in raw materials, intermediates and finished dosage forms.

| Type | Question it answers | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Qualitative | What is present? | Identification tests of the IP (flame test for sodium, silver nitrate test for chloride) |
| Quantitative | How much is present? | Assay of sodium chloride by titration with silver nitrate |
| Limit tests / purity tests | Is an impurity below the permitted level? | Limit test for iron, arsenic, heavy metals |

**Different techniques of analysis**

| Group | Techniques | Measured quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chemical (classical) | Titrimetric (volumetric) analysis; gravimetric analysis | Volume of a standard solution; mass of a pure precipitate |
| Instrumental: optical | UV–visible spectrophotometry, colorimetry, IR, fluorimetry, flame photometry, atomic absorption | Absorbed or emitted light |
| Instrumental: electrochemical | Potentiometry, conductometry, polarography | Potential, conductance, current |
| Instrumental: separation | Paper, thin-layer, column, gas and high-performance liquid chromatography | Separation and response of each component |
| Physical tests | Melting point, refractive index, optical rotation, specific gravity, viscosity | A physical constant |
| Biological | Microbiological assays, bioassays | Response of an organism or tissue |

**Titrimetric (volumetric) analysis**: a solution of exactly known strength (the **titrant**) is added from a burette to the substance until the reaction is just complete (the **equivalence point**). The point where the indicator changes colour is the **end point**; a good method keeps the end point as close as possible to the equivalence point. The amount of substance is calculated from the volume used.

Types of titration by reaction: acid–base (neutralisation), non-aqueous, precipitation, complexometric and redox. By technique: **direct** (titrant added straight to the sample), **back** (excess titrant added, the excess titrated with a second standard), and **replacement** (the sample releases an equivalent amount of another species that is titrated).

## 1.2 Methods of expressing the strength of solutions

| Expression | Definition | Formula |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Molarity (M) | Moles of solute in 1 litre of solution | M = (mass in g ÷ molar mass) ÷ volume in litres |
| Normality (N) | Gram equivalents of solute in 1 litre of solution | N = M × n (n = H⁺, OH⁻ or electrons per formula unit in that reaction) |
| Molality (m) | Moles of solute in 1 kg of solvent | Does not change with temperature |
| Mole fraction | Moles of one component ÷ total moles | No units |
| Percentage | % w/w (g per 100 g), % w/v (g per 100 ml), % v/v (ml per 100 ml) | Used for pharmacopoeial solutions |
| ppm | Parts per million; 1 ppm = 1 µg per g (or about 1 µg per ml for dilute water solutions) | Used for traces and limit tests |
| Titre / equivalence | Grams of a substance equivalent to 1 ml of the titrant | Written in IP assays as "1 ml of 0.1 M … is equivalent to … g" |

**Equivalent weight** = molar mass ÷ n. For acids n is the number of replaceable H⁺ used; for bases the number of OH⁻ (or H⁺ accepted); for oxidising and reducing agents the number of electrons gained or lost per formula unit.

| Substance | Reaction | n | Normality of a 0.1 M solution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HCl | Neutralisation | 1 | 0.1 N |
| H2SO4 | Neutralisation (both H⁺) | 2 | 0.2 N |
| Na2CO3 | Titrated to CO2 with HCl | 2 | 0.2 N |
| KMnO4 | MnO4⁻ → Mn²⁺ in acid (5 electrons) | 5 | 0.5 N |
| Na2S2O3 | 2S2O3²⁻ → S4O6²⁻ (1 electron per ion) | 1 | 0.1 N |

The IP now writes all volumetric solutions in **molarity**, so learn to convert both ways.

**Worked examples**

1. 4.9 g of H2SO4 (molar mass 98.07) in 500 ml: moles = 4.9/98.07 = 0.0500; M = 0.0500/0.500 = **0.100 M**; N = 0.100 × 2 = **0.200 N**.
2. 2.0 g NaOH (40.00) in 250 ml: M = (2.0/40.00)/0.250 = **0.200 M** (= 0.200 N).
3. To make 1 litre of 0.1 N Na2CO3 (equivalent weight 105.99/2 = 53.00): weigh **5.300 g** (this is 0.05 M).

**Basic relation used in every titration**

At the equivalence point: M1 × V1 × (ratio) = M2 × V2, or in normality N1V1 = N2V2. In IP assays the factor **f** of the titrant (actual strength ÷ nominal strength) multiplies the burette reading:

```text
% of substance = (V × f × equivalence factor × 100) ÷ weight of sample
```

## 1.3 Primary and secondary standards

A **standard solution** is a solution of accurately known concentration. It is made either by dissolving an exactly weighed amount of a primary standard, or by finding the strength of a roughly made solution by titration against a primary standard (**standardisation**).

**Primary standard**: a substance pure enough to be weighed directly to make a solution of exactly known strength.

Properties of an ideal primary standard:

1. High purity (about 99.9% or better), easy to purify and dry.
2. Stable in air: not hygroscopic, not efflorescent, not attacked by oxygen or carbon dioxide.
3. Unchanged by drying at a suitable temperature.
4. High equivalent weight, so weighing errors are small.
5. Freely soluble in the solvent used.
6. Reacts quickly, completely and in a known (stoichiometric) ratio.
7. Easily available and inexpensive.

| Primary standard | Used to standardise |
| --- | --- |
| Potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP) | NaOH (aqueous); perchloric acid (non-aqueous) |
| Anhydrous sodium carbonate | HCl, H2SO4 |
| Benzoic acid | Sodium/lithium methoxide (non-aqueous) |
| Sodium chloride | Silver nitrate |
| Potassium dichromate, potassium bromate, potassium iodate | Sodium thiosulphate |
| Arsenic trioxide, sodium oxalate | Ceric ammonium sulphate, potassium permanganate |
| Zinc (granulated), calcium carbonate | Disodium EDTA |

**Secondary standard**: a substance whose solution cannot be made to an exact strength by weighing, so it is standardised against a primary standard. Examples: NaOH (absorbs moisture and CO2), HCl (volatile, concentration of the concentrated acid not exact), KMnO4 (contains MnO2 and decomposes), sodium thiosulphate, iodine, silver nitrate (light-sensitive), EDTA, perchloric acid.

## 1.4 Errors in analysis

An **error** is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error; the analyst's job is to know where it comes from and keep it small.

**Sources of errors**

- Instrumental: uncalibrated balance, burette, pipette or flask; faulty weights.
- Method: incomplete reaction, side reactions, solubility of a precipitate, end point not matching equivalence point, co-precipitation.
- Reagents: impure chemicals, impure water.
- Personal: wrong reading of the meniscus (parallax), colour blindness for an end point, careless transfer, calculation slips.
- Environment: temperature (glassware volume and solution volume change), humidity, light (silver salts, iodine), dust.

**Types of errors**

| Type | Nature | Examples | Effect | How detected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Determinate (systematic)** | Have a definite cause; occur in the same direction; can be found and corrected | Instrumental, method, reagent and personal errors | Lower **accuracy** | Blank, standard reference material, different method |
| **Indeterminate (random)** | No single cause; small, scatter on both sides of the mean | Small fluctuations of reading, temperature, balance drift | Lower **precision** | Repeating measurements and statistics |
| Gross error | Large mistake | Spilling, wrong pipette, wrong weight recorded | Result discarded | Obvious outlier |

Determinate errors may be **constant** (same size whatever the sample size, e.g. indicator blank) or **proportional** (grow with sample size, e.g. an interfering impurity in the sample).

**Methods of minimising errors**

1. **Calibration** of balance, weights and volumetric glassware.
2. **Blank determination**: run the procedure without the sample and subtract the blank titre (removes reagent and indicator error).
3. **Control determination**: analyse a standard substance of known purity under the same conditions.
4. **Independent method**: check the result by a different method.
5. **Parallel determinations**: do the analysis in duplicate or triplicate and accept concordant results.
6. **Standard addition**: add a known amount of analyte and see if it is fully recovered.
7. Use good technique: read the lower meniscus at eye level, wash precipitates properly, protect light-sensitive solutions, keep temperature constant.

## 1.5 Accuracy, precision and significant figures

**Accuracy**: closeness of a result to the true value. Expressed as absolute error (measured − true) or relative error (error ÷ true × 100).

**Precision**: closeness of repeated results to one another. Expressed as standard deviation (SD) or relative standard deviation (RSD, also called coefficient of variation = SD ÷ mean × 100).

```text
      Accurate and precise     Precise, not accurate     Accurate (mean), not precise
            . . .                       . . .                  .        .
           ( ◎ )                       (   ◎ )  . .            (  ◎  )
            . .                                                  .    .
   all hits near the centre     close together but off      scattered around centre
```

Good precision does not prove good accuracy: a systematic error can give close but wrong results.

**Worked example.** Five titres for a sample: 10.12, 10.15, 10.10, 10.14, 10.13 ml; true value 10.00 ml.

- Mean = 50.64/5 = **10.128 ml**
- SD (n − 1) = **0.019 ml**; RSD = 0.019/10.128 × 100 = **0.19%** (very precise)
- Absolute error = 10.128 − 10.00 = **+0.128 ml**; relative error = **+1.28%** (a systematic error is likely)

**Significant figures** are all the certain digits of a number plus the first uncertain one.

Rules:

1. All non-zero digits count (4.25 has 3).
2. Zeros between non-zero digits count (20.05 has 4).
3. Leading zeros do not count (0.0042 has 2).
4. Trailing zeros after a decimal point count (25.00 ml has 4: it says the burette was read to 0.01 ml).
5. **Addition/subtraction**: the answer keeps as many decimal places as the number with the fewest decimal places (12.11 + 0.3 + 1.234 = 13.644 → **13.6**).
6. **Multiplication/division**: the answer keeps as many significant figures as the number with the fewest (2.50 × 3.1416 = 7.854 → **7.85**).
7. Round off only at the end; if the digit to be dropped is 5 or more, round up.

## 1.6 Impurities in pharmaceuticals

An **impurity** is any component of a drug substance or product that is not the intended chemical entity (or an excipient). Impurities may be harmless, but some are toxic (arsenic, lead), some reduce the activity or shelf life (moisture, oxidising agents), and some change the appearance, taste or smell.

**Types and contents of impurities** (the modern classification used in regulatory guidelines):

| Type | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Organic impurities | Starting materials, intermediates, by-products, degradation products |
| Inorganic impurities | Reagents, catalysts, heavy metals, inorganic salts (chloride, sulphate), filter aids |
| Residual solvents | Solvents left from manufacture |
| Toxic impurities | Arsenic, lead, mercury and other elemental impurities |
| Physical impurities | Dust, fibres, glass particles |

**Regulatory importance**

- Impurities can harm the patient directly (toxic metals), cause allergy, or lower the dose of active drug.
- They can catalyse decomposition, change colour, odour or taste, and shorten the shelf life.
- Pharmacopoeias (IP, BP, USP) fix limits for each impurity in a monograph and give the method of testing; a batch that fails cannot be sold.
- International guidelines (ICH) set thresholds for reporting, identifying and qualifying impurities and limits for residual solvents and elemental impurities.
- Limit tests and impurity profiles are part of quality control and of the documents submitted for approval of a drug.

## 1.7 Sources of impurities

```text
  Raw materials ─┐
  Method of      │
  manufacture ───┤
  Reagents and   ├──►  IMPURITY IN THE FINAL PRODUCT
  solvents ──────┤
  Plant/vessels ─┤
  Storage and ───┤
  decomposition  │
  Adulteration ──┘
```

1. **Raw materials**: natural minerals carry other elements. Rock salt contains calcium and magnesium salts; sulphuric acid made from pyrites may carry arsenic.
2. **Method of manufacture**:
   - Reagents left behind (sodium carbonate in sodium bicarbonate made by the Solvay process may contain ammonia and chloride).
   - Intermediate products or incomplete reaction.
   - Side reactions and by-products.
   - Solvents (water itself brings calcium, magnesium, chloride, sulphate; organic solvents may remain).
   - Catalysts (nickel, platinum).
3. **Reaction vessels and plant**: iron, copper, lead or zinc dissolve from metal vessels and pipes; glass gives alkali; filter aids give fibres.
4. **Atmospheric contamination**: dust, sulphur dioxide, carbon dioxide (NaOH absorbs CO2 and forms sodium carbonate), moisture.
5. **Storage**: chemical decomposition (light, heat, air), reaction with the container, contamination by insects or microbes, loss of water of crystallisation (efflorescence) or absorption of moisture (deliquescence), physical changes in crystal form.
6. **Manufacturing hazards**: cross-contamination from other products in the same plant, particles from machines, mistakes in labelling.
7. **Deliberate adulteration**: cheaper material added for profit.

## 1.8 Limit tests: the general idea

A **limit test** is a semi-quantitative test that shows whether a known impurity is present in an amount **above or below a fixed limit**. The sample (test) and a **standard** with exactly the permitted amount of impurity are treated identically, side by side, and the turbidity, opalescence, colour or stain is compared.

General rules:

- Use a matched pair of **Nessler cylinders** (same glass, size and diameter).
- Add the same reagents in the same order and volume to test and standard.
- Compare after the same time, viewing **down the length of the cylinder** against a black background (turbidity) or a white background (colour).
- If the test is less turbid or less coloured than the standard, the sample **passes**.

Why a limit test instead of a full assay? The impurity is present in traces; an exact value is not needed, only proof that it is below a safe limit; the test is quick and cheap.

## 1.9 Limit test for chloride

**Principle.** Chloride reacts with silver nitrate in the presence of dilute nitric acid to give a white opalescence of silver chloride.

Cl⁻ + AgNO3 → AgCl (white opalescence) + NO3⁻

**Role of dilute nitric acid**: dissolves carbonates, phosphates and other anions (which would also precipitate silver) and makes the opalescence fine and even.

**Procedure (outline).** Dissolve the stated amount of sample in water in a Nessler cylinder, add dilute nitric acid, make up to volume, add silver nitrate solution, stir and stand for about 5 minutes protected from light. Prepare a standard with a known volume of standard sodium chloride solution in the same way. Compare the opalescence against a black background. The older IP standard used 1 ml of 0.05845% w/v sodium chloride solution; follow the current IP for the exact quantity.

## 1.10 Limit test for sulphate

**Principle.** Sulphate reacts with barium chloride in the presence of dilute hydrochloric acid to give a white turbidity of barium sulphate.

SO4²⁻ + BaCl2 → BaSO4 (white turbidity) + 2Cl⁻

**Reagents.** Barium sulphate reagent (barium chloride with a little potassium sulphate in alcohol). The small amount of potassium sulphate **seeds** the precipitation, so the turbidity forms quickly and evenly; alcohol prevents supersaturation and keeps particles fine. Hydrochloric acid prevents the precipitation of other barium salts (carbonate, phosphate).

**Standard.** A known volume of standard potassium sulphate solution (the older IP used 0.1089% w/v K2SO4), treated the same way. Compare turbidity after about 5 minutes.

## 1.11 Modified limit tests for chloride and sulphate

Some substances cannot be tested by the general method because they interfere with it. The test is **modified** to remove the interference, and the standard receives the same extra reagents.

| Problem | Example | Modification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substance is a carbonate or bicarbonate (effervesces with acid; CO2 disturbs the test) | Sodium bicarbonate, sodium carbonate | Extra acid added; solution allowed to stand or boiled until effervescence stops |
| Substance is strongly coloured | Potassium permanganate | Colour destroyed first (reduced with alcohol and warmed); brown MnO2 filtered; clear filtrate tested |
| Substance is insoluble in water | Some insoluble salts and oxides | An acid solution or a water extract is prepared and filtered, then tested |
| Substance itself reacts with the reagent | Salts precipitated by silver or barium | Interfering ion removed or converted first |

Examiners usually want the **reason** for modification and one example with the exact change made.

## 1.12 Limit test for iron

**Principle.** Iron, as Fe³⁺ or Fe²⁺, reacts with **thioglycollic acid** in a solution made alkaline with ammonia in the presence of **citric acid**, giving a pale pink to deep reddish-purple colour (ferrous thioglycollate complex).

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Citric acid (20% w/v) | Forms a soluble complex with iron and stops it precipitating as hydroxide when ammonia is added |
| Thioglycollic acid | Reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe²⁺ and forms the coloured complex, so all iron is detected whatever its state |
| Iron-free ammonia | Makes the solution alkaline; the colour develops only in alkaline medium |

The standard uses standard iron solution (from ferric ammonium sulphate). The colours are compared after about 5 minutes, viewing down against a white background.

## 1.13 Limit test for arsenic (Gutzeit test)

Arsenic is highly toxic and accumulates in the body, so its limit is very low (a few ppm).

**Principle.** Arsenic in the sample is converted to **arsine gas (AsH3)** by nascent hydrogen produced from arsenic-free zinc and hydrochloric acid. Arsine passes up the tube and gives a **yellow stain** (turning brownish) on **mercuric chloride paper**. The length and intensity of the stain are compared with that from a standard arsenic solution.

```text
  As(V) ──KI, SnCl2──► As(III) ──Zn + HCl (nascent H)──► AsH3 ↑ ──HgCl2 paper──► yellow stain

        GUTZEIT APPARATUS
        ┌──────────────┐  ← HgCl2 paper held between two flat-ended glass tubes (clamped)
        │      ▲       │
        │ lead acetate │  ← cotton wool soaked in lead acetate (traps H2S)
        │  cotton wool │
        └──────┬───────┘
               │ narrow tube
        ┌──────┴───────┐
        │  wide-mouth  │  ← sample + stannated HCl + KI + SnCl2 + zinc
        │    bottle    │
        └──────────────┘
```

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Arsenic-free zinc | With HCl gives nascent hydrogen |
| Stannated hydrochloric acid | Acid medium; stannous chloride gives even evolution of gas |
| Potassium iodide | Reduces pentavalent arsenic (arsenate) to trivalent (arsenite), which forms arsine faster |
| Stannous chloride | Also reduces As(V) to As(III) and helps steady gas evolution |
| Lead acetate cotton wool | Absorbs hydrogen sulphide (from sulphide impurity) which would also stain the paper |
| Mercuric chloride paper | Gives the yellow-brown stain with arsine |

Precautions: all reagents arsenic-free; reaction at about 40 °C for a steady stream; test and standard run together; read the stain at once because it fades in light.

## 1.14 Limit test for lead

**Principle.** Lead forms a **red complex (lead dithizonate)** with **dithizone** (diphenylthiocarbazone) in alkaline solution. Dithizone itself is green in chloroform; the red lead complex is extracted into chloroform and its colour compared with a standard lead solution.

| Reagent | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Ammonium citrate | Keeps metals in solution in the alkaline medium |
| Potassium cyanide | Forms stable complexes with other metals (copper, zinc, nickel), which then do not react with dithizone |
| Hydroxylamine hydrochloride | Reduces Fe³⁺, which would otherwise oxidise dithizone |
| Dithizone in chloroform | Forms the red lead complex that moves into the chloroform layer |

Potassium cyanide is very poisonous: it is handled with care and never pipetted by mouth.

## 1.15 Limit test for heavy metals

"Heavy metals" means metals that form coloured sulphides under the test conditions (lead, copper, mercury, bismuth, antimony, tin, cadmium, silver). The result is expressed **as lead, in ppm**.

**Principle.** In a solution buffered at **pH about 3.5** with acetate buffer, **thioacetamide** releases hydrogen sulphide, which converts heavy metals to dark brown or black sulphides. The colour is compared with a standard made from standard lead solution.

M²⁺ + H2S → MS (brown/black) + 2H⁺

At pH 3.5 most heavy metals precipitate as sulphides, while iron, zinc and similar metals interfere little. Thioacetamide has replaced hydrogen sulphide gas because it is safer and gives H2S slowly and evenly. The IP gives different methods (A, B and others) depending on whether the substance dissolves in water, gives a coloured solution, or must first be ignited.

## 1.16 Summary of limit tests

| Test | Key reagent | Medium | Product seen | Compared as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chloride | Silver nitrate | Dilute HNO3 | White opalescence (AgCl) | Turbidity, black background |
| Sulphate | Barium sulphate reagent | Dilute HCl | White turbidity (BaSO4) | Turbidity, black background |
| Iron | Thioglycollic acid + citric acid | Ammonia (alkaline) | Pink-purple colour | Colour, white background |
| Arsenic | Zn + HCl, KI, SnCl2 | Acid | Yellow-brown stain on HgCl2 paper | Stain |
| Lead | Dithizone in chloroform | Alkaline, citrate, cyanide | Red colour in chloroform | Colour |
| Heavy metals | Thioacetamide | Acetate buffer pH 3.5 | Brown-black sulphides | Colour, white background |

## Remember

- Analysis = identity (qualitative), amount (quantitative, assay) and purity (limit tests).
- N = M × n; H2SO4 0.1 M = 0.2 N; KMnO4 0.02 M = 0.1 N in acid.
- Primary standards: KHP, anhydrous Na2CO3, NaCl, K2Cr2O7, KIO3, As2O3, zinc. Secondary: NaOH, HCl, KMnO4, thiosulphate, iodine, AgNO3, EDTA.
- Determinate errors affect accuracy and can be corrected (blank, calibration, control); random errors affect precision and are handled by repeats and statistics.
- Accuracy = closeness to true value; precision = closeness of repeats.
- Sources of impurities: raw materials, manufacturing process, plant, atmosphere, storage, cross-contamination, adulteration.
- Limit test pairs: Cl⁻/AgNO3/HNO3; SO4²⁻/BaCl2/HCl; Fe/thioglycollic acid; As/Gutzeit/HgCl2 paper; Pb/dithizone; heavy metals/thioacetamide pH 3.5.
- Modified limit tests remove an interference (effervescence, colour, insolubility) and treat the standard the same way.
