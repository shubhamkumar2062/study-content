# Unit 4: Gastrointestinal agents, topical antimicrobials and radiopharmaceuticals

This unit is about inorganic compounds that are medicines in their own right. For each compound learn a fixed set of points: **formula, preparation (with equation), properties, assay (principle and equation), uses and storage**. The syllabus marks some compounds with an asterisk (sodium bicarbonate, aluminium hydroxide gel, hydrogen peroxide, chlorinated lime); prepare their preparation and assay in full detail, as these are the ones usually expected at length. The second half of the unit introduces radioactivity and the safe use of radiopharmaceuticals.

## 4.2 Acidifiers

**Acidifiers** are acidic substances used to supply acid where the body lacks it (gastric acidifiers) or to make the urine or body fluids more acid (systemic and urinary acidifiers).

- **Gastric acidifiers** treat **achlorhydria** (absence of hydrochloric acid in gastric juice, as in pernicious anaemia, gastric cancer, some old patients). Without acid, digestion of protein, absorption of iron and calcium, and the killing of swallowed microbes suffer.
- **Systemic/urinary acidifiers** treat metabolic alkalosis or acidify the urine (for example to help some urinary antiseptics act, or to prevent certain phosphate stones). Ammonium chloride is the main systemic acidifier (Unit 5).

### Dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36.46)

- **Preparation**: made by diluting concentrated hydrochloric acid with purified water to the strength given in the pharmacopoeia (about 10% w/v HCl). Concentrated HCl is made by dissolving hydrogen chloride gas (from NaCl + H2SO4, or by burning hydrogen in chlorine) in water.
  NaCl + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HCl
- **Properties**: clear, colourless liquid, strongly acidic, no fumes at this strength.
- **Assay**: acid–base titration with 1 M NaOH using methyl red (or phenolphthalein); HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O → **1 ml of 1 M NaOH ≡ 0.03646 g HCl** (derived, 1 : 1).
- **Uses**: gastric acidifier in achlorhydria; given well diluted with water and sipped through a straw to protect the teeth. Also a pharmaceutical aid (adjusting pH).
- **Storage**: well-closed glass containers.

### Sodium acid phosphate (sodium dihydrogen phosphate, NaH2PO4·2H2O, 156.01)

- **Preparation**: phosphoric acid is partly neutralised with sodium carbonate or sodium hydroxide so that only one hydrogen is replaced; the solution is concentrated and crystallised.
  H3PO4 + NaOH → NaH2PO4 + H2O; 2H3PO4 + Na2CO3 → 2NaH2PO4 + H2O + CO2
- **Properties**: colourless crystals or white powder, freely soluble; its solution is acidic (H2PO4⁻ is a weak acid).
- **Assay (principle)**: the H2PO4⁻ ion is titrated with standard NaOH to HPO4²⁻ (a second-stage end point; a suitable indicator or potentiometric end point is used). From NaH2PO4 + NaOH → Na2HPO4 + H2O, **1 ml of 0.1 M NaOH ≡ 0.01560 g NaH2PO4·2H2O** (derived; follow the IP monograph for the exact conditions).
- **Uses**: urinary acidifier; with sodium phosphate (Na2HPO4) as a saline laxative and in phosphate enemas; in phosphate buffers; source of phosphate.

## 4.3 Antacids

**Antacids** are weak bases that react with gastric hydrochloric acid and raise the pH of the stomach contents, relieving hyperacidity, heartburn, dyspepsia and the pain of peptic ulcer. Raising gastric pH above about 4 also reduces the activity of pepsin.

**Classification**

| Type | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Systemic** (absorbed) | Sodium bicarbonate | Fast; can cause systemic alkalosis and sodium load |
| **Non-systemic** (not absorbed to a significant extent) | Aluminium hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, magnesium trisilicate, calcium carbonate, magnesium carbonate | Preferred for regular use |

By cation: aluminium-containing, magnesium-containing, calcium-containing, sodium-containing, and combinations.

**Ideal properties of an antacid**

1. Neutralises a large amount of acid per gram (high acid-neutralising capacity), quickly and for a long time.
2. Not absorbed, so it does not cause systemic alkalosis.
3. Does not raise gastric pH above about 4–5 (avoids **acid rebound** and preserves digestion).
4. Does not release CO2 (no belching, distension or risk to an ulcer).
5. Neither constipating nor laxative.
6. Does not interfere with absorption of food or other drugs.
7. Pleasant taste, cheap, stable, buffers in the range of about pH 3–5.

No single compound meets all these, so **combinations** are used.

**Combinations of antacids — why?**

| Reason | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Balance the bowel effect: aluminium salts constipate, magnesium salts are laxative | Aluminium hydroxide + magnesium hydroxide (or magnesium trisilicate) |
| Fast + slow action: one acts quickly, the other gives a longer effect | Magnesium hydroxide (fast) + aluminium hydroxide (slow) |
| Lower dose of each, so fewer side effects | |
| Add an antifoaming agent | **Simethicone (activated dimethicone)** breaks gas bubbles, relieving flatulence |
| Add a local anaesthetic or alginate in some products | Oxethazaine; sodium alginate forms a raft over the stomach contents |

**Side effects to remember**: sodium bicarbonate — alkalosis, sodium load (oedema, hypertension), belching, rebound; calcium carbonate — constipation, rebound, hypercalcaemia with milk ("milk-alkali syndrome"); aluminium — constipation, phosphate depletion; magnesium — diarrhoea, magnesium retention in kidney failure. Antacids also reduce the absorption of drugs such as tetracyclines, iron salts and fluoroquinolones; doses are separated in time.

**Acid-neutralising capacity (ANC)** is measured by adding a known excess of standard HCl to the antacid, keeping it at body temperature for a set time, and back-titrating the unused acid with standard NaOH. It is expressed as mEq of acid neutralised per dose (or per gram).

### Sodium bicarbonate* (NaHCO3, 84.01)

- **Preparation (Solvay / ammonia–soda process)**: carbon dioxide is passed into a saturated solution of sodium chloride saturated with ammonia; the less soluble sodium bicarbonate separates.
  NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O → NaHCO3↓ + NH4Cl
  Or: CO2 passed into a solution of sodium carbonate: Na2CO3 + CO2 + H2O → 2NaHCO3.
- **Properties**: white crystalline powder, saline taste, soluble in water, solution weakly alkaline; on heating loses CO2 and water: 2NaHCO3 → Na2CO3 + H2O + CO2. Common impurities: carbonate, ammonia, chloride (hence the modified limit test, Unit 1).
- **Assay**: acidimetry — dissolve about 1.5 g, accurately weighed, in water; titrate with **1 M HCl** using **methyl orange**.
  NaHCO3 + HCl → NaCl + H2O + CO2 → **1 ml of 1 M HCl ≡ 0.08401 g NaHCO3** (84.01 × 1 ÷ 1000).
  Example: 1.5040 g needed 17.85 ml (f 1.002): % = 17.85 × 1.002 × 0.08401 × 100/1.5040 = **99.9%**.
- **Uses**: systemic antacid (quick relief; not for long-term use); systemic alkaliniser in metabolic acidosis (by injection, under supervision); urinary alkaliniser; ingredient of effervescent granules and powders; with ear-drop formulations to soften wax.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers (decomposes slowly in moist air).

### Aluminium hydroxide gel* (dried gel: Al(OH)3, partly as hydrated aluminium oxide)

- **Preparation**: a hot solution of **potash alum** (or aluminium chloride) is added slowly to a hot solution of **sodium carbonate** with stirring; the gelatinous aluminium hydroxide is washed free of sulphate and suspended in water (flavoured and preserved) to give the gel, or dried at a low temperature to give dried aluminium hydroxide gel.
  2KAl(SO4)2 + 3Na2CO3 + 3H2O → 2Al(OH)3↓ + K2SO4 + 3Na2SO4 + 3CO2↑
- **Properties**: white, viscous suspension (or white amorphous powder); insoluble in water; dissolves in acids and in alkali hydroxides (amphoteric).
- **Assay (complexometric back titration)**: aluminium reacts slowly with EDTA and its complex with indicators is poor, so a **back titration** is used. The sample is dissolved in a little hydrochloric acid, a measured **excess of 0.05 M disodium EDTA** is added, the pH is adjusted with a buffer and the solution heated to complete complexation; the unreacted EDTA is titrated with **0.05 M zinc sulphate** (dithizone or another suitable indicator).
  Al³⁺ + H2Y²⁻ → AlY⁻ + 2H⁺. Results are expressed as Al2O3 (2 Al per Al2O3), so **1 ml of 0.05 M EDTA ≡ 0.002549 g Al2O3** (101.96 × 0.05 ÷ 2 ÷ 1000).
  Example: 2.000 g gel; 50.00 ml EDTA added; back titre 22.50 ml 0.05 M ZnSO4; EDTA used 27.50 ml → Al2O3 = 27.50 × 0.002549 = 0.0701 g = **3.5% w/w Al2O3**.
- **Test for purity**: acid-neutralising capacity (see above).
- **Uses**: non-systemic antacid (slow, prolonged action; astringent and demulcent coating); with magnesium salts to balance constipation; binds phosphate in the gut (used in high blood phosphate of kidney failure).
- **Side effects**: constipation; phosphate depletion with long use; reduces absorption of some drugs.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers; the gel should not be frozen.

## 4.4 Agents that promote bowel movements (saline/osmotic laxatives)

Salts whose ions (Mg²⁺, SO4²⁻, phosphate, tartrate) are **poorly absorbed** hold water in the intestine by osmosis. The bulky, watery contents stretch the bowel wall and increase peristalsis, giving a soft or watery stool within a few hours. Magnesium also releases cholecystokinin, which adds to the effect. These salts are taken with plenty of water. They are avoided in kidney failure (magnesium and phosphate retention), dehydration and intestinal obstruction.

### Magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)2, 58.32)

- **Preparation**: a soluble magnesium salt is precipitated with sodium hydroxide: MgSO4 + 2NaOH → Mg(OH)2↓ + Na2SO4. Washed and dried, or kept as an aqueous suspension (**milk of magnesia**, about 8% Mg(OH)2).
- **Properties**: white, fine, amorphous powder; practically insoluble in water; its suspension is alkaline.
- **Assay**: dissolve in dilute HCl, add ammonia buffer pH 10, titrate with 0.05 M disodium EDTA using Eriochrome Black T. **1 ml of 0.05 M EDTA ≡ 0.002916 g Mg(OH)2** (derived, 1 : 1).
- **Uses**: small doses — non-systemic antacid (does not release CO2); larger doses — osmotic laxative. Often combined with aluminium hydroxide.

### Sodium orthophosphate (disodium hydrogen phosphate, Na2HPO4·12H2O)

- **Preparation**: phosphoric acid is neutralised with sodium carbonate to the stage where two hydrogens are replaced (phenolphthalein end point); the solution is concentrated and crystallised.
  H3PO4 + Na2CO3 → Na2HPO4 + H2O + CO2
- **Properties**: colourless, efflorescent crystals; soluble; solution mildly alkaline.
- **Uses**: saline laxative; in phosphate enemas (with sodium dihydrogen phosphate) for bowel evacuation before procedures; source of phosphate; buffer component.

### Sodium potassium tartrate (Rochelle salt, KNaC4H4O6·4H2O)

- **Preparation**: potassium hydrogen tartrate (cream of tartar, from wine lees) is neutralised with sodium carbonate in hot water; the solution is filtered and crystallised.
  2KHC4H4O6 + Na2CO3 → 2KNaC4H4O6 + H2O + CO2
- **Properties**: colourless crystals or white powder, cooling saline taste, freely soluble.
- **Uses**: saline (osmotic) laxative; formerly in effervescent "Seidlitz" type powders (with sodium bicarbonate and tartaric acid); also used in Fehling's solution in the laboratory. (The syllabus also lists it under emetics in Unit 5.)

### Magnesium trisilicate (2MgO·3SiO2·xH2O)

- **Preparation**: a solution of magnesium sulphate is mixed with a solution of sodium silicate; the precipitate is washed and dried.
  2MgSO4 + Na4Si3O8 (that is, 2Na2O·3SiO2) + xH2O → 2MgO·3SiO2·xH2O + 2Na2SO4
- **Properties**: white, odourless, tasteless powder; insoluble in water.
- **Action**: a slow, prolonged antacid: 2MgO·3SiO2 + 4HCl → 2MgCl2 + 3SiO2 + 2H2O. The **colloidal silica** formed coats and protects the ulcer surface and adsorbs pepsin. The magnesium chloride formed gives a **mild laxative** effect — hence its place in this list.
- **Uses**: antacid in peptic ulcer and dyspepsia, often with aluminium hydroxide (Magnesium Trisilicate Mixture is a classic formulation).

## 4.5 Topical antimicrobials (antiseptics and disinfectants)

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Antiseptic** | Kills or inhibits microbes on living tissue (skin, wounds, mucous membranes) |
| **Disinfectant** | Kills microbes on non-living objects (floors, instruments, drains); usually too harsh for tissue |
| **Germicide / bactericide** | Kills microbes |
| **Bacteriostatic** | Stops growth without killing |
| **Sterilisation** | Removal or destruction of all living organisms, including spores |
| **Sanitiser** | Lowers microbial count to a safe public-health level |

**Mechanisms of action of inorganic antimicrobials**

1. **Oxidation**: oxidising agents release oxygen or act as strong oxidants and destroy essential –SH groups and other parts of cell proteins and enzymes (hydrogen peroxide, potassium permanganate, chlorinated lime).
2. **Halogenation**: chlorine and iodine combine with and denature proteins (iodine, hypochlorites from chlorinated lime).
3. **Protein precipitation**: metal ions (silver, mercury, copper, zinc) combine with protein –SH groups and precipitate them (also the basis of astringent action).

**Classification**

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Oxidising agents | Hydrogen peroxide, potassium permanganate, sodium perborate |
| Halogens and halogen compounds | Iodine and its preparations, chlorinated lime, sodium hypochlorite solution |
| Metal compounds (protein precipitants) | Silver nitrate, mercuric compounds (now rarely used), copper sulphate, zinc sulphate |
| Acids | Boric acid |
| Sulphur compounds | Sulphur, selenium sulphide |

**Qualities of a good antiseptic**: kills a wide range of microbes quickly at low concentration, not irritant or toxic to tissue, works in the presence of pus and body fluids, does not stain, stable, cheap.

### Potassium permanganate (KMnO4, 158.03)

- **Preparation**: manganese dioxide is fused with potassium hydroxide and an oxidant (potassium chlorate or air) to give green **potassium manganate**; this is then oxidised to permanganate (by CO2, chlorine, ozone or electrolytically).
  3MnO2 + 6KOH + KClO3 → 3K2MnO4 + KCl + 3H2O
  3K2MnO4 + 2CO2 → 2KMnO4 + MnO2 + 2K2CO3 (or 2K2MnO4 + Cl2 → 2KMnO4 + 2KCl)
- **Properties**: dark purple, almost black crystals with a metallic sheen; soluble in water giving a deep purple solution; strong oxidant; explodes with some organic materials and reducing agents.
- **Assay (iodometry)**: an aqueous solution is treated with KI and dilute H2SO4; the liberated iodine is titrated with 0.1 M thiosulphate (starch). 2MnO4⁻ + 10I⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 5I2 + 8H2O, so 1 KMnO4 ≡ 5 S2O3²⁻ → **1 ml of 0.1 M Na2S2O3 ≡ 0.003161 g KMnO4**.
- **Uses**: very dilute (pale pink) solutions as an antiseptic and astringent for weeping skin lesions, fungal infections of feet, wound cleansing; as an oxidising agent in analysis (titrant). Concentrated solutions and crystals burn tissue and stain skin brown.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers, away from organic and reducing substances.

### Boric acid (H3BO3, 61.83)

- **Preparation**: from borax (sodium tetraborate) with a mineral acid: Na2B4O7 + H2SO4 + 5H2O → 4H3BO3 + Na2SO4 (or with HCl). Boric acid crystallises on cooling.
- **Properties**: colourless, pearly scales or white powder, greasy to touch; soluble in water and more so in boiling water and in glycerin; very weak acid (pKa about 9.2).
- **Assay**: dissolve with **glycerin (or mannitol)**, which forms a stronger complex acid; titrate with standard NaOH using phenolphthalein. **1 ml of 0.1 M NaOH ≡ 0.006183 g H3BO3** (monobasic in the complex).
- **Uses**: weak bacteriostatic and fungistatic; eye washes and ear drops (as boric/borate buffers), dusting powders, ointments. It is absorbed through broken skin and mucous membranes and can be toxic, so it is not used on large raw areas or for infants.

### Hydrogen peroxide* (H2O2, 34.01)

- **Preparation**:
  - Laboratory: barium peroxide is added gradually to cold dilute sulphuric acid; insoluble BaSO4 is filtered off. BaO2 + H2SO4 → BaSO4↓ + H2O2.
  - Industrial: **auto-oxidation of an alkylanthraquinol** (anthraquinone process) — the quinol is oxidised by air to the quinone with formation of H2O2, which is extracted with water; the quinone is reduced back by hydrogen and reused. (Electrolysis of sulphuric acid/ammonium sulphate to peroxodisulphate, followed by hydrolysis, is an older method.)
- **Properties**: clear, colourless liquid, slightly acid; decomposes on standing, faster with heat, light, alkali, dust and metal ions: 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2. Acts as an oxidising agent and, towards strong oxidants like KMnO4, as a reducing agent. **Stabilisers** such as acetanilide or phosphoric acid are added.
- **Strength**: given as % w/v or as **volume strength** — the volume of oxygen (at NTP) given by 1 volume of the solution on complete decomposition. From 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2 (68 g gives 22.4 litres O2): 3% w/v ≈ **10 volume**; 6% w/v ≈ **20 volume**.
- **Assay (permanganometry)**: dilute 10 ml to 100 ml with water; to 10 ml of this add 20 ml dilute H2SO4 and titrate with **0.02 M KMnO4** to a permanent pink.
  2KMnO4 + 5H2O2 + 3H2SO4 → K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 8H2O + 5O2 → **1 ml of 0.02 M KMnO4 ≡ 0.001701 g H2O2**.
  Example: 17.80 ml used for the aliquot (= 1 ml of original): H2O2 = 17.80 × 0.001701 = 0.03028 g per ml = **3.03% w/v (about 10 volume)**.
- **Uses**: antiseptic for cleaning wounds and ulcers (tissue enzyme **catalase** releases oxygen; the foaming lifts debris mechanically), mouthwash and gargle (diluted), removal of ear wax, bleaching of hair; a sterilant in industry (vapour, concentrated forms).
- **Storage**: in a cool place, protected from light, in containers not completely filled and with a vented cap; contains a stabiliser.

### Chlorinated lime* (bleaching powder; approximate formula CaOCl2 or Ca(OCl)Cl)

- **Preparation**: chlorine gas is passed over dry **slaked lime** spread in layers at about 40 °C:
  Ca(OH)2 + Cl2 → Ca(OCl)Cl + H2O
- **Properties**: dull white powder with a strong smell of chlorine; partly soluble in water; decomposes on exposure to air and moisture, losing chlorine (CO2 of air releases HOCl). Its activity is expressed as **available chlorine** — the chlorine released when it is treated with acid; it should contain not less than 30% available chlorine.
  Ca(OCl)Cl + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + Cl2
- **Assay (iodometry)**: triturate a weighed sample with water, transfer to a flask, add KI and acetic acid; the liberated iodine is titrated with **0.1 M sodium thiosulphate** using starch.
  OCl⁻ + 2I⁻ + 2H⁺ → Cl⁻ + I2 + H2O; I2 + 2S2O3²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S4O6²⁻ (Cl2 ≡ I2 ≡ 2 S2O3²⁻) → **1 ml of 0.1 M Na2S2O3 ≡ 0.003545 g available chlorine**.
  Example: 0.5000 g needed 43.50 ml → 43.50 × 0.003545 × 100/0.5000 = **30.8% available chlorine**.
- **Uses**: disinfectant for drinking water, swimming pools, latrines, drains, excreta, hospital floors; bleaching agent; source for preparing surgical chlorinated soda solution (Dakin's solution) for wounds.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers in a cool, dry place.

### Iodine and its preparations (I2, 253.81)

- **Sources/preparation**: from the ash of seaweeds (kelp), from the mother liquors of Chile saltpetre (which contain sodium iodate) and from brines. From iodate: 2NaIO3 + 5NaHSO3 → 3NaHSO4 + 2Na2SO4 + H2O + I2. Purified by **sublimation**.
- **Properties**: greyish-black plates or granules with a metallic lustre; sublimes giving violet vapour; very slightly soluble in water but readily soluble in solutions of potassium iodide (forms soluble tri-iodide, I2 + I⁻ ⇌ I3⁻) and in alcohol.
- **Assay**: dissolve with KI in water; titrate with **0.1 M sodium thiosulphate** using starch. I2 + 2S2O3²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S4O6²⁻ → **1 ml of 0.1 M Na2S2O3 ≡ 0.01269 g I**.
- **Action**: rapid, broad-spectrum germicide (bacteria, fungi, viruses, spores at higher concentration) by iodination and oxidation of microbial proteins.
- **Preparations**:

| Preparation | Composition (outline) | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strong Iodine Solution (Lugol's solution) | Iodine 5% w/v and potassium iodide 10% w/v in water | Taken orally before thyroid surgery; also as a source of iodine |
| Weak Iodine Solution / Iodine Tincture | Iodine and KI in alcohol and water | Skin antiseptic for cuts and before injections |
| Povidone-iodine (iodophor) | Iodine complexed with polyvinylpyrrolidone; releases free iodine slowly | Surgical scrubs, skin preparation, wound care; less irritant and staining |
| Compound Iodine Paint (Mandl's paint) | Iodine with KI in glycerin (flavoured) | Painting the throat in sore throat |

Iodine stains skin and fabrics; it can cause sensitivity reactions; it should not be used with mercury compounds (irritant mercuric iodide).

## 4.6 Radiopharmaceuticals

**Radiopharmaceuticals** are medicinal preparations containing a **radioactive isotope (radionuclide)**, used to diagnose disease (imaging, function tests) or to treat it (killing diseased tissue).

### Basics of radioactivity

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers (¹²⁷I is stable; ¹³¹I is radioactive). Unstable nuclei emit radiation spontaneously until they become stable; this is **radioactivity**.

| Radiation | Nature | Charge | Penetration | Stopped by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Alpha (α)** | Helium nucleus (2p + 2n) | +2 | Very low (a few cm in air) | Paper, skin |
| **Beta (β⁻)** | Electron from the nucleus | −1 | Moderate (mm in tissue) | Few mm of aluminium or perspex |
| **Positron (β⁺)** | Positive electron | +1 | Annihilates with an electron → two 511 keV gamma rays | — |
| **Gamma (γ)** | Electromagnetic radiation | 0 | Very high | Thick lead or concrete |

**Decay law**: the number of atoms decaying per unit time is proportional to the number present: N = N0 e^(−λt). **Half-life (t½)** = time for the activity to fall to half = 0.693/λ. After 10 half-lives about 1/1000 (0.1%) of the original activity remains.

**Units of activity**: **becquerel (Bq)** = 1 disintegration per second (SI unit); **curie (Ci)** = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq (mCi and µCi in practice). Absorbed dose: gray (Gy); dose equivalent: sievert (Sv).

**Detection and measurement**: Geiger–Müller counter, scintillation counter (sodium iodide crystal; gamma cameras), ionisation chamber (dose calibrator), film badges and thermoluminescent dosimeters (personal monitoring).

**Ideal diagnostic radionuclide**: pure gamma emitter of suitable energy (about 100–200 keV) for imaging, short but practical half-life, low radiation dose to the patient, easily attached to the carrier molecule, available cheaply (for example from a generator).

### Applications of selected radioisotopes

| Isotope | Half-life | Emission | Applications |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sodium iodide I-131** | About 8 days | β and γ | Thyroid concentrates iodide: **treatment of hyperthyroidism** and **thyroid cancer** (β destroys thyroid tissue); thyroid uptake and scanning (diagnosis) |
| **Technetium-99m** | About 6 hours | γ (140 keV) only | The most widely used **diagnostic** radionuclide: bone, thyroid, brain, kidney, lung, heart and liver scans (as pertechnetate or bound to carrier molecules). Obtained from a **molybdenum-99/technetium-99m generator** ("cow") by eluting with saline |
| **Cobalt-60** | About 5.3 years | β and high-energy γ | Sealed source for **external radiotherapy (teletherapy) of cancer**; **gamma sterilisation** of disposable syringes, surgical items and some pharmaceuticals; radiography. (Radioactive cobalt-labelled vitamin B12 was historically used to study B12 absorption) |
| **Phosphorus-32** | About 14 days | Pure β | Treatment of **polycythaemia vera** and some leukaemias (concentrates in rapidly dividing bone marrow); intracavitary colloid for malignant effusions (historically); tracer studies in biochemistry |

"m" in Tc-99m means **metastable**: an excited nucleus that loses energy only as gamma rays, giving a low patient dose and good images.

### Safe handling of radiopharmaceuticals

The aim is **ALARA** — keep exposure **As Low As Reasonably Achievable**, using three rules:

```text
   TIME            DISTANCE                 SHIELDING
 less time near   dose falls with the       lead (γ), perspex/acrylic (β),
 the source  →    square of distance  →     lead-glass syringe shields,
 less dose        (tongs, forceps)          lead pots, L-blocks
```

- Work in a designated, labelled area (radiation **trefoil** sign); use trays lined with absorbent paper to contain spills.
- Wear lab coat, disposable gloves and a personal **dosimeter** (film badge or TLD); never eat, drink, smoke or pipette by mouth.
- Use syringe shields and vial shields; handle vials with tongs; for beta emitters use low-atomic-number shielding (perspex) first to reduce secondary X-rays (bremsstrahlung).
- Monitor hands, surfaces and the area with survey meters; decontaminate spills at once (absorb, clean inwards, monitor, record).
- Aseptic technique too: most radiopharmaceuticals are sterile injections.

### Storage

- In lead-shielded containers ("pots") in a dedicated, locked store with the trefoil sign, away from general stock and from areas where people stay.
- Labels show the radionuclide, chemical form, activity and calibration date and time, volume, batch number and expiry (short for short-lived nuclides).
- Records of receipt, use and disposal are kept; generators stored in their shielding.

### Disposal

- **Decay-in-storage**: short-lived waste (Tc-99m, I-131, P-32) is stored, shielded and labelled, until activity falls to background (usually about 10 half-lives), checked with a monitor, and then disposed of as ordinary or biomedical waste after removing radiation labels.
- **Dilution and dispersal**: small amounts of liquid waste may be released to the drain only within the limits permitted by the regulator.
- **Return to supplier**: sealed sources such as cobalt-60 are returned to the supplier or an authorised agency.
- Patients treated with I-131 may need isolation for a time; their excreta are radioactive.

### Regulatory guidelines for safety (India)

- Use of radiation and radioactive material is controlled under the **Atomic Energy Act, 1962**, and rules made under it, including the **Atomic Energy (Radiation Protection) Rules, 2004** and the **Atomic Energy (Safe Disposal of Radioactive Wastes) Rules, 1987**.
- The **Atomic Energy Regulatory Board (AERB)** licenses nuclear medicine facilities, approves designs, sets dose limits and inspects.
- Each facility has a qualified **Radiological Safety Officer (RSO)**; workers are monitored for radiation dose.
- Radiopharmaceuticals in India are supplied mainly by the **Board of Radiation and Isotope Technology (BRIT)**; as drugs they also come under drug law and pharmacopoeial standards (IP monographs for radiopharmaceutical preparations).
- Transport follows regulations for radioactive material (labelled packages, transport index).

## Remember

- Achlorhydria → dilute HCl (through a straw); sodium acid phosphate = NaH2PO4 (urinary acidifier, laxative with Na2HPO4).
- Ideal antacid: non-systemic, no CO2, no rebound, no bowel upset, long-acting; combine Al (constipating, slow) with Mg (laxative, fast); add simethicone.
- NaHCO3: Solvay process; assay 1 M HCl, methyl orange, 1 ml ≡ 0.08401 g.
- Al(OH)3 gel: alum + Na2CO3; assay EDTA back titration with ZnSO4; 1 ml 0.05 M EDTA ≡ 0.002549 g Al2O3; ANC test.
- Saline laxatives: poorly absorbed ions hold water — Mg(OH)2, Na2HPO4, Rochelle salt, Mg trisilicate (mild; also a slow antacid forming silica gel).
- Antimicrobial mechanisms: oxidation, halogenation, protein precipitation.
