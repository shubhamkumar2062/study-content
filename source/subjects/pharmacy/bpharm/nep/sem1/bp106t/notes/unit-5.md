# Unit 5: Expectorants, emetics, haematinics, and poisons and antidotes

This short unit (6 lecture hours) covers inorganic compounds used as expectorants, emetics and haematinics, and introduces poisons and antidotes with sodium thiosulphate as the example. As in Unit 4, learn each compound as **formula → preparation (equation) → properties → assay → uses → storage**. The syllabus marks ammonium chloride, copper sulphate and ferrous sulphate with an asterisk; prepare their preparation and assay in full.

## 5.1 Expectorants

An **expectorant** is a drug that helps to remove sputum (phlegm) from the respiratory tract by increasing the volume of bronchial secretion and/or making it thinner and easier to cough up.

**Types**

| Type | How it acts | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sedative (reflex) expectorants** | Irritate the gastric mucosa; a reflex through the vagus increases watery bronchial secretion, which soothes a dry, irritating cough | Ammonium chloride, potassium iodide (partly), ipecacuanha in small amounts |
| **Stimulant (direct) expectorants** | Act directly on the bronchial glands or are excreted into the airways, stimulating secretion | Potassium iodide (excreted by bronchial glands), volatile oils, guaiphenesin |
| **Mucolytics** (related group) | Break down the structure of mucus | Bromhexine, acetylcysteine |

Expectorants are used in productive cough with thick, sticky sputum (bronchitis, asthma with mucus plugs). They should not be given to suppress a cough; that is the job of antitussives.

### Potassium iodide (KI, 166.00)

- **Preparation**:
  1. Iodine is added to hot potassium hydroxide solution until a faint colour persists; iodide and iodate form:
     3I2 + 6KOH → 5KI + KIO3 + 3H2O
     The solution is evaporated to dryness and the residue heated with **charcoal**, which reduces the iodate to iodide:
     KIO3 + 3C → KI + 3CO
     The mass is extracted with water, filtered and crystallised.
  2. Alternatively, iron filings and iodine give iron iodide, which is boiled with potassium carbonate; iron oxide is filtered off and KI crystallised:
     Fe3I8 + 4K2CO3 → 8KI + Fe3O4 + 4CO2
- **Properties**: colourless or white cubic crystals or powder; very soluble in water (dissolves iodine to form tri-iodide); saline, bitter taste; slightly hygroscopic; turns yellow in moist air or light (iodine released).
- **Assay (potassium iodate titration, Andrews' method)**: dissolve about 0.4 g, accurately weighed, in water; add concentrated HCl and chloroform; titrate with **0.05 M potassium iodate**, shaking after each addition, until the violet colour of iodine in the chloroform layer, which appears first, finally **disappears**.
  KIO3 + 2KI + 6HCl → 3ICl + 3KCl + 3H2O (1 KIO3 ≡ 2 KI) → **1 ml of 0.05 M KIO3 ≡ 0.01660 g KI** (166.00 × 0.05 × 2 ÷ 1000).
  Example: 0.4000 g needed 24.00 ml → 24.00 × 0.01660 × 100/0.4000 = **99.6%**.
- **Uses**: expectorant (in cough mixtures); with iodine as **Lugol's solution** before thyroid surgery and in thyroid storm (blocks hormone release); to **solubilise iodine** in solutions; **iodine source** in iodised salt and iodine deficiency; protecting the thyroid in radiation emergencies (stable iodide blocks uptake of radioactive iodine); antifungal in sporotrichosis.
- **Side effects**: prolonged use causes **iodism** (metallic taste, running nose, salivation, skin rash, swelling of salivary glands) and can disturb thyroid function.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers, protected from light.

### Ammonium chloride* (NH4Cl, 53.49)

- **Preparation**:
  1. Neutralisation of ammonia with hydrochloric acid; the solution is evaporated and the salt crystallised:
     NH3 + HCl → NH4Cl
  2. By heating ammonium sulphate with sodium chloride; ammonium chloride **sublimes** and is collected:
     (NH4)2SO4 + 2NaCl → 2NH4Cl + Na2SO4
  (It is also a by-product of the Solvay process.)
- **Properties**: colourless crystals or white crystalline powder; cooling saline taste; freely soluble; slightly hygroscopic; **sublimes** on heating (dissociates into NH3 and HCl, which recombine on cooling); solution slightly acidic.
- **Assay (formol titration)**: NH4⁺ is too weak an acid to titrate directly. Dissolve about 0.1 g in water, add **formaldehyde solution previously neutralised to phenolphthalein**, stand for about 2 minutes, then titrate with **0.1 M NaOH** to pink.
  4NH4Cl + 6HCHO → (CH2)6N4 (hexamine) + 4HCl + 6H2O; HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O → **1 ml of 0.1 M NaOH ≡ 0.005349 g NH4Cl**.
  Example: 0.1000 g; 18.65 ml; f 1.002 → 18.65 × 1.002 × 0.005349 × 100/0.1000 = **99.96% ≈ 100.0%**.
- **Uses**:
  - **Expectorant** (reflex): in cough mixtures.
  - **Systemic acidifier** in metabolic alkalosis: the liver converts NH4⁺ to urea, releasing H⁺ (and the Cl⁻ remains): 2NH4Cl + CO2 → (NH2)2CO + 2HCl + H2O.
  - **Urinary acidifier**: helps excretion of basic drugs; helps some urinary antiseptics act.
  - Mild diuretic effect (temporary).
- **Contraindications**: severe liver disease (ammonia accumulates) and kidney failure (acidosis).
- **Storage**: well-closed containers.

## 5.2 Emetics

An **emetic** is a substance that causes vomiting. Emetics were once used to empty the stomach after swallowing a poison or to bring up sputum. Today they are **rarely used**: emptying the stomach is done, when needed, by gastric lavage or with activated charcoal under medical care, and emesis carries risks.

**Classification**

| Type | Mechanism | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Centrally acting** | Stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) of the medulla | Apomorphine |
| **Reflex (locally acting)** | Irritate the stomach lining; impulses through the vagus reach the vomiting centre | Copper sulphate, zinc sulphate, strong salt (NaCl) solution, mustard |
| **Mixed** | Both | Ipecacuanha (emetine) |

**Emetics must never be used** in: unconscious or drowsy patients, convulsions, swallowed **corrosives** (acids, alkalis — would burn the oesophagus again), swallowed **kerosene or other petroleum products** (aspiration into lungs), and in late pregnancy or heart disease.

### Copper sulphate* (CuSO4·5H2O, 249.69)

- **Preparation**:
  1. Copper (scrap) is heated with concentrated sulphuric acid: Cu + 2H2SO4 → CuSO4 + SO2 + 2H2O.
  2. Industrially, copper oxide (or copper exposed to air) is dissolved in dilute sulphuric acid: CuO + H2SO4 → CuSO4 + H2O. The blue crystals are obtained on cooling.
- **Properties**: blue, triclinic crystals or blue powder ("blue vitriol"); soluble in water; efflorescent in dry air; on heating loses water to form white anhydrous CuSO4 (which turns blue again with water — a test for water).
- **Assay (iodometry)**: dissolve about 1 g, accurately weighed, in water; add acetic acid and about 3 g potassium iodide; titrate the liberated iodine with **0.1 M sodium thiosulphate**, adding starch near the end point; add **potassium thiocyanate** near the end to free iodine adsorbed on the CuI precipitate (sharper end point).
  2CuSO4 + 4KI → 2CuI↓ + I2 + 2K2SO4; I2 + 2Na2S2O3 → 2NaI + Na2S4O6. So 2 Cu²⁺ ≡ I2 ≡ 2 S2O3²⁻, i.e. Cu : thiosulphate = 1 : 1 → **1 ml of 0.1 M Na2S2O3 ≡ 0.02497 g CuSO4·5H2O**.
  Example: 1.0000 g needed 40.00 ml → 40.00 × 0.02497 × 100/1.0000 = **99.9%**.
- **Uses**: formerly a reflex emetic (now obsolete for this purpose); astringent and caustic in small concentrations (granulations, fungal skin conditions — historical); copper supplement; fungicide and algicide (Bordeaux mixture in agriculture, swimming pools); laboratory reagent (Fehling's and Benedict's solutions, biuret test).
- **Caution**: poisonous when swallowed in quantity; kept out of reach of children.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers.

### Sodium potassium tartrate (as listed in the syllabus)

Sodium potassium tartrate (Rochelle salt, KNaC4H4O6·4H2O) is described in Unit 4 (preparation from cream of tartar and sodium carbonate; saline laxative). The syllabus lists it again under emetics. ## 5.3 Haematinics

A **haematinic** is a substance that increases the haemoglobin content of blood and is used to prevent or treat **anaemia**. The main inorganic haematinics are **iron salts**; others include vitamin B12 and folic acid (organic).

**Iron in the body**

- Total body iron is about 3–5 g; most is in **haemoglobin** (carries oxygen), some in myoglobin and enzymes (cytochromes), and the rest stored as **ferritin and haemosiderin** in liver, spleen and bone marrow. Transferrin carries iron in plasma.
- Iron is absorbed mainly in the **duodenum and upper jejunum**, as **ferrous (Fe²⁺)** iron; ferric iron is first reduced.
- Absorption is **increased** by gastric acid, ascorbic acid (vitamin C), iron deficiency and pregnancy; it is **decreased** by antacids, phosphates, phytates and tannins (tea), tetracyclines and food.
- **Iron-deficiency anaemia** (microcytic, hypochromic) results from blood loss, poor diet, increased need (pregnancy, growth) or poor absorption.

**Why ferrous salts?** They are absorbed better than ferric salts, are cheap and are the first choice for oral therapy. Common side effects of oral iron: nausea, epigastric pain, constipation or diarrhoea, black stools (harmless), and staining of teeth by liquids. Taking iron with food lessens upset but also absorption. Iron tablets are kept out of reach of children because accidental iron poisoning in children is dangerous.

### Ferrous sulphate* (FeSO4·7H2O, 278.01; dried ferrous sulphate also official)

- **Preparation**: iron wire or filings are dissolved in **dilute sulphuric acid** with warming; the solution is filtered, acidified a little and crystallised. An excess of iron and a slightly acid solution keep the iron in the ferrous state (oxidation to ferric is prevented).
  Fe + H2SO4 → FeSO4 + H2↑
  **Dried ferrous sulphate** is made by heating the crystals at about 40 °C, then at higher temperature, until most of the water of crystallisation is lost.
- **Properties**: pale bluish-green crystals or crystalline powder ("green vitriol"), astringent taste; freely soluble in water; efflorescent in dry air; in moist air it oxidises and becomes coated with yellowish-brown **basic ferric sulphate**. Contains about 20% iron (55.85/278.01).
- **Assay (cerimetry)**: dissolve about 0.5 g, accurately weighed, in a mixture of dilute sulphuric acid and **freshly boiled and cooled water** (removes dissolved oxygen, which would oxidise Fe²⁺); add **ferroin** solution; titrate with **0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate** until the red colour changes to pale blue (or greenish).
  Fe²⁺ + Ce⁴⁺ → Fe³⁺ + Ce³⁺ (1 : 1) → **1 ml of 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate ≡ 0.02780 g FeSO4·7H2O** (278.01 × 0.1 ÷ 1000).
  Example: 0.5000 g needed 17.90 ml (f 1.002) → 17.90 × 1.002 × 0.02780 × 100/0.5000 = **99.7%**.
  (Permanganometry may also be used: MnO4⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H2O; 1 ml of 0.02 M KMnO4 ≡ 0.02780 g FeSO4·7H2O, since 0.02 M × 5 = 0.1 equivalent.)
- **Uses**: oral haematinic for prevention and treatment of iron-deficiency anaemia (tablets, often with folic acid in pregnancy); laboratory reducing agent.
- **Storage**: well-closed containers (protect from moist air, which oxidises it).

### Ferrous gluconate ((C6H11O7)2Fe·2H2O, 482.17)

- **Preparation**: by double decomposition between **calcium gluconate** and **ferrous sulphate** in hot water; insoluble calcium sulphate is filtered off and ferrous gluconate crystallised from the filtrate:
  (C6H11O7)2Ca + FeSO4 → (C6H11O7)2Fe + CaSO4↓
  It can also be made by dissolving ferrous carbonate or iron in gluconic acid solution.
- **Properties**: yellowish-grey or pale greenish-yellow powder or granules, slight caramel-like odour; soluble in water (warm water more readily). Contains about 11.6% iron (55.85/482.17), less than ferrous sulphate.
- **Assay (cerimetry)**: dissolved in water with dilute sulphuric acid and titrated with **0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate** using ferroin indicator (follow the IP monograph for any preliminary reduction step). From the 1 : 1 reaction Fe²⁺ + Ce⁴⁺ → Fe³⁺ + Ce³⁺, **1 ml of 0.1 M ceric ammonium sulphate ≡ 0.04822 g of the dihydrate** (482.17 × 0.1 ÷ 1000; derived).
- **Uses**: oral haematinic; **less irritant** to the stomach than ferrous sulphate, so useful when ferrous sulphate is poorly tolerated (a larger amount of salt is needed for the same iron).
- **Storage**: well-closed containers, protected from light.

| | Ferrous sulphate | Ferrous gluconate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Iron content | About 20% (heptahydrate) | About 11.6% |
| Gastric irritation | More | Less |
| Appearance | Bluish-green crystals | Yellowish-grey/greenish-yellow powder |
| Preparation | Iron + dilute H2SO4 | Calcium gluconate + ferrous sulphate |
| Assay | Cerimetry, 0.02780 g per ml | Cerimetry, 0.04822 g per ml |

## 5.4 Poisons and antidotes

**Poison**: any substance which, when taken into or formed in the body in a sufficient amount, damages health or causes death by its chemical action. Almost any substance (even a medicine) can act as a poison if the amount is large enough.

**Antidote**: a substance that counteracts or neutralises the effect of a poison.

**General steps in treating poisoning** (in a hospital, under medical care): keep the airway, breathing and circulation going; remove the poison from the body (wash the skin, decontaminate the gut where appropriate, use activated charcoal); give a specific antidote where one exists; increase elimination (for example alkalinising the urine); supportive care.

### Classification of antidotes

| Class | How it works | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Physical (mechanical)** | Prevents absorption by physical means: adsorbs, coats or removes the poison | **Activated charcoal** (adsorbs many poisons); demulcents (milk, egg white) coat the gut; gastric lavage |
| **Chemical** | Reacts with the poison and makes it harmless or insoluble | Weak acids/bases to neutralise (not used for corrosives without medical advice); **sodium thiosulphate** (cyanide → thiocyanate); **chelating agents**: calcium disodium edetate (lead), dimercaprol (BAL: arsenic, mercury, gold), desferrioxamine (iron), penicillamine (copper, lead); potassium permanganate oxidises some alkaloids (historical) |
| **Physiological (pharmacological)** | Produces the opposite effect or blocks the poison's receptor | Atropine (organophosphate insecticides), naloxone (opioids), flumazenil (benzodiazepines), oxygen (carbon monoxide), N-acetylcysteine (paracetamol) |

The old **"universal antidote"** (activated charcoal with magnesium oxide and tannic acid) is of historical interest only; activated charcoal alone is used now.

### Sodium thiosulphate (Na2S2O3·5H2O, 248.18)

- **Preparation**: a solution of **sodium sulphite** is boiled with **powdered sulphur**; the filtered solution is concentrated and the pentahydrate crystallised.
  Na2SO3 + S → Na2S2O3
  (It is also obtained from the by-products of other sulphur industries, for example by oxidising sodium sulphide.)
- **Properties**: large colourless crystals or granules; very soluble in water; efflorescent in warm dry air; melts in its own water of crystallisation on gentle heating. With acids it decomposes giving sulphur (turbidity) and sulphur dioxide: S2O3²⁻ + 2H⁺ → S↓ + SO2 + H2O. It is a reducing agent: with iodine it forms tetrathionate.
- **Assay (iodimetry)**: dissolve about 0.5 g, accurately weighed, in water; titrate with **0.05 M iodine** using starch to the first permanent blue.
  2Na2S2O3 + I2 → Na2S4O6 + 2NaI → **1 ml of 0.05 M iodine ≡ 0.02482 g Na2S2O3·5H2O** (248.18 × 0.05 × 2 ÷ 1000).
  Example: 0.5000 g needed 20.10 ml → 20.10 × 0.02482 × 100/0.5000 = **99.8%**.
- **Use as an antidote in cyanide poisoning**:
  1. **Sodium nitrite** (given first, intravenously) converts part of the haemoglobin to **methaemoglobin**, which binds cyanide strongly (cyanmethaemoglobin), drawing it away from cytochrome oxidase so that cell respiration restarts.
  2. **Sodium thiosulphate** then acts as a **sulphur donor** for the liver enzyme **rhodanese**, which converts cyanide into the much less toxic **thiocyanate**, excreted in urine.
  CN⁻ + S2O3²⁻ ──rhodanese──► SCN⁻ + SO3²⁻
  (Hydroxocobalamin is another antidote that binds cyanide directly.)
- **Other uses**: topical antifungal (pityriasis/tinea versicolor); volumetric reagent in iodometry; photographic "hypo" (fixer); antichlor (removes excess chlorine).
- **Storage**: well-closed containers.

## 5.5 A note on astringents

The course outcomes also mention **astringents**. An astringent precipitates surface proteins of the skin or mucous membrane, forming a protective layer, tightening tissue and reducing secretion and minor bleeding. Inorganic examples: **potash alum**, **zinc sulphate** (eye drops, lotions), **zinc oxide** (dusting powders, ointments), dilute **copper sulphate**, **silver nitrate**, aluminium salts in antiperspirants. Their action is the protein-precipitating mechanism described in Unit 4.

## Remember

- Expectorant: increases or thins bronchial secretion; sedative (reflex: NH4Cl) vs stimulant (direct: KI); not for dry cough suppression.
- KI: I2 + hot KOH → KI + KIO3, charcoal reduces iodate; assay Andrews (KIO3, HCl, chloroform end point), 1 ml 0.05 M ≡ 0.01660 g; iodism.
- NH4Cl: NH3 + HCl; sublimes; formol assay 1 ml 0.1 M NaOH ≡ 0.005349 g; expectorant, systemic and urinary acidifier; avoid in liver/kidney failure.
- Emetics: central (apomorphine) vs reflex (CuSO4, ZnSO4); never in corrosives, kerosene, unconscious patients.
- CuSO4: Cu + conc H2SO4; iodometry with KI, KSCN near end; 1 ml 0.1 M thio ≡ 0.02497 g.
- Iron absorbed as Fe²⁺ in duodenum; vitamin C helps, antacids/phosphates/tea reduce.
- FeSO4: Fe + dilute H2SO4 with excess iron; cerimetry with ferroin, boiled-cooled water; 1 ml 0.1 M Ce ≡ 0.02780 g; about 20% Fe.
- Ferrous gluconate: Ca gluconate + FeSO4; less irritant; about 11.6% Fe; 1 ml 0.1 M Ce ≡ 0.04822 g.
- Antidotes: physical (activated charcoal), chemical (thiosulphate, chelators), physiological (atropine, naloxone).
- Na2S2O3: Na2SO3 + S; iodimetry 1 ml 0.05 M I2 ≡ 0.02482 g; cyanide: nitrite → methaemoglobin, thiosulphate → rhodanese → thiocyanate.
