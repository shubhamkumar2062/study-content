# Unit 2: Acid–base chemistry, buffers, isotonicity and electrolytes

Most drugs are weak acids or weak bases, and the body itself keeps its pH and salt balance within narrow limits. This unit explains what acids, bases and buffers are, how to calculate the pH of a buffer, why eye drops and injections must be isotonic, and which electrolytes are used to replace what the body loses.

## 2.1 Definitions of acids and bases

| Theory | Acid | Base | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arrhenius | Gives H⁺ ions in water | Gives OH⁻ ions in water | HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻; NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻ |
| Brønsted–Lowry | Proton (H⁺) donor | Proton acceptor | NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻ (water is the acid here) |
| Lewis | Electron-pair acceptor | Electron-pair donor | BF3 (acid) + NH3 (base) → F3B–NH3 |

**Conjugate pairs (Brønsted–Lowry).** When an acid gives up a proton, what remains is its conjugate base: CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + H⁺. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base and vice versa.

**Amphiprotic substances** can act as both acid and base: water, bicarbonate (HCO3⁻), amino acids.

**Strong and weak.** A strong acid (HCl, HNO3, H2SO4, HClO4) or strong base (NaOH, KOH) ionises completely in water. A weak acid (acetic acid, boric acid, carbonic acid) or weak base (ammonia, most alkaloids) ionises only partly; its strength is given by the dissociation constant Ka or Kb, usually written as pKa = −log Ka.

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA]

The lower the pKa, the stronger the acid. Acetic acid pKa about 4.76; boric acid about 9.2; ammonium ion about 9.25 (so ammonia pKb about 4.75).

## 2.2 The pH scale and its significance

Water ionises slightly: H2O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻, Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.

**pH** = −log[H⁺]; **pOH** = −log[OH⁻]; pH + pOH = 14 (at 25 °C).

```text
 0 ────────── 3 ────────── 7 ─────────── 11 ────────── 14
 strongly acid    weakly acid   neutral   weakly alkaline  strongly alkaline
 (gastric juice ~1–2)          (pure water)   (blood 7.35–7.45)
```

A change of 1 pH unit means a tenfold change in [H⁺].

**Quick calculations**

- 0.1 M HCl (strong): [H⁺] = 0.1 → pH = **1**.
- 0.1 M acetic acid (pKa 4.76): [H⁺] = √(Ka × C) = √(1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.1) → pH = ½(pKa − log C) = ½(4.76 + 1) = **2.88**.
- 0.1 M ammonia (pKb 4.75): pOH = ½(4.75 + 1) = 2.875 → pH = **11.12**.

**Significance of pH in pharmacy**

| Area | Why pH matters |
| --- | --- |
| Stability | Many drugs hydrolyse or oxidise faster at certain pH (esters, penicillins, adrenaline); formulations are buffered at the pH of best stability |
| Solubility | Weak acids dissolve better in alkaline, weak bases in acid solution; wrong pH causes precipitation |
| Absorption | Only the un-ionised form crosses membranes easily (pH-partition theory) |
| Comfort | Eye drops and injections far from body pH cause pain and irritation |
| Activity | Preservatives such as benzoic acid act only in the un-ionised (acid) form |
| Body | Blood pH 7.35–7.45; below 7.35 is acidosis, above 7.45 alkalosis |
| Analysis | Titrations, complexometry and limit tests need set pH ranges |

## 2.3 Buffers

A **buffer** is a solution that resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added, or when it is diluted.

**Types**

1. **Acidic buffer**: a weak acid + its salt with a strong base (acetic acid + sodium acetate; boric acid + borax).
2. **Basic buffer**: a weak base + its salt with a strong acid (ammonia + ammonium chloride).
3. Salt of a polybasic acid in two forms (NaH2PO4 + Na2HPO4, the phosphate buffer).

**How a buffer works (acetate buffer)**

```text
 Add acid (H⁺):    CH3COO⁻ + H⁺   → CH3COOH      (H⁺ removed by the salt)
 Add alkali (OH⁻): CH3COOH + OH⁻  → CH3COO⁻ + H2O (OH⁻ removed by the acid)
```

So the added H⁺ or OH⁻ is converted into the weak partner, and only the ratio salt : acid changes slightly.

**Pharmaceutical buffers and uses**

| Buffer | Approximate useful range | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Acetate | pH 3.8–5.8 | Parenteral and oral solutions, analysis |
| Phosphate | pH 6–8 | Injections, ophthalmic solutions, dissolution media |
| Borate (boric acid/borax) | pH 7–9 | Eye drops (not for injections) |
| Citrate | pH 3–6 | Syrups, injections |
| Ammonia/ammonium chloride | about pH 10 | Complexometric titrations |

**Ideal buffer for a formulation:** adequate capacity at the required pH, non-toxic, compatible with the drug, stable, does not support microbial growth, and (for eye and injection use) does not irritate.

**Buffer capacity (β)** is the amount of strong acid or base (in gram equivalents per litre) needed to change the pH by one unit: β = ΔB ÷ ΔpH. Capacity is highest when salt = acid (pH = pKa) and when the total concentration is high. A buffer works best within about pKa ± 1.

**Physiological buffers**: bicarbonate/carbonic acid (main buffer of plasma), phosphate (inside cells and in urine), proteins including haemoglobin.

## 2.4 Buffer equation and calculation of pH

The **Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** comes from the Ka expression of a weak acid:

```text
 Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]   →   [H⁺] = Ka [HA]/[A⁻]   →   take −log:

 Acidic buffer:  pH = pKa + log ([salt] / [acid])
 Basic buffer:   pOH = pKb + log ([salt] / [base]),  pH = 14 − pOH
```

Assumptions: the acid and salt concentrations equal what was added (weak acid barely ionised, salt fully ionised); it fails for very dilute or very concentrated solutions.

**Worked examples** (acetic acid pKa 4.76)

1. 0.1 M acetic acid + 0.2 M sodium acetate: pH = 4.76 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.76 + 0.301 = **5.06**.
2. Equal concentrations of acid and salt: log 1 = 0, so pH = pKa = **4.76**.
3. Effect of acid: 1 litre of buffer with 0.1 mol acetic acid and 0.1 mol acetate; add 0.01 mol HCl. Acetate becomes 0.09 mol, acetic acid 0.11 mol. pH = 4.76 + log(0.09/0.11) = **4.67** (a fall of only 0.09; the same HCl in 1 litre of water would give pH 2).
4. Ratio needed for pH 5.0: log(salt/acid) = 5.0 − 4.76 = 0.24 → salt : acid = **1.74 : 1**.
5. Basic buffer: 0.1 M NH3 + 0.1 M NH4Cl (pKb 4.75): pOH = 4.75 → pH = **9.25**.

## 2.5 Isotonicity

**Osmosis**: movement of water through a semipermeable membrane from a dilute solution to a more concentrated one. **Osmotic pressure** depends on the number of particles dissolved, not their nature (a colligative property).

| Term | Meaning | Effect on red blood cells |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Isotonic** | Same osmotic pressure as body fluids (0.9% w/v NaCl) | No change |
| **Hypotonic** | Lower osmotic pressure | Water enters; cells swell and burst (haemolysis) |
| **Hypertonic** | Higher osmotic pressure | Water leaves; cells shrink (crenation) |

```text
  Hypotonic        Isotonic        Hypertonic
   water in         no net          water out
    (  O  )         ( o )            (*)
   swells,         normal          shrinks,
   bursts                          crenated
```

"Iso-osmotic" refers to equal osmotic pressure measured physically; "isotonic" refers to the effect on a living membrane. Some iso-osmotic solutions (for example boric acid solutions) are not isotonic to red cells because the solute passes through the cell membrane.

Blood plasma and tears both freeze at about **−0.52 °C**. A 0.9% w/v NaCl solution has the same freezing point, so it is isotonic.

**Methods of adjusting tonicity**

1. **Freezing point depression (cryoscopic) method.** The amount of NaCl (W, g per 100 ml) needed is:

```text
 W = (0.52 − a) / b
 a = freezing point depression caused by the drug at its strength
     (= % of drug × FPD of a 1% solution)
 b = FPD of 1% w/v NaCl = 0.576 °C
```

2. **Sodium chloride equivalent (E) method.** E is the weight of NaCl giving the same osmotic effect as 1 g of the drug. NaCl to add = 0.9 g per 100 ml − (grams of drug × E).

3. **Graphical / White–Vincent method**: the drug is dissolved in enough water to make it isotonic, then made up with an isotonic vehicle.

**Worked example (made-up drug).** A 1.5% w/v eye drop of drug X; FPD of a 1% solution of X = 0.10 °C.

- a = 1.5 × 0.10 = 0.15 °C
- W = (0.52 − 0.15) ÷ 0.576 = **0.64 g NaCl per 100 ml**
- Check with E: E = 0.10/0.576 = 0.174; 1.5 g × 0.174 = 0.26 g NaCl-equivalent; NaCl needed = 0.90 − 0.26 = **0.64 g** — the same answer.

**Applications**

| Product | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| IV fluids (large volumes) | Must be isotonic or close to it; large volumes of hypotonic fluid cause haemolysis. Hypertonic solutions (concentrated dextrose for nutrition) are given slowly into a large central vein. Examples: Sodium Chloride Injection 0.9%, Dextrose 5%, Ringer's and compound sodium lactate solutions |
| Small-volume injections | Ideally isotonic, especially subcutaneous and intramuscular (less pain); intrathecal injections must be isotonic |
| Ophthalmic solutions | Should be isotonic with tears (eye tolerates about 0.6–2% NaCl equivalent); also buffered near pH 7.4 if stability allows, sterile and particle-free |
| Nasal drops, enemas, wound washes | Isotonic for comfort and to avoid damage to tissue |

## 2.6 Major extracellular and intracellular electrolytes

**Electrolytes** are substances that ionise in body water. About 60% of body weight is water: about two-thirds inside cells (intracellular fluid, ICF) and one-third outside (extracellular fluid, ECF: plasma and interstitial fluid).

| Fluid | Main cation | Main anions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Extracellular | **Sodium (Na⁺)** | **Chloride (Cl⁻)**, bicarbonate (HCO3⁻) |
| Intracellular | **Potassium (K⁺)**, magnesium (Mg²⁺) | **Phosphate**, proteins |

Usual plasma values (adult): sodium about 135–145 mEq/L; potassium about 3.5–5.0 mEq/L; chloride about 98–106 mEq/L; bicarbonate about 22–28 mEq/L; calcium about 8.5–10.5 mg/dL.

The difference between ECF and ICF is kept by the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump** in every cell membrane (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in).

**Units:** milliequivalent (mEq) = millimoles × valency. 1 g NaCl = 1000/58.44 = 17.1 mmol = **17.1 mEq** Na⁺; 1 g KCl = 1000/74.55 = **13.4 mEq** K⁺. 0.9% NaCl contains about 154 mmol each of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ per litre (about 308 mOsm/L).

## 2.7 Functions of major physiological ions

| Ion | Main functions | Too little / too much |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sodium** | Main ECF cation; controls ECF volume, osmotic pressure and blood pressure; nerve impulse and muscle contraction; absorption of glucose and amino acids | Hyponatraemia: confusion, weakness; hypernatraemia: thirst, oedema |
| **Potassium** | Main ICF cation; resting membrane potential; heart rhythm; nerve and muscle function; enzyme activity | Hypokalaemia: weakness, cramps, arrhythmia; hyperkalaemia: dangerous arrhythmia |
| **Calcium** | Bones and teeth; blood clotting (factor IV); muscle contraction; nerve transmission; heart contraction; hormone release | Hypocalcaemia: tetany; hypercalcaemia: stones, weakness |
| **Chloride** | Main ECF anion with sodium; osmotic balance; gastric HCl; chloride shift in red cells | Follows sodium and acid–base changes |
| **Bicarbonate** | Main plasma buffer; carries CO2 | Low: metabolic acidosis; high: alkalosis |
| **Phosphate** | Bones and teeth, ATP, nucleic acids, phospholipids; buffer in cells and urine | — |
| **Magnesium** | Cofactor of many enzymes (ATP reactions); nerve and muscle function | Low: tremor, arrhythmia |

## 2.8 Electrolytes used in replacement therapy

Replacement therapy restores water and ions lost by vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, sweating, surgery or use of diuretics.

### Sodium chloride (NaCl, molar mass 58.44)

- **Preparation**: from sea water or brine by evaporation; purified by passing HCl gas into a saturated solution, when pure NaCl separates (common ion effect).
- **Properties**: colourless cubic crystals or white powder, salty taste, freely soluble in water.
- **Assay (IP principle)**: precipitation titration with 0.1 M silver nitrate, potassium chromate indicator (Mohr), or the modified Volhard method (see Unit 3). **1 ml of 0.1 M AgNO3 ≡ 0.005844 g NaCl** (58.44 × 0.1/1000).
- **Uses**: electrolyte replenisher (0.9% injection, oral salt), to make solutions isotonic, nasal and wound washing, hypertonic solutions for some conditions, in ORS.

### Potassium chloride (KCl, 74.55)

- Prepared from natural minerals (sylvine, carnallite) and purified by crystallisation.
- White crystals, freely soluble, saline taste.
- Assay: argentometric titration, 1 ml of 0.1 M AgNO3 ≡ 0.007455 g KCl (74.55 × 0.1/1000; derived from the 1 : 1 reaction).
- Uses: hypokalaemia (diuretic therapy, vomiting, diarrhoea), in ORS. Intravenous potassium must always be diluted and given slowly because a high plasma potassium stops the heart; never given as a rapid injection.

### Calcium chloride (CaCl2·2H2O, 147.01)

- Prepared by dissolving calcium carbonate in hydrochloric acid: CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2.
- White, hygroscopic/deliquescent fragments or granules, very soluble.
- Assay: complexometric titration with 0.05 M disodium EDTA; 1 ml of 0.05 M EDTA ≡ 0.007351 g CaCl2·2H2O (derived, 1 : 1).
- Uses: calcium replacement in hypocalcaemia and tetany; in Ringer's solution. Irritant to tissues, so given intravenously only, slowly.

### Oral Rehydration Salt (ORS)

ORS is a mixture of glucose and salts dissolved in clean water and taken by mouth to treat dehydration, mainly from diarrhoea.

**Principle**: in the small intestine, glucose and sodium are absorbed **together** by a co-transporter (SGLT1), and water follows by osmosis. This pathway keeps working even during diarrhoea such as cholera, so oral glucose–salt solution replaces losses without an injection.

**Reduced-osmolarity ORS (WHO/UNICEF, also in the IP)**, per litre of water:

| Ingredient | g per litre | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium chloride | 2.6 | Sodium and chloride |
| Glucose, anhydrous | 13.5 | Drives sodium (and water) absorption |
| Potassium chloride | 1.5 | Replaces potassium lost in stools |
| Trisodium citrate dihydrate | 2.9 | Corrects acidosis (citrate → bicarbonate); more stable than sodium bicarbonate |

This gives about Na⁺ 75, Cl⁻ 65, K⁺ 20, citrate 10 and glucose 75 mmol per litre; total osmolarity about **245 mOsm/L**. The lower osmolarity (compared with the older 311 mOsm/L formula) reduces stool output and vomiting. Zinc supplements are given alongside ORS in children with diarrhoea.

## 2.9 Physiological acid–base balance

Normal arterial blood pH is **7.35–7.45**. The body keeps it there by three lines of defence:

```text
  Acid/base load
      │
      ▼
 1. Buffers (seconds)      HCO3⁻/H2CO3, phosphate, proteins, haemoglobin
      │
      ▼
 2. Lungs (minutes)        CO2 blown off faster or slower by changing breathing
      │
      ▼
 3. Kidneys (hours–days)   excrete H⁺ (as NH4⁺, H2PO4⁻) and reabsorb or make HCO3⁻
```

**Bicarbonate buffer**: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H⁺ + HCO3⁻. The pH depends on the ratio [HCO3⁻] : [H2CO3], normally about **20 : 1** (pKa about 6.1; 6.1 + log 20 ≈ 7.4).

**Disturbances**

| Disorder | Primary change | Common causes | Compensation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metabolic acidosis | HCO3⁻ falls | Diabetic ketoacidosis, kidney failure, severe diarrhoea | Fast deep breathing (loses CO2) |
| Metabolic alkalosis | HCO3⁻ rises | Prolonged vomiting, excess antacid (sodium bicarbonate) | Slow breathing |
| Respiratory acidosis | CO2 retained | Lung disease, depressed breathing | Kidneys retain HCO3⁻ |
| Respiratory alkalosis | CO2 lost | Hyperventilation (anxiety, high altitude) | Kidneys excrete HCO3⁻ |

Pharmaceutical agents: sodium bicarbonate and sodium lactate/citrate (alkalinisers, for acidosis); ammonium chloride (acidifier, for alkalosis; see Unit 5).

- Buffer = weak acid + its salt (or weak base + its salt); best near pH = pKa; capacity β = ΔB/ΔpH.
- pH = pKa + log(salt/acid); pOH = pKb + log(salt/base).
- Isotonic = 0.9% NaCl = freezing point −0.52 °C; W = (0.52 − a)/0.576.
