# How to use this subject

Pharmaceutical Inorganic and Analytical Chemistry joins two subjects of the older B.Pharm course: pharmaceutical analysis (errors, impurities, all kinds of titration) and inorganic pharmaceutical chemistry (antacids, antiseptics, electrolytes, haematinics, radiopharmaceuticals). It is a 3-credit theory course of the new B.Pharm (NEP 2020): 45 lecture hours, 75 marks in all (30 sessional, 45 end-semester). The matching practical (BP111P) uses the same titrations and limit tests, so what you learn here also earns marks in the laboratory.

The notes follow the five units of the PCI B.Pharm Syllabus 2026 exactly; no unit is split or joined. Unit 3 (all titrations, 14 hours) is the longest; give it two or three days. The other units take one day each. For each day: read the notes, close them and write the equations and equivalence factors from memory, redo every worked example with a calculator, then answer the questions of that unit.

**End-semester paper (PCI Table XV, 3-credit course): 45 marks, 2 hours.** Confirm the exact pattern with your university.

| Section | What it asks | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Multiple-choice / objective questions, all compulsory | 10 × 1 = 10 |
| II | Short answers: answer 5 of 7 | 5 × 4 = 20 |
| III | Long answers: answer 2 of 3 | 2 × 7.5 = 15 |

In this app the long answers are marked out of 8 (the app uses whole marks), so the full mock paper totals 46. The model long answers are written for 7.5 marks: about one and a half pages with the principle, equations, a table or diagram and 6–8 clear points. Short answers (4 marks) need about half a page: definition, principle or equation, 3–5 points, and an example. Very-short questions (2 marks) in the bank are for revision and for the objective section.

**Probability ratings**

- **High**: a standard topic of this syllabus that appears in most papers on the same content; prepare the full answer.
- **Medium**: asked every few years, or as part of a larger question.
- **Low**: less common; useful for MCQs and as a backup choice.

These ratings are estimates from the weight of each topic in the syllabus and from how often similar questions appear in pharmaceutical analysis and inorganic chemistry papers of the older PCI course. This is a new syllabus, so there are no past papers of this exact course yet; treat the ratings as guidance, not a promise.

**Where the marks usually come from**

| Unit | Topics to prepare first |
| --- | --- |
| 1 Analysis, errors, impurities | Primary and secondary standards; types of errors and how to minimise them; accuracy vs precision; sources of impurities; limit tests for arsenic, iron, chloride, sulphate, heavy metals; modified limit tests |
| 2 Acid–base, buffers, electrolytes | Buffer action and Henderson–Hasselbalch numericals; isotonicity and its adjustment; functions of Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺; ORS composition and principle; physiological acid–base balance |
| 3 Titrations | Theory of indicators; neutralisation curves; non-aqueous solvents and perchloric acid titrant; Mohr/Volhard/modified Volhard/Fajans; metal ion indicators and masking; EDTA standardisation; iodimetry vs iodometry; cerimetry; gravimetry steps |
| 4 GI agents, antimicrobials, radiopharmaceuticals | Ideal antacid and combinations; sodium bicarbonate and aluminium hydroxide gel (preparation, assay); hydrogen peroxide and chlorinated lime (assay, volume strength, available chlorine); mechanism of antimicrobials; radioisotopes and safe handling |
| 5 Miscellaneous compounds | Expectorants (KI, NH4Cl); emetics (CuSO4); ferrous sulphate and gluconate; classification of antidotes; sodium thiosulphate in cyanide poisoning |

**The asterisk compounds.** The syllabus marks sodium chloride, calcium gluconate, sodium bicarbonate, aluminium hydroxide gel, hydrogen peroxide, chlorinated lime, ammonium chloride, copper sulphate and ferrous sulphate with an asterisk. For these, be ready to write the full preparation, assay and uses. For the other compounds, the principle of the assay and the uses are usually enough.

**Scoring tips for this subject**

1. Every assay answer should have: principle in one line → balanced equation → titrant and indicator → end point colour → "1 ml of … M … ≡ … g" → formula for %. Examiners give a mark for each.
2. Do not memorise equivalence factors blindly: learn to derive them (molar mass × molarity × mole ratio ÷ 1000). If you forget the number, show the derivation and you still earn most of the marks.
3. Write every reaction balanced; check charges in ionic equations (for example 2MnO4⁻ + 5H2O2 + 6H⁺).
4. Draw small diagrams: Gutzeit apparatus, titration curves, buffer action, red cells in hypo/iso/hypertonic solutions, time–distance–shielding.
5. Use comparison tables: iodimetry vs iodometry, Mohr vs Volhard vs Fajans, primary vs secondary standards, systemic vs non-systemic antacids, accuracy vs precision.
6. Numericals: write the formula, substitute with units, and box the answer (pH, g NaCl per 100 ml, molarity, %).
7. Learn these numbers cold: Kw 10⁻¹⁴; blood pH 7.35–7.45; HCO3⁻ : H2CO3 = 20 : 1; FP of blood −0.52 °C; FPD of 1% NaCl 0.576 °C; 0.9% NaCl isotonic; ORS 245 mOsm/L; 3% H2O2 ≈ 10 volume; chlorinated lime ≥ 30% available chlorine; half-lives I-131 8 d, Tc-99m 6 h, P-32 14 d, Co-60 5.3 y.
8. Do all ten MCQs; they are compulsory and quick, and the MCQ bank here covers the facts they usually test.
