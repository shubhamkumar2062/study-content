# Unit 2: Developmental and behavioural psychology

People of different ages need different words, different explanations and different kinds of reassurance. People with different personalities react differently to the same illness. This unit covers how humans develop across life, the main personality theories, the mental factors that shape how a person sees and recovers from illness, the common psychological disorders seen in healthcare, and how people cope with stress.

## 2.1 Human developmental stages and healthcare needs

**Development** is the orderly change in body, mind, emotions and social behaviour from conception to death. It follows some general principles:

- It is continuous but happens at different rates in different areas.
- It follows a predictable order (sitting before standing, words before sentences).
- It moves from head to toe (cephalocaudal) and from the centre outwards (proximodistal).
- It depends on both heredity (nature) and environment (nurture).
- Each person develops at their own pace.

### Stages of life and their healthcare needs

| Stage | Approximate age | Main features | Healthcare and communication needs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prenatal | Conception to birth | Organ formation | Maternal nutrition, avoid harmful drugs and alcohol, antenatal care |
| Infancy | 0–2 years | Rapid growth, attachment to caregiver, first words | Immunisation, growth monitoring; all communication goes through the parent |
| Early childhood | 2–6 years | Language, play, fear of strangers and pain | Simple words, play, honest but gentle explanation; involve the parent; flavoured liquids |
| Middle childhood | 6–12 years | School, logic, rules, peer group | Explain simply and directly to the child as well as the parent; praise cooperation |
| Adolescence | 12–18 years | Puberty, identity, peer influence, risk-taking | Privacy and confidentiality, non-judgemental talk about smoking, alcohol, sexual health, body image, mental health |
| Early adulthood | 18–40 years | Work, marriage, parenthood | Contraception, pregnancy, lifestyle, stress at work |
| Middle adulthood | 40–65 years | Career peak, caring for children and parents | Screening for diabetes, hypertension, cancer; chronic disease management |
| Late adulthood (old age) | 65 years and above | Physical decline, retirement, loss of partner, many diseases | Many medicines (polypharmacy), poor sight and hearing, memory problems, loneliness; slow clear speech, large print, involve carers |

### Erikson's psychosocial stages

Erik Erikson described eight stages. At each stage a person faces a "crisis" (conflict); resolving it well gives a strength.

| Stage | Age (approx.) | Conflict | Strength if resolved |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Infancy (0–1½) | Trust vs mistrust | Hope |
| 2 | Early childhood (1½–3) | Autonomy vs shame and doubt | Will |
| 3 | Preschool (3–5) | Initiative vs guilt | Purpose |
| 4 | School age (5–12) | Industry vs inferiority | Competence |
| 5 | Adolescence (12–18) | Identity vs role confusion | Fidelity |
| 6 | Young adulthood (18–40) | Intimacy vs isolation | Love |
| 7 | Middle adulthood (40–65) | Generativity vs stagnation | Care |
| 8 | Old age (65+) | Integrity vs despair | Wisdom |

Use in healthcare: an adolescent struggling with identity may hide an illness from friends and stop treatment; an older person facing despair may lose interest in self-care.

### Piaget's stages of cognitive development

| Stage | Age | Thinking | Counselling tip |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sensorimotor | 0–2 years | Learns through senses and actions; object permanence develops | Comfort, touch; talk to parent |
| Preoperational | 2–7 years | Language and symbols; egocentric; magical thinking (may believe illness is a punishment) | Simple, concrete words; dolls and pictures |
| Concrete operational | 7–11 years | Logical about concrete things | Show and explain with real objects |
| Formal operational | 11+ years | Abstract and hypothetical thinking | Can discuss reasons, risks and future effects |

## 2.2 Personality theories and patient interaction styles

**Personality** is the unique and relatively stable pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving that makes one person different from another.

### Type theories

- **Hippocrates (humours)** — four temperaments: sanguine (cheerful), choleric (irritable), melancholic (sad), phlegmatic (calm). Historical interest only.
- **Sheldon** — linked body build to temperament: endomorph (round, relaxed), mesomorph (muscular, active), ectomorph (thin, sensitive). Not supported by evidence.
- **Jung** — introvert (turned inwards, reserved) and extrovert (outgoing, social).
- **Type A and Type B behaviour** — Type A is competitive, impatient, hurried and easily angered; Type B is relaxed and easy-going. Hostility, a part of Type A, has been linked with heart disease.

### Trait theories

A **trait** is a lasting tendency to behave in a certain way. Gordon Allport, Raymond Cattell (16 personality factors) and Hans Eysenck (extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism) developed trait approaches. The most widely used today is the **Big Five (OCEAN)** model:

| Trait | High scorer | Health relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Openness | Curious, imaginative | More willing to try new treatments |
| Conscientiousness | Organised, dependable | Better adherence and health habits |
| Extraversion | Sociable, energetic | More social support |
| Agreeableness | Cooperative, trusting | Easier relationships with staff |
| Neuroticism | Anxious, moody | More symptom reporting, more distress |

### Psychoanalytic theory (Sigmund Freud)

Personality has three parts:

| Part | Works on | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Id | Pleasure principle | Inborn drives; wants instant satisfaction; unconscious |
| Ego | Reality principle | Balances the id's wishes with reality |
| Superego | Morality principle | Conscience, rules learned from parents and society |

When the ego is threatened by anxiety it uses **defence mechanisms** (unconscious ways of protecting the self). Many appear when people fall ill:

| Defence mechanism | Meaning | Example in a patient |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Denial | Refusing to accept reality | "I don't have diabetes; the test was wrong" |
| Repression | Pushing painful memories out of awareness | Not remembering a frightening diagnosis discussion |
| Projection | Putting one's own feelings onto others | Blaming the pharmacist for being "rude" when the patient is angry |
| Displacement | Redirecting feelings to a safer target | Shouting at the counter staff after bad news from the doctor |
| Rationalisation | Giving a logical-sounding excuse | "I stopped the tablets because they are expensive anyway" |
| Regression | Returning to childlike behaviour | An adult becoming very dependent in hospital |
| Intellectualisation | Focusing on facts to avoid feelings | Asking only technical questions about cancer |
| Sublimation | Turning an unacceptable urge into an acceptable activity | Channelling anger into sport |

Freud also described psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital); these are mainly of historical interest.

### Humanistic theories

- **Carl Rogers** — people grow when given **unconditional positive regard**, empathy and genuineness. These three conditions are the basis of patient-centred counselling.
- **Abraham Maslow** — people strive towards self-actualisation (see Unit 1).

### Social–cognitive theory

**Albert Bandura** — behaviour, personal factors and environment influence each other (reciprocal determinism). **Self-efficacy**, a person's belief that they can do a task, strongly predicts health behaviour. **Locus of control** (Julian Rotter): people with an **internal** locus believe their actions shape their health; those with an **external** locus believe luck, fate or doctors decide it.

### Patient interaction styles

Patients behave in recognisable ways. The pharmacist adapts to each.

| Patient style | What it looks like | How to respond |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anxious | Many questions, worry, repeats concerns | Calm tone, simple facts, reassurance, written notes |
| Angry or aggressive | Raised voice, blaming | Stay calm, listen, acknowledge feelings, do not argue, move to a private area |
| Silent or withdrawn | Few words, avoids eye contact | Open questions, patience, gentle encouragement |
| Talkative | Wanders off topic | Polite summarising, bring back to the point |
| Demanding | Insists on a particular medicine | Explain reasons, offer options, keep to rules |
| Dependent | Wants to be told everything | Support but build confidence for self-care |
| Well-informed or internet-informed | Brings printed information | Respect their effort, correct errors gently |

**Models of the professional–patient relationship (Szasz and Hollender)**

| Model | Professional | Patient | Suited to |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Activity–passivity | Does something to the patient | Passive recipient | Emergency, unconscious patient |
| Guidance–cooperation | Advises | Cooperates and follows | Acute illness |
| Mutual participation | Helps the patient help themselves | Active partner | Chronic illness, long-term therapy |

Modern pharmacy practice favours **mutual participation** and shared decision-making.

## 2.3 Psychological factors affecting illness perception and recovery

**Illness perception** is the patient's own picture of their illness. It may differ from the medical picture and it guides what the patient does. Leventhal's **common-sense model** says people build this picture from five ideas:

| Component | Question the patient asks | Example belief |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | What is it? What are the symptoms? | "High blood pressure has no symptoms, so I'm not ill" |
| Cause | What caused it? | "Tension caused it" |
| Timeline | How long will it last? | "It will go once I feel better" |
| Consequences | How serious is it? | "It will not affect my life" |
| Control / cure | Can it be controlled? | "Tablets can't help; it's fate" |

If a patient believes hypertension is short-term and caused by tension, they may stop treatment when they feel relaxed.

**Other psychological factors that affect recovery**

- **Beliefs about medicines** — necessity (I need them) versus concerns (they are harmful or addictive).
- **Self-efficacy and locus of control** — confident, internally controlled patients take part actively in recovery.
- **Optimism** — optimistic patients usually cope better and recover faster after surgery.
- **Mood** — depression and anxiety slow recovery, reduce adherence and increase pain.
- **Stress** — affects immunity, wound healing and blood pressure.
- **Social support** — family and friends help with practical tasks and emotional comfort.
- **Sick role (Talcott Parsons)** — society excuses the sick person from normal duties, but expects them to want to get well and seek competent help. Some people stay in the sick role for its benefits (**secondary gain**).
- **Health literacy** — the ability to obtain, understand and use health information.
- **Culture and religion** — beliefs about causes of illness and acceptable treatments.
- **Previous experience** — earlier bad side effects make patients fearful.

## 2.4 Common psychological disorders in healthcare

Pharmacists do not diagnose mental illness, but they should recognise warning signs, respond kindly and refer. Classification systems in use are the ICD-11 (World Health Organization) and the DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association).

### Anxiety disorders

**Anxiety** is a feeling of worry, tension and fear about something that may happen. A little anxiety is normal and useful; it becomes a disorder when it is excessive, lasting and interferes with life.

| Type | Main features |
| --- | --- |
| Generalised anxiety disorder | Persistent, excessive worry about many things on most days, with restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, muscle tension and poor sleep |
| Panic disorder | Repeated sudden attacks of intense fear with palpitations, breathlessness, chest pain, sweating, trembling, fear of dying |
| Phobias | Intense, irrational fear of a specific object or situation (needles, blood, crowded places) |
| Social anxiety disorder | Fear of being judged or embarrassed in social situations |
| Obsessive–compulsive disorder | Unwanted repeated thoughts and repeated actions to reduce anxiety (now classed separately in DSM-5) |
| Post-traumatic stress disorder | Flashbacks, nightmares and avoidance after a traumatic event |

Physical symptoms of anxiety can mimic heart, lung and stomach disease, so anxious patients often present with bodily complaints.

### Depression

**Depression** is a mood disorder with persistent low mood or loss of interest and pleasure, lasting at least two weeks and affecting daily life.

Common symptoms:

- Low mood, sadness, hopelessness
- Loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)
- Fatigue and low energy
- Sleep change (too little or too much)
- Appetite and weight change
- Poor concentration and slowness
- Feelings of guilt or worthlessness
- Thoughts of death or suicide

Depression is common in people with chronic diseases (diabetes, heart disease, cancer, stroke). It reduces adherence and worsens outcomes. **Any mention of suicide must be taken seriously**: stay with the person, listen without judging, and connect them urgently with a doctor, family member or crisis helpline.

### Somatisation (somatic symptom disorders)

**Somatisation** is the expression of psychological distress as physical symptoms. In somatic symptom disorder the person has one or more distressing bodily symptoms (pain, fatigue, gastric upset) along with excessive thoughts, worry and time spent on them. Tests usually do not explain the symptoms fully, though the suffering is real. Related conditions include illness anxiety disorder (fear of having a serious disease) and conversion (functional neurological) disorder.

How to respond: never say "it is all in your mind"; acknowledge the symptoms are real and distressing, avoid unnecessary repeated medicines, encourage regular follow-up with one doctor and attention to stress.

## 2.5 Coping strategies, resilience and stress management

### Stress

**Stress** is the response of body and mind to demands (stressors) that a person sees as challenging or threatening. Stressors include illness, hospital admission, bereavement, exams, money and work problems, and daily hassles.

- **Eustress** — positive, motivating stress.
- **Distress** — harmful, overwhelming stress.

**General adaptation syndrome (Hans Selye)**

```text
Stressor
   |
ALARM ----------> RESISTANCE ----------> EXHAUSTION
fight-or-flight   body adapts, stays       resources used up;
adrenaline,       on guard; cortisol       illness, fatigue,
heart rate up     raised                   lowered immunity
```

**Transactional model (Lazarus and Folkman)** — stress depends on appraisal. **Primary appraisal**: is this a threat? **Secondary appraisal**: do I have the resources to cope? Stress arises when demands seem greater than resources.

Effects of long-term stress: high blood pressure, heart disease, weakened immunity, gastric problems, headache, poor sleep, anxiety, depression, and unhealthy habits such as smoking and overeating.

### Coping strategies

**Coping** is what a person thinks and does to manage stress.

| Type | Aim | Examples | When useful |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Problem-focused | Change the situation | Seeking information, making a plan, taking medicines correctly, asking for help | When the situation can be changed |
| Emotion-focused | Manage the feelings | Talking to friends, relaxation, prayer, positive reframing, acceptance | When the situation cannot be changed (incurable illness, bereavement) |
| Avoidant (maladaptive) | Escape the problem | Denial, alcohol, smoking, withdrawal, skipping appointments | Brief relief, but harmful long-term |

### Resilience

**Resilience** is the ability to adapt well and bounce back after stress, trauma or illness. Factors that build resilience:

- Supportive relationships
- Optimism and realistic hope
- Self-efficacy and problem-solving skills
- Finding meaning or purpose
- Healthy routines (sleep, exercise, food)
- Flexibility in thinking

### Stress management techniques

1. **Deep (diaphragmatic) breathing** — slow breaths from the abdomen calm the body.
2. **Progressive muscle relaxation** (Edmund Jacobson) — tensing and then relaxing muscle groups one by one.
3. **Mindfulness and meditation** — paying attention to the present moment without judging.
4. **Yoga** — combines posture, breathing and meditation.
5. **Regular physical exercise** — reduces tension and improves mood.
6. **Adequate sleep and balanced diet.**
7. **Time management** — prioritising, breaking tasks into steps, avoiding last-minute rush.
8. **Social support** — talking to trusted people.
9. **Cognitive restructuring** — replacing unhelpful thoughts ("I will never cope") with realistic ones.
10. **Hobbies and leisure.**
11. **Limiting caffeine, alcohol and tobacco**, which worsen stress over time.
12. **Professional help** — counselling or psychotherapy when stress is severe.

## Remember

- Erikson: 8 psychosocial conflicts from trust vs mistrust to integrity vs despair; Piaget: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, formal operational.
- Big Five = OCEAN; conscientiousness links with better adherence, neuroticism with more distress.
- Freud: id (pleasure), ego (reality), superego (morality); denial and displacement are common in ill patients.
- Rogers: unconditional positive regard, empathy, genuineness.
- Common-sense model: identity, cause, timeline, consequences, control.
- Anxiety, depression (2 weeks or more of low mood or anhedonia) and somatisation are the three disorders named in the syllabus; take suicide talk seriously and refer.
- Selye: alarm → resistance → exhaustion. Lazarus: primary and secondary appraisal.
- Coping: problem-focused, emotion-focused, avoidant. Resilience = bouncing back.
