# Unit 1: Introduction to psychology in healthcare

A pharmacist does not hand medicines to a body; a pharmacist hands them to a person who sees, hears, remembers, worries and decides. Whether a patient notices a warning label, remembers how to use an inhaler or keeps taking tablets after the symptoms go depends on psychology. This unit gives the basic ideas: what psychology is, which branches matter in healthcare, how we sense and perceive, how we learn and remember, and what drives our emotions and actions.

## 1.1 Definition, scope and relevance of psychology

**Psychology** is the scientific study of behaviour and mental processes.

- **Behaviour** — anything a person does that can be observed: speaking, walking, taking a tablet, skipping a dose.
- **Mental processes** — inner experiences that are inferred, not seen directly: thinking, feeling, remembering, deciding, perceiving pain.

The word comes from the Greek *psyche* (mind or soul) and *logos* (study). Psychology became a separate science when Wilhelm Wundt opened the first psychology laboratory at Leipzig, Germany, in 1879.

**Goals of psychology**

| Goal | Meaning | Healthcare example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Describe | Say what happens | Patients with chronic illness often miss doses |
| Explain | Say why it happens | They forget, fear side effects, or feel well |
| Predict | Say when it will happen | Patients on several daily doses miss more |
| Control (change) | Change behaviour for the better | Reminder packs and counselling improve adherence |

**Scope of psychology in health sciences**

- Understanding how people feel and react when they fall ill, are admitted or receive a diagnosis.
- Explaining health behaviours: smoking, diet, exercise, self-medication, taking or not taking medicines.
- Improving communication between patients and health professionals.
- Managing pain, stress and anxiety linked to illness and treatment.
- Identifying common mental health problems (anxiety, depression) early and referring patients.
- Designing health education and public health campaigns.
- Supporting the well-being of health workers themselves (stress, burnout).

**Why a pharmacist needs psychology**

1. The pharmacist is often the most easily reached health professional; people walk in without an appointment.
2. Counselling works only if the patient attends, understands, remembers and is motivated — all psychological processes.
3. Non-adherence to medicines is mainly a behavioural problem, not a pharmacological one.
4. Patients bring fear, embarrassment and beliefs to the counter; recognising these makes advice accepted.
5. Pharmacists meet people with depression, anxiety, substance use and suicidal thoughts and must respond correctly and refer.

**Health and the biopsychosocial model**

The World Health Organization defines health as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity. The older **biomedical model** explains illness only through biology (germs, genes, chemistry). The **biopsychosocial model**, proposed by George Engel in 1977, says that illness and recovery depend on three groups of factors acting together.

```text
               BIOLOGICAL
        genes, infection, hormones,
        drug action, organ damage
                 /      \
                /        \
   PSYCHOLOGICAL -------- SOCIAL
   beliefs, mood,         family, income,
   stress, coping,        culture, access to care,
   behaviour              social support
```

Example: two people with the same blood pressure respond differently to treatment because one believes medicines are harmful (psychological) and lives far from a pharmacy (social).

## 1.2 Branches of psychology relevant to healthcare

| Branch | What it studies | Healthcare relevance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clinical psychology | Assessment, diagnosis and treatment of mental disorders using psychological methods | Treatment of anxiety, depression, phobias; psychological testing; therapy alongside medicines |
| Health psychology | How psychological, behavioural and social factors affect physical health and illness | Adherence, lifestyle change, pain, coping with chronic disease, health promotion |
| Behavioural psychology (behaviourism) | Observable behaviour and how it is learned through reinforcement and conditioning | Habit formation, smoking cessation, rewarding healthy behaviour, reminder systems |
| Developmental psychology | Changes in body, thinking, emotion and social life from conception to old age | Communicating with children, adolescents and the elderly; age-appropriate counselling |

Other branches worth knowing for MCQs:

- **Counselling psychology** — helps people with everyday problems of adjustment.
- **Social psychology** — how others influence our thoughts and actions (peer pressure, attitudes, stigma).
- **Cognitive psychology** — memory, attention, thinking, problem solving.
- **Neuropsychology / biopsychology** — links between brain and behaviour.
- **Community psychology** — prevention and well-being at the level of a population.

**Difference between clinical and health psychology**

| Clinical psychology | Health psychology |
| --- | --- |
| Focus on mental disorders | Focus on physical health and illness |
| Diagnosis and therapy of individuals | Prevention, adherence, coping, lifestyle |
| Works mostly in mental health settings | Works in hospitals, public health, community |

## 1.3 Sensation, perception and attention in clinical assessment

### Sensation

**Sensation** is the process by which sense organs (receptors) detect physical energy from the environment — light, sound, pressure, temperature, chemicals — and send signals to the brain. Converting physical energy into nerve impulses is called **transduction**.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Absolute threshold | The smallest amount of a stimulus a person can detect half the time |
| Difference threshold (just noticeable difference) | The smallest change in a stimulus that can be detected |
| Weber's law | The just noticeable difference is a constant proportion of the original stimulus, not a fixed amount |
| Sensory adaptation | Reduced sensitivity to a constant, unchanging stimulus (a smell stops being noticed) |

### Perception

**Perception** is the process of organising and interpreting sensations so that they have meaning. Sensation is "receiving"; perception is "understanding". Two people can receive the same sensation and perceive it differently.

- **Bottom-up processing** — perception built from the raw details of the stimulus.
- **Top-down processing** — perception guided by expectations, past experience and knowledge (a familiar drug name is "read" correctly even if it is badly written — or wrongly, if a similar-looking name is expected).

**Gestalt principles of perceptual organisation** (the whole is more than the sum of its parts):

| Principle | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Figure–ground | We separate an object (figure) from its background |
| Proximity | Things close together are seen as a group |
| Similarity | Similar things are grouped together |
| Closure | We fill gaps to see a complete figure |
| Continuity | We see smooth continuous lines rather than broken ones |

**Perceptual constancy** — we see objects as having a stable size, shape and colour even when the image on the eye changes.

**Factors influencing perception:** past experience, expectations (perceptual set), needs and motives, emotions, culture, attention, and the intensity, size, contrast and movement of the stimulus.

### Attention

**Attention** is focusing awareness on some stimuli while ignoring others.

| Type | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Selective attention | Attending to one source and filtering out others | Hearing your name in a noisy ward ("cocktail party effect") |
| Divided attention | Attending to two or more tasks at once | Dispensing while answering the phone — error-prone |
| Sustained attention (vigilance) | Keeping attention over a long time | Checking a long batch record |

Attention is limited. Patients who are anxious, in pain or distracted take in little of what they are told.

### Why these matter in clinical assessment

1. **Pain is a perception, not just a sensation.** The same injury causes different pain in different people depending on attention, mood, expectation and culture. The **gate control theory** of pain (Melzack and Wall, 1965) proposes that a "gate" in the spinal cord can increase or reduce pain signals reaching the brain, and that thoughts and emotions can open or close it. Distraction, reassurance and relaxation can reduce pain; fear and focus on pain increase it.
2. **Placebo effect** — improvement because the patient expects a treatment to work; it shows how expectation shapes perceived symptoms.
3. **Sensory impairment** — elderly patients may have weak sight and hearing; small print on labels, low light and fast speech lead to errors. Use large print, good light, face the patient and check understanding.
4. **Look-alike, sound-alike drug names** — top-down perception and divided attention cause dispensing errors; double-checking and tall-man lettering help.
5. **Symptom reporting** — anxious patients may notice and report bodily sensations more strongly; others ignore warning signs.
6. **Counselling** — give the most important points first, when attention is highest; limit the number of messages.

## 1.4 Learning and memory: reinforcement in health behaviour change

### Learning

**Learning** is a relatively permanent change in behaviour or knowledge that results from experience.

**1. Classical conditioning (Ivan Pavlov)** — learning by association. A neutral stimulus, paired repeatedly with a stimulus that naturally causes a response, comes to cause that response by itself.

```text
Before:  Food (unconditioned stimulus) -> salivation (unconditioned response)
During:  Bell + food, repeated
After:   Bell alone (conditioned stimulus) -> salivation (conditioned response)
```

Healthcare examples: a patient who felt sick during chemotherapy begins to feel nauseated on seeing the hospital (anticipatory nausea); a child cries at the sight of a white coat after a painful injection.

Key terms: **extinction** (response fades when the pairing stops), **generalisation** (similar stimuli also trigger the response), **discrimination** (responding only to the specific stimulus).

**2. Operant (instrumental) conditioning (B. F. Skinner)** — learning from the consequences of behaviour. Behaviour followed by a pleasant result is repeated; behaviour followed by an unpleasant result is reduced.

| Consequence | What happens | Effect on behaviour | Health example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Positive reinforcement | Something pleasant is added | Increases | Praise from the pharmacist when blood sugar readings improve |
| Negative reinforcement | Something unpleasant is removed | Increases | Taking a tablet removes a headache, so tablets are taken again |
| Positive punishment | Something unpleasant is added | Decreases | A fine for smoking in a public place |
| Negative punishment | Something pleasant is removed | Decreases | Loss of a privilege |

Note: negative reinforcement is **not** punishment; it strengthens behaviour.

**Schedules of reinforcement:** continuous (every time — fast learning, fast extinction) and partial — fixed ratio, variable ratio, fixed interval, variable interval. Behaviour learned on a variable schedule is the hardest to extinguish.

**Shaping** — rewarding small steps towards a goal (walking 5 minutes, then 10, then 30).

**3. Observational (social) learning (Albert Bandura)** — learning by watching others (models) and the results of their actions. The Bobo doll studies showed children copying aggression they had observed. Steps: attention → retention → reproduction → motivation. Health messages using respected role models, and patients learning inhaler technique by watching a demonstration, use this.

**4. Cognitive learning** — insight and latent learning; learning involves thinking and understanding, not only conditioning.

### Reinforcement in health behaviour change

- Unhealthy behaviours (smoking, overeating) often give an **immediate reward** while the harm comes much later; healthy behaviours have delayed rewards. This is why they are hard to change.
- Effective programmes give **immediate, small, frequent rewards** for the healthy behaviour: praise, progress charts, tokens, self-reward.
- **Cues and habits** — linking a dose to a daily routine (brushing teeth, breakfast) uses association to build a habit.
- **Self-monitoring** — writing down doses, steps or readings gives feedback that reinforces progress.
- **Avoid relying on fear or punishment alone**; it may cause avoidance of the clinic instead of behaviour change.

### Memory

**Memory** is the ability to encode, store and retrieve information.

| Stage | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Encoding | Changing information into a form that can be stored |
| Storage | Keeping it over time |
| Retrieval | Bringing it back when needed (recall or recognition) |

**Multi-store model (Atkinson and Shiffrin, 1968)**

```text
Stimulus -> SENSORY MEMORY -> (attention) -> SHORT-TERM MEMORY -> (rehearsal, meaning) -> LONG-TERM MEMORY
            under 1-2 s                       limited, about 7 +/- 2 items,              very large,
                                              lasts seconds without rehearsal            long lasting
                                                       ^                                       |
                                                       |____________ retrieval ________________|
```

**Types of long-term memory:** explicit (declarative) — episodic (events) and semantic (facts); implicit — procedural (skills such as using an inhaler) and conditioning.

**Forgetting:** Hermann Ebbinghaus showed that most forgetting happens soon after learning (forgetting curve). Causes include decay, interference (old learning blocks new and vice versa), retrieval failure and anxiety.

**Memory and patient counselling** — patients forget a large part of what they are told, and forget more when anxious or given many points. To improve recall:

1. Give the most important instruction first (primacy effect) and repeat it at the end (recency effect).
2. Limit the number of points; group them (chunking).
3. Use simple words, concrete examples and demonstrations.
4. Give written information, pictograms and labelled packs.
5. Ask the patient to repeat back (teach-back).
6. Link doses to daily routines and use reminders, alarms and pill organisers.

## 1.5 Emotion and motivation: theories and implications

### Emotion

**Emotion** is a complex state with three parts: physiological arousal (heart rate, sweating), expressive behaviour (face, voice, posture) and conscious experience (the feeling, such as fear or joy). Basic emotions commonly listed include happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise and disgust.

| Theory | Key idea | Sequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| James–Lange | The feeling is our awareness of body changes | Event → body arousal → emotion ("I feel afraid because I tremble") |
| Cannon–Bard | Body arousal and the feeling happen at the same time, triggered by the thalamus | Event → arousal and emotion together |
| Schachter–Singer (two-factor) | Emotion = arousal + the label (cognitive appraisal) we give it | Event → arousal → interpretation → emotion |
| Lazarus (cognitive appraisal) | Our appraisal of the event comes first and decides the emotion | Event → appraisal → emotion and arousal |

**Implications in health contexts**

- Fear and anxiety reduce attention and memory; calm the patient before giving instructions.
- Strong emotion can mimic or worsen physical symptoms (palpitations, breathlessness, gastric upset).
- How a patient **appraises** a diagnosis ("a disaster" vs "a challenge I can manage") changes coping; reframing helps.
- Health workers' own emotions (irritation, fatigue) show in voice and face and affect the patient.

### Motivation

**Motivation** is the process that starts, directs and sustains goal-directed behaviour. **Motives** come from needs (hunger, safety), drives and goals.

| Theory | Key idea | Health implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Instinct theory | Behaviour is driven by inborn patterns | Of limited use; explains little of health behaviour |
| Drive-reduction theory (Clark Hull) | A need creates a drive (tension) that pushes us to act and reduce it, restoring homeostasis | People act to relieve discomfort — they seek treatment for pain but may stop once relief comes |
| Arousal theory | We seek an optimal level of arousal; performance is best at moderate arousal (Yerkes–Dodson law) | Some anxiety prompts action; too much paralyses the patient |
| Incentive theory | External rewards pull behaviour | Rewards, praise, insurance benefits encourage check-ups |
| Maslow's hierarchy of needs | Needs are arranged in levels; higher needs act when lower ones are met | A patient worried about food or safety cannot focus on long-term prevention |
| Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) | Lasting motivation needs autonomy, competence and relatedness | Involve patients in decisions; build confidence; support them |

**Maslow's hierarchy (from base to top)**

```text
                  /\
                 /  \        Self-actualisation (reaching one's potential)
                /----\
               /      \      Esteem (respect, achievement)
              /--------\
             /          \    Love and belonging (family, friends)
            /------------\
           /              \  Safety (security, health, income)
          /----------------\
         /                  \ Physiological (food, water, sleep, air)
        /--------------------\
```

**Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation**

| Intrinsic | Extrinsic |
| --- | --- |
| From inside: interest, satisfaction, personal values | From outside: rewards, praise, avoiding penalties |
| Lasts longer | Works quickly but may fade when rewards stop |
| "I walk because I enjoy feeling fit" | "I walk because the doctor told me to" |

**Using motivation in practice:** find out what matters to the patient (family, work, independence) and link the treatment to it; set small achievable goals; give feedback on progress; respect the patient's choice.

## Remember

- Psychology = scientific study of behaviour and mental processes; Wundt, Leipzig, 1879.
- Biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977): illness has biological, psychological and social causes.
- Health psychology = physical health and behaviour; clinical psychology = mental disorders.
- Sensation detects; perception interprets; attention selects. Pain is a perception — gate control theory.
- Classical conditioning = learning by association (Pavlov); operant = learning by consequences (Skinner); observational = learning by watching (Bandura).
- Negative reinforcement removes something unpleasant and **increases** behaviour; it is not punishment.
- Memory: encoding → storage → retrieval; short-term holds about 7 ± 2 items; give key instructions first, repeat, write down, use teach-back.
- Emotion theories: James–Lange, Cannon–Bard, Schachter–Singer, Lazarus. Motivation: drive-reduction, arousal (Yerkes–Dodson), incentive, Maslow, self-determination.
