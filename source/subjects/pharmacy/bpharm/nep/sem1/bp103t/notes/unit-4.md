# Unit 4: Professional communication in healthcare settings

In practice a pharmacist talks to patients, worried relatives, doctors, nurses and administrators, often on the same morning. Some conversations are routine; some carry bad news or strong emotions; all must respect confidentiality and consent, and many must be written down accurately. This unit covers communication with patients, caregivers and teams, breaking difficult news, handling emotional situations, the legal and ethical rules, written records, and digital communication tools.

## 4.1 Communication with patients

**Patient-centred communication** puts the patient's needs, values and preferences at the centre. The professional explains, but also listens and involves the patient in decisions.

**Stages of a pharmacist–patient counselling session**

```text
1. OPENING        greet, introduce yourself, confirm identity, explain purpose, ensure privacy
       |
2. GATHERING      what does the patient know? (three prime questions), history, allergies,
       |          other medicines, concerns
3. EXPLAINING     name and purpose of medicine, how and when to take it, what to expect,
       |          common side effects and what to do, storage, missed dose, interactions
4. CHECKING       teach-back: "Tell me how you will take this, so I know I explained it well"
       |
5. CLOSING        summary, written information, invite questions, how to contact the pharmacy
```

**Good practices**

- Use the patient's name and introduce yourself.
- Sit or stand at the patient's level; maintain comfortable eye contact.
- Speak slowly, in plain words, in the patient's language.
- Give the most important points first; limit to a few key messages.
- Demonstrate devices (inhaler, insulin pen, eye drops) and ask the patient to show you.
- Ask about the patient's concerns and expectations.
- Respect the patient's right to make choices.
- Provide written or pictorial information to take home.

**Teach-back method** — the patient explains in their own words what they have been told. If it is wrong, the professional explains again differently and rechecks. It tests the clarity of the explanation, not the patient's intelligence.

## 4.2 Communication with caregivers

**Caregivers** (carers) are family members or others who look after a patient — the parent of a child, the son of an elderly person, the spouse of a stroke patient.

- Caregivers often give medicines, notice side effects and attend appointments, so they need clear instructions too.
- **Respect the patient's confidentiality and autonomy**: share information with a carer only with the patient's consent, except when the patient lacks capacity (young child, unconscious, severe dementia), when the legal guardian or authorised representative is informed.
- Include the patient in the conversation even when a carer is present; do not talk "over" the patient.
- Recognise caregiver stress (burden, exhaustion, guilt); offer support and information about help available.
- Give practical tools: dosing charts, pill organisers, a list of warning signs and whom to call.

## 4.3 Communication within interdisciplinary teams

Healthcare is delivered by teams: doctors, pharmacists, nurses, dieticians, physiotherapists, laboratory staff, social workers. Most serious medication errors involve a breakdown in team communication — illegible orders, verbal orders misheard, incomplete handover.

**Principles of good team communication**

- Know and respect each member's role and expertise.
- Speak up about safety concerns, whatever your position in the hierarchy.
- Be clear, specific and brief; give facts and a recommendation.
- Confirm that the message was received correctly.
- Document what was agreed.

**SBAR — a structured tool** for passing information, for example when a pharmacist phones a doctor about a problem prescription:

| Step | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S – Situation | What is happening now | "I am calling about Mr Rao in bed 12; the prescription has two drugs that interact." |
| B – Background | Relevant history | "He was admitted with pneumonia and is on long-term therapy for epilepsy." |
| A – Assessment | What you think the problem is | "The new antibiotic may raise the level of his anti-epileptic medicine." |
| R – Recommendation | What you suggest | "Please consider an alternative antibiotic, or we can monitor drug levels." |

Variants: **ISBAR** adds **I – Identify** yourself and the patient at the start.

**Other safety techniques**

- **Read-back / repeat-back** — the receiver repeats a verbal or telephone order; the sender confirms it. Spell out drug names and say numbers digit by digit ("one-five, fifteen").
- **Closed-loop communication** — sender gives a message → receiver repeats it → sender confirms → task done and reported.
- **Structured handover** at shift change, using a checklist.
- **Avoid error-prone abbreviations** (such as "U" for units or "µg" written badly) and trailing zeros ("1.0 mg" may be read as 10 mg).
- **Assertive communication** — express concerns clearly and respectfully: "I am concerned this dose is higher than usual; can we check it together?"

**Communication styles**

| Style | Features | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive | Avoids conflict, does not express needs | Concerns go unheard; errors continue |
| Aggressive | Blaming, loud, disrespectful | Others become defensive |
| Passive-aggressive | Indirect resentment, sarcasm | Damages trust |
| Assertive | Clear, honest, respectful, firm | Best for teamwork and safety |

## 4.4 Delivering difficult news

**Bad or difficult news** is any information that seriously and negatively changes a person's view of their future — a diagnosis of cancer or HIV, a serious side effect, the need for lifelong treatment, an error that affected the patient, or a death. Diagnoses are given by the doctor, but pharmacists often answer the questions that follow, explain treatment with serious consequences, or disclose errors.

**SPIKES protocol** (Baile and Buckman) — a six-step approach:

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| S – Setting up | Private room, sit down, switch off phone, enough time, invite a support person if the patient wishes, tissues available |
| P – Perception | Find out what the patient already knows: "What have you been told so far?" |
| I – Invitation | Ask how much the patient wants to know: "Would you like me to explain the details?" |
| K – Knowledge | Give a warning shot ("I'm afraid I have some difficult news"), then information in small pieces, plain words, no jargon; check understanding |
| E – Emotions (with Empathy) | Notice the emotion, name it, allow silence, respond with empathy: "I can see this is a shock" |
| S – Strategy and Summary | Summarise, agree a plan and next steps, offer support and follow-up |

**Do's and don'ts**

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Be honest and kind | Give news in a corridor or over a crowded counter |
| Allow silence and tears | Use jargon or euphemisms that confuse |
| Offer hope that is realistic (symptom control, support) | Give false reassurance ("Everything will be fine") |
| Check what was understood | Rush or overload with information |
| Arrange follow-up | Leave the patient alone without a plan |

## 4.5 Handling emotionally charged situations

Patients and relatives may be angry, frightened, grieving or distressed — because of illness, waiting, costs, a medicine being out of stock, or an error.

**De-escalation steps for an angry person**

1. **Stay calm** — keep your voice low and steady; control your own reaction.
2. **Ensure safety** — keep a safe distance; do not block exits; call for help if there is any risk of violence.
3. **Move to privacy** if possible.
4. **Listen fully** without interrupting.
5. **Acknowledge the feeling** — "I can see you're upset that you had to wait so long."
6. **Apologise for the experience** where appropriate, without blaming others.
7. **Find out what is needed** and explain what you can do; offer choices.
8. **Agree on a solution** and follow through; involve a senior if needed.
9. **Document** the incident.

**Responding to other emotions**

- **Anxiety** — reassure with facts, explain step by step, give written information.
- **Grief** — allow silence, show compassion, avoid clichés; practical help with arrangements.
- **Crying** — offer tissues and time; do not rush to stop the tears.
- **Denial** — do not force acceptance at once; give information gradually.
- **Disclosure of an error** — be open and honest, apologise, explain what happened and what will be done to put it right and prevent recurrence; report as per policy.

## 4.6 Legal and ethical issues in health communication

### Ethical principles

| Principle | Meaning | In communication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Autonomy | Respect the person's right to decide | Give full information; respect refusal |
| Beneficence | Act in the patient's best interest | Give advice that helps |
| Non-maleficence | Do no harm | Avoid misleading or frightening information |
| Justice | Fairness and equal treatment | Same quality of communication for every patient |
| Veracity | Truthfulness | Do not deceive the patient |
| Fidelity | Keeping promises and loyalty | Keep confidences, follow up as promised |

### Confidentiality

**Confidentiality** is the duty not to disclose personal health information that a patient shares with a professional, without the patient's permission.

**Why it matters:** patients disclose sensitive information (sexual health, mental illness, substance use) only if they trust it will be protected. Breach damages trust and can cause discrimination.

**Good practice**

- Discuss health matters in a private area, in a low voice.
- Do not discuss patients with friends, family or on social media — even without names, if they could be identified.
- Keep records locked or password-protected; log out of computers; do not leave prescriptions in view.
- Share information only with those directly involved in care, on a need-to-know basis.
- Confirm identity before giving information on the telephone.

**When disclosure may be allowed without consent** (limited and only as much as necessary): when required by law or a court order; when there is a serious risk of harm to the patient or others; mandatory reporting such as notifiable diseases or suspected child sexual abuse (under the POCSO Act); and when the patient lacks capacity and information must go to the legal guardian. Always follow local law and institutional policy and seek senior advice.

**Relevant Indian provisions (outline):** the Pharmacy Practice Regulations, 2015 of the Pharmacy Council of India describe the professional conduct expected of pharmacists, including respect for patient confidentiality; the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 gives persons with mental illness the right to confidentiality; and the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 governs the processing of digital personal data.

### Consent

**Consent** is the permission a patient gives for a procedure, treatment or sharing of information. **Informed consent** requires:

1. **Capacity** — the person can understand, retain and weigh the information and communicate a decision.
2. **Disclosure** — information about the nature, purpose, benefits, risks and alternatives, including doing nothing.
3. **Understanding** — the person has understood it (check, do not assume).
4. **Voluntariness** — given freely, without pressure or coercion.
5. **Decision** — the person agrees (or refuses) and may withdraw at any time.

| Type of consent | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Implied | Patient holds out an arm for a blood pressure check |
| Verbal (expressed orally) | Patient agrees to a medication review |
| Written | Surgery, clinical trials, high-risk procedures |

**Special situations:** minors (consent usually by parent or guardian, while involving the child), emergencies (treatment necessary to save life may proceed), patients lacking capacity (decision by legal representative in the patient's best interest), and research (written informed consent under ethics committee approval, as required by the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules, 2019).

## 4.7 Writing patient records, reports and discharge summaries

### Principles of good documentation

Records are legal documents and a means of communication between professionals.

- **Accurate and factual** — record what was seen, said and done; avoid opinions and labels.
- **Complete** — include all relevant information.
- **Clear and legible** — readable handwriting or typed; standard terms only.
- **Contemporaneous** — written at the time or as soon as possible.
- **Dated, timed and signed** with name and designation.
- **Concise and objective** — "Patient states she missed four doses this week", not "Patient is careless".
- **Corrections** — draw a single line through the error so it stays readable, write the correction, initial and date it; never erase, overwrite or use correction fluid.
- **Confidential** and securely stored.
- "If it was not documented, it was not done."

### SOAP format

Widely used for clinical and pharmacist notes:

| Part | Content | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S – Subjective | What the patient reports: symptoms, history, concerns | "Feels dizzy on standing since the new tablet was started" |
| O – Objective | Measurable findings: vital signs, lab values, medication list | Blood pressure readings, current drugs |
| A – Assessment | The professional's interpretation of the problem | Possible drug-related postural hypotension |
| P – Plan | What will be done: recommendation, monitoring, education, follow-up | Discuss with prescriber; counsel to rise slowly; review in one week |

### Common records and reports in pharmacy

- **Medication history** — current and past medicines, OTC, herbal, allergies, adherence.
- **Medication reconciliation** — comparing the medicines a patient was taking with those ordered at admission, transfer and discharge, and resolving differences.
- **Pharmacist intervention record** — problem found, recommendation made, outcome.
- **Adverse drug reaction report** — suspected reactions reported on the Suspected Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Form to the Pharmacovigilance Programme of India.
- **Incident or medication error report.**
- **Counselling record.**

### Discharge summary

A **discharge summary** is a written account of a patient's hospital stay, given to the patient and the next care provider so care continues safely.

**Usual contents**

1. Patient details (name, age, sex, hospital number, address)
2. Dates of admission and discharge; name of the consultant and department
3. Diagnosis (final and secondary)
4. Reason for admission and brief history
5. Key examination findings and investigation results
6. Treatment given (medicines, procedures, surgery)
7. Course in hospital and condition at discharge
8. **Discharge medicines** — name, strength, how to take, duration, with changes from pre-admission medicines clearly stated (started, stopped, changed)
9. Allergies and adverse reactions
10. Advice: diet, activity, wound care, warning signs that need urgent return
11. Follow-up appointments and pending results
12. Name, signature and contact of the responsible doctor

The pharmacist's role: reconcile discharge medicines, check for errors, and counsel the patient and carer on the new regimen.

### Writing reports

A professional report has a title, introduction (purpose), method or background, findings, conclusions, recommendations, and the author's name and date. Use headings, short paragraphs, tables where helpful, and neutral factual language.

## 4.8 Use of technology and digital communication tools in healthcare

| Tool | Use | Points to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electronic health records (EHR) | Shared, legible, searchable patient records | Data entry errors, privacy, log out after use |
| Electronic prescribing | Removes handwriting errors, alerts for interactions and allergies | Alert fatigue, wrong selection from drop-down lists |
| Hospital information systems | Link pharmacy, laboratory, wards and billing | Downtime procedures needed |
| Telehealth and telepharmacy | Remote consultation and counselling | Identity check, privacy, documentation |
| Mobile health apps and SMS reminders | Adherence reminders, health education | Reliability of apps, data security |
| Email and messaging | Quick team communication | Professional tone; avoid sending identifiable data on unsecured platforms |
| Social media | Public health messages, professional networking | Never post patient information; keep professional boundaries; avoid spreading unverified claims |
| Clinical decision-support and drug databases | Check doses, interactions | Use as an aid to judgement, not a replacement |
| Barcode scanning | Right drug to the right patient | Do not override without checking |

**National digital health initiatives in India:** the **Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission** creates a digital health ecosystem in which each person can have an **ABHA (Ayushman Bharat Health Account)** number linking their health records with consent; **eSanjeevani** offers national telemedicine consultations.

**Digital etiquette for professionals**

- Use official, secure systems for patient information.
- Write messages that are clear, polite and complete; check the recipient before sending.
- Respect consent and privacy in every digital exchange.
- Keep records of advice given remotely.
- Remember that written digital messages are permanent and can be forwarded.

## Remember

- Counselling stages: open → gather → explain → check (teach-back) → close.
- Share information with carers only with consent, unless the patient lacks capacity.
- SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (ISBAR adds Identify). Read-back and closed-loop communication prevent verbal-order errors.
- Assertive is the best style for safety.
- SPIKES: Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions/Empathy, Strategy and Summary.
- De-escalation: stay calm, stay safe, listen, acknowledge, offer options, document.
- Ethics: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice (plus veracity, fidelity).
- Informed consent: capacity, disclosure, understanding, voluntariness, decision; implied, verbal, written.
- Documentation: accurate, complete, legible, contemporaneous, signed; correct with a single line. SOAP = Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan.
- Discharge summary must list discharge medicines with all changes clearly marked.
- ABDM gives the ABHA number; eSanjeevani is the national telemedicine service.
