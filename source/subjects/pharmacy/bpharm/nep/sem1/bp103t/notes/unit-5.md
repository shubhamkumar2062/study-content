# Unit 5: Health psychology and behavioural interventions

Knowing that smoking harms, or that blood pressure tablets must be taken every day, does not by itself make people stop smoking or take their tablets. Health psychology explains why people act as they do about their health and offers tested ways to help them change. This unit covers the health belief model and illness behaviour, the mind–body link and psychosomatic illness, behaviour change theories used to improve treatment adherence, psychological first aid and crisis communication, and how communication can promote mental health and reduce stigma.

## 5.1 Health behaviour

**Health behaviour** is any action a person takes to stay healthy, prevent disease or detect it early — eating well, exercising, using seat belts, getting vaccinated, going for screening.

- **Health-enhancing behaviours** — exercise, balanced diet, adequate sleep, handwashing.
- **Health-compromising behaviours** — smoking, alcohol misuse, unsafe sex, sedentary lifestyle.

Health behaviours depend on knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, habits, emotions, social influence, culture, cost and access.

## 5.2 Health belief model and illness behaviour

### Health belief model (HBM)

The **health belief model** was developed in the 1950s by social psychologists in the US Public Health Service (Hochbaum, Rosenstock and colleagues) to explain why people did not use free tuberculosis screening. It says a person's decision to take a health action depends on their beliefs.

| Component | Meaning | Example: taking blood pressure tablets regularly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perceived susceptibility | Belief about one's chance of getting the problem | "I could have a stroke" |
| Perceived severity | Belief about how serious the problem would be | "A stroke would leave me disabled" |
| Perceived benefits | Belief that the action will reduce the risk | "Tablets lower my risk" |
| Perceived barriers | Costs, side effects, inconvenience, fear | "They make me tired and cost money" |
| Cues to action | Triggers that prompt action | A relative's stroke, a reminder SMS, a doctor's advice |
| Self-efficacy (added later) | Confidence in one's ability to take the action | "I can remember to take them every morning" |

**Modifying factors:** age, gender, education, culture, personality and knowledge influence these beliefs.

```text
Modifying factors -> perceived susceptibility + severity  = perceived THREAT ----+
(age, culture,                                                                   |
 knowledge)        -> perceived benefits minus barriers                     ---> LIKELIHOOD OF ACTION
                   -> self-efficacy                                         ---+        ^
                                                    cues to action -----------------------+
```

**Using the HBM in counselling:** explain personal risk honestly (susceptibility), explain consequences without terrifying (severity), stress benefits, find and remove barriers (cost, side effects, timing), provide cues (reminders), build confidence.

**Limitations:** assumes people make rational decisions; ignores emotions, habits and social pressure; does not explain how beliefs change over time.

**Theory of planned behaviour (Icek Ajzen)** — another belief-based model: behaviour follows **intention**, which depends on **attitude** towards the behaviour, **subjective norms** (what important others expect) and **perceived behavioural control**.

### Illness behaviour

**Illness behaviour** is the way people perceive, evaluate and respond to symptoms — whether they ignore them, treat themselves, consult someone or seek medical care. The term was introduced by David Mechanic.

**Factors affecting illness behaviour (Mechanic):** how visible and recognisable the symptoms are; how serious they seem; how much they disrupt daily life; how often they occur; tolerance of others; knowledge and cultural beliefs; denial; competing needs (work, family); other explanations for the symptoms; and availability and cost of treatment.

**Stages of illness experience (Suchman)**

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Symptom experience | The person notices something is wrong and decides it means illness |
| 2. Assumption of the sick role | Seeks advice from family and friends; may self-treat |
| 3. Medical care contact | Consults a professional for diagnosis and legitimacy |
| 4. Dependent patient role | Accepts treatment and follows the professional's advice |
| 5. Recovery or rehabilitation | Gives up the sick role and returns to normal duties (or adjusts to chronic illness) |

**Sick role (Talcott Parsons)** — rights: exemption from normal duties and from blame. Obligations: wanting to get well and seeking and cooperating with competent help.

**Types of illness behaviour**

- **Appropriate** — seeking help at the right time.
- **Delay** — ignoring serious symptoms through fear, denial or cost (common in cancer and heart disease).
- **Excessive** — frequent consultations for minor symptoms, health anxiety.
- **Self-medication** — common in India; the pharmacist guides responsible self-care and refers when needed.

## 5.3 Psychosomatic illnesses and the mind–body connection

### The mind–body connection

The mind and body continually influence each other. Thoughts and emotions cause changes in the nervous, endocrine and immune systems, and physical illness affects mood and thinking.

**Pathways linking stress and emotion to the body**

```text
Stressor -> brain (appraisal) -> hypothalamus
                                    |
               +--------------------+---------------------+
               |                                          |
  Sympathetic nervous system                 HPA axis: hypothalamus -> pituitary
  -> adrenal medulla -> adrenaline,          -> adrenal cortex -> cortisol
     noradrenaline                                      |
  -> heart rate, BP, blood sugar up          -> immunity suppressed with long exposure,
                                                blood sugar up, healing slowed
```

- **Psychoneuroimmunology** studies how psychological factors affect the immune system through nerves and hormones. Long-term stress is associated with slower wound healing and greater vulnerability to infection.
- **Behavioural pathway** — stressed or depressed people may smoke more, drink, sleep poorly, eat badly and miss medicines.
- **Placebo and nocebo effects** — positive expectations can improve symptoms (placebo); negative expectations can produce side effects (nocebo). This shows why the way a medicine is explained matters.

### Psychosomatic illnesses

**Psychosomatic (psychophysiological) illnesses** are physical conditions in which psychological factors such as stress, anxiety or emotional conflict play an important role in starting or worsening the disease. The physical changes are real, not imagined.

| System | Conditions where psychological factors contribute |
| --- | --- |
| Cardiovascular | Hypertension, coronary heart disease, palpitations |
| Respiratory | Bronchial asthma (attacks triggered by emotion), hyperventilation |
| Gastrointestinal | Irritable bowel syndrome, functional dyspepsia; stress worsens peptic ulcer symptoms (though *Helicobacter pylori* is the main cause) |
| Skin | Psoriasis, eczema, urticaria, acne flares |
| Musculoskeletal | Tension headache, back pain, fibromyalgia |
| Endocrine | Poor control of diabetes under stress |
| Other | Migraine, sleep disturbance, menstrual irregularity |

**Distinguish:** psychosomatic illness = real physical disease worsened by the mind; somatic symptom disorder (Unit 2) = distressing symptoms with excessive worry, often without a full physical explanation; malingering = deliberately faking symptoms for gain.

**Management approach:** treat the physical disease, and also address stress, sleep and mood (relaxation, counselling, exercise, support). The pharmacist reinforces both.

## 5.4 Behaviour change theories in treatment adherence

### Adherence

**Adherence** is the extent to which a person's behaviour — taking medicines, following a diet, changing lifestyle — matches the recommendations agreed with a health professional. The word "adherence" is preferred over "compliance" because it implies the patient's agreement rather than obedience. The World Health Organization has stressed that poor adherence to long-term therapy is a worldwide problem.

| Type of non-adherence | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Primary | Prescription never collected |
| Unintentional | Forgetting, misunderstanding, inability to use a device |
| Intentional | Deciding to stop due to side effects, cost, feeling better or beliefs |

**WHO's five dimensions affecting adherence:** social and economic factors; health system and team factors; condition-related factors; therapy-related factors (complexity, duration, side effects); and patient-related factors (knowledge, beliefs, motivation, memory).

### Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)

**CBT** is a structured, short-term, goal-oriented psychological therapy. Aaron Beck developed cognitive therapy; Albert Ellis developed rational emotive behaviour therapy. Its central idea is that **thoughts, feelings and behaviours are linked**, so changing unhelpful thoughts and behaviours changes feelings.

```text
          THOUGHTS
     "These tablets will damage
        my kidneys"
         /        \
        /          \
   FEELINGS ------ BEHAVIOUR
   fear, worry      skips doses
```

**Ellis's ABC model:** **A**ctivating event → **B**elief about it → **C**onsequence (emotion and behaviour). Changing B changes C.

**Common thinking errors (cognitive distortions):** all-or-nothing thinking, catastrophising, overgeneralisation, jumping to conclusions, mental filter (seeing only the negative), "should" statements.

**CBT techniques**

- Identifying automatic negative thoughts
- Challenging them with evidence (cognitive restructuring)
- Behavioural activation (planning rewarding activities, useful in depression)
- Graded exposure (facing fears step by step, used in phobias)
- Problem-solving training
- Relaxation and breathing
- Homework and self-monitoring diaries

**CBT and adherence:** helps patients challenge false beliefs about medicines ("I'll get addicted to inhalers"), solve practical problems (forgetting, timing), and manage the depression and anxiety that reduce adherence. CBT is used for depression, anxiety, insomnia, chronic pain, smoking cessation and weight management. A pharmacist uses CBT-style questions in counselling; formal CBT is delivered by trained therapists.

### Transtheoretical model (TTM) — stages of change

Developed by James Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente from studies of smokers. It says people change behaviour through stages, and advice should match the stage.

| Stage | Description | What helps (pharmacist's role) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Precontemplation | Not thinking of change in the next six months; may not see a problem | Raise awareness gently; personalised information about risks; do not lecture |
| Contemplation | Thinking of change within six months; weighing pros and cons; ambivalent | Discuss benefits and barriers; build motivation; explore ambivalence |
| Preparation | Intends to act soon (within a month); may have taken small steps | Help set a quit date or plan; discuss options and support |
| Action | Has changed behaviour recently (less than six months) | Encourage, reward, manage withdrawal or side effects, prevent relapse |
| Maintenance | Has kept the change for more than six months | Support, identify high-risk situations, celebrate success |
| Termination | No temptation to return (not reached by everyone) | — |

**Relapse** to an earlier stage is common and is treated as part of the process, not a failure. The model is often drawn as a cycle or spiral.

**Processes of change** (how people move): consciousness raising, dramatic relief, self-re-evaluation, self-liberation (commitment), counter-conditioning (healthier substitutes), stimulus control (removing cues), reinforcement management (rewards), helping relationships.

**Decisional balance** (pros versus cons) and **self-efficacy** shift as people move through the stages.

### Motivational interviewing (MI)

A collaborative counselling style (William Miller and Stephen Rollnick) that strengthens a person's own motivation for change, especially useful in contemplation. Core skills, **OARS**:

- **O**pen questions — "What concerns you most about your smoking?"
- **A**ffirmations — "You've done well to cut down."
- **R**eflective listening — "You want to quit, but you're worried about the cravings."
- **S**ummarising.

MI avoids arguing; it draws out the patient's own reasons for change ("change talk").

### Practical strategies to improve adherence

1. Simplify the regimen (fewer daily doses, combination products where appropriate — decided by the prescriber).
2. Clear verbal and written instructions; teach-back.
3. Link doses to daily routines; pill organisers, blister calendar packs, alarms, SMS reminders.
4. Discuss and manage side effects.
5. Address beliefs and fears (HBM, CBT approach).
6. Match advice to the stage of change (TTM).
7. Involve family support.
8. Regular follow-up and refill reminders; praise progress (reinforcement).

## 5.5 Psychological first aid and crisis communication

### Psychological first aid (PFA)

**Psychological first aid** is humane, supportive, practical help for people who have recently suffered a serious crisis — disaster, accident, violence, sudden death of a loved one. It is not professional counselling or therapy and does not ask people to describe the event in detail. It can be given by any trained helper, including pharmacists.

**Aims:** safety, calm, connection, hope and self-efficacy.

The World Health Organization's guide describes three action principles:

| Principle | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| LOOK | Check for safety; check for people with urgent basic needs; look for people with serious distress reactions |
| LISTEN | Approach people who may need support; ask about needs and concerns; listen and help them feel calm |
| LINK | Help people meet basic needs and access services; help them cope with problems; give information; connect them with loved ones and social support |

**Do's:** be honest and trustworthy; respect the person's right to make decisions; be aware of your own biases; offer practical help (water, a phone, a quiet place); keep information confidential; respect culture.

**Don'ts:** don't pressure people to tell their story; don't give false promises; don't judge; don't think you must solve every problem; don't put yourself at risk.

**Normal reactions to crisis:** shock, fear, crying, anger, numbness, sleep problems, difficulty concentrating. Most people recover with support; refer those who cannot care for themselves, may harm themselves or others, or remain severely distressed.

### Crisis communication

**Crisis communication** is the communication used during emergencies — epidemics, disasters, drug recalls, contaminated products — to give the public accurate information quickly so they can protect themselves.

**Principles (crisis and emergency risk communication)**

1. **Be first** — information should come quickly.
2. **Be right** — accuracy builds credibility; say what is known, what is not known and what is being done.
3. **Be credible** — honesty and openness.
4. **Express empathy** — acknowledge fear and suffering.
5. **Promote action** — give simple, specific things people can do.
6. **Show respect.**

**Good practice:** one consistent message from a trusted spokesperson; simple language; repeat key messages; use several channels (TV, radio, SMS, social media, community leaders); correct rumours and misinformation quickly; update regularly. Experience during the COVID-19 pandemic showed how misinformation spreads quickly and how trusted local health workers, including pharmacists, can counter it.

**Pharmacist's role:** give accurate information about medicines and vaccines, counter rumours (false "miracle cures"), manage panic buying, communicate drug recalls, and support distressed people.

## 5.6 Mental health promotion and stigma reduction through communication

### Mental health

The World Health Organization describes mental health as a state of well-being in which a person realises their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and can contribute to the community. Mental health is more than the absence of mental illness.

**Mental health promotion** aims to strengthen well-being for everyone, not only to treat illness:

- Awareness campaigns (for example on World Mental Health Day, 10 October)
- Life-skills and stress-management education in schools and colleges
- Workplace mental health programmes
- Early recognition and referral
- Support for vulnerable groups (elderly, people with chronic disease, carers, students)
- Easy access to help — in India, the National Mental Health Programme (started 1982) and the Tele-MANAS tele-mental-health service

### Stigma

**Stigma** is a mark of disgrace attached to a person because of a characteristic — here, mental illness (also seen with HIV, tuberculosis, leprosy, epilepsy and substance use). Sociologist Erving Goffman described stigma as an attribute that discredits a person.

| Type of stigma | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Public (social) stigma | Negative attitudes of society: "mentally ill people are dangerous" |
| Self-stigma | The person believes the stereotypes about themselves and feels shame |
| Structural (institutional) stigma | Policies and systems that disadvantage people |
| Associative stigma | Stigma felt by family members and carers |

Components: stereotypes (beliefs) → prejudice (feelings) → discrimination (actions).

**Effects of stigma:** people hide symptoms, delay seeking help, stop medicines, lose jobs and relationships, feel isolated and hopeless.

### Reducing stigma through communication

**Three recognised strategies:**

1. **Education** — replacing myths with facts (mental illness is common and treatable; it is not a weakness or a curse).
2. **Contact** — meeting and hearing from people with lived experience of recovery; considered one of the most effective strategies.
3. **Protest / advocacy** — challenging stigmatising media portrayals and policies.

**Language matters — use person-first, respectful words**

| Avoid | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| "a schizophrenic", "a diabetic" | "a person with schizophrenia", "a person with diabetes" |
| "mad", "mental", "psycho" | "a person with a mental health condition" |
| "committed suicide" | "died by suicide" |
| "suffering from", "victim of" | "living with", "has" |
| "addict" | "a person with a substance use disorder" |

**What a pharmacist can do**

- Treat every patient with mental illness with the same respect as any other.
- Counsel privately and without judgement about psychiatric medicines; explain that they take time to work and should not be stopped suddenly without advice.
- Correct myths ("these medicines are addictive", "you only need them when you feel bad").
- Recognise warning signs and refer; respond to suicidal talk seriously and connect the person to help.
- Display mental health information and helpline details.
- Support carers.

**Legal support in India:** the Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 gives persons with mental illness the right to access mental healthcare, to be treated with dignity, to confidentiality and to protection from cruel treatment. It also states that a person who attempts suicide is presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress and is not to be punished, and directs the government to provide care to such persons.

## Remember

- HBM: perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits, barriers, cues to action, self-efficacy.
- Illness behaviour (Mechanic); Suchman's five stages; Parsons' sick role.
- Psychosomatic illness = real physical disease influenced by psychological factors; HPA axis and sympathetic system link stress to the body; placebo and nocebo.
- Adherence > compliance; non-adherence is primary, unintentional or intentional; WHO five dimensions.
- CBT: thoughts–feelings–behaviour triangle; ABC model; cognitive restructuring.
- TTM: precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance (→ termination); relapse is normal; match advice to stage.
- Motivational interviewing uses OARS.
- PFA (WHO): Look, Listen, Link — not therapy, no forcing to talk.
- Crisis communication: be first, be right, be credible, show empathy, promote action, show respect.
- Stigma: public, self, structural; reduce through education, contact, advocacy and person-first language.
