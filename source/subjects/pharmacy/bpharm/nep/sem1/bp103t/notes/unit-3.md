# Unit 3: Foundations of health communication

Good medicines fail when the message about them fails. Health communication is the sharing of information, feelings and decisions between patients, carers, health workers and the public so that people can protect and improve their health. This unit explains the parts of the communication process, the main models, the types of communication used in healthcare, what blocks it, and the core skills of listening, questioning and empathy, delivered in a way that respects every patient's culture and needs.

## 3.1 Communication: meaning and importance

**Communication** is the process of sending and receiving messages — facts, ideas, feelings — so that they are understood. The word comes from the Latin *communicare*, "to share" or "to make common". Communication is complete only when the receiver understands the message as the sender meant it.

**Health communication** uses communication to inform and influence decisions that improve health — from a one-to-one counselling session to a national vaccination campaign.

**Why communication matters in healthcare**

- Accurate history-taking and assessment depend on it.
- Patients follow treatment they understand and agree with (adherence).
- It prevents medication errors between team members.
- It reduces patient anxiety and builds trust.
- It supports informed consent and patient rights.
- Poor communication is a major cause of complaints and legal action.

## 3.2 Elements of communication

| Element | Meaning | Pharmacy example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sender (source) | The person who starts the message | Pharmacist |
| Message | The information, idea or feeling | "Take this antibiotic for the full seven days" |
| Encoding | Turning the idea into words, signs or symbols | Choosing simple words and a demonstration |
| Channel (medium) | The route the message travels | Face-to-face talk, label, leaflet, phone, video call |
| Receiver | The person who gets the message | Patient or carer |
| Decoding | The receiver interpreting the message | The patient understanding "full seven days" |
| Feedback | The receiver's response, showing what was understood | Patient repeats the instructions back |
| Noise | Anything that distorts the message | Crowd, jargon, pain, worry, accent |
| Context | The setting — physical, social, cultural, emotional | A busy counter versus a private counselling room |

```text
          encodes                 channel                 decodes
 SENDER ----------> MESSAGE ======================> RECEIVER
   ^                          ~~~ NOISE ~~~             |
   |                                                    |
   +-------------------- FEEDBACK <---------------------+
                     (all inside a CONTEXT)
```

**The 7 Cs of effective communication** — clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete and courteous.

## 3.3 Models of communication

### Linear models (one-way)

**Shannon and Weaver model (1949)** — developed for telephone signals: information source → transmitter → channel (with noise) → receiver → destination. It introduced the idea of **noise** but has no feedback. A printed label or a mass advertisement works in a mainly linear way.

**Berlo's SMCR model (1960)** — Source, Message, Channel, Receiver. Each part has factors that affect communication:

| Source and Receiver | Message | Channel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communication skills, attitudes, knowledge, social system, culture | Content, elements, treatment (how it is arranged), structure, code (language) | The five senses: hearing, seeing, touching, smelling, tasting |

The model stresses that sender and receiver communicate best when their skills, knowledge and culture are similar.

### Interactive model

**Schramm's model (1954)** — communication is two-way. Both people encode, decode and interpret, and understanding happens where their **fields of experience** overlap. Feedback is included. The bigger the overlap, the better the understanding — which is why medical jargon fails with lay patients.

### Transactional model

**Barnlund's transactional model (1970)** — both people send and receive messages **at the same time**, verbal and non-verbal, and are shaped by context, relationship and past experience. While the pharmacist speaks, the patient's frown is already a message. This is the most realistic model for face-to-face healthcare.

### Models used specifically in healthcare consultations

- **Calgary–Cambridge guide** — a structure for consultations: initiating the session → gathering information → physical examination (where relevant) → explanation and planning → closing the session, with "providing structure" and "building the relationship" running throughout.
- **Patient-centred communication** — exploring the patient's ideas, concerns and expectations (often shortened to ICE), finding common ground and sharing decisions.

| Model type | Direction | Feedback | Best example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Linear | One-way | None | Label, poster, broadcast |
| Interactive | Two-way, turn by turn | Yes | Phone call, email exchange |
| Transactional | Two-way, simultaneous | Continuous | Face-to-face counselling |

## 3.4 Types of communication

### By form

| Form | Description | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbal (oral) | Spoken words | Counselling, phone calls, ward rounds |
| Written | Words on paper or screen | Labels, prescriptions, leaflets, case notes, emails |
| Non-verbal | Messages without words | Facial expression, eye contact, posture, gestures, touch, distance |
| Paraverbal | How words are spoken | Tone, pitch, speed, volume, pauses |
| Visual | Images and symbols | Pictograms, charts, videos, demonstration |

**Non-verbal communication (body language)** often carries feelings more strongly than words. When words and body language disagree, people tend to believe the body language.

- **Kinesics** — body movements, gestures, facial expressions, posture.
- **Oculesics** — eye contact.
- **Proxemics** — use of space: intimate (very close), personal (about arm's length), social and public distances. Counselling usually happens at personal-to-social distance.
- **Haptics** — touch; use with care and respect for culture and gender.
- **Chronemics** — use of time; keeping someone waiting sends a message.
- **Appearance** — clean, professional dress builds confidence.

### By number of people involved

**1. Intrapersonal** — talking to oneself (self-talk). Important in coping and stress.

**2. Interpersonal communication** — between two people or a very small number, face-to-face or by phone. Examples: pharmacist–patient counselling, pharmacist–doctor discussion. Features: immediate feedback, personal, can be adapted to the listener. The quality of interpersonal communication decides trust and adherence.

**3. Group communication** — among three or more people with a shared purpose. Examples: a patient education session for diabetics, a ward team meeting, a support group. Features: more ideas and support, but needs a leader, turn-taking and a clear goal; quiet members may not speak.

**4. Mass communication** — one source reaching very large audiences through media: television, radio, newspapers, posters, social media. Examples: national campaigns on immunisation, tobacco control, tuberculosis and anaemia. Features: wide reach and low cost per person, but little feedback and no tailoring; best for awareness, less for changing personal behaviour on its own.

**5. Telehealth communication** — providing health services and information at a distance using technology.

| Form of telehealth | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Real-time (synchronous) | Video or voice consultation |
| Store-and-forward (asynchronous) | Sending photos or reports for later review |
| Remote monitoring | Home blood pressure or glucose readings sent to a clinic |
| Mobile health (mHealth) | SMS reminders, health apps, WhatsApp-based follow-up |

In India, the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines (2020) set rules for doctors consulting remotely. The national eSanjeevani service provides free online consultations, and Tele-MANAS provides tele-mental-health support.

**Advantages of telehealth:** reaches rural and remote patients, saves travel and time, useful for follow-up and chronic disease, reduces infection spread.

**Limits and good practice:** no physical examination; poor network and devices; difficulty reading body language; privacy risks; some older people struggle with technology. Good practice: confirm the patient's identity, ensure a private setting, speak clearly, check understanding more often, document the consultation and protect data.

### By direction (inside an organisation)

- **Downward** — from senior to junior (instructions, policies).
- **Upward** — from junior to senior (reports, suggestions, error reporting).
- **Horizontal (lateral)** — between people at the same level or between departments (pharmacist to nurse).
- **Formal** (official channels) and **informal** (grapevine) communication.

## 3.5 Barriers to effective communication in clinical settings

A **barrier** is anything that stops, distorts or delays a message.

| Barrier | Examples in clinical settings | How to reduce it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical / environmental | Noise, crowding, no privacy at the counter, glass screen, poor lighting | Counselling area, reduce noise, face the patient |
| Physiological | Pain, hearing loss, poor vision, speech problems, fatigue, illness, effects of medicines | Speak clearly, use large print, hearing aids, return when the patient is comfortable |
| Psychological / emotional | Fear, anxiety, anger, denial, depression, embarrassment | Calm approach, reassurance, empathy, privacy |
| Semantic / language | Jargon ("hypertension", "PRN", "bd"), different language, words with two meanings | Plain language, local language, interpreter, check understanding |
| Cultural | Beliefs about illness, gender norms, taboos, different meanings of gestures | Cultural awareness, respect, ask rather than assume |
| Health literacy | Difficulty reading or using health information, poor numeracy | Pictograms, demonstrations, teach-back |
| Attitudinal | Prejudice, stereotyping, superiority, impatience, lack of interest | Respect, non-judgemental attitude, self-awareness |
| Organisational / system | Heavy workload, short time, poor handover, rigid hierarchy, unclear roles | Structured tools, team training, adequate staffing |
| Technological | Poor network, faulty devices, illegible handwriting, e-record errors | Backup methods, clear typed orders, double checks |
| Perceptual | Selective attention, hearing only what one expects | Summarise, repeat key points |

**Barriers created by the professional:** using jargon, talking too fast, interrupting, giving too much information at once, not looking at the patient, writing while the patient speaks, sounding rushed, judging the patient.

## 3.6 Active listening, questioning techniques and empathy

### Hearing and listening

**Hearing** is the passive physical process of sound reaching the ear. **Listening** is the active mental process of attending to, understanding, remembering and responding to the message.

**Types of listening:** discriminative (sounds), informational (facts), critical (judging content), empathetic (understanding feelings) and appreciative (enjoyment).

### Active listening

**Active listening** means giving full attention to the speaker, understanding both words and feelings, and showing that you have understood.

**Skills of active listening**

| Skill | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Attending | Face the patient, comfortable eye contact, open posture, lean slightly forward, put away distractions |
| Minimal encouragers | Nods, "I see", "go on", "mm-hm" |
| Paraphrasing | Repeat the content in your own words: "So the cough wakes you at night" |
| Reflecting feelings | Name the emotion: "You sound worried about the side effects" |
| Clarifying | "When you say dizzy, do you mean the room spins?" |
| Summarising | Pull the main points together at the end |
| Silence | Allow pauses so the patient can think and continue |
| Not interrupting | Let the patient finish |

The **SOLER** reminder for attending posture: **S**it squarely facing the patient, **O**pen posture, **L**ean slightly forward, **E**ye contact, **R**elaxed manner.

**Blocks to listening:** planning your reply while the other speaks, judging, giving advice too early, interrupting, daydreaming, being distracted by noise or the phone, and filtering (hearing only what you want).

### Questioning techniques

| Type of question | Purpose | Example | Use carefully because |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Open | Invites a full answer; gathers information and feelings | "How have you been taking these tablets?" | Talkative patients may wander |
| Closed | Gets a specific fact, often yes/no | "Are you allergic to penicillin?" | Gives little detail |
| Probing | Explores further | "Tell me more about the rash" | Can feel intrusive |
| Clarifying | Removes doubt | "What do you mean by 'often'?" | — |
| Reflective | Turns the patient's words back | "You feel the medicine isn't working?" | — |
| Leading | Suggests the expected answer | "You take it after food, don't you?" | Patient may agree to please you — avoid |
| Multiple (stacked) | Several questions at once | "Do you smoke, drink, and take other medicines?" | Confusing — avoid |
| Hypothetical | Explores "what if" | "What would you do if you missed a dose?" | — |

**Funnel technique** — start with broad open questions, then narrow to specific closed questions to confirm details.

**The three prime questions** used in patient counselling (from the Indian Health Service model):

1. What did the doctor tell you the medicine is for?
2. How did the doctor tell you to take it?
3. What did the doctor tell you to expect?

These find out what the patient already knows so the pharmacist fills the gaps.

### Empathy

**Empathy** is the ability to understand another person's feelings and point of view as if from their position, and to communicate that understanding — "walking in their shoes" while remaining yourself.

| Empathy | Sympathy | Apathy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feeling *with* the person | Feeling *for* (pity) the person | No concern |
| "It sounds frightening to start insulin" | "Oh, poor you" | "That's the rule" |
| Keeps professional balance; patient feels understood | Can feel patronising; may focus on the helper's feelings | Damages trust |

**Components of empathy:** cognitive (understanding), emotional (sharing the feeling) and compassionate (wanting to help). Empathy is shown through attentive listening, reflecting feelings, a warm tone and appropriate non-verbal behaviour.

**Benefits:** greater patient satisfaction and trust, more information shared, better adherence, less anxiety.

## 3.7 Culturally appropriate and inclusive communication

**Culture** is the shared beliefs, values, language, customs and behaviour of a group. India's diversity of language, religion, region, caste and economic background makes cultural sensitivity essential.

**Cultural competence** is the ability to communicate and provide care effectively to people from different cultures. It grows through:

- **Awareness** of one's own beliefs and biases
- **Knowledge** of other cultures' health beliefs and practices
- **Skill** in adapting communication
- **Respect** and a genuine wish to understand

**Practical guidance**

1. Use the patient's preferred language; use trained interpreters rather than children or untrained relatives for important information.
2. Ask about beliefs: "What do you think caused this?", "Are you taking any home or traditional remedies?"
3. Respect religious practice — fasting (Ramadan, Navratri) may change dosing times; some patients avoid gelatin or alcohol-containing products.
4. Respect gender preferences; some patients prefer a professional of the same gender.
5. Be careful with touch, eye contact and gestures, which carry different meanings in different cultures.
6. Avoid stereotypes; treat each person as an individual.

**Inclusive communication** means making sure everyone can access and understand information, whatever their ability, age, literacy, gender identity or background.

| Group | Inclusive practice |
| --- | --- |
| Hearing impairment | Face the person, speak clearly (do not shout), written notes, sign language interpreter |
| Visual impairment | Large print, tactile markers, verbal explanation, identify yourself |
| Low literacy | Pictograms, colour-coded schedules, demonstration, teach-back |
| Children | Age-appropriate words, involve parent, play |
| Elderly | Slower pace, check hearing and vision, involve carer, simple schedules |
| Cognitive or intellectual disability | Short sentences, one idea at a time, visual aids, involve carer |
| Gender and sexual minorities | Respectful, non-assuming language, use preferred name and pronouns, confidentiality |
| People with mental illness | Respect, avoid stigmatising words, patience |

**Plain language rules:** short sentences, common words, active voice, one idea per sentence, explain any medical term, use numbers carefully ("one tablet in the morning and one at night" rather than "bd").

## Remember

- Elements: sender, message, encoding, channel, receiver, decoding, feedback, noise, context.
- Models: Shannon–Weaver (linear, introduced noise), Berlo SMCR, Schramm (interactive, field of experience), Barnlund (transactional, simultaneous). Calgary–Cambridge guide for consultations.
- Types: interpersonal, group, mass, telehealth (real-time, store-and-forward, remote monitoring, mHealth).
- Barriers: physical, physiological, psychological, semantic, cultural, health literacy, attitudinal, organisational, technological.
- Active listening: attend (SOLER), encourage, paraphrase, reflect, clarify, summarise, allow silence.
- Prefer open questions first (funnel technique); avoid leading and multiple questions.
- Empathy = feeling with; sympathy = feeling for.
- Cultural competence = awareness, knowledge, skill, respect; use interpreters, plain language and teach-back.
