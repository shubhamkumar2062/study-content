# Unit 2: Cultivation, collection, processing and storage of drugs of natural origin

The quality of a herbal medicine is decided long before it reaches the factory. The seed, the soil, the time of harvest, the way the plant is dried and the store it waits in all change how much active constituent it holds. This unit covers how medicinal plants are grown and handled, the WHO rules for doing it well, how plant hormones and genetics can raise yield, and how threatened medicinal plants are conserved.

## 2.1 Why cultivate medicinal plants instead of collecting them wild?

| Wild collection | Cultivation |
| --- | --- |
| Plants vary in age, chemotype and constituent content | Uniform, known variety; steady quality |
| Risk of wrong species and adulteration | Correct identity from authenticated seed |
| Over-harvesting endangers species (kutki, chirata, sarpagandha) | Protects wild populations |
| Supply depends on season and forest access | Planned, regular supply |
| Contamination hard to control | Pesticides, heavy metals and microbes can be controlled |
| Cheap labour, no land cost | Needs land, inputs and expertise |

## 2.2 Methods of plant cultivation (propagation)

### (a) Sexual method — by seeds

New plants grow from seeds. Used for annuals and plants that set seed well: fennel, coriander, isabgol (*Plantago ovata*), datura, ashwagandha, tulsi, fenugreek.

- **Merits**: cheap, easy, many plants at once, seeds store and transport well, plants are long-lived and hardy.
- **Demerits**: offspring vary (cross-pollination), so constituent content varies; slower to mature; some seeds have poor germination or dormancy.
- Seed treatment to improve germination: soaking in water, scarification (scratching a hard coat, or treating with acid), stratification (cold treatment), gibberellic acid.

### (b) Asexual (vegetative) method

A part of the plant other than seed gives a new plant identical to the parent (a clone). Used to keep a high-yielding plant true to type.

| Method | How it works | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Natural** — rhizomes | Pieces of rhizome with buds are planted | Ginger, turmeric |
| Bulbs | Bulbs or cloves planted | Garlic, onion |
| Tubers / tuberous roots | Planted with an eye or bud | Potato (tubers), sweet potato and dahlia (tuberous roots) |
| Runners and suckers | Stems that root where they touch the soil | Mint (runners), banana (suckers) |
| **Artificial** — cuttings | A piece of stem, root or leaf is planted and forms roots | Stem: rose, mint; root: rauwolfia |
| Layering | A branch is made to root while still on the parent, then cut off; air layering (gootee) wraps a wounded branch in moist moss | Lemon, guava, jasmine |
| Grafting | The scion (upper shoot of a good variety) is joined to the rootstock of a hardy plant | Mango, citrus, cinchona (to combine vigour and alkaloid yield) |
| Budding | Only a bud of the good variety is inserted in the rootstock | Rose, citrus |
| **Micropropagation** (tissue culture) | Small pieces (explants) grown on sterile medium with hormones give thousands of identical plantlets | Rare and slow-growing medicinal plants, kutki, stevia |

- **Merits of vegetative propagation**: true-to-type, uniform chemistry; faster maturity; useful for plants with no or poor seed.
- **Demerits**: costlier, plants may be shorter-lived, diseases pass from parent to offspring.

## 2.3 Factors influencing cultivation

| Factor | Effect | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Altitude** | Some plants need hills, others plains | Tea and cinchona grow well on hills; kutki and chirata are high-altitude Himalayan plants |
| **Temperature** | Controls growth and secondary metabolism; frost kills tropical plants | Volatile oil content of mint varies with temperature |
| **Rainfall and irrigation** | Water stress can raise or lower constituents; waterlogging rots roots | Isabgol needs dry weather at maturity |
| **Day length and light** | Flowering and many metabolites depend on light | Shade lowers volatile oil in some herbs |
| **Soil** | Texture (sandy, clay, loam), pH, drainage and nutrients | Root drugs prefer loose, well-drained soil |
| **Fertilisers / manures** | N, P, K and organic manure raise biomass; nitrogen often raises alkaloid content | Nitrogen feeding raises tropane alkaloids in belladonna |
| **Pests and diseases** | Damage yield; pesticides leave residues | Organic or integrated pest management preferred |
| **Genetic make-up** | Chemical races (chemotypes) of one species differ | Oil composition of tulsi chemotypes |

## 2.4 Collection

The right **time** and **part** matter because constituent levels change with age and season.

| Part | Best time of collection |
| --- | --- |
| Leaves and herbs | When the plant is flowering or just before, when photosynthesis is most active (senna, vasaka, tulsi) |
| Flowers | Before or just at full opening (clove is collected as buds) |
| Fruits | When fully grown; ripe or unripe as required (amla when ripe) |
| Seeds | When fully ripe and dry |
| Barks | In spring, when the cambium is active and the bark peels off easily (cinchona, arjuna) |
| Roots and rhizomes | After the aerial parts die down (late autumn/winter), when food reserves are stored |
| Gums and resins | In the dry season, by making cuts in the bark (acacia gum, boswellia) |

- Collection should be by trained people who know the plant, with clean tools, avoiding damaged or diseased plants and foreign matter.
- Collect only a fraction of a wild population and leave enough to regenerate (sustainable harvesting).

## 2.5 Processing

After collection the material is prepared so it keeps well.

1. **Garbling (cleaning)**: removing soil, stones, other plant parts and foreign organic matter. Example: removing stalks from clove.
2. **Washing** (for roots) and **cutting/slicing** to speed drying.
3. **Special treatments**: peeling (ginger, liquorice), fermentation or "curing" to develop flavour (vanilla, tea), parboiling (turmeric is boiled before drying), sweating.
4. **Drying**: the most important step. It removes water so that enzymes and microbes cannot act.

| Drying method | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Sun drying | Seeds, barks, roots (cheap but uneven, contamination risk) |
| Shade drying | Leaves, flowers and drugs with volatile oil or colour (tulsi, mint) to protect volatile and light-sensitive constituents |
| Artificial (hot-air ovens, tray driers) | Controlled temperature and time; aromatic drugs are dried at low temperature |
| Freeze-drying / vacuum drying | Heat-sensitive material and enzymes |

Some drugs need enzyme action to develop their principle before drying (vanilla, where glucovanillin breaks down to vanillin during curing); others must be dried quickly to stop enzymes destroying the glycoside (digitalis).

5. **Grading and packing**: sorting by size, colour and quality; packing in suitable containers with labels.

## 2.6 Storage

Badly stored drugs lose activity, gain moisture and get mouldy or insect-infested.

**Factors that spoil stored drugs**: moisture, heat, light, air (oxygen), microbes, insects (beetles, moths) and rodents.

**Good storage practice**

- Store in clean, dry, cool, well-ventilated stores, away from direct light.
- Use well-closed or airtight containers; hygroscopic drugs (squill, digitalis) are kept with a desiccant.
- Drugs with volatile oil in airtight, well-filled containers.
- Light-sensitive drugs in amber or opaque containers.
- Protect from insects by cleanliness and, where permitted, by fumigation; protect from rodents.
- First-in, first-out stock rotation; label with name, batch and date of collection.
- Check moisture content and look for mould and insects at intervals.

## 2.7 Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP)

The **WHO guidelines on Good Agricultural and Collection Practices (GACP) for medicinal plants** (2003) set rules so that herbal raw material is safe and of uniform quality. India's National Medicinal Plants Board has similar GAP and GFCP (good field collection practice) guidelines.

**Main points of GACP**

1. **Identification**: correct botanical identity of seeds and planting material; voucher specimens kept.
2. **Seeds and propagation material**: authentic, free from disease and contamination.
3. **Site selection**: soil and water free from heavy metals, pesticides and pollution; past use of the land known.
4. **Climate**: suited to the species.
5. **Soil, fertiliser and irrigation**: properly composted manure only (no human faeces); clean irrigation water.
6. **Crop maintenance and protection**: minimum pesticide use, only approved agents, records kept.
7. **Harvest**: at the right time for best quality; clean equipment; avoid wet weather.
8. **Wild collection**: permits, sustainable quantities, protection of endangered species.
9. **Primary processing**: prompt cleaning and drying, separate from contaminants.
10. **Packaging, storage and transport**: clean, labelled, protected.
11. **Personnel**: trained, healthy workers with hygiene and protective clothing.
12. **Documentation**: every step recorded so the batch can be traced (traceability), and quality assurance by audit.

## 2.8 Plant hormones and their applications

**Plant hormones (phytohormones)** are organic substances made in small amounts in one part of a plant that control growth or development elsewhere. Synthetic ones with the same effect are called plant growth regulators.

| Hormone | Examples | Main effects | Use in cultivation of medicinal plants |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Auxins** | Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA, natural), indole-3-butyric acid (IBA), naphthalene acetic acid (NAA), 2,4-D | Cell elongation, root formation, apical dominance | Rooting of stem cuttings; callus induction in tissue culture; 2,4-D as a weedicide at higher strength |
| **Gibberellins** | Gibberellic acid (GA3) | Stem elongation, breaking seed dormancy, flowering, fruit growth | Better germination and larger biomass; GA treatment is reported to change alkaloid and volatile-oil yield in some crops |
| **Cytokinins** | Kinetin, zeatin, benzylaminopurine (BAP) | Cell division, shoot formation, delay of ageing | Shoot multiplication in micropropagation (with auxin: high auxin → roots, high cytokinin → shoots) |
| **Abscisic acid (ABA)** | ABA | Dormancy, stomatal closure, stress response | Stress signalling that can switch on secondary metabolism |
| **Ethylene** | Ethylene gas; ethephon (releases ethylene) | Fruit ripening, leaf fall, flowering | Ethephon increases latex flow in rubber; ripening of fruits |

Related signal molecules such as **jasmonic acid / methyl jasmonate** and **salicylic acid** act as **elicitors**: they switch on defence pathways and are used in cell cultures to raise secondary metabolite yield.

## 2.9 Polyploidy, mutation and hybridisation

These genetic methods produce plants with more of the desired secondary metabolite.

### (a) Polyploidy

Having more than two sets of chromosomes (triploid 3n, tetraploid 4n …).

- Produced by treating seeds or growing tips with **colchicine** (from *Colchicum autumnale*), which stops spindle formation so the chromosome number doubles.
- Polyploids often have larger organs (leaves, flowers, seeds) and may have higher constituent content.
- **Examples**: tetraploid *Datura* strains with higher alkaloid content; in *Acorus calamus* (sweet flag) the diploid American form has almost no β-asarone, while triploid and tetraploid (Indian) forms are rich in it — so ploidy controls chemistry.

### (b) Mutation

A sudden heritable change in a gene or chromosome.

- Induced by **physical mutagens** (X-rays, gamma rays, UV light) or **chemical mutagens** (ethyl methanesulfonate, nitrosoguanidine, colchicine).
- Mutants are screened and high-yielding ones kept.
- **Examples**: repeated mutation and selection of *Penicillium chrysogenum* raised penicillin yield many-fold over Fleming's strain; poppy lines that accumulate thebaine instead of morphine; mutant mint strains resistant to wilt.

### (c) Hybridisation

Crossing two plants of different varieties or species to combine their useful characters in the offspring.

- **Examples**: peppermint (*Mentha × piperita*) is itself a hybrid of water mint and spearmint; hybrids of *Cinchona ledgeriana* (high alkaloid) and *C. succirubra* (vigorous) give good yield and hardiness; high-yielding hybrid varieties of many crops.
- **Hybrid vigour (heterosis)**: hybrids often grow faster and bigger than both parents.

## 2.10 Conservation of medicinal plants

Most Indian medicinal plants used by industry are still collected from the wild, and many are threatened. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List grades threat (critically endangered, endangered, vulnerable); CITES controls international trade in listed species; the **National Medicinal Plants Board (NMPB)** under the Ministry of AYUSH supports cultivation and conservation; the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 regulates access and benefit-sharing.

| | In-situ conservation | Ex-situ conservation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meaning | Protecting plants in their **natural habitat** | Protecting plants **outside** their natural habitat |
| Methods | National parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves, Medicinal Plants Conservation Areas (MPCAs) | Botanical and herbal gardens, field gene banks, seed banks, tissue culture banks, cryopreservation (storage in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C), cultivation on farms |
| Merits | Whole ecosystem and natural evolution preserved; cheap per species | Rescue of very rare species; material available for research and planting back |
| Limits | Needs protection from people and grazing; habitat loss | Limited genetic variety; costly; plants lose adaptation to the wild |

## 2.11 Strategies for value addition

Value addition means turning raw herbs into products worth more, so that growers earn more and have a reason to cultivate rather than over-collect.

- Good primary processing (cleaning, drying, grading) to fetch a higher price.
- Making standardised extracts, oils, powders and formulations.
- Organic and GACP certification; geographical indication tags.
- Farmer producer organisations and contract farming with industry (buy-back).
- Using renewable parts (leaves, fruits) instead of roots where the action allows.
- Developing nutraceuticals, cosmetics and export products.

## 2.12 Eco-pharmacognosy

**Eco-pharmacognosy** is the study and practice of using natural resources for medicines in a sustainable, environmentally friendly way, so that the plants will still be available to future generations. Its main ideas:

1. Know which species are threatened and how much can safely be harvested.
2. Shift from wild collection to cultivation.
3. Use biotechnology (tissue culture, cell culture) to produce compounds without destroying plants.
4. Use renewable parts and avoid destructive harvesting of roots and barks.
5. Prevent waste and ensure fair sharing of benefits with the local communities who hold the knowledge.
6. Standardise products so less raw material is needed for the same effect.

### Example 1: Kutki

- **Biological source**: rhizomes and roots of *Picrorhiza kurroa*, family Plantaginaceae (formerly Scrophulariaceae).
- Grows in the alpine Himalaya at high altitude; slow-growing.
- Constituents: bitter iridoid glycosides — **picroside I, picroside II** and **kutkoside** (the mixture is called **kutkin**).
- Use: liver protection, bitter tonic.
- **Threat**: the whole underground part is dug up, so heavy trade has made it threatened in the wild.
- **Conservation**: cultivation in Himalayan states, micropropagation, nursery raising of planting stock, sustainable harvesting limits, and in-situ protection in high-altitude reserves.

### Example 2: Chirata

- **Biological source**: dried whole plant of *Swertia chirayita*, family Gentianaceae.
- Grows in the temperate Himalaya.
- Constituents: very bitter secoiridoid glycosides — **amarogentin**, **swertiamarin**, gentiopicrin — and xanthones such as **mangiferin**.
- Use: bitter tonic, fever, appetite, liver.
- **Threat**: the whole plant is uprooted before seeds set; widespread adulteration with other *Swertia* species also shows the shortage.
- **Conservation**: seed-based cultivation, tissue culture, harvesting only after seed fall, and community-based conservation.

## Remember

- Seeds → variable but cheap; vegetative propagation → true-to-type clones (cuttings, layering, grafting, budding, rhizomes, bulbs, micropropagation).
- Collection times: leaves at flowering; barks in spring; roots after aerial parts die; flowers before full bloom; gums in dry season.
- Drying stops enzyme and microbial action; shade-dry aromatic and coloured drugs.
- WHO GACP (2003): identity, site, cultivation, harvest, processing, personnel, documentation.
- Auxins → roots; gibberellins → elongation and germination; cytokinins → shoots/cell division; ABA → dormancy; ethylene → ripening; jasmonate → elicitor.
- Colchicine doubles chromosomes (polyploidy); *Acorus calamus* ploidy changes β-asarone; mutation raised penicillin yield; peppermint is a natural hybrid.
- In-situ = natural habitat (sacred groves, MPCAs); ex-situ = gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation.
- Kutki (*Picrorhiza kurroa*, picrosides) and chirata (*Swertia chirayita*, amarogentin, swertiamarin) are threatened Himalayan bitters — protected by cultivation and sustainable harvest (eco-pharmacognosy).
