# Unit 1: Fundamentals of pharmacognosy

Pharmacognosy is the oldest branch of pharmacy. Before chemists could make medicines in a flask, every medicine came from a plant, an animal or a mineral. This unit explains what the subject is, where crude drugs come from, how a few famous natural products changed medicine, which official books describe herbal drugs, and how crude drugs are classified. Expect one long answer from this unit (classification, or history and scope) and two or three short answers or MCQs on pharmacopoeias and drug discovery.

## 1.1 Definition

The word comes from two Greek words: **pharmakon** = drug and **gnosis** = knowledge. So pharmacognosy literally means "knowledge of drugs".

**Pharmacognosy** is the study of drugs of natural origin (plants, animals, micro-organisms, marine organisms and minerals): their sources, identification, cultivation, collection, preparation, chemical constituents, quality evaluation, uses and conservation.

Key terms:

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Crude drug | A natural product that has only been collected and dried (or given simple treatment such as cutting or powdering); not purified. Example: senna leaf, clove bud |
| Biological source | The plant or animal that gives the drug, written with its botanical (Latin) name and family. Example: clove = dried flower buds of *Syzygium aromaticum*, family Myrtaceae |
| Geographical source | The region where the drug is grown or collected |
| Active constituent | The chemical responsible for the drug's action. Example: hyoscyamine in belladonna |
| Phytochemistry | Chemistry of plant constituents: their extraction, isolation, structure and biosynthesis |
| Herbal drug / phytomedicine | A medicine whose active part is a plant material or its extract |

## 1.2 History of pharmacognosy

```text
Ancient texts ──► Greek & Roman materia medica ──► Arab scholars ──► 19th-century chemistry ──► Modern pharmacognosy
(Ayurveda, China,   (Hippocrates, Dioscorides,       (Avicenna,       (isolation of morphine,      (phytochemistry,
 Egypt)              Galen)                            Unani system)     quinine; the word           biotechnology,
                                                                         "pharmacognosy")            standardisation)
```

- **Ancient India**: the Vedas, and later the *Charaka Samhita* (medicine) and *Sushruta Samhita* (surgery), describe hundreds of medicinal plants and how to prepare them.
- **China**: the herbal attributed to the legendary emperor Shen Nong lists several hundred drugs; Traditional Chinese Medicine grew from these texts.
- **Egypt**: the *Ebers Papyrus* (about 1500 BC) records drugs such as castor oil, aloe and opium.
- **Greece and Rome**: **Hippocrates** (often called the father of medicine) used plant remedies; **Dioscorides** wrote *De Materia Medica* (1st century AD), describing about 600 plants — the main reference on drugs for more than 1,500 years; **Galen** gave his name to "galenical" preparations (extracts and tinctures made by simple methods).
- **Arab period**: **Avicenna (Ibn Sina)** wrote the *Canon of Medicine*; Arab physicians developed distillation, syrups and the Unani system.
- **Origin of the word**: the term "pharmacognosy" came into use in the early 19th century; it is usually credited to **C. A. Seydler** (1815, *Analectica Pharmacognostica*), with J. A. Schmidt having used a similar term a few years earlier.
- **19th century**: chemists isolated pure active principles — morphine, quinine, strychnine, atropine — and pharmacognosy moved from describing the outer look of drugs to studying their chemistry.

## 1.3 Present status and scope

Pharmacognosy today is no longer only "drug identification". It joins botany, chemistry, pharmacology and biotechnology.

**Present status**

- A large share of modern medicines are natural products, or are derived from or modelled on them, especially anticancer drugs and antibiotics.
- The World Health Organization accepts that a large part of the world's population depends on traditional medicine for primary health care.
- India has a strong herbal industry under the Ministry of AYUSH, and is among the leading exporters of medicinal plants and extracts.
- Herbal drugs are now sold as medicines, dietary supplements, nutraceuticals and cosmetics, so their quality control has become a regulatory need.

**Scope** (where a pharmacognosist works)

1. **Identification and authentication** of crude drugs, including detection of adulteration (microscopy, chromatographic fingerprints, DNA barcoding).
2. **Standardisation and quality control** of herbal raw materials and finished products.
3. **Cultivation and conservation** of medicinal plants; Good Agricultural and Collection Practices.
4. **Phytochemistry**: extraction, isolation and structure of active constituents.
5. **Drug discovery**: natural products as leads for new medicines (taxol, artemisinin).
6. **Biotechnology**: plant tissue culture, genetic improvement, microbial production of drugs.
7. **Herbal formulation**: tablets, capsules, creams and standardised extracts.
8. **Pharmacovigilance and herb–drug interactions** of herbal medicines.
9. **Nutraceuticals, cosmetics, food colours and flavours** of natural origin.
10. **Regulatory work**: writing monographs for pharmacopoeias, export and import quality certificates.

**Development of pharmacognosy** in short: descriptive (morphology) → analytical (microscopy, chemistry) → experimental (biosynthesis, tissue culture) → molecular (DNA methods, genomics, metabolomics).

## 1.4 Sources of drugs

| Source | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plants** | Morphine (opium poppy), digoxin (*Digitalis lanata*), quinine (cinchona bark), reserpine (*Rauwolfia*), vincristine (*Catharanthus*), senna, ispaghula | Largest source; any part may be used — leaf, bark, root, rhizome, flower, fruit, seed, whole plant, exudate |
| **Animals** | Honey, beeswax, lanolin (wool fat), gelatin, cod-liver oil, shark-liver oil, musk, insulin (earlier from pancreas), heparin | Give fats, waxes, proteins, hormones |
| **Microbes** | Penicillin (*Penicillium*), streptomycin (*Streptomyces griseus*), cephalosporins, ergot alkaloids (*Claviceps purpurea*), lovastatin (*Aspergillus terreus*) | Grown by fermentation, so supply is steady |
| **Marine** | Agar and alginates (seaweeds), cytarabine (modelled on nucleosides from a marine sponge), trabectedin (sea squirt), ziconotide (from cone snail venom), cod-liver oil | Large, still under-explored source |
| **Minerals** | Kaolin, talc, bentonite, calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, sulfur; bhasmas of Ayurveda | Inorganic substances, mainly excipients or antacids |
| **Plant tissue culture** | Shikonin (from *Lithospermum* cell culture, produced commercially), paclitaxel by plant cell fermentation | Plant cells grown in sterile nutrient medium; supply independent of season and land |

## 1.5 Historical milestones in drug discovery

These eight drugs show how a natural product moves from traditional use to a pure medicine.

| Drug | Natural source | Key events | Importance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Morphine** | Latex of the unripe capsule of the opium poppy, *Papaver somniferum* (Papaveraceae) | Isolated by the German pharmacist **F. W. Sertürner** in the early 1800s; named after Morpheus, god of dreams | First alkaloid ever isolated; proved that a plant's action comes from a pure chemical; still a standard analgesic |
| **Quinine** | Bark of *Cinchona* species (Rubiaceae) | Bark used in South America for fevers; isolated by **Pelletier and Caventou** in 1820 | First effective treatment of malaria; led to synthetic antimalarials such as chloroquine |
| **Aspirin** | Willow bark (*Salix*) and meadowsweet (*Spiraea / Filipendula ulmaria*) | Salicin isolated from willow bark; salicylic acid made from it but irritated the stomach; **Felix Hoffmann** at Bayer made acetylsalicylic acid (1897) | First semisynthetic drug from a natural lead; name: "a" (acetyl) + "spir" (*Spiraea*) |
| **Warfarin** | Spoiled sweet clover (*Melilotus*) hay | Cattle bled to death after eating mouldy sweet clover; **K. P. Link** isolated the anticoagulant **dicoumarol**; warfarin was later made as a rat poison and then as a medicine | Oral anticoagulant; name from WARF (Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation) + coumarin |
| **Penicillin** | Mould *Penicillium notatum* (now *P. chrysogenum*) | **Alexander Fleming** noticed in 1928 that the mould killed staphylococci; **Florey and Chain** purified it in the 1940s; Nobel Prize 1945 | First antibiotic; began the antibiotic era and modern fermentation industry |
| **Cephalosporin** | Fungus *Cephalosporium acremonium* (now *Acremonium chrysogenum*) | Found by **Giuseppe Brotzu** in sea water near a sewage outlet in Sardinia (1940s); cephalosporin C isolated later at Oxford | β-lactam antibiotics resistant to penicillinase; base for many generations of semisynthetic cephalosporins |
| **Taxol (paclitaxel)** | Bark of the Pacific yew, *Taxus brevifolia* (Taxaceae) | Found in a US National Cancer Institute plant screening programme; isolated by **Wall and Wani**; unique action of stabilising microtubules found later; now made semisynthetically from 10-deacetylbaccatin III in needles of *Taxus baccata* | Major anticancer drug (ovarian, breast cancer); classic case of conservation problem solved by semisynthesis |
| **Artemisinin** | Leaves of sweet wormwood, *Artemisia annua* (Asteraceae) — "qinghao" of Chinese medicine | **Tu Youyou** found it in the 1970s after reading an old Chinese text that advised steeping the herb in cold water; Nobel Prize 2015 | Sesquiterpene lactone with an endoperoxide bridge; artemisinin-based combinations are the standard treatment of falciparum malaria |

**Lessons from these milestones**: traditional knowledge points to the plant; isolation gives the pure compound; chemistry then improves it (aspirin, semisynthetic cephalosporins, paclitaxel); and supply problems lead to fermentation, semisynthesis or cultivation.

## 1.6 Herbal and traditional pharmacopoeias

A **pharmacopoeia** is an official book of standards that describes drugs (monographs) and the tests they must pass — identity, purity and strength.

| Pharmacopoeia | Published by | What it covers for herbal drugs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Indian Pharmacopoeia (IP)** | Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission, Ghaziabad (Ministry of Health and Family Welfare); first edition 1955, revised periodically | Legal standard under the Drugs and Cosmetics Act; includes a section of monographs on herbs and herbal products (identity by macroscopy, microscopy and TLC, ash values, extractive values, assay of marker compounds) |
| **British Herbal Pharmacopoeia (BHP)** | British Herbal Medicine Association | Monographs of herbs used by herbal practitioners: description, microscopy, identity tests, quantitative standards, action and indications |
| **United States Pharmacopeia – Herbal Medicines and Dietary Supplements** | United States Pharmacopeial Convention | Monographs for botanical dietary supplements and their extracts (for example ginkgo, garlic, turmeric); USP also publishes a separate Herbal Medicines Compendium for herbs used worldwide |
| **Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India (API)** | Pharmacopoeia Commission for Indian Medicine & Homoeopathy (PCIM&H), Ministry of AYUSH | Part I: single plant drugs; Part II: compound Ayurvedic formulations; gives Sanskrit name, identity, purity, TLC, and Ayurvedic properties (rasa, guna, virya, vipaka) |
| **Unani Pharmacopoeia of India (UPI)** | PCIM&H, Ministry of AYUSH | Single drugs and compound formulations of the Unani system, with Unani names and temperament (mizaj) |
| **American Herbal Pharmacopoeia (AHP)** | An independent non-government organisation in the USA | Detailed monographs with botany, analytical methods (HPTLC, HPLC), pharmacology and safety; not a legal standard but widely respected |

Similar official books exist for Siddha (Siddha Pharmacopoeia of India) and Homoeopathy (Homoeopathic Pharmacopoeia of India), also under PCIM&H.

## 1.7 Official and non-official drugs

- **Official drugs**: drugs that have a monograph in the official pharmacopoeia of a country (for India, the IP). They must meet those standards. Example: ispaghula husk, senna, clove in the IP.
- **Non-official (unofficial) drugs**: drugs not listed in the pharmacopoeia, though they may be well known and used. Example: many folk remedies, newer herbs.

## 1.8 Codified and non-codified drugs

- **Codified drugs**: drugs of traditional systems whose use is written down in authoritative classical texts — Ayurvedic, Unani, Siddha and Sowa-Rigpa texts. Their names, preparation and uses are standardised by tradition. Example: Triphala churna, Ashwagandharishta.
- **Non-codified drugs**: remedies passed on orally through folk, tribal and household practice, not written in classical texts. They are an important lead for ethnopharmacology and must be documented (for example in the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library, which protects Indian traditional knowledge from wrongful patents).

## 1.9 Classification of crude drugs

Crude drugs are arranged in a system so they can be studied and compared. Six systems are used.

### (a) Alphabetical classification

Drugs listed A to Z by their Latin or English name. Used in pharmacopoeias (IP, BP, USP) and dictionaries.

- Example: Acacia, Agar, Aloe, Belladonna, Benzoin, Cardamom, Clove …
- **Merits**: very easy to locate a drug; suits reference books.
- **Limitations**: no link between drugs; drugs from the same plant family, part or chemical group fall far apart.

### (b) Morphological classification

Drugs grouped by the plant or animal part used.

| Group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| **Organised drugs** (have cell structure) | |
| Leaves | Senna, digitalis, vasaka, tulsi |
| Barks | Cinchona, cinnamon, arjuna |
| Roots and rhizomes | Rauwolfia, ginger, turmeric, ashwagandha |
| Flowers | Clove (buds), saffron (stigmas) |
| Fruits | Fennel, coriander, amla, chasteberry |
| Seeds | Nux vomica, ispaghula, fenugreek |
| Woods | Sandalwood, quassia |
| Whole herbs | Brahmi, kalmegh, chirata |
| **Unorganised drugs** (no cells) | Gums, resins, latex, dried juices (aloe), oils, waxes, extracts |

- **Merits**: helps learn identification (macroscopy and microscopy) because drugs of one group look alike; useful for practical study.
- **Limitations**: does not relate the drugs' chemistry or action; one plant may give several parts.

### (c) Taxonomical classification

Drugs arranged according to the natural plant classification: phylum → class → order → family → genus → species (systems of Bentham and Hooker, Engler and Prantl). Example: Solanaceae — belladonna, datura, hyoscyamus, ashwagandha; Apiaceae — fennel, coriander, cumin.

- **Merits**: shows natural relationships; plants of one family often have similar constituents (Solanaceae → tropane alkaloids).
- **Limitations**: not useful for therapeutic study; drugs of one family may have very different actions; classification changes when botanists revise families.

### (d) Chemical classification

Drugs grouped by their main active constituent.

| Chemical group | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Carbohydrates | Acacia, agar, ispaghula, starch |
| Proteins / enzymes | Papain, gelatin |
| Lipids | Castor oil, beeswax, wool fat |
| Alkaloids | Cinchona, belladonna, opium, vasaka |
| Glycosides | Senna, digitalis, aloe |
| Tannins | Catechu, myrobalan |
| Volatile oils | Clove, peppermint, eucalyptus |
| Resins | Benzoin, colophony, boswellia |

- **Merits**: links drugs of similar chemistry; suits phytochemical study and assay methods.
- **Limitations**: many drugs contain more than one important group (ginger has volatile oil and pungent resinous principles); constituents of some drugs are not fully known.

### (e) Pharmacological (therapeutic) classification

Drugs grouped by their action or use.

| Action | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Laxatives / purgatives | Senna, aloe, ispaghula, castor oil |
| Cardiotonics | Digitalis, arjuna |
| Expectorants | Vasaka, liquorice |
| Antimalarials | Cinchona, artemisia |
| Hepatoprotectives | Milk thistle, kutki |
| Anticancer | Catharanthus, taxus |
| Immunomodulators | Tulsi, giloy, ashwagandha |

- **Merits**: most useful for pharmacologists and prescribers; helps choose substitutes.
- **Limitations**: one drug may have several actions (garlic: antimicrobial and lipid-lowering); unrelated plants fall together.

### (f) Chemotaxonomic classification

Plants classified by both **botanical relationship and chemical constituents**, on the idea that related plants make related compounds. Example: tropane alkaloids in Solanaceae; isoquinoline alkaloids in Papaveraceae; iridoids in Plantaginaceae; caffeine-containing plants (tea, coffee, cola) from different families.

- **Merits**: most modern system; helps find new sources of a known compound and correct doubtful taxonomy.
- **Limitations**: needs full chemical data on many plants; a compound may appear in unrelated families; chemical content changes with environment.

## Remember

- Pharmacognosy = pharmakon (drug) + gnosis (knowledge); term usually credited to Seydler (1815).
- Sources: plants, animals, microbes, marine organisms, minerals and plant tissue culture.
- Morphine — first alkaloid (Sertürner); quinine — Pelletier and Caventou, 1820; aspirin — Hoffmann, 1897; dicoumarol from spoiled sweet clover → warfarin; penicillin — Fleming 1928; cephalosporin — Brotzu, Sardinia; taxol — *Taxus brevifolia*, microtubule stabiliser; artemisinin — Tu Youyou, *Artemisia annua*, Nobel 2015.
- IP — Indian Pharmacopoeia Commission; API and UPI — PCIM&H under Ministry of AYUSH.
- Official = in the pharmacopoeia; codified = written in classical texts of a traditional system.
- Six classifications: alphabetical, morphological (organised/unorganised), taxonomical, chemical, pharmacological, chemotaxonomic — learn one merit and one limitation for each.
