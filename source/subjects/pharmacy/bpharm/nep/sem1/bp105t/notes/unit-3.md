# Unit 3: Quality control of drugs of natural origin (WHO guidelines)

A herbal drug can be the wrong plant, a mixture of plants, an old exhausted sample or full of sand and still look much like the real thing. **Evaluation** means confirming a crude drug's identity and checking its quality and purity. The World Health Organization's *Quality control methods for medicinal plant materials* (1998, later updated) gives the standard methods, and pharmacopoeias such as the IP and the Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India use the same tests. This unit is a favourite for long answers: "methods of evaluation of crude drugs" and "adulteration" are asked often, and numericals on stomatal index or foaming index appear as short answers.

## 3.1 Adulteration

**Adulteration** is the substitution of the original crude drug, partly or fully, with another material, or the addition of material that lowers its quality, purity or strength.

### Why it happens

- Shortage of the genuine drug and high price.
- Confusion of vernacular names (one local name used for several plants).
- Lack of knowledge of the true source.
- Similar appearance of two plants.
- Careless collection and poor storage.
- Deliberate fraud to increase weight or profit.

### Types of adulteration

Adulteration may be **unintentional** (accidental, through ignorance or careless collection) or **intentional** (deliberate). Common types:

| Type | What happens | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Substitution with inferior commercial varieties** | A cheaper, related variety or species is sold as the official drug | Arabian senna (*Cassia angustifolia* from Arabia) for Indian (Tinnevelly) senna; *Strychnos nux-blanda* seeds for nux vomica |
| **Substitution with superficially similar, inferior drugs** | A different plant that looks alike is used | Belladonna leaves mixed with *Ailanthus* or *Phytolacca* leaves; papaya seeds in black pepper; other *Swertia* species for chirata |
| **Substitution with exhausted drugs** | The drug is used once (active part extracted) and then sold again, sometimes re-coloured | Exhausted ginger and clove (volatile oil removed); saffron re-dyed after extraction |
| **Substitution with artificially made substances** | A fake article is made to look like the drug | Artificial coffee beans or nutmeg moulded from paste |
| **Addition of worthless heavy materials** | Sand, stones, brick powder, lead pieces added to raise weight | Stones in liquorice root; sand in saffron or in powdered drugs |
| **Addition of synthetic principles** | A cheaper synthetic chemical added to fake the active principle or strength | Citral added to lemon oil; benzyl benzoate added to balsam of Peru |
| **Use of other parts of the same plant** | Parts with less activity mixed in | Clove stalks and mother cloves (fruits) mixed with clove buds; stems with senna leaves; saffron mixed with its own stamens |
| **Mixing with other parts or foreign plants** | Florets of other flowers added | Safflower (*Carthamus tinctorius*) florets in saffron |
| **Deterioration / spoilage** | Loss of quality from age, moisture, mould, insects | Mouldy, insect-eaten drugs |

Some textbooks also use the words **admixture** (addition of one substance to another by accident), **sophistication** (deliberate addition of inferior material), **substitution** (a totally different article in place of the genuine) and **inferiority** (natural sub-standard material).

## 3.3 Organoleptic (morphological) evaluation

Evaluation by the **senses**: sight, touch, smell, taste and sometimes sound (fracture). It is quick and is the first step in the market.

| Character | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Colour | Yellow of turmeric; red-brown of cinnamon |
| Odour | Aromatic (clove, cardamom); characteristic (asafoetida, garlic) |
| Taste | Bitter (chirata, kutki, andrographis); sweet (liquorice); pungent (ginger); mucilaginous (ispaghula) |
| Size and shape | Quills of cinnamon; boat-shaped ispaghula seeds; nail-shaped clove |
| Surface and texture | Smooth, wrinkled, hairy |
| Fracture | Short (starchy roots), fibrous (barks), splintery |
| Special features | Wavy cork of cinnamon; transverse wrinkles of ginger |

It needs experience and is not enough alone, because many adulterants look similar.

## 3.4 Microscopic evaluation

Used to identify drugs, especially **powders**, where the outer form is lost.

### (a) Qualitative microscopy

A thin transverse section or the powder is mounted in a reagent and the characteristic cells and contents are looked for:

- **Trichomes** (covering and glandular hairs) — kind and shape.
- **Stomata** — types: anomocytic (ranunculaceous), anisocytic (cruciferous), diacytic (caryophyllaceous), paracytic (rubiaceous).
- **Calcium oxalate crystals** — prisms, rosettes (cluster crystals), needles (raphides), microsphenoidal sand.
- **Starch grains** — shape, size, hilum.
- **Fibres, sclereids (stone cells), vessels, cork, oil glands, secretory cells.**

**Staining reagents used**

| Reagent | Detects | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phloroglucinol + concentrated HCl | Lignin | Pink to red |
| Iodine solution | Starch | Blue |
| Sudan III | Fats, oils, cutin | Red |
| Ruthenium red | Mucilage, pectin | Pink |
| Ferric chloride | Tannins | Blue-black or green-black |
| Chloral hydrate | Clears tissues (removes starch and chlorophyll) so crystals and hairs are seen | — |
| Weak acetic acid / HCl | Calcium carbonate dissolves with effervescence; calcium oxalate does not dissolve in acetic acid | — |

### (b) Quantitative microscopy (leaf constants and spore method)

| Constant | Definition | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Stomatal number** | Average number of stomata per square mm of epidermis | Varies with leaf age and environment, so less reliable |
| **Stomatal index** | Percentage proportion of stomata to all epidermal cells (each stoma counted as one cell): **SI = S × 100 / (E + S)**, where S = stomata and E = epidermal cells in the same area | Fairly constant for a species; distinguishes close species (e.g. Indian and Alexandrian senna) |
| **Vein-islet number** | Number of vein-islets (small areas of green tissue enclosed by the smallest veins) per square mm of leaf surface, midway between midrib and margin | Constant for a species, independent of leaf size |
| **Vein-termination number** | Number of vein endings (veinlet terminations) per square mm | Identification of leaf drugs |
| **Palisade ratio** | Average number of palisade cells beneath one epidermal cell | Distinguishes leaves of related species |

**Worked example (stomatal index)**: in one field of the lower epidermis, 18 stomata and 82 epidermal cells are counted. SI = 18 × 100 / (82 + 18) = 1,800/100 = **18**.

**Lycopodium spore method** (quantitative microscopy of powders): lycopodium spores are very uniform in size and number — about **94,000 spores per mg**. A known weight of lycopodium is mixed with a known weight of the powdered drug; in the same microscope fields the characteristic particles of the drug and the spores are counted. Since the number of spores added is known, the amount of genuine drug can be calculated:

```text
% of genuine drug = (N × W × 94000 × 100) / (S × M × P)
   N = characteristic particles counted        S = lycopodium spores counted in the same fields
   W = weight of lycopodium (mg)               M = weight of sample (mg)
   P = number of characteristic particles per mg of pure drug
```

It is used for powders with countable particles (starch grains, pollen, cells of uniform size) — e.g. to find the amount of genuine drug in a mixed powder.

## 3.5 Physical evaluation

Physical constants of drugs are fixed within limits. Main ones:

- **Moisture content** (see 3.7), **ash values**, **extractive values**.
- **Viscosity** of gums and mucilages.
- **Melting point** of waxes and fats; **solubility**.
- **Optical rotation** and **refractive index** of oils.
- **Swelling index** of mucilage-containing seeds.
- **Foreign organic matter**, **crude fibre** content.

## 3.6 Chemical evaluation

- **Chemical tests** for groups of constituents: Dragendorff's and Mayer's reagents for alkaloids; Borntrager's test for anthraquinones; Keller–Killiani test for cardiac glycosides; foam test for saponins; ferric chloride for tannins; Shinoda test for flavonoids.
- **Chemical constants**: acid value, saponification value, iodine value, ester value of oils and fats.
- **Assay** of the active constituent: titration, UV spectrophotometry, HPLC, HPTLC. Example: total alkaloids in cinchona; sennosides in senna.
- **Chromatographic fingerprinting** (TLC, HPTLC, HPLC) shows the pattern of constituents and detects adulterants.

## 3.7 Physicochemical parameters (WHO methods)

### (a) Foreign organic matter

Any part of the plant other than that specified (stems with leaves) or other plants, insects and moulds. A weighed sample is spread out and the foreign matter picked out, weighed and expressed as a percentage. WHO generally allows not more than **2%** unless the monograph says otherwise.

### (b) Moisture content (loss on drying)

Excess moisture leads to enzyme action, mould growth and weight fraud.

- **Loss on drying (gravimetric)**: the sample is heated at 105 °C to constant weight; loss in weight = water (plus any volatile matter).
- **Azeotropic (toluene) distillation**: for drugs containing volatile oil; the drug is distilled with toluene, water co-distils and separates in a graduated receiver, so the oil loss does not give a false result.
- **Karl Fischer titration**: chemical method for small amounts of water.

### (c) Ash values

The residue left after burning a drug shows its inorganic content. Too much ash = earth, sand or other mineral matter.

| Ash value | How obtained | What it shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Total ash** | Drug incinerated in a silica crucible at a temperature not above 450 °C until free of carbon | All inorganic matter — natural (physiological) plus added (non-physiological, e.g. sand) |
| **Acid-insoluble ash** | Total ash boiled with dilute HCl, the insoluble residue weighed | Mostly silica (sand, soil) — best indicator of earthy contamination |
| **Water-soluble ash** | Total ash boiled with water, the soluble part found by difference | Detects material exhausted with water |
| **Sulphated ash** | Drug ignited after treatment with sulfuric acid | Used for some drugs; gives a more constant value |

### (d) Extractive values

The amount of a drug that dissolves in a solvent; useful when no good assay exists and to detect exhausted drugs.

- **Alcohol-soluble extractive**: 5 g drug macerated with 100 ml ethanol (of stated strength) for 24 h, shaken often for the first 6 h; 20 ml of filtrate evaporated and the residue dried at 105 °C and weighed.
- **Water-soluble extractive**: same with chloroform water (cold maceration) or by hot extraction.
- **Ether-soluble extractive**: volatile (and non-volatile) ether extract, for drugs with oils and resins.

A low extractive value suggests an exhausted or adulterated drug.

### (e) Bitterness value

Used for bitter drugs (chirata, kutki, gentian, andrographis). The bitterness of a series of dilutions of the drug extract is compared with that of **quinine hydrochloride**, whose bitterness value is set at **200,000** units per g. Volunteers taste dilutions and find the lowest concentration that still tastes bitter (threshold).

```text
Bitterness value (units/g) = (2000 × C) / (A × B)
   A = concentration of the stock solution of drug (mg/ml)
   B = volume (ml) of stock solution in the tube with the threshold bitter concentration
   C = amount of quinine hydrochloride (mg) in the tube with the threshold bitter concentration
```

### (f) Foaming index

Saponins produce a lasting foam when shaken with water. A decoction of the drug is placed in a series of tubes in increasing volumes (made up to 10 ml with water); each tube is shaken for 15 seconds and left for 15 minutes, and the foam height measured.

```text
Foaming index = 1000 / a
   a = volume (ml) of decoction in the tube where the foam height is 1 cm
```

If the foam is less than 1 cm in every tube, the foaming index is reported as less than 100. Example: if 1 cm foam appears in the tube with 4 ml decoction, foaming index = 1000/4 = **250**.

### (g) Haemolytic activity (haemolytic potential)

Saponins rupture red blood cells (haemolysis). Dilutions of the drug extract are mixed with a blood suspension and compared with a reference saponin of known activity (1,000 units per g).

```text
Haemolytic activity = 1000 × a / b
   a = amount (g) of reference saponin giving total haemolysis
   b = amount (g) of plant material giving total haemolysis
```

Used for saponin drugs (liquorice, shatavari, quillaia); haemolysis by saponins is why such drugs must not be injected.

### (h) Swelling index

The volume (ml) occupied by **1 g** of a drug, including any adhering mucilage, after it has swollen in water in a measuring cylinder for the time stated in the monograph. Used for mucilage-containing drugs — ispaghula (psyllium), linseed, fenugreek. A low value suggests an adulterant or old drug.

### (i) Viscosity

Measured with an Ostwald (capillary) viscometer or a rotational viscometer; used for gums and mucilages (acacia, tragacanth, ispaghula) and oils.

### (j) Optical rotation

Many plant constituents are optically active. Rotation of plane-polarised light by a liquid (volatile oil) or a solution is measured with a **polarimeter**. Specific rotation [α] = 100 α / (l × c), where α = observed rotation, l = tube length in dm, c = g per 100 ml. Example: used to check volatile oils and to detect adulteration of oils with cheaper oils of different rotation.

### (k) Refractive index

The ratio of the speed of light in air to that in the substance, measured with an **Abbe refractometer** at a stated temperature (usually 20 or 25 °C). Each oil has a fixed range; a change suggests adulteration.

### (l) Acid value

The number of **mg of potassium hydroxide (KOH)** needed to neutralise the **free fatty acids** in **1 g** of a fat or oil. A high acid value means the oil has become rancid (hydrolysis on storage).

### (m) Saponification value

The number of **mg of KOH** needed to **saponify** (convert to soap) the esters and neutralise the free acids in **1 g** of fat or oil. It is inversely related to the average molecular weight of the fatty acids and detects adulteration with mineral oil (which does not saponify, so the value falls).

Related constants: **ester value** = saponification value − acid value; **iodine value** = g of iodine absorbed by 100 g of oil (shows unsaturation).

## 3.8 Biological evaluation

Used when the active principle cannot be measured chemically, or when the total activity matters.

- **Bioassay on animals or tissues**: the response produced by the drug is compared with that of a standard preparation. Classic examples: digitalis activity measured in pigeons or guinea pigs; ergot alkaloid activity on the cock's comb or isolated uterus.
- **Microbiological assay**: antibiotics (zone of inhibition against a test organism).
- **Microbial limit tests**: total aerobic count, yeasts and moulds, absence of *E. coli* and *Salmonella* — required by WHO and pharmacopoeias for herbal materials.
- Other contaminants checked: **pesticide residues**, **heavy metals** (lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic), **aflatoxins** and radioactive contamination.

## 3.9 DNA barcoding

**DNA barcoding** identifies a species from a short, standard stretch of its DNA, just as a barcode identifies a product in a shop.

```text
Sample (powder, leaf) → DNA extraction → PCR amplification of barcode region
   → sequencing → compare sequence with reference library (e.g. BOLD, GenBank) → species identified
```

- **Plant barcode regions**: chloroplast genes **rbcL** and **matK** (the core plant barcode), plus the nuclear **ITS / ITS2** region and the chloroplast **trnH–psbA** spacer.
- **Uses**: identifies powdered and cut drugs where microscopy fails; detects substitution and adulteration in herbal products (for example finding other species in commercial "chirata" or herbal capsules); confirms identity of endangered species in trade; does not depend on plant age, season or part.
- **Limitations**: shows **identity only**, not quality, constituent content or potency; DNA is broken down in heavily processed extracts and oils; needs a good reference database and laboratory; closely related species may share the same barcode.

## Remember

- Adulteration types: inferior varieties, look-alike drugs, exhausted drugs, artificial articles, heavy materials, synthetic principles, other parts of the same plant (clove stalks, saffron stamens, safflower).
- Five evaluation methods: organoleptic, microscopic, physical, chemical, biological.
- Stomatal index = S × 100/(E + S); lycopodium ≈ 94,000 spores per mg.
- Phloroglucinol + HCl → lignin pink; iodine → starch blue; Sudan III → oil red; ruthenium red → mucilage.
- Total ash at not more than 450 °C; acid-insoluble ash shows sand; water-soluble ash shows water-exhausted drug.
- Foaming index = 1000/a; haemolytic activity = 1000a/b; bitterness value against quinine HCl (200,000).
- Acid value and saponification value are in mg KOH per g of oil.
- DNA barcode regions: rbcL, matK, ITS2, trnH–psbA; barcoding gives identity, not potency.
